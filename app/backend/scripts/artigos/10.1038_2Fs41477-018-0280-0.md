# **RNA polymerase II activity revealed by GRO-seq and pNET-seq in** *Arabidopsis*

**Jiafu Zhu1,2,5, Min Liu1,3,4,5, Xiaobin Liu1 and Zhicheng Dong  1,3\***

**RNA polymerase II (Pol II) plays an essential role in gene expression. We used plant native elongating transcript sequencing and global run-on sequencing to profile nascent RNAs genome wide in** *Arabidopsis***. We found that Pol II tends to accumulate downstream of the transcription start site (TSS). Moreover, Pol II with an unphosphorylated carboxyl-terminal domain (CTD) mainly accumulates downstream of the TSS, while Pol II with a Ser 5P CTD associates with spliceosomes, and Pol II with a Ser 2P CTD presents a sharp peak within 250 base pairs downstream of the polyadenylation site (PAS). Pol II pausing both at promoter-proximal regions and after PAS affects the transcription rate. Interestingly, active genes can be classified into three clusters based on the different modes of transcription. We demonstrate that these two methods are suitable to study Pol II dynamics in planta. Although transcription is conserved overall within eukaryotes, there is plant-specific regulation.**

I n eukaryotic cells, protein-coding genes, as well as some noncoding genes, are transcribed by DNA-dependent RNA polymerase II (Pol II). In a typical transcription cycle in the cells of higher eukaryotes, the first step of gene expression includes the recruitment of Pol II to form the pre-initiation complex (PIC) at a promoter, followed by transcription initiation, pausing, elongation, and termination. Large-scale assays, such as microarrays and RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq), have been used to study the transcriptome. However, these assays only measure the static RNA levels that result from the combination of transcription, post-transcriptional processing, and degradation. In animals and yeast, methods such as global run-on sequencing (GRO-seq)[1](#page-10-0) , precision nuclear run-on sequencing (PRO-seq)[2](#page-10-1) , native elongating transcript sequencing (NET-seq)[3](#page-10-2) , sequencing of short metabolically labelled RNA (4sUseq)[4](#page-10-3) and transient transcriptome sequencing (TT-seq)[5](#page-10-4) have been developed to investigate nascent RNAs genome wide. For example, GRO-seq detects nascent RNAs genome wide via the massive deep sequencing of nuclear run-on RNAs.

In addition to the catalytic core, the carboxyl-terminal domain (CTD) of the largest Pol II subunit is an important recipient of regulatory signals during the various steps of transcription[6](#page-10-5) . The CTD consists of a stretch of heptapeptide repeats with the consensus sequence Tyr 1-Ser 2-Pro3-Thr 4-Ser 5-Pro6-Ser 7, which receives various types of modifications, such as phosphorylation, methylation and acetylation. The CTD can be phosphorylated on Tyr 1, Ser 2, Thr 4, Ser 5, and Ser 7. Differential phosphorylation on Ser 2 (Ser 2P) and Ser 5 (Ser 5P) is well documented to play an important role in the transition between different transcription stages[6](#page-10-5) . To monitor different modified forms of Pol II genome wide, Nojima et al. developed a protocol called mammalian native elongating transcript sequencing (mNET-seq), in which certain modified forms of the Pol II-nascent RNA complex are immunoprecipitated using an antibody specific to the phosphorylated or unphosphorylated (unph) residues within the heptapeptide, followed by the high-throughput sequencing of the associated nascent RN[A7](#page-10-6) . The mNET-seq protocol revealed that unph Pol II accumulates at the transcription start site (TSS), that Ser 5P and Ser 2P Pol II increase gradually along the gene body and that Ser 2P Pol II accumulates around the polyadenylation site (PAS).

In plants, a plethora of studies have revealed that transcription factors play an essential role in responses to intrinsic and external stimuli[8,](#page-10-7)[9](#page-10-8) . Nonetheless, the downstream steps of transcription, such as elongation, termination and transcription-coupled processing, are much less well studied. Recently, GRO-seq has been carried out with fresh tissue from maize and *Arabidopsis*[10–](#page-10-9)[12.](#page-10-10) However, a GROseq protocol suitable for plant samples collected at multiple time points after stress treatment or at different developmental stages has not been reported. Although Ser 2P and Ser 5P Pol II profiles have been reported using ChIP-se[q13,](#page-10-11) single-nucleotide resolution maps of differential CTD isoforms throughout the transcription cycle are lacking.

Here, we used GRO-seq and plant native elongating transcript sequencing (pNET-seq) to study the global transcriptional activity in the model plant *Arabidopsis*. In contrast to a previous study, we observed that Pol II promoter-proximal pausing is a common feature in *Arabidopsis*. Although divergent promoters are frequently observed in mammalian genome[s1](#page-10-0)[,2](#page-10-1) , the promoters of most *Arabidopsis* genes are directional. Pol II isoforms with differentially phosphorylated CTDs accumulate at different stages during the transcription cycle. Ser 2P Pol II pauses immediately after the PAS, indicating the stringent control of termination in plants. Many genes display a distinct distribution pattern of Pol II CTD isoforms. The success of using nascent RNA-seq to investigate transcriptional activity in plants provides a solution to unveil rapid changes in plant transcriptional responses to a fluctuating environment.

For both GRO-seq and pNET-seq, the Pearson correlation revealed a strong correlation between the two replicates (Supplementary Fig. 2). Then, two replicates were merged for further analysis. A curve showing the percentage of active genes in libraries of different sizes indicates that for GRO-seq, 20 million reads would be sensitive enough to detect most active genes (Supplementary Fig. 3). For protein-coding genes, similar coverage was observed for both exonic and intronic regions (Supplementary Fig. 4), suggesting that the sequenced RNA was unprocessed nascent RNA. By contrast, low Pol II occupancy was observed on the annotated antisense strand (GRO-seq 4.5%; pNET-seq total 3.7%, unph 4.3%, Ser 2P 7.3%, Ser 5P 4.0%) (Supplementary Fig. 5). When expanding the annotations by 500base pairs (bp) to either side, Pol II occupancy on the sense strand increased to 93% for GRO-seq and 86% (unph) to 96% (total) for pNET-seq, indicating that the majority of engaged Pol II occupies the unannotated regions directly adjacent to annotated TSS and PAS (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 2).

We further optimized the run-on reaction by comparing the results of GRO-seq under different conditions (Supplementary Table 3). In the run-on reaction, the detergent sodium lauroyl sarcosinate (sarkosyl) was always included to prevent further transcription initiation and to remove physical barriers for Pol II elongation[14](#page-10-12)[,15](#page-10-13). GRO-seq experiments without sarkosyl displayed meta-profiles with more 5′ accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 6a,b), confirming the role of sarkosyl in preventing plant Pol II from binding to promoters. Run-on assays for *Arabidopsis* have been conducted at different temperatures[10,](#page-10-9)[11](#page-10-14)[,16.](#page-10-15) In our GRO-seq experiments, no significant differences were observed between 22 °C and 30 °C (Supplementary Fig. 6a,c). We then adopted 30 °C and 1% sarkosyl as the optimized run-on conditions.

**Pol II pauses after transcription initiation.** To profile transcriptionally engaged RNA Pol II along transcription units, we plotted the GRO-seq densities from both the sense and antisense strands of protein-coding and long non-coding genes (Fig. [6](#page-7-0)). For proteincoding genes, to rule out the effect of overlapping transcription, genes >1 kilobase (kb) in length and more than 1 kb away from their neighbouring genes on the same strand were selected. The non-coding genes used here were >1 kb long and were annotated via GRO-seq and 5′ GRO-seq in a previous study[11](#page-10-14). For both protein-coding and non-coding genes, after initiation, Pol II accumulates downstream of the TSS, as shown by GRO-seq analyses in flies[17](#page-10-16) and maiz[e10](#page-10-9), indicating that Pol II pausing occurs early in elongation. For coding genes, signals from the antisense strand were low, especially for those genes >1 kb away from their neighbours (Supplementary Fig. 7), which means that divergent transcription is rare. Thus, as in flies and maize, the promoters of *Arabidopsis* genes are unidirectional, while mammalian promoters are usually bidirectional. Regarding the non-coding genes, most of which are natural antisense RNA genes, strong antisense signals were observed due to the transcriptional activity of according protein-coding genes.

To further confirm 5′ pausing, we conducted a meta-analysis on nascent RNA that had been obtained using a second method, pNET-seq. The signals for all Pol II isoforms increased sharply immediately downstream of the TSS; the total, unph, and Ser 5P Pol II showed a maximum signal at approximately +200 bp, while Ser 2P showed a maximum at approximately +400bp (Fig. [2a,b](#page-3-0)). The signal decreased on entering the gene body region. Therefore, promoter-proximal pausing is further confirmed by the fact that all forms of the Pol II CTD accumulate within the 500 bp region downstream of the TSS.

To compare the profiles of different Pol II forms, we normalized the unph, Ser 2P, and Ser 5P signals to that of total Pol II. For coding genes, different Pol II isoforms showed different patterns: unph Pol II peaks in the promoter-proximal region and decreases toward the 3′ end of a gene, Ser 2P Pol II is low in the promoter-proximal region and peaks in after the PAS, and Ser 5P Pol II shows a similar but lower-density pattern as Ser 2P Pol II (Fig. [2c](#page-3-0)). For non-coding genes, the unph Pol II is high at the 5′ end and low at the 3′ end of non-coding genes and Ser 2P and Ser 5P Pol II show a spindle-like distribution; unlike coding genes, signals from all Pol II isoforms were not observed after the PAS (Fig. [2d\)](#page-3-0), indicating a distinct termination mechanism.

**Pausing at the promoter-proximal region as a regulatory step for transcription.** For convenience of discussion, we divided each gene into a promoter-proximal region (−150 to +150bp), gene body (gene body, +301bp to PAS), and terminator (within 300bp downstream of the PAS). The 5′ pause index (5′PI) was calculated to measure the Pol II accumulation at the promoter-proximal region (Fig. [3a,](#page-4-0) see also Methods). To further explore the relationship between pausing and transcriptional activity, we plotted the gene body density (transcriptional activity) against the promoter density (Pol II recruited for transcription initiation) or 5′PI by GRO-seq and total pNET-seq. As shown in the upper panel of Fig. [3b,](#page-4-0) genes with higher transcriptional activity (higher density in the gene body) also had higher Pol II density in the promoter-proximal region, suggesting that the transcription rate correlates positively with the rate of Pol II recruitment to the promoter. However, the 5′PI decreases as gene activity increases, suggesting that actively transcribed genes usually show a high Pol II release rate from the pausing state to the elongation state (Fig. [3b,](#page-4-0) bottom). These two observations are consistent between GRO-seq and total Pol II pNET-seq. Hence, both Pol II recruitment and pausing/ releasing are regulatory steps of gene expression at the transcriptional level in *Arabidopsis*.

**Active genes are paused or non-paused as defined by GRO-seq.** To avoid interference from promoter signals, nascent RNA density in the gene body was used to measure the transcriptional activity of genes. Genes were considered active when the signal in the gene body was >1 reads per kilobase per million, which was significantly enriched over the background in a Poisson distribution.

We further classified active genes into paused and non-paused genes based on the density of GRO-seq in both the promoter-proximal region and the gene body (Fig. [3a](#page-4-0), see Methods). In *Arabidopsis* seedlings, we found 7,533 active protein-coding genes, among which 2,894 (38.4%) were paused and 4,639 (61.6%) were non-paused (Fig. [3c](#page-4-0)). Examples of paused and non-paused genes are shown in Fig. [3d.](#page-4-0) A reanalysis of previously published data[11](#page-10-14) classified approximately 21% of active genes as paused genes (Supplementary Fig. 8). Furthermore, the unph Pol II density ratio downstream of the TSS is slightly higher in paused genes, indicating that paused Pol II is mainly unphosphorylated (Fig. [3c\)](#page-4-0). Gene ontology analysis indicated that genes involved in gravitropism and the mitotic cell cycle are enriched in the paused category, while the non-paused category is enriched with genes that function in development, hormone signalling and immune responses (Fig. [3e\)](#page-4-0).

Ser 5P Pol II is involved in cotranscriptional splicing. In eukaryotic cells, transcription and pre-mRNA splicing are coupled, and this finding has been confirmed by Ser 5P Pol II mNET-seq7. To ask whether this is also true in plants, we performed meta-analyses of all four Pol II isoforms over exon-intron junctions (Fig. 4). The Ser 2P, unph and total Pol II decrease downstream of the 5' splice site (5'SS). Around the 3' splice site (3'SS), more Pol II was detected in the downstream exon than in the upstream intron (Fig. 4a). These findings substantiate the idea that Pol II elongates more slowly in the exon than in the intron so that the spliceosome can recognize the exon and splice site and thereby splice. Regarding Ser 5P, a sharp peak was detected at the 5'SS, which was also observed in mammalian cells via mNET-seq7 and proved to be a splicing intermediate within the spliceosome complex that coprecipitated with Ser 5P Pol II (Fig. 4a,b). Hence, similar to mammalian cells, in plants, the spliceosome complex interacts with Ser 5P Pol II and performs 5'SS cleavage during elongation.

Phosphorylated Pol II pauses within a narrow region downstream of the PAS. The GRO-seq profile shows a pausing peak around the PAS regions of protein-coding genes (Fig. 2a), as reported by Hetzel et al.<sup>11</sup> To determine which Pol II isoform is enriched around the PAS, we performed meta-analyses on all four Pol II isoforms (Fig. 2a,c). From the gene body to the PAS, the signals of all four isoforms decrease; the unph and total Pol II signals continue to decrease past the PAS. However, a sharp peak of Ser 2P Pol II within 250 bp downstream of the PAS was observed, along with a minor similarly shaped peak of Ser 5P Pol II. This phenomenon is specific to plants; in mammalian cells, the Ser 2P and Ser 5P Pol II metaprofiles show a wide peak within the 3 kb region downstream of the PAS7. Therefore, in the GRO-seq profile, the pre-PAS peak is probably a mixture of different phosphorylated forms of Pol II, while the post-PAS peak is mainly Ser 2P Pol II, although we do not know which form of Pol II is more active in the run-on reaction.

Given the compact size of the Arabidopsis genome, the intergenic regions are smaller than those in mammalian genomes. The median distance from the PAS of a gene to the TSS of the downstream gene is less than 3 kb, and a quarter of gene pairs are separated by less than 1 kb (Supplementary Fig. 9). Therefore, Arabidopsis may have evolved an efficient termination mechanism that allows termination to occur within a short post-PAS region and prevents the terminating Pol II from interfering with the transcription initiation of the neighbouring downstream gene. To ask whether the distance to the downstream gene affects the termination efficiency, we categorized the genes into six classes based on the distance between the PAS and the TSS of the nearest downstream gene (Fig. 5a). No differences in the meta-profiles post-PAS were observed among these six classes of genes, indicating stringent control of termination, no matter how far away the downstream gene is (Fig. 5a). Notably, even for the first class, where the distance from the PAS to the TSS of the downstream gene is less than 250 bp, and the terminator and proximal promoter may overlap, the 3' pausing peak (GRO-seq) and post-PAS Ser 2P Pol II peak (pNET-seq) still appear (Fig. 5a, left panel). To ask whether this compact gene arrangement affects the transcriptional activity of downstream genes, we investigated the density of GRO-seq reads in the bodies of genes with different distances from their upstream genes. Neither a significant difference nor a correlation of gene activity between upstream and downstream genes was observed among all six classes with different intergenic lengths (Supplementary Fig. 10). Therefore, the termination of one gene and the initiation of its downstream gene must be highly coordinated to avoid interference. Furthermore, the most active 10% of genes display a meta-profile distinct from that of the bottom 90%. In particular, GRO-seq and Ser 2P and Ser 5P pNETseq showed much less Pol II accumulation around the PAS of the most active genes than around those of less-active genes (Fig. 5b

#### **Discussion**

**pNET-seq and GRO-seq can be used to study Pol II-mediated transcription in plants.** We analysed nascent RNAs using two different methodologies. The overall correlation between GRO-seq and total Pol II pNET-seq is 0.570 (Supplementary Fig. 12). The consistency between GRO-seq and pNET-seq confirms that we did observe nascent RNA synthesis by using these protocols. Lower correlations between GRO-seq/ pNET-seq and RNA-seq (0.351 and 0.434) suggest complicated regulation at the post-transcriptional level. There are also loci that are inconsistent between GRO-seq and total Pol II pNET-seq because GRO-seq maps the transcriptionally engaged RNA polymerases at a resolution of 50–100 nucleotides (nt), while pNET-seq profiles the nascent RNA associated with specific Pol II CTD modifications at single-nucleotide resolution. Nascent RNAs revealed by pNET-seq may be from transcriptionally engaged, paused, stopped or backtracking Pol II. Hence, a combination of these methods will reveal the overall transcriptome, as well as transcriptional details at the subgene level.

The published plant GRO-seq protocol starts with fresh tissue immediately after sample collectio[n11.](#page-10-14) We modified the nuclear isolation protocol for both run-on and Pol II immunoprecipitation by starting from frozen tissue. Therefore, both the GRO-seq and pNET-seq methods can be applied to plant samples under different conditions, such as different treatments, samples collected during a time course, or specific tissue or organs that require time to collect. As the CTD of RNA Pol II is conserved in eukaryotes, the pNETseq protocol could be applied to non-model plants without needing species-specific antibodies. Collectively, these tools allow the study of transcriptional dynamics in plants in response to an ever-changing environment and/ or through their developmental stages.

**Pol II pausing is an important feature of transcription in plants.** It is controversial whether Pol II pauses at the promoter-proximal region after transcription initiation. GRO-seq and pNET-seq metaanalyses show that Pol II accumulates at the promoter-proximal region, indicating that Pol II pauses or at least slows down after initiation (Fig. [7\)](#page-8-0). Under a stringent definition, approximately 38.4% of active genes are classified as paused. We reanalysed the published *Arabidopsis* GRO-seq data and obtained similar results (Supplementary Fig. 8). However, unlike mammalian Pol II, which pauses in a narrow region (25–50 nt), *Arabidopsis* Pol II shows a broad peak in the promoter-proximal region, indicating a loose control of Pol II pausing and release in plants. This phenomenon can be explained by the absence of the protein components that control this process, such as negative elongation factor (NELF), in the *Arabidopsis* genome. Nonetheless, CDK9-like kinase activity is required for Pol II to be released from the paused state, as supported by the FLA inhibitor experiments (Figs. [3e,f\)](#page-4-0).

We have shown that transcriptional activity, measured by the GRO-seq signal within the gene body, is positively correlated with promoter-proximal density but negatively correlated with the 5′ PI (Figs. [3b](#page-4-0) and [6b,f\)](#page-7-0). This observation supports the idea that to achieve a high transcription rate, both quick Pol II recruitment to and release from the promoter are essential. In other words, both could be important regulatory steps of transcription in plants. Although transcription factor-mediated Pol II recruitment is well studied in various plant developmental and/ or physiological processes, the significance of Pol II release from the promoter-proximal region might be under-appreciated. To address this question, it is

of interest to study GRO-seq and pNET-seq profile changes during developmental stage transitions, such as vegetative to reproductive growth, and/ or environmental changes, such as abiotic stress and the dark-to-light transition.

**Different Pol II CTD isoforms are involved in different stages of the transcription cycle.** Several lines of evidence support the idea that Ser 2 and Ser 5 phosphorylations are important for plant transcription and are involved in stress responses and the regulation of plant development[20–](#page-10-19)[23.](#page-10-20) We report in detail the distribution of unph, Ser 2P and Ser 5P genome wide; all three patterns are conserved in animals and yeast (Fig. [7b](#page-8-0)). Our data confirmed the model of dynamic Pol II phosphorylation established in these systems. Unph Pol II is recruited to the promoter and assembles in the PIC. After initiation, Pol II is phosphorylated at Ser 5 and Ser 2 and is released from the proximal promoter region to engage in productive elongation. As elongation proceeds, Pol II becomes fully phosphorylated at Ser 2P and/ or Ser 5P. Ser 2P Pol II stalls immediately after the PAS, where the nascent pre-mRNA is cleaved from Pol II complex. The Pol II complex is disassembled and the RNA fragment encoded by the DNA downstream of the PAS is degraded.

A striking finding is that Ser 2P Pol II pauses immediately downstream of the PAS. It is tempting to speculate that 3′ cleavage and polyadenylation occur in this narrow space. *Arabidopsis* has a compact genome: its 140 megabases harbour more than 27,000 genes and even more transcription units. However, there are plant species with much larger genomes. To determine whether this phenomenon is specific to small genomes or is common in the plant kingdom, it would be of interest to compare the Ser 2P Pol II meta-profile near the PAS in genomes of different sizes.

Interestingly, three different transcription modes were revealed by the abundance of Pol II isoforms along the genes. These modes described gene clusters with high, medium and low activity. Furthermore, the chromatin states are distinct in these three clusters. However, the effects of epigenetic marks on each specific step through the transcription cycle remain be elucidated via genetic and biochemical approaches, as well as via nascent RNA analysis.

**Summary.** Given that transcription is a fundamental process in almost all organisms, it is not surprising that RNA Pol II in plants shares many features with its counterparts from animals and yeast. For example, the distributions of different Pol II isoforms suggest that in *Arabidopsis*, the overall regulation of Pol II phosphorylation at different stages of transcription is conserved. However, we do observe characteristics in *Arabidopsis* that are distinct from those in mammals, including the lack of divergent promoters, the loose control of pausing in the promoter-proximal region, and a narrow post-PAS window for termination (Fig. [7\)](#page-8-0). The two methods to study nascent RNA presented here will shed light on transcriptional regulation in model and non-model plants.

#### **Methods**

**Plant growth, drug treatments and preparation of nuclei.** Seeds of the Col-0 ecotype were germinated and grown on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium for 12 d at 20 °C under 16h light and 8h dark cycles. For kinase inhibitor treatment, 200μM DRB or 200μM FLA were applied to the roots of 12-day-old plants for 0h, 0.5h, 1h, 2h or 4h. Dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) was used as a mock treatment. Approximately 2 g seedlings without roots were harvested, fash frozen and ground to a powder in liquid nitrogen. Total protein was isolated using TRIzol (Invitrogen) according to the manual and then subjected to western blotting. Te nuclear isolation protocol for GRO-seq was modifed from a previously published metho[d14](#page-10-12). Te fne seedling powder was solubilized in 30ml extraction bufer (2M hexylene glycol, 20mM PIPES bufer pH 7.0, 10mM MgCl2, 5mM 2-mercaptoethanol) and fltered through 110 μm and 60 μm nylon mesh. Triton X-100 was added to the lysates to a fnal concentration of 1% and lef on ice for 10min. Cell fractionation was performed using a 30–80% Percoll gradient solution and nuclei were collected at the 30–80% interface. Nuclei for pNET-seq were isolated according to Li et al*.* [24](#page-10-21) Te tissue powder was solubilized in 30ml lysis bufer (50mM HEPES bufer, pH 7.5, 150mM NaCl, 1mM EDTA bufer, 1%Triton X-100, 10%glycerol, 5mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 2μgml<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> pepstatin A, 2μgml<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> aprotinin, 1mM PMSF), mixed well and fltered through 110μm nylon mesh and a 40 μm cell strainer. Te nuclei were spun down at 3,200*g* for 20min at 4 °C, then washed twice with HBB bufer (25mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6, 0.44M sucrose, 10mM MgCl2, 0.1%Triton X-100, 10mM 2-mercaptoethanol) and HBC bufer (20mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 352mM sucrose, 8mM MgCl2, 0.08%Triton X-100, 8mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 20%glycerol). Te nuclei were then ready for MNase treatment.

**Nuclear run-on and nascent RNA purification.** The nuclei were washed twice with 1x transcription buffer (25mM Tris-HCl pH 7.2, 2.5mM MgCl2, 2.5mM KCl, 100mM NH4Cl, 0.5mM MnCl2, 2.5mM DTT) and then stored in 100μl nuclei storage buffer (50mM Tris-HCl pH 7.2, 5mM MgCl2, 5mM KCl, 1mM MnCl2, 5mM DTT, 50%glycerol). A total of 100μl of 2x reaction buffer (2x transcription buffer, 2% sarkosyl, 2Uμl –1 RNasin, 500μM ATP, 500μM GTP, 500μM Br-UTP, 2μM α-32P-CTP) was added to the nuclei, mixed and incubated at 30 °C for 5min. Nuclear RNA was extracted using TRIzol and subjected to alkaline hydrolysis. The reaction was neutralized using 1M Tris pH 7.0 and desalted using a p-30 RNase-free spin column (Bio-Rad). The fragmented RNA was treated with RQ1 RNase-free DNase (Promega) and purified again through a p-30 column (Bio-Rad). The RNA was dephosphorylated using Antarctic phosphatase (NEB) in the presence of SUPERase-In (Ambion). After heat-inactivating the phosphatase, the denatured RNA was incubated with anti-BrdU agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotech), which were pre-blocked in blocking buffer (0.5 x SSPE, 1mM EDTA, 0.05%Tween-20, 0.1% PVP, and 1mgml<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> BSA). Then, the beads were washed once in low-salt buffer (0.2 x SSPE, 1mM EDTA, 0.05% Tween-20), twice in high-salt buffer (0.5 x SSPE, 1mM EDTA, 0.05% Tween-20, 150mM NaCl) and twice in TET buffer (TE pH 7.4, 0.05% Tween-20). BrU-incorporated RNA was eluted four times with elution buffer (20mM DTT, 300mM NaCl, 5mM Tris pH 7.5, 1mM EDTA and 0.1%SDS). Nascent RNA was then extracted with acidic phenol/ chloroform once and with chloroform once and then coprecipitated with glycogen in ethanol overnight.

**Construction and sequencing of GRO-seq complementary DNA libraries.** We constructed the GRO-seq libraries according to the protocol by Wang et al[.25](#page-10-22) Briefly, nascent RNA was treated with T4 PNK (NEB), followed by acidic phenol/ chloroform purification. A poly-A tail was added to the nascent RNA using *Echerichia coli* Poly(A) Polymerase (NEB) and the RNA was reverse transcribed with Superscript III reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen) with an RT primer (GROoligo2, synthesized by IDT DNA, 5′-/5Phos/GA TCG TCG GAC TGT AGA ACT CTG AAC /idSp/CC TTG GCA CCC GAG AAT TCC ATT TTT TTT TTT TTT TTT TTT VN-3′). Free RT primer was removed by Exonuclease I (Fermentas) and the RNA was eliminated by alkaline hydrolysis. The resulting first-strand cDNA was resolved on a 10% TBE-urea polyacrylamide gel. Any cDNA longer than 120nt was recovered and self-ligated using CircLigase (Epicentre). The circularized single-stranded cDNA was relinearized with APE 1 (NEB). The linearized cDNA was recovered again using a 10% TBE-urea polyacrylamide gel. The cDNA was PCR-amplified using Kapa HiFi polymerase with primers (universal primer 5′AAT GAT ACG GCG ACC ACC GAG ATC TAC ACG TTC AGA GTT CTA CAG TCC GA 3′, reverse index primer R 5′CAA GCA GAA GAC GGC ATA CGA GAT NNNNNN GTG ACT GGA GTT CCT TGG CAC CCG AGA ATT CCA 3′, NNNNNN: index). The PCR products were resolved on a native 8% TBE polyacrylamide gel and the 200–330bp region was recovered. The cDNA library concentration was determined using a Qubit QuantIT (Invitrogen) and with Kapa quantitative real-time PCR according to the manual. Single-end reads of 75nt were generated using the Illumina NextSeq500 platform.

**MNase digestion and Pol II immunoprecipitation.** The obtained pellet of nuclei was resuspended in 1ml MNase buffer (20mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 5mM NaCl and 2.5mM CaCl2) and digested with 20 U MNase (TaKaRa, 20 Uμl ) for 5min at 37 °C rotating at 1,400 r.p.m. The digestion was stopped by adding 40μl of 500mM EDTA. Pol II-nascent RNA complexes were released via mild sonication. Supernatants were collected by centrifuging at 16,000*g* for 5min at 4 °C, and Pol II complexes were immunoprecipitated according to Nojima et al.[7](#page-10-6) The Pol II-bound nascent RNA was treated with T4 PNK on beads and extracted with 500μl of TRIzol reagent. The IP RNA was resolved on an 8% TBE-urea polyacrylamide gel, and RNA with sizes from 35 to 100nt was recovered.

**Construction and sequencing of pNET-seq cDNA libraries.** The 35–100 nt IP RNA was used to construct the pNET-seq cDNA library using a NEXTflex Small RNA-Seq Kit v3 (Bio Scientific) according to the manual. The cDNA library was resolved on a native 6% TBE polyacrylamide gel, and the 150–230bp range was recovered. The cDNA library concentration was determined using a Qubit QuantIT (Invitrogen) and with Kapa quantitative real-time PCR. Paired-end reads were generated on the Illumina HiSeq2500 platform.

**GRO-seq data processing.** After trimming the adaptors and filtering the lowquality data by cutadap[t26,](#page-10-23) reads originating from rRNA were filtered by aligning against an index consisting of the 45S and 5S transcripts using STA[R27](#page-10-24) with default

settings. Unmapped reads were subsequently aligned to the TAIR10 version of the *Arabidopsis* genome (option --outFilterMultimapNmax 1). The 5′ coordinate of each read was considered the position of the engaged polymerase for future analyses. Reads mapped to non-coding RNAs (rRNA, tRNA, snRNA and snoRNA) and their 300bp upstream and downstream regions were removed. Unmappable regions with a read size of 50bp of the genome (TAIR10) were identified using the Tallymer programme in the Genome Tools packag[e28.](#page-10-25) Read densities for each region were calculated by counting the number of reads in each region and then normalizing to library size and mappable length.

**pNET-seq data processing.** PCR duplications were filtered by clumpify.sh from BBMap[29](#page-10-26) and then adaptor trimming; mapping to the reference genome; and removing reads from rRNAs, tRNAs and snoRNAs were conducted as described above. Since the 5′ end of read2 represented the last nucleotide incorporated by the polymerase, the aligned reads were trimmed to keep only the 5′ nucleotide of read2, with the directionality indicated by read1.

**5′ pause index.** Promoter regions were defined as ±150bp from the TSS, where the promoter-proximal peaks were identified in 50bp windows that shifted by 5bp. In each window, GRO-seq read density was calculated. The window with the highest read density was chosen as the promoter-proximal peak, and if multiple windows had the same read density, then the most 5′ of these windows was chosen. Significantly paused genes were identified using Fisher's exact test to compare the density of reads in the promoter-proximal peak to the density of reads in the gene body compared to a uniform distribution of all reads based on the number of mappable bases. The *P* values from the tests were converted to FDR-corrected *q* values, and a cutoff of *q*=0.001 and 5′PI ≥3 was used for significance.

**Metagene profiles and heatmaps.** The average Pol II distributions across TSS regions (±1 kb) or PAS regions (±1 kb) are illustrated by metagene profiles. Regions were divided into 10bp bins. The read density in each bin was calculated and then the average value was plotted. To determine the average profiles of splice junctions, actively transcribed genes with no alternative isoforms in their TAIR10 annotations were used to present the average read density in a region of 50bp upstream and downstream of the splice sites. Reads were counted and normalized for each base, and then the average value was plotted. For each meta-profile, ≥1% of the most extreme coverage values in each location were trimmed before averaging; error bars indicate the s.e.m. across each bin.

To generate heatmaps, each gene was divided into 100 bins, the region was extended to 500bp upstream of the TSS and 500bp of downstream of the PAS, and the region was binned into 10bp bins. In each bin, read density was calculated and log2 transformed. Then, heatmaps were generated using the R package pheatma[p30](#page-10-27).

**Cluster and gene ontology enrichment analysis.** The log10-transformed read density of the promoter-proximal region (−150 to +150bp from the TSS), gene body (+301bp from the TSS to the PAS) and terminator (PAS to 300bp downstream of the PAS) for each Pol II isoform (unph, Ser 2P, Ser 5P) were used for cluster analysis. The datasets comprised nine variables on 7,533 active protein genes. Principal component analysis was used to reduce the dimensions of the data, and the first five principal components accounted for 95.6% of the variance. These five principal components were retained for further clustering by k-means. The number of clusters was chosen based on average silhouette widths, which were calculated using the R package fpc[31](#page-10-28). A higher silhouette width is preferred to determine the optimal number of clusters. For each cluster, gene ontology enrichment was performed using the Bioconductor package clusterProfiler[32](#page-11-0).

**Chromatin state analysis of different gene clusters.** The chromatin states were downloaded from a public database (PCSD, [http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/](http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/chromstates) [chromstates\)](http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/chromstates)[19](#page-10-18). The mean percentage of different chromatin states in the promoterproximal, gene body and terminator regions of the genes were calculated for each cluster. BEDTools[33](#page-11-1) was used to calculate the breadth of coverage for each chromatin state in each gene region. TEs and the centromere-specific chromatin states 31–36 were omitted.

**RNA-seq analysis.** RNA-seq libraries were prepared using a TruSeq Stranded Total RNA kit (Illumina) according to the manual and sequenced on a HiSeq2000 platform by Shanghai Hanyu Biotech. After removing adaptor sequences and low-quality data, the sequences were aligned to the TAIR10 version of the *Arabidopsis* genome using Tophat[234,](#page-11-2) and the mapped reads in each gene were quantified using HTSeq[35.](#page-11-3)
