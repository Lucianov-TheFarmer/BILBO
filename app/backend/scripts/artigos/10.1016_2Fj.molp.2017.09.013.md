**Running title:** m<sup>5</sup>C RNA modification in *Arabidopsis* 

# **Short Summary:**

We have applied m<sup>5</sup>C RNA immunoprecipitation followed by deep sequencing approach to achieve transcriptome-wide profiling of m<sup>5</sup>C sites in *Arabidopsis* seedlings, and revealed several novel features of m<sup>5</sup>C in *Arabidopsis* mRNA. Characterization of tRNA specific methyltransferase 4B (TRM4B) suggests that it acts as a potential mRNA m<sup>5</sup>C methyltransferase, and mediates m<sup>5</sup>C levels and mRNA stability of target genes involved in root development.

#### ABSTRACT

5-methylcytosine (m<sup>5</sup>C) is a well-characterized DNA modification, and is also predominantly reported in abundant noncoding RNAs in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes. However, the distribution and biological functions of m<sup>5</sup>C in plant mRNAs remain largely unknown. Here we report transcriptome-wide profiling of RNA m<sup>5</sup>C in Arabidopsis thaliana through applying m<sup>5</sup>C RNA immunoprecipitation followed by deep sequencing approach (m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq). LC-MS/MS and dot blot analyses reveal a dynamic pattern of m<sup>5</sup>C mRNA modification in various tissues and at different developmental stages. m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq analysis identifies 6.045  $m^5$ C peaks in 4.465 expressed genes in young seedlings. m<sup>5</sup>C is enriched in coding sequences with two peaks located immediately after start codons and before stop codons, and is associated with mRNAs with low translation activity. We show that a RNA (cytosine-5)-methyltransferase, tRNA specific methyltransferase 4B (TRM4B), exhibits the m<sup>5</sup>C RNA methyltransferase activity. Mutations in *TRM4B* display defects in root development and decreased m<sup>5</sup>C peaks. TRM4B affects transcript levels of the genes involved in root development, which is positively correlated with their mRNA stability and m<sup>5</sup>C levels. Our results suggest that m<sup>5</sup>C in mRNA is a new epitranscriptome marker in Arabidopsis, and that regulation of this modification is an integral part of gene regulatory networks underlying plant development.

**Key words:** 5-methylcytosine (m<sup>5</sup>C), *Arabidopsis*, RNA methylation, TRM4B, root development

#### INTRODUCTION

Among over 100 distinct chemically modified nucleotides characterized in RNA, most of them are identified in noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), such as transfer RNA (tRNA) and ribosomal RNA (rRNA) (Machnicka et al., 2013). Although RNA modifications are best characterized in short ncRNAs like tRNAs, recent technical advances have allowed to reveal several widespread modifications in eukaryotic mRNAs, including N<sup>6</sup>-methyladenosine (m<sup>6</sup>A), N<sup>1</sup>-methyladenosine (m<sup>1</sup>A), 5-methylcytosine (m<sup>5</sup>C), 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (hm<sup>5</sup>C), inosine and pseudouridine (Frye et al., 2016; Li et al., 2016a; Zhao et al., 2017). While it has been suggested that these modifications may affect various aspects of mRNA metabolism and function, their biological roles in living organisms are still largely unknown.

m<sup>5</sup>C is present in both DNA and RNA. m<sup>5</sup>C modification in DNA is well studied and plays an important role in epigenetic regulation (Jones, 2012). However, understanding of m<sup>5</sup>C in RNA is rather limited, although it has long been known that m<sup>5</sup>C occurs in eukaryotic mRNA (Dubin and Taylor, 1975). Research on m<sup>5</sup>C has so far been focused on its functions in tRNA and rRNA, and has revealed that m<sup>5</sup>C could affect RNA stability, tRNA recognition, translational fidelity and RNA processing (Chen et al., 1993; Hong et al., 1997; Alexandrov et al., 2006; Schaefer et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2012; Hussain et al., 2013b; Gigova et al., 2014). Until recently, the advances in high-throughput sequencing techniques have provided potent tools for transcriptome-wide profiling of m<sup>5</sup>C in both coding and non-coding RNAs (Squires et al., 2017). For examples, m<sup>5</sup>C sites have been identified in mRNAs from several archaeal species (Edelheit et al., 2013), while more than 10,000 m<sup>5</sup>C sites have been found in mRNAs from human HeLa cells (Squires et al., 2012).

Along with the studies in investigating m<sup>5</sup>C sites in various organisms, several m<sup>5</sup>C methyltransferases have been reported in yeast and metazoan organisms. In yeast, tRNA methyltransferase 4 (TRM4) modifies multiple tRNAs (Motorin and Grosjean, 1999), while nucleolar proteins, Nop2 and Rcm1, methylate rRNA (Sharma et al., 2013). In addition, the methyltransferases, tRNA aspartic acid methyltransferase 1 (TRDMT1, also known as DNMT2) and NOP2/Sun RNA methyltransferase family member 2 (NSUN2), have been shown to modify tRNAs in mammals (Goll et al., 2006; Schaefer et al., 2010; Tuorto et al., 2012).

Despite the progress in understanding m<sup>5</sup>C in RNA, distribution and biological functions of m<sup>5</sup>C in mRNA as well as the relevant modifying enzymes remain largely unknown in eukaryotic organisms, including plants. A recent study has applied bisulfite sequencing to identify transcriptome-wide m<sup>5</sup>C sites in mRNAs, long non-coding RNAs, and other non-coding RNAs across three tissue types in Arabidopsis thaliana (David et al., 2017). While this study demonstrates the dynamic presence of m<sup>5</sup>C in Arabidopsis RNAs, limitations of the bisulfite sequencing approach have lowered down the resolution and accuracy on m<sup>5</sup>C sites found in Arabidopsis. Bisulfite sequencing relies on the conversion of every single unmethylated cytosine residue to uracil, which further allows the mapping of global m<sup>5</sup>C methylation patterns. However, there are two major disadvantages that limit its application in identifying m<sup>5</sup>C sites in RNA. First, the bisulfite-mediated cytosine conversion is inhibited by RNA secondary structures and several other cytosine modifications other than m<sup>5</sup>C, including hm<sup>5</sup>C, N<sup>4</sup>-methylcytosine, 3methylcytosine, N4,2'-O-dimethylcytidine and N<sup>4</sup>-acetylcytosine (Shapiro et al., 1973; Schaefer et al., 2009; Huang et al., 2010; Behm-Ansmant et al., 2011; Amort et al., 2017). Thus, bisulfite sequencing is unable to distinguish m<sup>5</sup>C from other incompletely converted cytosines or other cytosine modifications in RNA, such as hm<sup>5</sup>C. Notably, hm<sup>5</sup>C has been reported to occur at high levels in RNA in many species, including Arabidopsis (Huber et al., 2015; Delatte et al., 2016). Thus, there are inevitable false positives in RNA m<sup>5</sup>C sites identified by bisulfite sequencing. Second, bisulfite treatment leads to significant degradation of RNA and prevents pre-enrichment of m<sup>5</sup>C-containing RNA, which make it technically difficult to detect m<sup>5</sup>C in low-abundance RNA (Hussain et al., 2013a). These disadvantages indicate that it is necessary to identify m<sup>5</sup>C sites in Arabidopsis using an alternative approach.

In this study, we have applied m<sup>5</sup>C RNA immunoprecipitation followed by deep sequencing approach (m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq) using an m<sup>5</sup>C-specific antibody to achieve transcriptome-wide profiling of m<sup>5</sup>C sites in *Arabidopsis*. High specificity of the m<sup>5</sup>C antibody and avoidance of harsh chemical and temperature treatment encountered in bisulfite sequencing have allowed us to identify 6,045 m<sup>5</sup>C peaks in 4,465 expressed genes in young seedlings, which was around 5 times higher than the total number of m<sup>5</sup>C sites identified in all RNAs from various tissues by bisulfite sequencing (David et al., 2017). Further analyses of m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq data have provided several novel features of m<sup>5</sup>C in *Arabidopsis* mRNA, including hitherto unknown m<sup>5</sup>C consensus motifs and their enrichment pattern in coding sequences (CDSs) with two peaks

located immediately after start codons and before stop codons. We have also found that m<sup>5</sup>C modification is associated with mRNAs with low translation activity. In addition, characterization of potential RNA (cytosine-5)-methyltransferases (RCMTs) has revealed that tRNA specific methyltransferase 4B (TRM4B) (Pavlopoulou and Kossida, 2009; Chen et al., 2010; David et al., 2017) serves as a potential mRNA m<sup>5</sup>C methyltransferase. Mutations in *TRM4B* display defects in root development and decreased m<sup>5</sup>C levels in root mRNA. We have further demonstrated that TRM4B affects transcript levels of the genes involved in root development, which is positively correlated with their mRNA stability and m<sup>5</sup>C levels. Taken together, our findings provide new insights into the transcriptome-wide distribution of m<sup>5</sup>C sites in mRNA and its potential regulatory functions in *Arabidopsis*.

#### RESULTS

#### m<sup>5</sup>C RNA Modification Is Dynamic in Arabidopsis

To examine m<sup>5</sup>C RNA modification (Figure 1A) in Arabidopsis, we first performed dot blot assays of various tissues and seedlings at different developmental stages using an anti-m<sup>5</sup>C antibody. This antibody has been widely used to specifically detect m<sup>5</sup>C modification in DNA, and has also been recently used in m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq in *Sulfolobus solfataricus* RNA (Edelheit et al., 2013). However, its specificity and sensitivity in detecting m<sup>5</sup>C RNA modification have never been examined. We found that this m<sup>5</sup>C antibody selectively bound to a m<sup>5</sup>C-modified RNA oligo, but exhibited negligible binding to an unmodified cytosine (C) oligo and other modified oligos, including m<sup>1</sup>A, m<sup>6</sup>A, hm<sup>5</sup>C and 5-formylcytosine (f<sup>5</sup>C) (Figure 1B; Supplemental Figure 1A). Binding of this  $m^5C$  antibody to the  $m^5C$  oligo was competed by pre-incubation with an increasing amount of the m<sup>5</sup>C RNA oligo, but not by the unmodified C oligo (Supplemental Figure 1B). Similarly, this binding was competed by 5-methylcytosine triphosphate (m<sup>5</sup>CTP), but not by cytosine triphosphate (CTP) or other modified CTPs, including 5hvdroxymethylcytosine triphosphates ( $hm^5CTP$ ) and 5-formylcytosine ( $f^5CTP$ ) (Supplemental Figure 1C). Collectively, these data demonstrate high sensitivity and specificity of the m<sup>5</sup>C antibody for detecting m<sup>5</sup>C RNA modification. We then used this antibody to carry out dot blot assays for examining m<sup>5</sup>C in different RNA species in Arabidopsis seedlings, including total RNA, mRNA, small RNA and rRNA. m<sup>5</sup>C was present in all RNA species examined with the strongest signal detected in mRNA (Figure 1C). m<sup>5</sup>C was detected at various levels in mRNA extracted from different tissues examined, including roots, rosette leaves, cauline leaves, stems, open flowers, flower buds, and siliques (Figure 1D), indicating a spatial change in m<sup>5</sup>C levels in different tissues. Furthermore, there was a slightly increasing trend of m<sup>5</sup>C modification in mRNA from 3- to 15-day-old whole seedlings undergoing vegetative, floral transitional, and reproductive stages (Figure 1E).

To confirm the results of dot blot assays and accurately quantify m<sup>5</sup>C levels, RNA was digested into individual ribonucleosides and analyzed by liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). We included a pure commercial m<sup>5</sup>C standard as a positive reference, and identified m<sup>5</sup>C in a given sample only when both its retention time and fragmentation pattern matched those of the m<sup>5</sup>C standard (Supplemental Figure 2A). In

agreement with the results of dot blot assays, mRNA has the highest abundance of m<sup>5</sup>C (0.028% m<sup>5</sup>C/C) among the different RNA species tested (Figure 1C; Supplemental Figure 2B). Among various tissues examined, siliques and rosette leaves displayed the highest (0.036%) and lowest abundance (0.010%) of m<sup>5</sup>C in their mRNA, respectively (Figure 1D; Supplemental Figure 2C). The m<sup>5</sup>C abundance slightly increased in mRNA extracted from 3-day-old (0.027%) to 15-day-old (0.033%) whole seedlings (Figure 1E; Supplemental Figure 2D). In contrast, DNA m<sup>5</sup>C was not detectable in these mRNA samples, thus excluding the possibility of DNA contamination (Supplemental Figure 2E). Taken together, these results demonstrate a dynamic pattern of m<sup>5</sup>C RNA modification in various *Arabidopsis* tissues and at different developmental stages. In addition, we found m<sup>5</sup>C RNA modification in various plant species, including *Oryza sativa* (Nipponbare), *Medicago truncatula* (R108), *Zea mays* (B73) and *Setaria italica* (Yugu1), with the highest level in *Medicago truncatula* (Figures 1F and 1G), suggesting that m<sup>5</sup>C RNA modification widely occurs in plants.

# Transcriptome-Wide Mapping of m<sup>5</sup>C in *Arabidopsis* by m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq

To identify and localize m<sup>5</sup>C sites at the transcriptome-wide level, we applied m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq to RNA extracted from 9-day-old *Arabidopsis* whole seedlings. RNA was fragmented into ~100nucleotide-long oligonucleotides (input) and immunoprecipitated using the anti-m<sup>5</sup>C antibody (Figure 2A). Libraries were prepared from immunoprecipitated (IP) and input control RNA, and two biological replicates with high Pearson correlation coefficients were generated (Supplemental Figure 3A). After filtering out reads aligned to annotated rRNA sequences, approximately 2.1–2.6 and 3.5–3.7 million distinct reads for IP and input, respectively, were uniquely aligned to the *Arabidopsis* TAIR 10 genome. After combining the libraries of two replicates, we identified 6,045 putative m<sup>5</sup>C peaks (P < 0.00001, fold change > 2) (Figure 2B; Supplemental Table 1). Moreover, we validated m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq results with independent m<sup>5</sup>C-RIPqPCR on randomly selected m<sup>5</sup>C targets (Figure 2D; Supplemental Figure 4), substantiating that our m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq data were accurate and robust.

To test the correlation between transcript abundance and the presence of the m<sup>5</sup>C peak, we performed RNA-seq using the same 9-day-old *Arabidopsis* seedlings, and generated 51.2-56.5 million reads with two highly reproducible biological replicates (R > 0.98). 45.9-50.5 million reads were uniquely mapped to the *Arabidopsis* reference genome (TAIR10). # Analysis of m<sup>5</sup>C-Methylated Genes

As transcripts were fragmented before IP in our m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq process, our analysis of methylation patterns was at the gene level rather than the isoform level. The m<sup>5</sup>C epitranscriptome contained 4,465 expressed *Arabidopsis* genes in total with an average of 1.4 m<sup>5</sup>C peaks per gene, and most of these genes were protein coding ones (Figure 3A). About 74% of the methylated genes carried only one m<sup>5</sup>C peak (Figure 3B). We next compared the distribution of m<sup>5</sup>C peaks along protein-coding transcripts relative to landmarks in gene architecture, based on the single-transcript transcriptome dataset. We assigned each m<sup>5</sup>C peak based on the location of a peak summit to one of the five non-overlapping transcript segments: 5' untranslated regions (UTRs), CDSs, 3' UTRs, intronic and intergenic regions. The majority of m<sup>5</sup>C peaks were markedly located in CDSs (Figure 3C), and the observed peak number in CDSs was significantly higher than expected (Figure 3D), indicating that the m<sup>5</sup>C distribution was not random. By plotting the distribution of m<sup>5</sup>C peaks across mRNA segments (5' UTR, CDS and 3' UTR), we found that m<sup>5</sup>C was generally enriched in CDSs, respectively (Figure 3E).

To determine whether the identified  $m^5C$  peaks share common sequence elements that are characteristic of  $m^5C$  RNA modification, we performed an unbiased search for consensus motifs enriched in regions surrounding  $m^5C$  peak summits (Figure 3F; Supplemental Figure 3C). The most significantly enriched motifs were HACCR (P = 5.6e-44) and CTYCTYC (P = 1.4e-166), which were present in more than 50% and around 42% of the methylated peak summits, respectively (Figure 3F).

To provide functional insights about m<sup>5</sup>C RNA modified genes in *Arabidopsis*, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, and found that these genes could regulate multiple biological processes, including regulation of transcription and plant response to stresses and phytohormones (Figure 3G). We then performed different stress and hormone treatments to the 9-day-old wild-type *Arabidopsis* seedlings, and quantified mRNA m<sup>5</sup>C levels by LC-MS/MS.

#### m<sup>5</sup>C Is Associated with mRNA with Low Translation Activity

As m<sup>5</sup>C in tRNA and rRNA has been found to affect protein translation through various mechanisms, including tRNA recognition and translational fidelity (Chen et al., 1993; Hong et al., 1997; Alexandrov et al., 2006; Schaefer et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2012; Hussain et al., 2013b; Gigova et al., 2014), we proceeded to investigate whether m<sup>5</sup>C in mRNA affects translation. We examined the distribution of m<sup>5</sup>C in mRNA with different translational status in 9-day-old *Arabidopsis* seedlings using standard sucrose-gradient fractionation (Figure 4A) followed by dot blot (Figures 4B and 4C) and LC-MS/MS (Figure 4D) analyses. m<sup>5</sup>C levels were highest in the fractions containing mRNA associated with ribosomal subunits (40S and 60S) and monosomes (80S), but were low in polysome fractions. Since poorly translated or translationally repressed transcripts are usually associated with the ribosomal subunits and monosomes, our results suggest that m<sup>5</sup>C is associated with mRNA with low translation activity in *Arabidopsis*. This finding is consistent with a previous study showing that m<sup>5</sup>C is high in monosomes and low in polysomes in *Drosophila* S2 cell (Delatte et al., 2016).

# TRM4B Encodes a Potential mRNA m<sup>5</sup>C Methyltransferase

It has been known that RCMTs install  $m^5C$  on rRNA and tRNA in eukaryotes (Pavlopoulou and Kossida, 2009). The *Arabidopsis* genome encode eight RCMTs (Supplemental Figure 6A), which may also mediate mRNA  $m^5C$  methylation. To this end, we examined the *Arabidopsis* T-DNA insertion mutants of these RCMTs, and found that single mutants of TRM4B (Pavlopoulou and Kossida, 2009; Chen et al., 2010; David et al., 2017), *trm4b-3* and *trm4b-4*, exhibited defects in root development (Supplemental Figure 7). The full-length transcript of TRM4B and its 3' fragment downstream of the T-DNA insertion sites were greatly decreased in *trm4b-3* and *undetectable* in *trm4b-4* (Supplemental Figure 7A), implying that *trm4b-3* and *trm4b-4* could be reduction-of-function and loss-of-function mutants, respectively. The primary root length and number of lateral roots were significantly reduced in *trm4b* mutants as compared to wild-type

plants (Supplemental Figures 7B and 7C). Further analysis revealed that the root meristem size, which is represented as the number of cells in a cortex file from the quiescent center to the first elongated cortex cell, was significantly reduced in *trm4b* mutants (Supplemental Figures 7D and 7E). These results were in agreement with the phenotypes observed in other *trm4b* mutants (David et al., 2017). We also transformed *trm4b-3* and *trm4b-4* with the *TRM4B* cDNA fused to 3HA tag driven by its own promoter, and found that most of the homozygous progenies showed fully rescued root phenotypes (Supplemental Figures 7C and 7D), indicating that the defects observed in *trm4b* mutants are due to the loss of function of *TRM4B*.

To test whether TRM4B possesses the m<sup>5</sup>C methyltransferase activity, we performed an in vitro methylation assay, in which the RNA oligo containing the most enriched m<sup>5</sup>C motifs found in this study (Figure 3F) or its derived version without cytosines was incubated with either GST or the recombinant GST-TRM4B protein followed by measurement of m<sup>5</sup>C levels by either LC-MS/MS or dot blot assays. In contrast to undetectable m<sup>5</sup>C levels in the RNA oligo incubated with GST, 4.4% cytosines were methylated in the RNA oligo incubated with GST-TRM4B (Figures 5A and 5B; Supplemental Figure 8), demonstrating that GST-TRM4B confers the m<sup>5</sup>C methyltransferase activity. Consistently, quantitative measurement of the mRNA m<sup>5</sup>C levels by LC-MS/MS revealed that m<sup>5</sup>C levels were significantly decreased in *trm4b* roots (Figure 5C). Notably, m<sup>5</sup>C levels in roots of the *trm4b pTRM4B:TRM4B-3HA* rescued lines were restored to the wild-type levels (Figure 5C). We further applied m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq to RNA extracted from 9-dayold trm4b-4 seedlings. After combining two biological replicates (R>0.97), we identified 1,780 m<sup>5</sup>C peaks within 1,418 coding gene transcripts and 5 non-coding RNAs (Figure 5D; Supplemental Table 2), demonstrating a significant decrease in m<sup>5</sup>C peaks in *trm4b-4* (Figure 5E) versus wild-type plants (Figure 3A). Like those in wild-type plants (Figure 3B), most of the methylated genes in *trm4b-4* carried only one m<sup>5</sup>C peak (Figure 5F). Furthermore, most of the m<sup>5</sup>C peaks in *trm4b-4* were located in CDSs with an enrichment of peaks immediately after start codons and before stop codons (Figures 5G to 5I), demonstrating a comparable m<sup>5</sup>C distribution pattern along protein-coding transcripts in *trm4b-4* to that in wild-type plants (Figures 3C to 3E). The two most enriched motifs for m<sup>5</sup>C peaks in *trm4b-4* (Figure 5J) were also similar to those in wild-type plants (Figure 3F). To assess the biological effects of TRM4B-mediated m<sup>5</sup>C RNA modification, we then performed GO analysis of the genes bearing m<sup>5</sup>C peaks in wild-type but not in *trm4b-4* seedlings, and found that defective in *TRM4B* activity affected many biological

processes including root development (Supplemental Figure 9). Taken together, our data suggest that TRM4B is a potential mRNA m<sup>5</sup>C methyltransferase involved in root development in *Arabidopsis*. In addition, we found that although loss of function of *OLIGOCELLULA 2 (OLI2)*, which is another member of RCMTs in *Arabidopsis* (Supplemental Figure 6B), also exhibited short primary roots as previously reported (Fujikura et al., 2009), m<sup>5</sup>C levels in mRNA remained unchanged in *oli2* mutants (Supplemental Figure 6B), implying that members in RCMTs may target to different RNA species.

# TRM4B-Mediated m<sup>5</sup>C Methylation Is Positively Correlated with Transcript Levels of Genes in Root Development

To test whether there is a potential correlation between m<sup>5</sup>C mRNA methylation and gene expression mediated by TRM4B, we performed RNA-seq on 9-day-old *trm4b-4* and wild-type seedlings with two highly reproducible biological replicates (R> 0.98) (Figure 6A). Differential expression analysis identified 2,217 upregulated and 2,557 downregulated genes (P < 0.01), respectively, in *trm4b-4* versus wild-type (Figure 6B; Supplemental Table 3). Comparison of these genes with our list of m<sup>5</sup>C methylated genes (Supplemental Table 1) revealed 1,635 genes with altered transcript levels bearing m<sup>5</sup>C modification. This overlapping pattern is significant (P < 2.2e-16, hypergenometric test) (Figure 6C), suggesting that m<sup>5</sup>C mRNA modification affects gene transcript levels. Notably, transcript levels of 948 out of the 1,635 overlapped m<sup>5</sup>C-modified genes (58%) was downregulated in *trm4b-4* (Figure 6D), indicating that m<sup>5</sup>C mRNA modification tends to be positively correlated with gene transcript levels.

To further study the mechanism underlying which TRM4B regulates primary root development, we screened out candidate genes that exhibited reduced m<sup>5</sup>C modification levels and were differentially expressed in *trm4b-4*, and were also relevant to root development, such as *SHORT HYPOCOTYL 2* (*SHY2*) and *INDOLEACETIC ACID-INDUCED PROTEIN 16* (*IAA16*) (Supplemental Table 3) (Tian and Reed, 1999; Rinaldi et al., 2012). Loss of function of *SHY2* exhibits a short root phenotype, while gain of function of *IAA16* results in longer roots as compared with wild-type (Tian and Reed, 1999; Rinaldi et al., 2012), suggesting their biological function in root development. Expression of *SHY2* and *IAA16* was significantly downregulated in the roots of *trm4b-4* versus wild-type and *trm4b pTRM4B:TRM4B-3HA* plants, but were not obvious changed in the aerial tissues (Figure 6E). As a previous study suggests that synthetic m<sup>5</sup>C modified mRNAs display increased stability (Warren et al., 2010), we then tested whether m<sup>5</sup>C modification by TRM4B affects the mRNA decay of SHY2 and IAA16 by measuring their mRNA levels in roots of 9-day-old wild-type, trm4b and trm4b pTRM4B:TRM4B-3HA plants treated with the transcription inhibitor actinomycin D. Both SHY2 and IAA16 showed significant decreases in their cumulative RNA stability in the roots of *trm4b* versus wild-type and *trm4b* pTRM4B:TRM4B-3HA plants from 8 to 24 hr after actinomycin D treatment, whereas a non-m<sup>5</sup>C targeted transcript, such as *b*-tubulin (*TUB2*), displayed unchanged RNA stability (Figure 6G). These results suggest that  $m^5C$ methylation by TRM4B on SHY2 and IAA16 delays decay of both transcripts, which explains the positive correlation between m<sup>5</sup>C modification in *SHY2* and *IAA16* transcripts and their transcript levels mediated by TRM4B in roots. To examine if the reduction of m<sup>5</sup>C on SHY2 and IAA16 in trm4b is also associated with changes in their translational status, we performed polysome fractionation of wild-type and trm4b-4 roots and examined the association patterns of SHY2 and IAA16 mRNAs within fractions bearing different translational status. The unaltered association patterns of SHY2 and IAA16 in wild-type versus trm4b-4 roots (Supplemental Figure 10) indicate that reduction of m<sup>5</sup>C on SHY2 and IAA16 in trm4b-4 may not affect their translation status.

#### DISCUSSION

Recent functional studies of m<sup>6</sup>A, the most abundant internal modification of eukaryotic mRNA, in multicellular metazoans and plants have shed light on regulatory roles of mRNA modifications in eukaryotes. In contrast, although  $m^5C$  is also among the most prevalent posttranscriptional modifications in eukaryotic mRNA, its distribution and function are still largely unknown. In this study, we have performed transcriptome-wide profiling of m<sup>5</sup>C sites in Arabidopsis RNA through m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq using an m<sup>5</sup>C-specific antibody. The high sensitivity and specificity of the m<sup>5</sup>C antibody (Figure 1B; Supplemental Figure 1) facilitate effective immunoprecipitation, thus allowing detection of low-abundance methylated RNAs without the requirement of extremely deep sequencing. As a result, our m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq has identified 6,045 m<sup>5</sup>C peaks in 4.465 expressed genes in young *Arabidopsis* seedlings, and most of these sites have not been reported in previous studies. A recent study has applied bisulfite sequencing to similarly examine transcriptome-wide distribution of RNA m<sup>5</sup>C modification in Arabidopsis (David et al., 2017). However, bisulfite sequencing is unable to distinguish m<sup>5</sup>C from other cvtosine modifications in RNA, such as hm<sup>5</sup>C, which is detectable at considerable levels in total RNA and mRNA extracted from different tissues in *Arabidopsis* (Supplemental Figure 11) (Huber et al., 2015). This indicates that a certain portion of the previously reported m<sup>5</sup>C sites might not be m<sup>5</sup>C sites. In addition, as bisulfite treatment causes RNA degradation and prevents pre-enrichment of m<sup>5</sup>C-containing RNA, this approach is technically difficult to detect m<sup>5</sup>C in low-abundance RNAs. This might explain why the bisulfite sequencing approach could only identify 1,098 m<sup>5</sup>C sites even under relatively loose criteria (David et al., 2017), among which only 122 sites are partially overlapped with the 6.045  $m^5$ C peaks identified in this study.

Notably, our analysis of m<sup>5</sup>C peaks identified by m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq has revealed two hitherto unknown features of m<sup>5</sup>C distribution in *Arabidopsis* RNA. First, m<sup>5</sup>C is predominantly located in CDSs of protein-coding transcripts. The number of m<sup>5</sup>C peaks in CDSs is significantly higher than the expected number (Figures 3A, 3C, and 3D). Second, there are two peaks of m<sup>5</sup>C enrichment within CDSs (Figure 3E). One is located immediately after start codons, while the other that shows the highest enrichment of m<sup>5</sup>C sites is located just before stop codons. These distribution features, together with the observation of high m<sup>5</sup>C levels in mRNA factions with low translation activity (Figure 4), imply that m<sup>5</sup>C modification in CDSs could play a negative role in mediating the subsequent translational process in *Arabidopsis*. This is in agreement with a previous study showing that m<sup>5</sup>C is associated with poorly translated or translationally repressed transcripts in *Drosophila* S2 cell (Delatte et al., 2016). However, in contrast to m<sup>5</sup>C distribution predominantly in CDSs in *Arabidopsis*, another recent study has shown that m<sup>5</sup>C sites are accumulated in the vicinity of start codons, but depleted in CDSs in mouse (Amort et al., 2017), indicating the potential divergence of m<sup>5</sup>C methylation machinery and its biological functions in various eukaryotic organisms. In addition to m<sup>5</sup>C, other mRNA modifications, such as m<sup>6</sup>A and m<sup>1</sup>A, have also been implicated to affect translation. In mammalian cells, m<sup>6</sup>A residues within 5' UTRs promote cap-independent translation (Wang et al., 2015), while m<sup>1</sup>A is enriched around start codons upstream of the first splice site, and is positively correlated to translation in human mRNA (Li et al., 2016b). In addition, enrichment of hm<sup>5</sup>C in CDSs also favours mRNA modification may play important roles in affecting subsequent protein synthesis.

Our findings have also provided two pieces of evidence supporting the role of TRM4B, which is one of the eight RCMT family members in the Arabidopsis genome (Pavlopoulou and Kossida, 2009), as a potential mRNA  $m^5$ C methyltransferase. First, in vitro methylation assay has shown that GST-TRM4B catalyzes m<sup>5</sup>C modification in cytosines in the RNA oligo containing the most enriched m<sup>5</sup>C motif found in this study, suggesting that TRM4B has the in vitro m<sup>5</sup>C methyltransferase activity. Second, in agreement with the root defects observed in this study and a previous report (David et al., 2017), loss of TRM4B activity results in decreased  $m^5C$ levels in Arabidopsis roots, but not in aerial tissues, implying that TRM4B indeed affects m<sup>5</sup>C modification in vivo. Consistently, m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq analysis of RNA extracted from trm4b-4 seedlings has revealed a significant decrease in  $m^5C$  peaks in *trm4b-4* versus wild-type plants. Furthermore, we have demonstrated that TRM4B promotes m<sup>5</sup>C modification in mRNAs of SHY2 and IAA16, two genes involved in root development (Tian and Reed, 1999; Rinaldi et al., 2012). This m<sup>5</sup>C modification is associated with TRM4B effect on the decay of these two transcripts and the resulting transcript levels in roots. Thus, characterization of TRM4B role in root development implicates another biological role of m<sup>5</sup>C in affecting mRNA stability in Arabidopsis in addition to its potential function in influencing translation. This is similar to the effect of m<sup>6</sup>A on both mRNA stability and translation (Wang et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2015),

indicating that mRNA modifications would at least affect both mRNA levels and protein synthesis.

Investigation of TRM4B function has also revealed the complexity of RNA methylation in *Arabidopsis*. For example, in *trm4b* mutants, total m<sup>5</sup>C levels only decrease by 20%-30% in roots and remain unchanged in the aerial tissues. This not only suggests the presence of other m<sup>5</sup>C methyltransferase(s) in *Arabidopsis*, but also indicates that the function of m<sup>5</sup>C methyltransferase(s) is dependent on various developmental contexts. The latter is substantiated by our observations on the dynamic pattern of m<sup>5</sup>C RNA modification in various tissues and at different developmental stages in *Arabidopsis*. In addition, like the RNA methyltransferase NSUN2 in human (Brzezicha et al., 2006; Squires et al., 2012), TRM4B has also been reported to act as a tRNA methyltransferase (Burgess et al., 2015), suggesting that m<sup>5</sup>C methyltransferase(s) may catalyze different RNA populations. Therefore, it is necessary to further investigate the mechanisms underlying which methyltransferases are regulated to mediate m<sup>5</sup>C modification in different spatial and temporal patterns as well as in different RNA species.

#### METHODS

#### **Plant Material and Growth Conditions**

Seeds of *trm4b-3* (SALK\_073628) and *trm4b-4* (SALK\_014194) were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (http://www.arabidopsis.org/). *Arabidopsis thaliana* wild-type plants and mutants in the Columbia (Col) background were grown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium under long day conditions (16 h light/8 h dark) at  $23^{\circ}C \pm 2^{\circ}C$ .

All treatments were applied to 9-day-old seedlings. For drought treatment, seedlings were removed from the agar and desiccated in plastic dishes for 2 h. For heat or cold treatment, plants were grown at 38°C for 1 h or at 4°C for 5 h, respectively. For high-salinity stress treatment, plants were transferred to liquid MS medium containing 250 mM NaCl for 2 h. For phytohormone treatment, seedlings were transferred to liquid MS medium containing 10 nM IAA, 1 M NAA, 10 nM 2,4-D, 5  $\mu$ M ABA or 100 nM 6-BA for 3 h.

#### **Plasmid Construction**

To construct *pTRM4B:TRM4B-3HA*, a 1.0-kb *TRM4B* 5' upstream sequence and the coding sequence were amplified and cloned into a modified pENTR 4 vector containing 3HA. The resulting *pTRM4B:TRM4B-3HA* was then recombined into pGWB (Yan et al., 2014) by Gateway LR recombination (Invitrogen).

#### **Plant Transformation**

*Agrobacterium tumefaciens*-mediated transformation of *Arabidopsis* plants in the Col background was carried out by a floral dipping method. *pTRM4B:TRM4B-3HA* construct was transformed into *trm4b-3* and *trm4b-4*, and the homozygous progenies were identified and used for further experiments.

#### **Isolation of Different RNA Species**

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini kit (Qiagen). mRNA was enriched from total RNA using the Oligotex mRNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), and small RNA was separated from rRNA in the remaining fraction using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA concentration and quality were measured by the Qubit® RNA Assay Kit using a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies) and the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit using a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent).

# Dot Blot Analysis of m<sup>5</sup>C Levels

Dot blot analysis was performed as previously described (Shen et al., 2016; Shen et al., 2017). Purified RNA was first denatured by heating at 95°C for 3 min, followed by chilling on ice immediately. RNA was spotted on a Hybond-N+ membrane (Amersham) optimized for nucleic acid transfer. After UV crosslinking, the membrane was washed by 1x PBST buffer (PBS with Tween-20), blocked with 5% of non-fat milk in PBST, and incubated with anti-m<sup>5</sup>C antibody (1:1000; MAb-081-010, Diagenode) overnight at 4°C. After incubating with horseradishperoxidase-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Santa Cruz), the membrane was visualized by the ECL Western Blotting Detection Kit (Thermo).

# Measurement of the m<sup>5</sup>C/C Ratio by LC-MS/MS Analysis

Purified mRNA was digested into single ribonucleosides as previously described (Su et al., 2014), followed by a spin-filter cleanup. Samples were subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis on an Agilent 6490 Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer. Ribonucleosides were quantified using the nucleoside-to-base ion mass transitions of 258.1 to 126.1 for  $m^5C$ , 244 to 112 for C, and 274.0 to 142.1 for  $hm^5C$ .

# m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-Seq and Validation

m<sup>5</sup>C-RIP-seq was performed as previously described (Dominissini et al., 2013; Shen et al., 2016) with minor modifications. The amount and quality of RNA were tested by Qubit RNA Assay Kit (Life Technologies), gel electrophoresis and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. RNA samples were fragmented into ~100-nucleotide-long fragments by RNA fragmentation buffer. Fragmented RNA was incubated for 2 h at 4°C with 0.5 mg/ml anti-m<sup>5</sup>C monoclonal antibody (Diagenode) in IP buffer supplemented with RNasin Plus RNase inhibitor (Promega). The mixture was then immunoprecipitated by incubation with Protein A/G Plus-Agarose (Santa Cruz) that was pre-bound with BSA at 4°C for an additional 2 hr. After extensive washing, bound RNA was eluted from the beads with 6.7 mM 5-methylcytosine (Sigma-Aldrich) in IP buffer, and precipitated by ethanol. NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB) was used to construct the libraries from IP and input RNA. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina Hiseq 2500 platform.

For validation of  $m^5C$  sequencing results,  $m^5C$ -RIP-qPCR was performed using 9-day-old wild-type whole seedlings. RNA samples were fragmented into ~200-nucleotide-long fragments using RNA fragmentation buffer (Ambion). The input RNA and immunoprecipitated RNA were reverse transcribed with random hexamers (Invitrogen) using M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (Promega). Relative enrichment of each gene was determined by quantitative real-time PCR and calculated first by normalizing the amount of a target cDNA fragment against that of *ACTIN* as an internal control, and then by normalizing the value for the immunoprecipitated sample against that for the input. The primers used for real-time PCR are listed in Supplemental Table 4.

#### **Peak Calling and Analysis**

Sequence data were analyzed according to the procedure previously described (Dominissini et al., 2013; Shen et al., 2016). Briefly, the BWA (Li and Durbin, 2009) with MEN algorithm was run to align read sequences of the input and IP samples to the TAIR 10 reference annotation file. Non-unique reads with MAPQ less than 13 were discarded from downstream analysis. MACS (Feng et al., 2011) was used for peak detection with default settings, and the parameter of effective genome size was adjusted to the calculated transcriptome size (65084215). Peaks were considered if their *P* value < 0.00001 and fold change > 2. Based on the peak summit position, peaks were assigned to 5' UTRs, CDSs, 3' UTRs, intronic and intergenic regions. For example, a peak was assigned to CDS when the peak summit was inside an annotated CDS. The upstream and downstream 50 bp sequences from the peak summit located on protein coding gene transcript were extracted for m<sup>5</sup>C motif identification using MEME-ChIP (Ma et al., 2014).

#### **Gene Ontology Analysis**

Gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the DAVID database (Huang da et al., 2009). GO classification for biological process were performed using default settings.

#### **RNA-seq**

Nine-day-old *Arabidopsis* wild-type and *trm4b-4* seedlings were harvested for total RNA extraction. The amount and quality of RNA were tested by Qubit RNA Assay Kit (Life

Technologies), gel electrophoresis and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB) was used to construct the libraries according to the manufacturer's instructions, and sequencing was performed on Illumina Hiseq 4000 platform. After removing reads containing adapter, reads containing ploy-N and low-quality reads from raw data, the clean reads were aligned to the *Arabidopsis* reference TAIR10 using TopHat v2.0.12 (Trapnell et al., 2009). The mapped reads were assembled by Cufflinks v2.1.1 (Trapnell et al., 2010). HTSeq v0.6.1 was used to count the number of reads mapped to each gene (Anders et al., 2015). Cuffdiff was used to calculate FPKMs of each gene. Differential expression analysis of wild type and *trm4b-4* was performed using DESeq (Anders and Huber, 2010). Genes with *P* value < 0.01 were assigned as differentially expressed genes.

#### **Polysome Fractionation and RNA Extraction**

Nine-day-old whole seedlings or roots were collected and homogenized in liquid nitrogen, and the polysome were extracted using the polysome extraction buffer (200 mM Tris-HCl, pH 9.0, 200 mM KCl, 25 mM EGTA, 35 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 1× Detergent mix, 1% polyoxyethylene 10 tridecyl ether, 5 mM DTT, 1 mM Phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 50 µg/mL cycloheximide, and 50 µg/mL chloramphenicol) as previously described (Juntawong et al., 2015). The 10%-50% sucrose gradients were made in RNase-free polypropylene tube and spun using 107 Gradient Master Ip Biocomp gradient-maker. 300 µL of cell lysate was added to the gradients and then topped up with 400 µL of 10% sucrose. The gradients were subsequently centrifuged in SW41 bucket rotors ultracentrifuge at 36,000 rpm at 8°C for 2 hours. After centrifugation, polysome fractionation was performed using the gradient fractionator machine connected to piston, UV monitor and fraction collector. Twelve different fractions were collected using the fraction collector. Polysomal RNA of each fractions was extracted by phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) followed by chloroform: isoamyl (24:1). mRNA was further purified from the total RNA as previously described (Shen et al., 2016). For quantitative real-time PCR analysis, spikein RNA from the GeneChip Eurkaryotic Poly-A RNA Control kit (Affymetrix) was added to each fraction prior to RNA extraction.

#### Analysis of Root Phenotypes and Confocal Microscopy

Roots of 9-day-old *Arabidopsis* wild-type and mutants were stained by 10 µM propidium iodide (Sigma-Aldrich) solution and incubated for 2-3 minutes, rinsed and mounted in water. Images were taken by Leica SP1 confocal system. Root length was measured with Image J software (http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij). Root meristem size was determined by measuring the cortex cell number from the quiescent center to the first elongated cortex cell.
