# Dynamic and spatial restriction of Polycomb activity by plant histone demethylases

Wenhao Yan<sup>1,7\*</sup>, Dijun Chen<sup>1,7</sup>, Cezary Smaczniak<sup>1,0</sup>, Julia Engelhorn<sup>2,6</sup>, Haiyang Liu<sup>3</sup>, Wenjing Yang<sup>3</sup>, Alexander Graf<sup>4</sup>, Cristel C. Carles<sup>2</sup>, Dao-Xiu Zhou<sup>3,5</sup> and Kerstin Kaufmann<sup>1\*</sup>

Targeted changes in chromatin state at thousands of genes are central to eukaryotic development. RELATIVE OF EARLY FLOWERING 6 (REF6) is a Jumonji-type histone demethylase that counteracts Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2)mediated gene silencing in plants and was reported to select its binding sites in a direct, sequence-specific manner<sup>1-3</sup>. Here we show that REF6 and its two close paralogues determine spatial 'boundaries' of the repressive histone H3K27me3 mark in the genome and control the tissue-specific release from PRC2-mediated gene repression. Targeted mutagenesis revealed that these histone demethylases display pleiotropic, redundant functions in plant development, several of which depend on trans factor-mediated recruitment. Thus, Jumonjitype histone demethylases restrict repressive chromatin domains and contribute to tissue-specific gene activation via complementary targeting mechanisms.

PRC2-mediated placement of the repressive H3K27me3 mark is associated with stable gene repression in plants<sup>4,5</sup>, allowing genes to be activated only in a highly stage- or organ-type specific manner<sup>6-8</sup>. Up to now, four Jumonji (JMJ)-type proteins, including REF6, were reported to have H3K27 demethylase activity in Arabidopsis<sup>1,2,9-11</sup>, thereby antagonizing PRC2 action. These genes were shown to be involved in epigenetic reprogramming<sup>9</sup>, flowering time control<sup>12</sup>, brassinosteroid signalling<sup>13</sup> and circadian clock<sup>14,15</sup>. While the *ref6* single mutant shows only a delay in flowering<sup>12</sup>, genome-wide mapping of REF6 binding sites showed that it binds to thousands of putative targets directly via a Cys<sub>2</sub>His<sub>2</sub> zinc finger (C2H2-ZnF) domain<sup>2,3</sup>. This direct binding mode raises the question of how REF6 and related H3K27me3 demethylases exert a dynamic antagonistic control of PRC2 repression<sup>6</sup>. In this regard, the molecular mechanisms that ensure selective and properly timed release of PRC2 repression are still poorly understood.

We dissected the functions of *REF6* and its two close paralogues in *Arabidopsis* (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1), *EARLY FLOWERING* 6 (*ELF6*) and *JMJ13* (*At5G46910*) that show a similar expression pattern (Supplementary Fig. 2). We found that JMJ13 possesses H3K27me3 demethylase activity, similar to REF6 and ELF6, in a well-established<sup>1</sup> in vivo system (Fig. 1b,c, Supplementary Fig. 3). *jmj13* single mutant alleles showed early flowering and changes in leaf petiole length but wild-type-like development at reproductive stages (Fig. 1d–f, Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Text). In contrast, strong pleiotropic developmental defects were observed in an *elf6-3 ref6<sup>c</sup> jmj13<sup>G</sup>* triple mutant generated by combining a *Cas9*-

based ref6<sup>c</sup> and T-DNA insertion-based elf6-3 and imi13<sup>G</sup> mutant alleles (Supplementary Text). The triple mutant showed a dwarf phenotype with changes in leaf morphology, shortened internodes, onequarter of flowers having more floral organs (especially petals) and 70% of flowers with partial loss of determinacy, producing heavily bent siliques (Fig. 1g-m). Notably, these phenotypes were not found in elf6-3 ref6-3  $imi13^{G}$  triple mutant in which the ref6-3 allele was generated by a T-DNA insertion in 3' end of the coding sequence (Fig. 1g, h, Supplementary Fig. 5). Cas9-based ref6<sup>C</sup> displayed a more dramatic delay in flowering time compared with other ref6 mutant alleles, but developed normal flowers (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Further co-segregation and complementation tests confirmed that the late flowering phenotype was caused by loss of function of REF6 (Supplementary Fig. 6b,c). Interestingly, as for the elf6-3, ref6<sup>C</sup> jmj13<sup>G</sup> triple mutant, the elf6-3 ref<sup>C</sup> double mutant displayed a dwarf phenotype with downward curled and dark green leaves and produced more petals (Fig. 1n,o,p). However, more severe defects appeared in the triple mutant, such as the loss of floral determinacy and the heavily bent siliques (Fig. 1m,o), which shows a synergistic effect of the three *JMJ* genes on flower development. A quintuple mutant with jmj30-2 and jmj32-1, two mutations affecting genes that encode more distantly related JMJ-type H3K27me3 demethylases<sup>10</sup> (Supplementary Fig. 1), did not further enhance the phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 7). In accordance with a redundant function of REF6, ELF6 and JMJ13, we found that global H3K27me3 levels were strongly elevated in the triple mutant compared to wildtype, single and double mutants (Fig. 2a). Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) revealed that 92.7% of the differentially enriched H3K27me3 genomic loci in the triple mutant were hypermethylated compared to wild-type (Supplementary Table 1) and associated with prevalent downregulation of gene activity (Fig. 2b,c, Supplementary Fig. 8, Supplementary Table 2). A mutation in the PRC2 gene CURLY LEAF (CLF) largely rescued the leaf and flower phenotypes of the *jmj* triple mutant (Fig. 2d-f). In line with a role in activation of gene expression, REF6 binding sites showed strong positional enrichment for active H3K4me3 and H3K9ac marks (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). The target sites became enriched for H3K27me3 in the triple mutant, from 11.2% in wild-type to 51.5% in jmj triple mutant (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Table 3), associated with downregulation of the target genes (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 11). It is still unclear how H3K27me3 demethylase activity can be recruited to specific developmental genes because REF6 has a large number of genomic binding sites and can directly bind to DNA via a C-terminal C2H2-ZnF domain. To study whether additional mechanisms are involved in the recruitment of REF6, we used combined formaldehyde/disuccinimidyl glutarate (DSG) fixatio[n21](#page-7-19) followed by ChIP-seq (Supplementary Table 3). While target sites were overall similar between the two fixation protocols, the double fixation captured additional REF6 targets (Supplementary Fig. 18), indicating stabilization of REF6 DNA-binding via indirect recruitment in vivo. We analysed the ability of endogenously expressed REF6 versions lacking the C-terminal C2H2-ZnF domain, and/or with abolished enzymatic activity (H246A[\)1](#page-7-0) , to complement the *elf6*-*3 ref6C jmj13G* triple mutant. Ten out of eleven transgenic lines with fulllength REF6 expressed from the native promoter complemented the triple mutant phenotype (Fig. [4a\)](#page-4-0). Six out of seven independent transgenic REF6<sup>∆</sup>ZnF lines showed clear complementation of the triple mutant phenotypes, in particular plant height, inflorescence architecture and flower defects (Fig. [4b](#page-4-0), Supplementary Fig. 19). In contrast, REF6H246A and REF6H246A∆ZnF plant lines that were deficient in enzymatic activity did not show complementation (Fig. [4c,d](#page-4-0)). Previously, REF6<sup>∆</sup>ZnF was reported to not bind DNA in a *ref6* single mutant (see also Supplementary Fig. 20), and its overexpression can reduce H2K27me3 levels similar to full-length REF6[2](#page-7-6) . In order to test the possibility that REF6<sup>∆</sup>ZnF may be competed out by its paralogues in the *ref6* mutant, we determined REF6∆ZnF binding sites by ChIP-seq in the *jmj* triple mutant. We identified 7,368 significant DNA-binding sites of REF6<sup>∆</sup>ZnF in the *elf6*-*3 ref6C jmj13G* background, and 37.9% of the associated genes were in common with REF6 target genes identified in the *ref6-3* single mutant (Fig. [4e](#page-4-0)). The average enrichment in the REF6<sup>∆</sup>ZnF ChIP-seq data was about 55% relative to full-length REF6, indicating that the C2H2-ZnF domain may stabilize the interaction with DNA (Fig. [4f](#page-4-0)). The REF6 DNA-binding motif CTCTGYT[Y2,](#page-7-6)[3](#page-7-1) is only present in 34.1% of the genomic regions bound by REF6<sup>∆</sup>ZnF, compared to 95.2% of the regions bound by full-length REF6 (Fig. [4g](#page-4-0)). The binding consensus was not preferentially localized in the REF6<sup>∆</sup>ZnF ChIP-seq peak summits (Fig. [4h\)](#page-4-0). These data indicate that REF6 can be recruited to DNA by other factors. Indeed, interactions between REF6 and TFs have been reported to facilitate REF6 recruitment to several flowering-related loc[i22](#page-7-20)[–24](#page-7-21). We found that REF6∆ZnF binding sites preferentially co-localized with several TF-bound genomic regions, including those of MADS factors, determined by ChIP-seq (Fig. [4i](#page-4-0)). Interactions between REF6 and MADS factors were found to be independent of the C2H2-ZnF domain in liquid chromatography– mass spectrometry (LC–MS)/MS based protein complex isolation experiments from native inflorescences (Fig. [4j](#page-4-0)). This indicates that the two versions of REF6 could be similarly recruited to targets by MADS factors, which may help to explain the complementation of the triple mutant phenotypes by REF6<sup>∆</sup>ZnF. To further assess if REF6 could be recruited by MADS proteins, we tested binding dynamics of REF6 at five loci that do not contain the REF6 DNA-binding motif CTCTGYTY, but that are also bound by SEPALLATA3 (SEP3) and other homeotic MADS proteins (Supplementary Fig. 21). For this purpose, we used a previously established system for synchronized floral induction, pAP1:AP1-GR *ap1 cal*[25,](#page-7-22)[26](#page-7-23), which allows the collection of tissue at specific early floral meristematic stages. As expected, the expression of major floral homeotic MADS factors was induced 2–4 days after dexamethasone treatment, while the REF6 transgene was expressed constantly (Fig. [4k](#page-4-0)). Out of five genomic regions analysed, four showed significantly elevated REF6 binding during flower development (Fig. [4i](#page-4-0)). These results further support the idea that REF6 is recruited to its targets by the activity of MADSdomain TFs. Together, these data show that tissue-specific changes in REF6 DNA-binding coincide with, and potentially contribute to, changes in H3K27me3 levels. They also indicate that REF6 dynamics may be facilitated by interactions with TFs conferring selectivity or stabilizing REF6 binding to target genes in a native chromatin context, and thereby enhancing its enzymatic activity at specific genomic regions. However, REF6∆ZnF cannot rescue the leaf petiole length phenotype in the triple mutant (Supplementary Fig. 22), which is in line with the previously reported situation in the *ref6-1 or ref6-3* single mutan[t2](#page-7-6)[,3](#page-7-1) , indicating that REF6 may participate in leaf and flower development via distinct mechanisms.

In conclusion, our data show that REF6 and its two closely related paralogues spatially and temporally restrict H3K27me3 to allow tissue-specific gene activation. While spatial boundaries of H3K27me3 are determined by REF6 activity, developmental dynamics of H3K27me3 removal is facilitated by TF-assisted recruitment of REF6 to specific target sites for gene activity. Thus, both the deposition of the H3K27me3 mark by PRC2 complexes[16](#page-7-14) and the removal of this mark by REF6 can be modulated by TFs that act in a stage- and tissue-specific manner. Our results also show that not all REF6 functions are associated with its direct binding to target loci, emphasizing the need for alternative methods to detect transient interactions that result in enzymatic modifications of histone residues in a native context. In addition, the binding pattern of other H3K27me3 demethylases, especially JMJ13 which differs from ELF6 and REF6 by having no DNA-binding domain will help to deepen our insights into the dynamics of JMJ-mediated H3K27me3 removal.

The plant materials used for flowering time measurements were grown in growth chamber under long day conditions (16 h light, 8 h dark) with 120 μmolm-2 s-1 light intensity (22 °C daytime, 20 °C at night). **Plasmid construction.** A construct expressing a REF6-GFP fusion protein from the *pREF6* promoter has been described previously[24](#page-7-21). In this construct, a REF6 genomic fragment including an 800 bp promoter region and the complete coding region without stop codon was cloned into vector pMDC107. All derivatives of REF6 were generated based on the full-length REF6-GFP construct. In brief, oligos GTTTAGTTGGTTTGCTTGGGCTGTGGAGGACCATGACCTT and AAGGTCATGGTCCTCCACAGCCCAAGCAAACCAACTAAAC were used to introduce the mutation that changes histidine at position 246 to alanine, while oligos GAATGAGGAAGAGGAATGTGCAGCATCGGTTAAGAAGACCAAC AAAAGG and CCTTTTGTTGGTCTTCTTAACCGATGCTGCA CATTCCTCTTCCTCATTC were used to delete the four 3'-terminal C2H2 zinc finger tandem repeats. To produce constructs for in vivo enzymatic activity assays, the JMJ13 coding sequence was isolated using cDNA prepared from plant material that was enriched in shoot apical meristem tissues, and then cloned into pFA121[27.](#page-7-24) Overlap PCR was performed using primers AATGGTCCTCAACAGCCCAGGCAAACATACTGAAAAGCATTCC and GTATGTTTGCCTGGGCTGTTGAGGACCATTATTTGTACAGC to create enzyme-inactive JMJ13 that has alanine instead of histidine at position 293. [28.](#page-7-25) To mutagenize *REF6* or *ELF6*, two sgRNAs were combined into a construct for large fragment deletion, while only one sgRNA was expressed to generate a mutation for *JMJ13*. Information of primers used for gRNA synthesis can be found in Supplementary Table 5.

**Genotyping and phenotyping.** The PCR reactions for genotyping Cas9-based mutations and T-DNA insertions were performed by using Phire plant direct PCR master mix kit (Thermo Scientific) following the manufacturer's instructions. To select positive T1 transformants for Cas9-based gene mutagenesis, pieces (0.5 mm diameter for each piece) from three to five cauline leaves were mixed to prepare the template for PCR; whereas material from one leaf was sufficient for T-DNA insertion lines. Information of primers used for genotyping can be seen in Supplementary Table 5. The phenotypes of whole plants, inflorescences and siliques were documented by camera (NEX-5R, SONY, Japan), and a stereomicroscope (Discovery, Zeiss) was used for imaging single flowers or floral organs. To measure leaf petiole length, specific leaves (4th, 5th or 6th) were harvested when the plant started to bolt (4 weeks after sowing) while for silique length measurement, the 6th–20th siliques of main stem were measured when the last flowers of the inflorescence started to produce siliques. The mean value for these 15 siliques represents the silique length of this plant.

**Western blotting.** The substrate specificity of H3K27me3 antibody (07-449, Millipore) was confirmed by applying Absurance™ Histone H3 Antibody Specificity Array (16-667, Millipore) following the manufacturer's instructions. In detail, 1/1,000 dilution of primary antibody was incubated with the membrane at 4 °C overnight. 1/5,000 dilution of Donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody was used to detect the primary antibody. Western blot was performed to detect the total amount of H3K27me3 and H3. Equal amount of denatured protein was loaded for detecting H3K27me3 modification and total H3, respectively. 1/2,000 dilution of primary antibody was incubated with the membrane at 4°C overnight and 1/5,000 dilution of Donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody was used to detect the primary antibody. The signal was detected by Odyssey imaging system from LI-COR (LI-COR).

**In vivo histone demethylation assay.** The in vivo histone demethylation assay was performed according to a previously described protocol[27.](#page-7-24) Specifically, 35 S:JMJ13-2 × HA-2 × FLAG and 35 S:JMJ13H293A-2 × HA-2 × FLAG were expressed with *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* strain EHA105, together with *Agrobacterium* cells harbouring P19 plasmids in tobacco leaf cells. Two days after infection, nuclei were isolated and immunostaining was performed as describe[d27](#page-7-24). The signal was detected by a confocal microscope, Olympus FV1200 (Olympus).

Antibodies used in this experiment: anti-HA (M20003M, Abmart), anti-H3K4me2 (07-030, Millipore), anti-H3K4me3 (ab8580, Abcam), anti-H3K27me3 (07-449, Millipore), anti-H3 (ab1791, Abcam), anti-H3K9me2 (07-441, Millipore), anti-H3K9me3 (ab8898, Abcam), anti-H3K36me2 (07-274, Millipore), anti-H3K36me3 (ab9050, Abcam), goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (A-11029, Invitrogen), and goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (A-11036, Invitrogen).

**Expression profiling by RNA-seq.** Inflorescences including flowers of all stages before opening were collected from 5-week-old plants for RNA-seq analysis and 0.1 g tissue was collected in liquid nitrogen to serve as one single experiment. Total RNA was extracted using RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to manufacturer's instructions and 2 μg total RNA was used to produce libraries using TruSeq Stranded Total RNA LT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina). Pooled libraries were submitted to a NextSeq® 550 sequencing platform (Illumina) for sequencing. Three biological replicates were generated for each genotype, and at least 20 million reads were produced per replicate.

**Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by qPCR or high-throughput sequencing (ChIP-seq).** Around 0.7 g tissue was collected for one replicate into 50ml falcon tube on ice. To map the REF6 targets in inflorescences, three independent pREF6:REF6-GFP *ref6-3* transgenic lines that showed full complementation were used for tissue collection. For dynamic binding assay of REF6, pREF6:REF6-GFP or pUBQ10:GFP was introduced to pAP1:AP1-GR *ap1cal*, respectively, and 0.7 g synchronized floral meristematic tissues were collected before DEX induction, 2days and 4days after induction, respectively. DEX induction was carried out as described previously[26](#page-7-23). ChIP was performed according to a published protocol with minor modifications[29.](#page-7-26) Briefly, the tissue was fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 20min. When disuccinimidyl glutarate (DSG) was applied, plant tissue was treated with 2mM DSG under vacuum for 20min. Then, formaldehyde was added directly to reach 1% final concentration to the fixation buffer containing plant tissue and DSG for another 20min fixation. After nuclei isolation, the chromatin was sonicated to obtain the desired DNA fragment size (major size at 500bp) by a Covaris S220 sonicator. The sonicated chromatin was pre-cleaned by two rounds of centrifugation with maximum speed at 4 °C. We used 5μl of anti-H3K27me3 (07-449, Millipore) to precipitate H3K27me3 marked histones and REF6 targets were mapped by using GFP coupled beads (Miltenyi Biotec). ChIPed DNA were used for either qPCR or sequencing analysis. ChIP-seq libraries and control libraries that were based on 'input' DNA (sonicated chromatin) were prepared using ThruPLEX DNA-seq kit (RUBICON GENOMICS, United States) following the manufacturer. All libraries for ChIP-seq were sequenced with a NextSeq®550 sequencer (Illumina). At least 25 million reads were generated per biological replicate. For REF6-GFP ChIP, magnetic GFP beads (Miltenyi) were used a previously described procedur[e30](#page-7-27). Library preparation and sequencing were performed as described earlier, and 10–20 million reads were generated for each biological replicate.

**Protein complex isolation.** Biochemical complex isolations from plants were performed on material from GFP tagged transgenic plant lines using magnetic GFP beads (Miltenyi) as describe[d31](#page-7-28). We used 0.5 g inflorescence tissue from pREF6-REF6-GFP *ref6*-*1* or pREF6-REF6<sup>∆</sup>ZnF-GFP *ref6*-*1* for each IP, and IPs were performed in three biological replicates. The purified, desalted peptides were submitted to LC–MS/MS. Peptides were re-suspended in 25 μl 5% v/v acetonitrile, 2% v/v TFA and analysed using a Q Exactive Plus coupled to an Easy nLC1000 high-performance liquid chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were loaded onto an Acclaim PepMap RSLC reversed-phase column (75 μm inner diameter, 15 cm long, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and eluted at a flow rate of 0.3 μl min-1 using an acetonitrile gradient of 3% to 30% v/v over 70 min, 30% to 40% over 15 min and 40% to 60% over 5 min, followed by a washing step with 90% v/v acetonitrile for 10 min. Peptide ions were detected in a full scan (m/z 200–1,600). Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) scans were performed for the 10 peptides with the strongest MS signal (AGC target 1e5, isolation width mass-to-charge ratio 3 m/z, relative collision energy 30%). Peptides for which MS/MS spectra had been recorded were excluded from further MS/MS scans for 15s. The RAW peptide sequencing data obtained from Q Exactive Plus were processed by the MaxQuant (version 1.6.0.16) softwar[e32](#page-7-29) that integrates peptide identification and quantification. After the MaxQuant analysis, the output proteinGroups.txt file was taken for further filtering and statistical analysis essentially as described before[31](#page-7-28). The LFQ (label-free quantification) data was taken as input for the Perseus (version 1.6.0.7) softwar[e33](#page-7-30) for omics data analysis and log2-transformed before analysis. Proteins identified by at least two peptides, including one unique peptide, were kept (others were removed) from the analysis. Protein contaminants, proteins only identified by post-translational modifications (PTM) and protein identifications by the reverse protein database were removed from the analysis. Missing protein LFQ values were replaced by the imputation from the left arm of the LFQ normal distribution to emulate background protein level. Relative protein amount differences were evaluated by two-sample *t*-tests with permutation-based FDR correction and S0 parameter set to 1. Results were reported as total protein abundance versus relative protein abundance plots with indicated different FDR thresholds.

**Phylogenetic analysis.** Protein sequences for Jumonji C domain-containing protein[s34](#page-8-1) were obtained from TAIR10. The neighbour-joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree (Supplementary Fig. 1) was constructed based on amino acid sequence alignment using MEGA (version 6.06[\)35](#page-8-2) with default settings. The annotated domains for each protein were also obtained from TAIR10.

**RNA-seq data analysis.** RNA-seq reads were mapped to *Arabidopsis thaliana* reference genome (TAIR10) using STAR (version 020201[\)36.](#page-8-3) Expression levels (in terms of FPKM, fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) of all annotated protein-coding genes were estimated by RSEM (version 1.2.22)[37.](#page-8-4) Differentially expressed genes between mutants and wild-type were identified by DESeq[238.](#page-8-5) Genes were considered as differentially expressed if they showed at least two-fold changes with FDR < 0.01 (Supplementary Table 2). To compare the expression changes of REF6 target genes between seedlings and inflorescences, RNA-seq data in seedling[s3](#page-7-1) were reanalysed as earlier.

**ChIP-seq data analysis.** We followed the ChIP-seq data analysis guidelines[39,](#page-8-6)[40](#page-8-7) recommended by the ENCODE project and have developed an analysis pipeline[41](#page-8-8) consisting of quality control, read mapping, peak calling, assessment of reproducibility among biological replicates, and peak annotation to preprocess all raw data in a standardized and uniform manner. Specifically, the quality of the raw data (FASTQ files) was evaluated by FastQC ([http://www.bioinformatics.](http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) [babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/](http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/)). Reads were then mapped to the *A. thaliana* genome (TAIR10) using Bowtie (version 1.1.2[\)42](#page-8-9) with parameters '--threads 8 -n 2 -m 3 -k 1 --best --chunkmbs 256 -q'. Redundant reads were removed using Picard tools (v2.60; <http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/>). Peak calling was performed using MACS2 (version 2.1.0[\)43](#page-8-10). Duplicated reads were not considered (--keep-dup = 1) during peak calling in order to achieve a better specificity[40](#page-8-7). The '--mfold' parameter was set at '2-20' to build the model. The parameter '-broad' was used in the calling of H3K27me3-enriched regions (broad peaks), whereas '--call-summits' was used in the calling of narrow peaks for REF6, H3K4me3 and other TF ChIPseq data (see later). A relaxed threshold of *P*-value (*P*≤1×10-2) was suggested in order to enable the correct computation of IDR (irreproducible discovery rate) value[s39](#page-8-6). Following the recommendations for the analysis of self-consistency and reproducibility between replicates [\(https://sites.google.com/site/anshulkundaje/](https://sites.google.com/site/anshulkundaje/projects/idr) [projects/idr](https://sites.google.com/site/anshulkundaje/projects/idr)[\)44](#page-8-11), control samples were combined into one single control among the replicated experiments. Peaks across replicates with an IDR≤0.05 were retained. For visualization purpose, wiggle tracks (using pooled data across biological replicates) were generated by DeepTools[45](#page-8-12) and the command 'bamCoverage' read coverage was normalized as RPKM (reads per kilobase per million reads). ChIPseq reproducibility among different replicates was assessed by pairwise Spearman's correlation coefficients of the RPKM normalized read counts in each 10-kb window for all ChIP-seq datasets (see Supplementary Fig. 24).

We also applied this analysis pipeline to other published ChIP-seq datasets as mentioned in the main text. REF6 and H3K27me3 ChIP-seq data in seedlings were taken from a recent study[3](#page-7-1) . H3K27me3, FIE ChIP-seq data in embryos were from ref. [16](#page-7-14). H3K4me3 ChIP-seq in inflorescences were obtained from ref. [46.](#page-8-0) TF ChIPseq data for floral regulators: A[G47](#page-8-13), AP1[25](#page-7-22)[,26](#page-7-23), AP2[48,](#page-8-14) AP[349,](#page-8-15) BL[R50](#page-8-16), ET[T51,](#page-8-17) FLC[52](#page-8-18)[,53](#page-8-19), FL[M54](#page-8-20), JA[G55,](#page-8-21) LFY[56,](#page-8-22) P[I49](#page-8-15), SEP[326](#page-7-23)[,57,](#page-8-23) SOC1[58](#page-8-24) and SVP[52](#page-8-18)[,59](#page-8-25). The differentially enriched peaks of REF6 binding in seedlings and inflorescences (Fig. [3a](#page-3-0)) were determined by a more than two-fold change in normalized peak heights[60.](#page-8-26) Hypermethylation of H3K27me3 in mutants was determined by a more than two-fold change in RPKM (like peak area) in comparison with wild-type.

Based on the observation that REF6 binding sites tend to locate in promoters and gene bodies (Supplementary Fig. 25), while H3K27me3 marked regions can spread one or several genes, we used different strategies to assign peaks to their proximal genes. For REF6, a peak summit that was positioned in the gene body or within 2 kb upstream of its transcription start site (TSS) or was considered as a REF6 target gene (Supplementary Table 3). For H3K27me3, a gene overlapping at least one base pair with a peak region was assigned to the corresponding H3K27me3 marked gene (Supplementary Table 1).

**Code availability.** Data visualization was partially done by our HTPmod online tool (<http://www.epiplant.hu-berlin.de/shiny/app/HTPmod/>[\)61.](#page-8-27) Custom code for data analysis is available upon reasonable request.

**DNA sequence motif analysis.** We first extracted DNA sequences encompassing each REF6 peak summit (500bp upstream and 500bp downstream). We then used Find Individual Motif Occurrences (FIMO[\)62](#page-8-28) to scan all possible matches of the CTCTGYTY motif in extracted sequences. The relative distance of the centre of the matched region with respect to the peak summit was calculated and plotted based on a certain peak threshold (Fig. [4g,h](#page-4-0)).

### >> RNA-seq

RNA-seq reads were mapped to A. thaliana reference genome (TAIR10) using STAR (version 020201). Expression levels (in terms of FPKM, fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) of all annotated protein-coding genes were estimated by RSEM (version 1.2.22). Differentially expressed genes between mutants and wild type were identified by DESeq2. Genes were considered as differentially expressed if they showed at least two-fold changes with FDR < 0.01.

### >> ChIP-seq

The quality of the raw data (FASTQ files) was evaluated by FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Reads were then mapped to the A. thaliana genome (TAIR10) using Bowtie (version 1.1.2) with parameters "--threads 8 -n 2 -m 3 -k 1 --best - chunkmbs 256 -q". Redundant reads were removed using Picard tools (v2.60; http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Peak calling was performed using MACS2 (version 2.1.0). Duplicated reads were not considered (--keep-dup=1) during peak calling in order to achieve a better specificity. The "--mfold" parameter was set as "2-20" to build the model. The parameter "-broad" was used in the calling of H3K27me3-enriched regions (broad peaks), whereas '--call-summits' was used in the calling of narrow peaks for REF6 ChIP-seq data (see below). A relaxed threshold of p-value (p-value ≤ 1e-2) was suggested in order to enable the correct computation of IDR (irreproducible discovery rate) values. Following the recommendations for the analysis of self-consistency and reproducibility between replicates (https://sites.google.com/site/anshulkundaje/projects/idr), control samples were combined into one single control among the replicated experiments. Peaks across replicates with an IDR ≤ 0.05 were retained. For visualization purpose, wiggle tracks (using pooled data across

biological replicates) were generated by DeepTools with the command "bamCoverage"; read coverage was normalized as RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase per Million reads). >> Statistics and data visualization

R (https://cran.r-project.org/; version 3.2.3) was used to compute statistics and generate plots. ChIP-seq data signal tracks were visualized in the WashU Epigenome Browser.
