### 30 **ABSTRACT**

31 Plants perceive UV-B, an intrinsic component of sunlight, via a signaling pathway that is 32 mediated by the photoreceptor UV RESISTANCE LOCUS8 (UVR8) and induces UV-B 33 acclimation. To test whether similar UV-B perception mechanisms exist in the evolutionarily 34 distant green alga *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* (Chlamydomonas), we identified 35 Chlamydomonas orthologs of UVR8 and the key signaling factor CONSTITUTIVELY 36 PHOTOMORPHOGENIC1 (COP1). Cr-UVR8 shares sequence and structural similarity to 37 *Arabidopsis thaliana* UVR8, has conserved tryptophan residues for UV-B photoreception, 38 monomerizes upon UV-B exposure, and interacts with Cr-COP1 in a UV-B-dependent 39 manner. Moreover, Cr-UVR8 can interact with At-COP1 and complement the Arabidopsis 40 *uvr8* mutant, demonstrating that it is a functional UV-B photoreceptor. Chlamydomonas 41 shows apparent UV-B acclimation in colony survival and photosynthetic efficiency assays. 42 UV-B exposure, at low levels that induce acclimation, led to broad changes in the 43 Chlamydomonas transcriptome, including in genes related to photosynthesis. Impaired UV-B-44 induced activation in the Cr*-COP1* mutant *hit1* indicates that UVR8-COP1 signaling induces 45 transcriptome changes in response to UV-B. Also, *hit1* mutants are impaired in UV-B 46 acclimation. Chlamydomonas UV-B acclimation preserved the photosystem II core proteins 47 D1 and D2 under UV-B stress, which mitigated UV-B-induced photoinhibition. These 48 findings highlight the early evolution of UVR8 photoreceptor signaling in the green lineage to 49 induce UV-B acclimation and protection.

### 50 **INTRODUCTION**

51 UV-B radiation (280 to 315 nm) accompanies exposure to sunlight and is an abiotic factor 52 that photosynthetic organisms must tolerate. The plant photoreceptor UV RESISTANCE 53 LOCUS8 (UVR8) specifically perceives UV-B via an intrinsic tryptophan-based mechanism, 54 mainly involving Trp-285 (W285) and W233 (Rizzini et al., 2011; Christie et al., 2012; Wu et 55 al., 2012). UVR8 is a 7-bladed β-propeller protein that exists as a homodimer maintained by 56 interactions of charged amino acids across the dimer interaction surface (Rizzini et al., 2011; 57 Christie et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2012; Zeng et al., 2015). The charged amino acids that 58 maintain the homodimer are adjacent to the tryptophans involved in photoreception, 59 suggesting that excitation of these tryptophans by UV-B neutralizes dimer-maintaining 60 interactions (Christie et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2012; Mathes et al., 2015).

61 UV-B activation of UVR8 initiates a molecular signaling cascade leading to UV-B 62 acclimation and the ability to tolerate constant UV-B exposure (Kliebenstein et al., 2002; Li et 63 al., 2013; Tilbrook et al., 2013; Jenkins, 2014). Briefly, the UVR8 homodimer monomerizes 64 upon UV-B exposure and interacts with CONSTITUTIVELY PHOTOMORPHOGENIC1 65 (COP1) (Oravecz et al., 2006; Favory et al., 2009; Rizzini et al., 2011). Two separate domains 66 of UVR8 participate in the interaction with COP1: the β-propeller core of UVR8 mediates 67 UV-B-dependent interaction with COP1 and the UVR8 C-terminal C27 domain further 68 stabilizes the interaction and regulates COP1 activity (Cloix et al., 2012; Yin et al., 2015). 69 UVR8-COP1 interaction induces UV-B signaling governed by the bZIP transcription factor 70 ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL5 (HY5) and its homolog HYH (Ulm et al., 2004; Brown and 71 Jenkins, 2008; Stracke et al., 2010; Binkert et al., 2014), which leads to UV-B acclimation 72 (Brown et al., 2005; Oravecz et al., 2006; Favory et al., 2009). The UVR8 UV-B photocycle 73 is completed via the action of negative feedback regulators REPRESSOR OF UV-B 74 PHOTOMORPHOGENESIS1 (RUP1) and RUP2 (Gruber et al., 2010) which directly interact 75 with UVR8 and facilitate redimerization to downregulate UV-B signaling (Heijde and Ulm, 76 2013; Yin et al., 2015).

77 The single-celled motile freshwater alga *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* (herein referred 78 to as Chlamydomonas) is a model system for studying photosynthesis, which is useful to 79 complement and extend the understanding of photosynthetic eukaryotes when compared with 80 Arabidopsis (Rochaix, 2002; Gutman and Niyogi, 2004). Chlamydomonas is a 81 photoautotrophic organism in the wild and is exposed to a complex light environment that 82 includes UV-B. When found in aqueous habitats, Chlamydomonas UV-B exposure is 83 expected to be less than that of sunlight-exposed land plants, as UV-B attenuates rapidly in

84 the water column. The depth of UV-B penetration depends on the amount of dissolved or 85 suspended UV-B-absorbing compounds present, and thus can vary from within a few 86 centimeters to a few meters of freshwater surfaces (Hader, 2000). Other motile aquatic 87 organisms, such as *Euglena gracilis*, migrate throughout the water column using a 88 combination of gravitaxis and phototaxis to select an optimal position for photoautotrophic 89 growth whilst avoiding potential light stress (Richter et al., 2007). Phototaxis is also a well-90 characterised feature of Chlamydomonas (Kateriya et al., 2004). However, whilst 91 Chlamydomonas may exhibit mobile UV-B avoidance strategies, a compromise must still be 92 made regarding UV-B exposure in the water column to ensure that the cells intercept adequate 93 photosynthetically active radiation. Also, physical UV-B protection in the water column does 94 not contribute towards survival of Chlamydomonas in damp soil, an environment from which 95 the *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* wild-type laboratory strains have been isolated (Harris, 96 2009). It can be assumed that in stagnant water or on damp soil, Chlamydomonas UV-B 97 exposure would be comparable to levels that land plants can tolerate via engagement of the 98 UVR8 UV-B signaling pathway.

99 Like higher plants, Chlamydomonas makes use of a sophisticated photoreceptor 100 network to monitor the light environment and regulate cellular processes in response to 101 changes in light intensity, direction, and quality (Kianianmomeni and Hallmann, 2014). 102 Specifically, Chlamydomonas possesses a single phototropin (Huang and Beck, 2003), 103 rhodopsins, including channelrhodopsins, (Nagel et al., 2002; Nagel et al., 2003; Kateriya et 104 al., 2004), a plant-like cryptochrome (Reisdorph and Small, 2004), and an animal-like 105 cryptochrome that responds to both blue and red light (Beel et al., 2012). Furthermore, a 106 histidine kinase rhodopsin was recently characterised that exists in both UV-A- and blue-107 light-absorbing isoforms (Luck et al., 2012). No phytochromes can be found in the 108 Chlamydomonas genome, leading to speculation that the observed red- and far-red-light 109 responses in the organism are controlled by other photoreceptors in a manner that differs from 110 phytochrome regulation in higher plants (Kianianmomeni and Hallmann, 2014).

111 Arguably, all sun-exposed organisms require a means of UV-B protection, and initial 112 evolutionary analysis revealed good conservation of UVR8 in the plant lineage (Rizzini et al., 113 2011). We set out to establish if evolutionary conserved UVR8-dependent UV-B signaling 114 existed in Chlamydomonas. Here we show that UV-B acclimation occurs in Chlamydomonas 115 and a conserved UVR8 UV-B photoreceptor is present that monomerizes under UV-B and 116 interacts with Chlamydomonas COP1. Mutation of Cr*-COP1* in the Chlamydomonas *hit1* 117 strain results in impaired UV-B responses and acclimation. When expressed in Arabidopsis

118 *uvr8* null mutants, Cr-UVR8 is able to restore UV-B perception and signaling. We also 119 explore the link between UV-B acclimation, the levels of photosystem II (PSII) proteins D1 120 and D2, and photosynthesis.

### 122 **RESULTS**

### 123 **Structural conservation and UV-B dynamics of Chlamydomonas UVR8**

124 The presence of a Chlamydomonas UVR8-like UV-B photoreceptor was proposed previously 125 (Rizzini et al., 2011) based on its identification in the Chlamydomonas genome sequence 126 (Merchant et al., 2007). However, the available sequence annotation at the time indicated that 127 the putative Cr*-UVR8* encoded a UVR8-like protein missing the C-terminal portion, including 128 the C27 domain (Rizzini et al., 2011). For this work, we queried the predicted 129 Chlamydomonas proteome (Phytozome v10) with *Arabidopsis thaliana* UVR8 (herein 130 referred to as At-UVR8) in conjunction with analysis of the At-UVR8 Gene Homologs 131 resource on Phytozome (http://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov), which allowed us to identify 132 Cre05.g230600 as the likely candidate for Chlamydomonas UVR8 (herein referred to as Cr-133 UVR8). Cr-UVR8 was found to possess a similar sequence length (465 amino acids versus 134 440 amino acids for At-UVR8) and the same domain profile as At-UVR8, including the C27 135 domain (Figure 1). This indicated that the annotated coding sequence in the initial genome 136 release was incorrect and Chlamydomonas possesses a full-length UVR8-like protein. 137 Alignment of At-UVR8 and Cr-UVR8 revealed high pairwise sequence identity of over 45% 138 (Supplemental Figure 1). Greater sequence conservation was observed surrounding most 139 tryptophan residues, including those of the tryptophan triad (highly conserved GWRHT/S 140 motif of W233, W285 and W337 in At-UVR8 (Tilbrook et al., 2013)) (Figure 1A; 141 Supplemental Figure 1). Comparable positioning of RCC1 (PF00415) domains was also 142 observed in both At-UVR8 and Cr-UVR8 (Figure 1A). Indeed, structural modelling of Cr-143 UVR8 suggests the protein has a β-propeller structure similar to At-UVR8 (Figure 1C). Most 144 importantly, modelling predicted a comparable localization of tryptophans at the homodimeric 145 interface of both proteins, and Cr-UVR8 was shown to have equivalent residues to At-UVR8 146 W285 and W233 (Figure 1C)*.* Also apparent was good conservation of the charged amino 147 acids that facilitate electrostatic interactions across the dimer interaction surface in At-UVR8 148 (Supplemental Figure 1). This notably includes the potential for interactions of R282 (*R286* in 149 At-UVR8) with D91 (*D96*) and D102 (*D107*), and R346 (*R338*) with E38 (*E43*) and D39 150 (*D44*), which are particularly important in maintaining the At-UVR8 homodimer (Christie et 151 al., 2012; Wu et al., 2012). Moreover, the C27 domain of At-UVR8 is conserved in Cr-UVR8 (Figure 1B; Supplemental Figure 1), including the Val-Pro pair (Val420-Pro421 152 in Cr-UVR8; Val410-Pro411 153 in At-UVR8) important for interaction of At-UVR8 with AtCOP1 (Yin et al., 154 2015).

155 To analyse the Cr-UVR8 protein, we generated an anti-CrUVR8 antibody against an Nterminal peptide (Cr-UVR834-49 156 ). Cr-UVR8 was detected as two distinct bands in 157 immunoblots of non-boiled protein extracts of Chlamydomonas grown in the absence of UV-158 B (Figure 1D). As previously established for At-UVR8 (Rizzini et al., 2011), the larger of 159 these two bands was only visible when the protein extracts were left unboiled prior to gel 160 electrophoresis and the gel itself was UV-B irradiated (Figure 1D). Based on the previously 161 described properties of non-heat-denatured At-UVR8 in SDS–polyacrylamide gel 162 electrophoresis (Rizzini et al., 2011) and conservation of the key residues known to maintain 163 At-UVR8 homodimers in Cr-UVR8, the upper band very likely represents the Cr-UVR8 164 homodimer and the lower band represents the Cr-UVR8 monomer (Figure 1D). It should be 165 noted, however, that this upper band corresponds to a lower molecular weight than the 166 predicted 96.4 kDa for the denatured Cr-UVR8 homodimer (predicted Cr-UVR8 monomer: 167 48.2 kDa). However, analogous aberrant migration of non-heat-denatured At-UVR8 168 homodimers is well documented (Rizzini et al., 2011) and most importantly also observed 169 with purified homodimeric recombinant At-UVR8 (Christie et al., 2012; Heilmann and 170 Jenkins, 2013). In agreement, only the lower band corresponding to the Cr-UVR8 monomer 171 was detected upon UV-B irradiation of Chlamydomonas, and in a higher amount (Figure 1D). 172 This suggested Cr-UVR8 monomer accumulation due to monomerization of the Cr-UVR8 173 dimer, confirming that Cr-UVR8 is UV-B responsive in vivo. Cr-UVR8 dimer was restored 174 following recovery of cells in white light devoid of UV-B (Figure 1D), in agreement with 175 known redimerization of At-UVR8 (Heijde and Ulm, 2013; Heilmann and Jenkins, 2013). 176 Thus, we conclude that Cr-UVR8 is likely a homodimer in the absence of UV-B and that Cr-177 UVR8 monomerizes in response to UV-B in Chlamydomonas. This is further supported by 178 the highly conserved structure of Cr-UVR8 and the well-established properties of the At-179 UVR8 photoreceptor in Arabidopsis.

### 181 **Cr-UVR8 interacts with CrCOP1 in a UV-B-dependent manner in yeast**

182 In the same manner that Cr-UVR8 was identified, the putative ortholog of Arabidopsis At-183 COP1 was identified as Cre02.g085050 (herein referred to as Cr-COP1; previously also 184 described as PUTATIVE LIGHT RESPONSE SIGNALING PROTEIN 1, LRS1 185 (Schierenbeck et al., 2015)). The identification of Cr-COP1 further suggests the presence of a 186 conserved UV-B signaling pathway similar to the known pathway in Arabidopsis (Heijde and 187 Ulm, 2012; Li et al., 2013; Tilbrook et al., 2013; Jenkins, 2014). Cr*-UVR8* and Cr*-COP1* 188 cDNAs were cloned and verified to match their respective sequence annotations. Of note, Cr-189 COP1 (1,443 amino acids) is more than twice as large as At-COP1 (675 amino acids) but 190 possesses the same functional domains and good conservation of protein sequence 191 corresponding to RING, coiled-coil and WD40-repeat domains (Figure 2).

192 In a yeast two-hybrid assay, Cr-UVR8 interacted with Cr-COP1 and At-COP1 193 specifically under UV-B (Figure 2B), mimicking the UV-B-dependent interaction previously 194 observed between At-UVR8 and At-COP1 (Rizzini et al., 2011; Cloix et al., 2012; O'Hara and 195 Jenkins, 2012; Heijde et al., 2013; Huang et al., 2014; Yin et al., 2015). This suggests that a 196 UVR8 photocycle exists in Chlamydomonas (Figure 2C) that is very similar to what has been 197 demonstrated in Arabidopsis (e.g. Tilbrook et al., 2013).

### 199 **Cr-UVR8 complements the Arabidopsis** *uvr8* **null mutant**

200 A Cr*-UVR8* cDNA driven by the CaMV 35S-promoter was introduced into the Arabidopsis 201 *uvr8-6* null mutant (herein referred to as *uvr8*). Multiple independent transgenic lines were 202 generated with Cr-*UVR8* (Figure 3) and Cr-*UVR8* fused with cyan fluorescent protein, CFP 203 (both CFP-Cr-UVR8 and Cr-UVR8-CFP, predicted molecular weight of 76.3 kDa; Supplemental Figure 2), which were detected at the protein level using the anti-CrUVR834-49 204 205 antibodies. Cr-UVR8 was detected only in transformed *uvr8* lines and at a size expected from 206 its predicted molecular weight (48.2 kDa; Figure 3A). In all transgenic lines tested, restored 207 UV-B perception and signaling was shown using known markers as output of the UVR8 UV-208 B signaling pathway. *HY5* and *CHALCONE SYNTHASE* (*CHS*) gene activation under UV-B, 209 which is observed in wild type but absent in *uvr8*, was restored in *uvr8* lines complemented 210 with either Cr-UVR8 or Cr-UVR8 fused with CFP (Figure 3B and 3C; Supplemental Figure 211 2B and 2C). Similarly, CHS protein accumulation in response to UV-B, a normal response of 212 wild type but absent in *uvr8* mutants, was present in complemented lines (Figure 3D; 213 Supplemental Figure 2D). Finally, complementation of *uvr8* with either Cr-UVR8 or Cr-214 UVR8 fused with CFP restored UV-B-induced inhibition of hypocotyl growth, which is not 215 seen in *uvr8* seedlings (Figure 3E and 3F; Supplemental Figure 2E and 2F). The 216 complementation of *uvr8* confirmed that Cr-UVR8 is similar enough to At-UVR8 to 217 seamlessly restore UV-B perception and signaling in Arabidopsis and thus is a bona-fide UV-218 B photoreceptor.

### 220 **UV-B exposure induces UV-B acclimation and stress tolerance in Chlamydomonas**

221 With Cr-UVR8 established as a UV-B photoreceptor capable of initiating a UV-B signaling 222 pathway, further investigation was conducted to address the in vivo UV-B responses in 223 Chlamydomonas. Low-level narrowband UV-B exposure was seen to affect Chlamydomonas 224 in that the rapid increase in cell density during the exponential phase of liquid culture growth 225 was delayed, but ultimately +UV-B cultures reached a similar stationary phase cell density as 226 those cultures shielded from UV-B (Supplemental Figure 3). We further tested whether a 227 diluted "+UV-B culture" is less affected by UV-B than a naïve culture. However, the UV-B 228 effect on growth was maintained in subsequent liquid cultures, even when inoculated with 229 cells prior exposed to UV-B (Supplemental Figure 3B), indicating that daughter cells of UV-230 B exposed Chlamydomonas were also affected by continuing exposure.

231 We used these irradiation conditions to test whether Chlamydomonas is able to 232 acclimate to UV-B, similar to the UVR8-dependent acclimation response known in 233 Arabidopsis (Favory et al., 2009; González Besteiro et al., 2011). Indeed, continuous low-234 level narrowband UV-B exposure of Chlamydomonas was seen to improve tolerance and 235 survival under subsequent broadband UV-B stress treatment (Figure 4). This process of UV-B 236 acclimation was demonstrated by assessing the survival of Chlamydomonas colonies 237 subjected to broadband UV-B stress, comparing colonies previously grown under 238 supplemental low-level narrowband UV-B versus colonies grown with no prior exposure to 239 UV-B. Broadband UV-B stress resulted in a reduced number of surviving colonies for those 240 pre-grown under non-acclimating conditions, whereas a significantly greater survival rate was 241 observed in acclimated colonies (Figure 4A, "Acclimated" vs. "Non-acclimated").

242 We further tested whether UV-B acclimation is also apparent when focussing on 243 photosynthesis and the damage inflicted to PSII by broadband UV-B. We first established that 244 under our conditions broadband UV-B stress indeed impacted photosynthetic efficiency by 245 decreasing the maximum quantum yield of PSII as determined by chlorophyll fluorescence 246 spectroscopy (Fv/Fm) (Figure 4B, "Non-acclimated"). Decreased photosynthetic efficiency 247 due to light-induced damage is generally referred to as photoinhibition (Hakala-Yatkin et al., 248 2010). The extent of UV-B-induced photoinhibition increased with broadband UV-B 249 treatment time (Figure 4B). However, UV-B acclimated cells previously grown under low-250 level narrowband UV-B displayed less UV-B-induced photoinhibition following broadband 251 UV-B stress than non-acclimated cultures (Figure 4B). It is of note that supplemental 252 narrowband UV-B had no detrimental effect of its own regarding both colony survival and 253 UV-B-induced photoinhibition in control samples not treated with broadband UV-B (-UV-B) 254 (Figure 4). We thus conclude that in Chlamydomonas low-level narrowband UV-B treatment 255 results in UV-B acclimation and stress tolerance resembling the UVR8-mediated acclimation 256 response in Arabidopsis.

# 258 **UV-B acclimation in Chlamydomonas maintains D1 and D2 protein levels under high-**259 **level UV-B**

260 Chlamydomonas UV-B acclimation preserves photosynthetic efficiency upon UV-B treatment 261 that causes photoinhibition (Figure 4B). The PSII protein complex, with D1 and D2 at its 262 core, is susceptible to photodamage leading to photoinhibition, particularly by UV-B 263 (Takahashi et al., 2010). Photoinhibition is closely linked to the turnover of D1 and, to a 264 lesser extent, D2 during such photodamage (Schuster et al., 1988). Thus we investigated UV-265 B acclimation at the molecular level by measuring levels of the PSII core proteins D1 and D2 266 (Figure 5). In agreement with UV-B-induced photoinhibition, levels of D1 and D2 were 267 reduced following broadband UV-B stress (Figure 5A-D; "-CAM", "Non-acclimated"). 268 However, in acclimated cells D1 and D2 levels decreased less following broadband UV-B 269 stress (Figure 5A-D; "-CAM", "Acclimated"). We then investigated whether the protective 270 effect of UV-B acclimation on D1 and D2 protein levels was due to regeneration of damaged 271 components of the photosynthetic apparatus. For this, the UV-B acclimation experiments 272 were repeated but chloramphenicol (CAM) was added during the broadband UV-B stress. A 273 30-minute incubation with 100 µg/mL CAM was deemed sufficient to inhibit chloroplast 274 translation (Supplemental Figure 4). Greater UV-B-induced photoinhibition, indicating a lack 275 of chloroplast translation and PSII subunit resynthesis, was observed after approximately 20 276 minutes in Chlamydomonas cultures treated with CAM compared to non-treated controls 277 (Supplemental Figure 4). Maintenance of D1 and D2 following UV-B acclimation thus 278 depended on chloroplast translation as the addition of CAM equalized the effect that 279 broadband UV-B stress had on non-acclimated and acclimated culture cells, with equal and 280 larger reduction in D1 and D2 levels observed for both (Figure 5A-D; "+CAM", "Non-281 Acclimated" vs. "Acclimated"). The dynamics of D1 and D2 protein levels in these 282 experiments were consistent with UV-B-induced photoinhibition monitored by measuring 283 Fv/Fm in the UV-B acclimation assay. As presented above, less UV-B-induced 284 photoinhibition was observed in UV-B acclimated culture cells following broadband UV-B 285 stress (Figure 5E). In the absence of UV-B stress, the presence of CAM alone did not appear 286 to affect photosynthetic efficiency in either non-acclimated or acclimated cells over the course 287 of CAM treatment. However, the combination of CAM and broadband UV-B stress resulted 288 in equal and drastic UV-B-induced photoinhibition in both non-acclimated and acclimated 289 culture cells, and the UV-B acclimation effect seen in the control experiment was no longer 290 apparent (Figure 5E). We conclude that UV-B acclimation in Chlamydomonas alleviates 291 subsequent UV-B-induced photoinhibition and is associated with the induction of faster rates 292 of D1 and D2 resynthesis.

### 294 **UV-B exposure at acclimation-inducing levels causes global changes in gene expression**

295 As UV-B acclimation was evident in Chlamydomonas, we were thus interested in 296 characterising the genes regulated by UV-B exposure at acclimation-inducing levels. One-297 hour exposure of Chlamydomonas cells growing in liquid culture to low-level narrowband 298 UV-B was accompanied by broad changes in gene expression, as determined by RNA 299 sequencing (RNA-Seq) (Figure 6). With a 5% false discovery rate (FDR) and a two-fold 300 change threshold applied (|log2 FC| ≥ 1), a total of 2,601 genes were seen to vary in 301 expression upon UV-B exposure, with a fairly equal distribution of 1,326 genes up-regulated 302 and 1,275 genes down-regulated in response to UV-B (Figure 6A; Supplemental Data Set 1). 303 A set of 5 up-regulated and 4 down-regulated genes across a wide range of fold-change 304 expression were selected as marker genes for the Chlamydomonas response to acclimation-305 inducing low-level narrowband UV-B. The magnitudes of expression change of these 9 genes 306 in response to UV-B were confirmed using RT-qPCR (Figure 6B), supporting the quality of 307 the RNA-Seq data. It is of note that among the confirmed up-regulated genes there are two 308 that we have identified as putative orthologs of the Arabidopsis genes *HY5* (Cre06.g310500) 309 and *RUP1*/*RUP2* (Cre01.g053850) (Figure 6B; Supplemental Data Set 1). *HY5*, *RUP1* and 310 *RUP2* are UV-B-induced genes in Arabidopsis as well and play important roles in UV-B 311 signaling (Ulm et al., 2004; Brown et al., 2005; Gruber et al., 2010; Heijde and Ulm, 2013). 312 Independent of this, among all the UV-B induced genes, 24 were found to be induced over 313 10-fold by UV-B. Interestingly, many of them are light-harvesting complex (LHC)-like 314 members of the chlorophyll A-B binding family (Table 1). This suggests that UV-B 315 acclimation may involve protection of the photosynthetic apparatus using a broad range of 316 LHC-like proteins.

317 It is of note that a significant number of genes we report as differentially expressed in 318 response to acclimatory UV-B have been previously identified as differentially expressed 319 within 30 min when Chlamydomonas cells were transferred from dark to white light (Duanmu 320 et al., 2013) (Supplemental Figure 5). Similar to supplemental UV-B exposure, the transition 321 from darkness to white light may result in an acclimatory response that, amongst other 322 processes, promotes protection of the photosynthetic machinery. Indeed, of the 1,326 UV-B-323 induced and 1,275 UV-B-repressed genes that could be compared to the dark-to-white-light 324 dataset, >29% (388) and >24% (312), respectively, were found to be common to both light 325 transitions (Supplemental Figure 5; genes listed in Supplemental Data Sets 2 and 3). Within 326 these comparisons, it is most apparent that the commonly induced genes encompass those 327 encoding proteins that can be associated with photosynthesis and photoprotection (including 328 *PSBS1*, *PSBS2*, *LHCSR1*, *LHCSR3.1*, *LHCSR3.2*, *ELI1*).

### 330 **Cr-COP1 is required for UV-B-induced responses and acclimation in Chlamydomonas**

331 The *high light tolerant 1* mutant (*hit1*) was recently described containing a mutation replacing 332 Arg-1256 with Pro in the WD40-repeat domain of Cre02.g085050 (Schierenbeck et al., 2015), 333 thus representing a putative Cr*-COP1* mutant strain. Interestingly, in contrast to the enhanced 334 UV-B tolerance that was seen to evolve in wild type, a lesser survival rate in response to UV-335 B stress was observed in UV-B acclimated *hit1* colonies (Figure 7 A). Moreover, higher 336 maintenance of D1 following UV-B stress of acclimated cells seen for wild-type was not 337 observed in *hit1* cultures (Figure 7B). Furthermore, comparison of UV-B-induced 338 photoinhibition following broadband UV-B stress in wild-type and *hit1* showed that prior 339 exposure to low-level narrowband UV-B did not reduce UV-B-induced photoinhibition in *hit1* 340 (Figure 7C). Finally, the *hit1* strain was impaired in UV-B-induced expression changes of 341 several tested marker genes (Figure 7D). These results all imply that the Cr*-COP1* mutation in 342 *hit1* impaired its ability to respond and acclimate to UV-B. The *hit1* strain was then 343 complemented with wild-type Cr*-COP1* under expression control of the *psaD* promoter 344 (*hit1/PropsaD:CrCOP1*). This restored a resistance to UV-B-induced photoinhibition after 345 acclimation (Figure 7C), as well as most UV-B-induced expression changes (Figure 7D). 346 These results confirmed that the absence of UV-B responses and acclimation in the *hit1* strain 347 is indeed due to a mutation in the Cr*-COP1* gene.

### 349 **DISCUSSION**

### 350 **The UVR8-COP1 UV-B perception and signaling pathway in Chlamydomonas**

351 When considering the well-established features of UVR8-UV-B signaling in Arabidopsis, our 352 results indicate that Chlamydomonas has an analogous UV-B signaling pathway governed by 353 Cr-UVR8. Importantly, Cr-UVR8 contains conserved key tryptophans for UV-B perception, 354 as well as corresponding residues of crucial importance for stable homodimerisation of At-355 UVR8 in the absence of UV-B. Moreover, Cr-UVR8 is detected as two bands in non-heat-356 denatured protein samples of Chlamydomonas grown in the absence of UV-B, one at the 357 expected size of the Cr-UVR8 monomer and one slower migrating band. Even though we 358 presently cannot formally exclude that the slower migrating form of Cr-UVR8 represents a 359 heterodimer with an unknown interaction partner that is released upon UV-B treatment, we 360 suggest that this band represents homodimeric Cr-UVR8. This is despite the fact that the band 361 migrates at a lower apparent molecular weight than is expected for the denatured Cr-UVR8 362 homodimer. Such band patterns have been previously extensively described, including for 363 highly purified recombinant homodimeric At-UVR8 (e.g. Rizzini et al., 2011; Christie et al., 364 2012; Heilmann and Jenkins, 2013). Thus, there is substantial evidence that Cr-UVR8, like 365 At-UVR8, is homodimeric in the absence of UV-B. Independent of this, Cr-UVR8 was shown 366 to monomerize in response to UV-B, to interact UV-B-dependently with Cr-COP1 and At-367 COP1 in yeast and to functionally complement UV-B perception and response when 368 expressed in Arabidopsis *uvr8* null-mutant plants. The presence of Chlamydomonas orthologs 369 for additional known UV-B signaling players, including Cr-COP1 shown to partake in a 370 typical Cr-UVR8/Cr-COP1 UV-B photocycle, a putative At-HY5 ortholog (Cre06.g310500) 371 and a putative At-RUP1/At-RUP2 ortholog (Cre01.g053850), further indicates evolutionary 372 conservation of the canonical UVR8 UV-B signaling pathway.

373 COP1 proteins also show functional conservation between diverse phototrophic 374 eukaryotes, as demonstrated recently between *Physcomitrella patens*, rice, and Arabidopsis 375 (Ranjan et al., 2014) suggesting widespread presence of the UVR8/COP1 UV-B photocycle. 376 However, in addition to the conserved activity of COP1 in UV-B signaling, it is presently 377 unclear whether in Chlamydomonas Cr-COP1 acts as a repressor of physiological responses 378 analogous to photomorphogenesis in flowering plants and whether Chlamydomonas 379 cryptochrome photoreceptors signal through Cr-COP1. A comprehensive characterisation of 380 the role Cr-COP1 plays in Chlamydomonas aside from UV-B signaling may shed light on 381 how COP1 function evolved in higher plants, leading to a more comprehensive understanding 382 of this multi-faceted protein. Of note, humans have a version of COP1 (Yi and Deng, 2005) 383 indicating its far-reaching evolutionary conservation, although there is no evidence for a 384 human UVR8 counterpart for UV-B perception.

385 Taken together, our results suggest wide conservation of the UVR8 photoreceptor and 386 UV-B signaling pathway over a remarkable evolutionary distance. Moreover, conservation of 387 the number and position of key residues, including Trps and salt-bridging Args strongly 388 suggests that all UVR8 photoreceptor proteins function with a similar molecular mechanism. 389 It is clear that a UVR8-mediated UV-B perception and signaling pathway must have existed 390 in a shared common ancestor of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis >1,000 million years ago, 391 perhaps helping photosynthetic organisms to colonise environments with high levels of 392 incidental UV-B (Rozema et al., 1997; Gutman and Niyogi, 2004).

### 394 **UV-B acclimation and tolerance in Chlamydomonas**

395 A clear UV-B acclimation effect was seen in Chlamydomonas colony survival following UV-396 B stress and in Chlamydomonas cultures when using the chlorophyll fluorescence parameter 397 Fv/Fm as a measure of UV-B-induced photoinhibition. UV-B stress exposure resulted in UV-398 B-induced photoinhibition that was ameliorated by prior UV-B acclimation. When chloroplast 399 translation was blocked by using CAM, greater UV-B-induced photoinhibition was observed 400 in response to UV-B stress and, interestingly, both UV-B acclimated and non-acclimated 401 cultures were affected to the same extent. The UV-B-induced photoinhibition we observed 402 following broadband UV-B stress is presumably due to UV-B induced PSII damage that 403 exceeds the rate of repair (Takahashi et al., 2010; Takahashi and Badger, 2011). PSII subunit 404 D1 in particular is a well-known photosynthetic target of UV-B damage (Jansen et al., 1996; 405 Takahashi et al., 2010) and photoinhibition has long been associated with a decrease in D1 406 and, albeit to a lesser extent, D2 protein level (Schuster et al., 1988). In agreement, we 407 observed decreases in D1 and D2 protein levels in UV-B exposed Chlamydomonas that 408 correlated well with the extent of UV-B-induced photoinhibition. Our observed D1 and D2 409 protein decrease accompanied by photoinhibition following UV-B stress agrees with previous 410 studies (e.g. Schuster et al., 1988; Booij-James et al., 2000; Chaturvedi and Shyam, 2000; Wu 411 et al., 2011). When chloroplast translation was blocked, D1 and D2 protein level were also 412 lower in response to UV-B stress and decreased to the same extent in both UV-B acclimated 413 and non-acclimated cultures. This implies that at least part of the UV-B acclimation effect on 414 maintaining photosynthetic efficiency involves a faster rate of de novo D1 and D2 synthesis 415 within chloroplasts, similar to processes observed with high-light acclimation in pea (Aro et 416 al., 1993) and recovery following UV-B inactivation of photosynthesis in Chlamydomonas 417 (Chaturvedi and Shyam, 2000).

418 A more direct role for UVR8 UV-B signaling in Chlamydomonas UV-B acclimation 419 could be established by performing similar experiments to those we present here alongside a 420 mutant strain where the function of Cr-UVR8 is impaired. However, such a mutant strain 421 remains to be generated, despite our best efforts to knock-out/-down Cr*-UVR8* using codon 422 optimized TALEN as well as amiRNA approaches (Molnar et al., 2009; Boch, 2011). Of note, 423 an amiRNA developed concurrently to target Arabidopsis UVR8 was successfully used to 424 generate lines where UVR8 was physically and functionally absent (Vandenbussche et al., 425 2014). Nevertheless, a mutant Chlamydomonas strain was recently identified that displayed 426 enhanced high-light tolerance and was found to have an amino acid substitution in the WD40 427 domain of Cr-COP1 (reported as LRS1) (Schierenbeck et al., 2015). Since in Arabidopsis the 428 interaction of COP1 with UVR8 is at the base of the UVB signaling pathway, the *hit1* strain 429 was used in our experiments to investigate the dependence of UV-B acclimation on Cr-UVR8 430 signaling. By analysing Chlamydomonas colony survival, maintenance of D1 protein and a 431 prevention of UV-B-induced photoinhibition following UV-B stress, UV-B acclimation was 432 clearly found to be abolished in the *hit1* strain. It should be noted however that a greater basal 433 resistance to UV-B stress was observed in non-UV-B-acclimated *hit1* colonies. Such a 434 phenomenon may be the result of a constitutive *hit1* phenotype in the absence of UV-B due to 435 its potential role as repressor of light responses similar to Arabidopsis *cop1* mutant plants (Yi 436 and Deng, 2005; Schierenbeck et al., 2015). Further experiments based on UV-B-induced 437 photoinhibition alone showed that UV-B acclimation was subsequently restored in 438 *hit1/PropsaD:CrCOP1* complementation strains, indicating that UV-B acclimation phenotypes 439 are dependent on a Cr-UVR8-CrCOP1 interaction under UV-B.

440 The potential role of UVR8 UV-B signaling in photosynthesis is a current and 441 interesting question. Recent work has begun to address this and has shown in Arabidopsis that 442 UVR8 plays a role in maintaining photosynthetic efficiency under elevated UV-B (Davey et 443 al., 2012). D1 protein levels were also investigated, however the authors did not observe any 444 difference in the magnitude of D1 protein decrease under UV-B between wild-type and *uvr8* 445 plants. While this study does highlight an interesting involvement of UVR8, it does not 446 address the role of UV-B acclimation in maintaining photosynthetic efficiency under elevated 447 UV-B. It is of interest whether UV-B acclimated plants would maintain photosynthetic 448 efficiency and whether this process was abolished in *uvr8* plants. Similarly, levels of the D1 449 protein might be preserved under elevated UV-B in UV-B acclimated plants and this may 450 depend on UVR8. The question remains however what the linking mechanisms would be 451 between UVR8 UV-B signaling and D1/D2 protein resynthesis in the chloroplast. 452 Involvement of nuclear regulators of chloroplastic *psbA* (encoding D1) and *psbD* (encoding 453 D2) expression and mRNA stability would be likely. Interestingly, the Chlamydomonas 454 nuclear *NAC2* is among the UV-B induced genes in our transcriptomic analysis (2.5-fold), and 455 its product, the chloroplast TPR-like NAC2 protein, is important for *psbD* mRNA stability 456 (Boudreau et al., 2000). Also, it is possible that UV-B-induced secondary metabolites interact 457 with thylakoid membranes and promote efficient damage repair (Dobrikova and Apostolova, 458 2015; Järvi et al., 2015). However, these and several other mechanisms can be envisaged and 459 need to await experimental verification.

460 Independent of D1 and D2, it should be noted that our RNA-Seq analysis (see also 461 below) has identified a large number of genes that are associated with photosynthesis and play 462 a predicted role in photoprotection are regulated by UV-B (including *PSBS1*, *PSBS2*, 463 *LHCSR1*, *LHCSR3.1*, *LHCSR3.2*), all of which may be implicated in retaining photosynthetic 464 efficiency through UV-B acclimation. The PSBS proteins play an essential role in energy-465 dependent non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) in land plants, a mechanism that rapidly 466 promotes heat-dissipation of excess light-energy collected by the light-harvesting antenna 467 (Niyogi and Truong, 2013). In Chlamydomonas, however, the PSBS protein has so far not 468 been detected nor shown to play a role in NPQ (Bonente et al., 2008). The high light-induced 469 LHCSR proteins are essential for NPQ in Chlamydomonas and other green algae, but are 470 absent in vascular plants (Peers et al., 2009). Thus the question arises whether in 471 Chlamydomonas UV-B is perceived as a proxy for high-light exposure to trigger acclimation 472 responses and photoprotection. Alternatively, the process of UV-B acclimation may alleviate 473 the consequences of damage inflicted by UV-B to the oxygen-evolving complex. The latter 474 normally provides electrons to rapidly re-reduce PSII that has been oxidized by light-driven 475 charge separation. When the oxygen-evolving complex is damaged, down-regulation of light-476 harvesting in response to UV-B could limit the formation of oxidized PSII and ensuing 477 damage caused by this powerful oxidant.

478 When considering the interplay between UV-B and photosynthesis, there is a general 479 view that UV-B exerts an overall deleterious effect, and photosynthetic yield would be greatly 480 enhanced in its absence. However, the way UV-B experiments are performed in controlled 481 environments can create artificial situations that would rarely occur in nature whereby 482 photosynthetic organisms are subject to higher-than-ambient levels of UV-B without any prior 483 exposure. It has recently been suggested that UV-B exposure in a manner allowing UV-B 484 acclimation leads to higher photosynthetic rates and cross-photoprotection resulting in higher

485 final photosynthetic yield (Wargent et al., 2011; Wargent et al., 2015). With the current use of

486 glasshouses and UV-B impermeable cladding in modern agriculture the precise relationship

487 between UV-B and photosynthesis is important to define in detail.

### 489 **Global transcriptomic analysis of UV-B acclimation in Chlamydomonas**

490 Continuous low-level narrowband UV-B exposure allows UVR8-mediated UV-B acclimation 491 in Arabidopsis, experimentally shown as increased UV-B tolerance during a subsequent UV-492 B stress treatment in wild type, but not in *uvr8* mutants (Favory et al., 2009; Gruber et al., 493 2010; González Besteiro et al., 2011). Under these conditions, Arabidopsis seedlings can 494 grow and acclimate for many days without displaying UV-B stress symptoms. Using the same 495 low-level narrowband UV-B exposure, a previous genome-wide microarray experiment of 496 Arabidopsis wild type in comparison to *uvr8* and *cop1* mutants showed that > 97% of genes 497 induced after both 1 h and 6 h of UV-B are UVR8- and COP1-dependent (Favory et al., 498 2009). It should be noted that the UVR8-dependent transcriptome response in Arabidopsis 499 involves induction of a number of stress-related genes, for example, the *EARLY LIGHT-*500 *INDUCED PROTEIN1* (*ELIP1*) and *ELIP2* encoding stress-related chlorophyll A-B binding 501 family proteins, and *UV RESISTANCE2*/*PHOTOREACTIVATING ENZYME1* (*UVR2*/*PHR1*), 502 encoding a photolyase DNA repair enzyme (Favory et al., 2009). However, the clear role for 503 UVR8 in this process demonstrates that activity of the UV-B photoreceptor, rather than a 504 general UV-B stress, is causal for induction of these stress-related genes.

505 In this study, we designed UV-B treatment conditions using a similar approach in order 506 to analyse Chlamydomonas transcriptome changes during a likely UV-B acclimation 507 response. The same low-level narrowband UV-B shown to induce UV-B acclimation was 508 applied and global gene expression changes following 1 h of exposure were analysed by 509 RNA-Seq. Similar to the UVR8-mediated UV-B transcriptome in Arabidopsis, many of the 510 induced genes can be associated with UV-B stress protection and/or damage repair. Such gene 511 associations were expected from the UV-B acclimation observed in cultures grown under the 512 same exposure levels. The similarities between our designed RNA-Seq growth conditions and 513 those demonstrated specifically to deploy a UVR8-dependent UV-B transcriptome in 514 Arabidopsis imply that our RNAseq data is also a reflection of a Chlamydomonas UV-B 515 response governed by its Cr-UVR8 UV-B photoreceptor. Even though at present we cannot 516 unequivocally link all identified UV-B-responsive genes directly to Cr-UVR8 and Cr-COP1, 517 they underlie the physiological response of Chlamydomonas to low-level acclimatory UV-B.

518 Similar to the lack of UVR8-associated gene expression changes following UV-B 519 exposure of Arabidopsis *cop1* plants (Oravecz et al., 2006), the *hit1* strain was impaired in 520 UV-B-responsive expression changes of several UV-B marker genes. For most of these genes, 521 this phenotype could be complemented by expression of the wild-type Cr*-COP1* gene. This 522 highlights that Cr-COP1 is required at least for some of the UV-B-responsive expression 523 changes in Chlamydomonas, likely via a UV-B-dependent interaction with Cr-UVR8. We 524 thus conclude that the transcriptome changes described in here will provide an important basis 525 for a further detailed understanding of UVR8 signaling and UV-B acclimation in 526 Chlamydomonas.

527

528 In summary, here we describe evolutionarily conserved UVR8 UV-B perception and 529 signaling in Chlamydomonas. This underlines the evolutionary conservation of UVR8 UV-B 530 signaling and its importance for life under sun exposure. Also, this work establishes 531 Chlamydomonas as a novel model platform to study UV-B signaling. In addition, we 532 demonstrate that Chlamydomonas UV-B acclimation can prevent UV-B-induced 533 photoinhibition and that this process modifies rates of D1 and D2 protein repair. Future work 534 can combine these two research themes by using Chlamydomonas as a model system to 535 dissect the relationship between UVR8 UV-B signaling, UV-B exposure and photosynthesis.

### 597 **Chlamydomonas UV-B acclimation assay and chloroplast translation inhibition**

COR Biosciences)) and, if intended, 0.07 mW/cm2 605 narrowband UV-B (underneath a 311 nm 606 filter). Plate cultures were shaken at 90 rpm. ### 678 **RNA-Seq and transcriptome analysis**

679 For expression analysis in Chlamydomonas in response to UV-B, six 15-mL plate cultures 680 were grown under weak light for four days before three cultures were exposed to 1 h of narrowband UV-B (0.07 mW/cm2 681 ). Two mL of each culture was sampled following UV-B 682 treatment and pellets of cells were frozen immediately. Total RNA was extracted with a 683 NucleoSpin RNA II Total RNA isolation kit (Machery-Nagel) and final RNA samples were 684 DNase treated. Stranded RNAseq analysis of the above six RNA samples (three –UV-B, three 685 1h +UV-B) was performed in conjunction with the iGE3 genomics platform of the University 686 of Geneva (http://www.ige3.unige.ch/genomics-platform.php). The libraries were prepared 687 with 400 ng of total RNA as starting material and processed with the Illumina Truseq RNA 688 stranded kit according to the manufacturer instruction. The Accuprime High Fidelity PCR 689 enzyme was used in the DNA fragment enrichment step. Sequencing quality control was done 690 with FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). The length of 691 reads was 100 single reads. The reads were mapped using TopHat v.2 (Trapnell et al., 2009) 692 to the *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* 236 release reference (Phytozome v10.2) on new junctions 693 and known junctions annotations. Counts of the number of reads mapping to each gene 694 feature of *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* 236 reference was prepared with HTSeq v0.5.3p9 695 (htseq-count) (http://www-huber.embl.de/users/anders/HTSeq/). Differential expression 696 analysis was performed with the statistical analysis R/Bioconductor package EdgeR v. 3.4.2 697 (Robinson et al., 2010). Briefly the counts were normalized according to the library size and 698 filtered. Those genes having a count above 1 count per million reads in triplicate samples 699 were kept for the analysis. The differentially expressed genes tests were performed using 700 exact negative binomial test statistics. Differentially expressed genes with greater than 2-fold 701 change between conditions and FDR < 0.05 were plotted in R using the heatmap.2 function.

702 RNA-Seq data have been deposited in NCBI's Gene Expression Omnibus (Edgar et al., 703 2002) and are accessible through GEO Series accession number GSE68739. Coverage of 704 RNA-Seq data is shown in Supplemental Table 2.

705 For transcriptome comparison, genes with significantly altered expression (FDR <= 706 0.05 and FC >= 2 or FC <=-2) resulting from UV-B treatment were compared with those 707 genes with significant differential regulation following a dark to light transition, as previously 708 described (Duanmu et al., 2013). Genes that were updated in Phytozome 10 compared to 709 Phytozome 9 have been updated using the Phytozome 10 transfer tables as far as possible. A 710 hypergeometric test was applied to evaluate the significance of shared genes between the two 711 datasets. In order to normalize the data-sets prior plotting, a z-score was calculated for both 712 over all expressed genes and differently expressed genes were plotted against each other.

### 741 **Protein Structure Modelling**
