Running title: Factors affecting expression variation in grapes

**Keywords:** Haplotype sharing, regulatory variation, gene body methylation, purifying selection, chromosomal location, *Vitis vinifera* 

### Abstract

The transcriptional regulatory structure of plant genomes is still relatively unexplored and little is known about factors that influence expression variation in plants. We used a genetic system consisting of 10 heterozygous grape varieties with high consanguinity and high haplotypic diversity to: (i) identify regions of haplotype sharing through whole genome resequencing and SNP genotyping; (ii) analyse gene expression through RNA-seq in four stages of berry development; (iii) associate gene expression variation with genetic and epigenetic properties. We found that haplotype sharing in and around genes was positively correlated with similarity in expression and negatively correlated with the fraction of differentially expressed genes. Genetic and epigenetic properties of the gene and the surrounding region showed significant effects on the extent of expression variation, with negative associations for the level of gene body methylation and the mean expression level and positive ones for nucleotide diversity, structural diversity and ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous nucleotide diversity. We also observed a spatial dependency of covariation of gene expression among varieties. These results highlight relevant roles for *cis*-acting factors, selective constraints and epigenetic features of the gene and the regional context in which the gene is located in the determination of expression variation.

Understanding the significance of genetic variants in the non-coding portion of the genome is the next big challenge (Di Iulio *et al.*, 2018) not only to gain more information on the complex relationship between molecular genotypes and phenotypes but also for the practical purposes of being able to intervene in a targeted manner on genes to modify their expression pattern. Expression variation has a fundamental role both in plant adaptation in nature as well as in evolution under artificial selection during the breeding process. Biochemical data collected in the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) project, coupled with genetic and comparative genomics information, has led to considerable progress in the understanding of the regulatory elements in the human genome (Kellis *et al.*, 2014). The Genotype-Tissue Expression project (The GTEx Consortium, 2013) has built on this information to greatly improve our understanding of the genetic bases for gene expression variation in humans (The GTEx Consortium, 2015; The GTEx Consortium, 2017). Relatively little is known about the transcriptional regulatory structure of plant genomes in comparison to animals in terms of number, location and evolutionary conservation of cisregulatory elements (Weber *et al.*, 2016) and even less about factors that influence expression variation in plants. Information from either eQTL studies or allele-specific expression analysis has shown that *cis*-regulatory variation is more relevant than *trans*regulatory variation to determine expression variation (Li et al., 2013; Mähler et al., 2017; Waters *et al.*, 2017). The recent hyperactivity of transposable elements (TEs) observed in many Angiosperm species is a specific characteristic of plant genomes. The impact of such movement has been described in terms of effects on structural variation at the DNA sequence level (Brunner et al., 2005; Morgante et al., 2007) but much less is known on the global impact on regulatory variation.

The number of grape varieties amounts to several thousand unique genotypes, most of which are close relatives (Laucou et al., 2018). The grape germplasm shows extensive phenotypic diversity, especially for berry traits. Berry transcriptomics has been widely used to look into this diversity. Differential expression under contrasting conditions and coexpression networks have suggested candidate genes for developmental changes (Palumbo et al., 2014; Carbonell-Bejerano et al., 2014; Cramer et al., 2014; Wong et al., 2016), responses to stimuli (Deluc et al., 2009; Rienth et al., 2014; Blanco-Ulate et al., 2015), and variation in metabolite composition (Dal Santo et al., 2016; Zenoni et al., 2016). Gene expression is usually analysed in one or a few genotypes per experiment, and genotypes usually differ between experiments. A fundamental question the previous studies were not seeking to answer is the extent to which correlation in gene expression is influenced by the degree of consanguinity among grape varieties. The genetic structure of the cultivated germplasm is relatively simple, with three ancestral populations and extensive admixture (Aradhya et al., 2013). A few progenitors generated large descent groups by crossfertilization. The most popular varieties are all close relatives, connected to one another by relationships of first or second degree (Myles et al., 2011). Even local varieties, today grown in geographically distant wine regions, are separated from their common ancestors by a few generations. Modern varieties are thus expected to show extended regions of shared haplotypes (identity-by-descent, IBD).

Here, we used information on sequence and expression variation derived from DNA and RNA sequencing data, respectively, from 10 varieties to identify shared haplotypes and correlate the degree of genetic variation in *cis*-regulatory DNA sequences to divergence in gene expression. We hypothesized that correlation of gene expression between varieties depends on the level of haplotype sharing for individual genes, under the assumption that

transcriptional regulation is chiefly controlled by *cis*-acting elements. We additionally correlated the extent of expression variation among varieties with a range of genetic, epigenetic and genomic properties of the genes or of their surrounding regions, with their functional properties in primary and secondary metabolism, with the selective constraints acting on individual genes and with their role as switch genes in the transition from berry growth to berry ripening.

### Results

## Genetic distances among varieties

Pairs of varieties were compared based on the degree of haplotype sharing at 100 Kb singlecopy DNA genomic windows. Windows were sorted into three categories: (i) windows for which the pair of individuals do not share any haplotype (IBD0), (ii) windows for which the pair of individuals share one haplotype (IBD1), and (iii) windows for which the pair of individuals share both haplotypes and are therefore genetically identical (IBD2). The haplotypic distance in 45 pairwise comparisons involving the 10 varieties ranged from 0.58 to 0.78, with a median value of 0.75 (Figure S1a and S1b). The aggregate length of shared haplotypes in each pairwise comparison is reported in Table S1. Examples of haplotype sharing in the karyograms of three white-skinned grapes are illustrated in Figure 1. The varieties 'Garganega' and 'Passerina' showed the largest aggregate length of shared haplotypes (Figure 1a). They are genetically identical across 9.1 % of the genome, where they share both haplotypes (IBD2), and they share one haplotype across 65.6 % of the genome (IBD1). They have different haplotypes (IBD0) across the remaining 24.4 % of the genome. The varieties 'Passerina' and 'Vermentino' showed the smallest aggregate length

of shared haplotypes (Figure 1b). They are identical across 3.6 % of the genome and they share one haplotype across 36.3 % of the genome. They have different haplotypes across the remaining 58.7 % of the genome. We hypothesized that gene expression differences between varieties may not only depend on IBD in the region containing the expressed gene, but also on the amount of nucleotide diversity whenever the gene was not in an IBD2 region. Thus, we also expressed genetic distance in terms of genotypic distance (Figure S1a and S1c). The mean genotypic distance showed limited variation across pairwise comparisons and a significant correlation (*p*-value = 0.0017) with the haplotypic distance using a Mantel test with 10,000 permutations. The genotypic distance among varieties with the same berry colour ( $D = 0.125 \pm 0.014$  among white grapes and  $D = 0.127 \pm 0.011$  among red grapes) was similar to the genotypic distance among varieties with contrasting berry colour ( $D = 0.127 \pm 0.011$  white versus red grapes). White-skinned varieties are homozygous for a loss-of-function mutation at the *MybA* berry colour locus on chromosome 2 (Walker *et al.*, 2007). Our set of five white grapes showed absence of genetic variation around the *MybA* locus as expected (Figure S2).

# Global gene expression similarity among varieties and genetic distances

We compared berry transcriptomes of 10 varieties and used Spearman (Figure 2) and Pearson (Figure S3) correlation as a measure of similarity in expression between pairs of varieties. Figure 2a shows the distribution of Spearman correlation in 45 pairwise comparisons between varieties, at four stages of berry development. During the green phase of berry development (pea size, P, and pre-veraison, PV, stages), transcriptomes showed high similarity among genotypes. Correlation of expression was consistently high in

all pairwise comparisons, fluctuating within a narrow range between 0.964 and 0.968, and with median R values of 0.967 at the P stage and 0.966 at the PV stage. The distributions of R did not differ significantly between the P and PV stages (Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney p-value = 0.8534). As berry ripening commenced, the correlation of gene expression decreased. At the end of veraison (EV), the distribution of R shifted towards significantly lower values (Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney p-value <  $2.2e^{-16}$ ). The median R value in EV berries was 0.957. At harvest (H), the distribution of R was significantly different compared to the EV stage (Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney p-value = 0.0017). The median R value in H berries was 0.953. The H stage of berry ripening was also marked by the amplest variation in R values among pairwise comparisons of varieties. During ripening, berry transcriptomes became increasingly differentiated according to the genotype. At harvest, part of this variation in gene expression could be the result of variability in environmental factors. Berry development was asynchronous among genotypes. Within each sampling stage, we scheduled the day of sampling in a genotype-specific manner, in order to collect berries synchronized by phenology across genotypes. Shifts in phenology among genotypes widened as berry development progressed (Massonnet et al., 2017). At P and PV stages, the interval of sampling spanned three and four days, respectively. At EV and H stages, the concentration of soluble solids in grapes dictated the day of sampling in each genotype. The interval of sampling among genotypes spanned 20 and 22 days at EV and H stages, respectively. During berry ripening (EV and H stages), the correlation of gene expression was lower in pairwise comparisons of red varieties than in pairwise comparisons of white varieties (Figure 2b and 2c). Correlation of gene expression between genotypes showed a mild but statistically significant (p-value 0.019) tendency to increase as haplotypic distance decreased (Figure S4).

#### Individual gene expression similarity among varieties and haplotype sharing

For each pairwise comparison of varieties we sorted the predicted genes into three categories based on the level of haplotype sharing at their genomic location: (i) genes for which the pair of individuals do not share any haplotype (IBD0), (ii) genes for which the pair of individuals share one haplotype (IBD1), and (iii) genes for which the pair of individuals share both haplotypes and are therefore genetically identical (IBD2). We assumed the absence of *cis*-regulatory effects on gene expression under the condition of complete haplotype sharing (IBD2). As expected if *cis*-acting effects on gene regulation are prevalent over *trans*-acting ones, the similarity in expression levels between varieties, as estimated by Spearman correlation, was highest for genes located in genomic regions identical by descent (IBD2), intermediate for genes in regions with partial haplotype sharing (IBD1), and lowest for the subset of genes in regions with no haplotype sharing (IBD0) (Figure 3a). The distributions of correlation coefficients showed significant differences among genes in IBD0 versus IBD1 versus IBD2 condition at all berry development stages, with the exception of genes in IBD1 versus IBD2 condition at harvest. We obtained similar results when similarity in expression levels between varieties was estimated by Pearson correlation (Figure S5). Because Pearson correlation is less robust than Spearman against outliers (e.g. highly expressed genes), the significance of the differences among IBD categories improved as we removed a very limited number (from 9 to 31) of highly expressed genes from the analysis.

We looked at consequences of different levels of haplotype sharing using two additional measures of expression similarity, the absolute value of the log<sub>2</sub> ratio of expression and the proportion of differentially expressed genes. Absolute log<sub>2</sub> expression ratios diverged more from 0 (indicating equal expression) as the level of haplotype sharing decreased in all four stages (Figure 3b). In terms of significant differences in gene expression, we found the highest fraction of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for genes with IBD0, and the lowest for genes with IBD2 (Figure 3c). We thus concluded that haplotype sharing in *cis*-regulatory regions explained most of the observed differences in gene expression among synchronous berries of different varieties. A small fraction of genes showed significant differences in gene expression among synchronous differences in gene to either *trans*-acting regulation, or differential responses among genotypes to uncontrolled environmental variation, or residual asynchrony in sampled berries.

We hypothesized that expression variation in specific groups of genes may show a different dependency on sequence variation. For example, groups of genes that are controlled by master regulators may be less dependent on haplotype sharing in their *cis*-regulatory regions and genes encoding for enzymes that are not operating in isolation but as components of a metabolic pathway may have more constraints on their gene expression variation. To address these issues, we analysed two sets of coexpressed genes involved either in berry anthocyanin accumulation (secondary metabolism) or in lysine biosynthesis (primary metabolism) (Figure S6 and S7). The first set consisted of 18 genes: (i) a *trans*-acting master regulator (VIT\_02s0033g00410, *VvMYBA1*, Walker et al., 2007) encoding a R2R3 Myb-type transcription factor that in other species physically interacts with (ii) a WD-40 repeat protein (the gene product of VIT\_16s0098g00870, *VvWDR1*, Matus et al., 2010)

and activates the transcription of (iii) VIT\_16s0039g02230 (*VvUFGT*, Cutanda-Perez et al., 2009) encoding a UDP-glucose:flavonoid 3-O-glucosyl transferase necessary for the conversion of colourless anthocyanidins into their coloured glucosyl conjugates, (iv) other regulatory genes (VIT\_01s0011g04760, *VvMYBC2-L1*, Cavallini et al., 2015;

VIT 15s0046g00170, VvMybPA1, Bogs et al., 2007; VIT 06s0004g00570, VvMyb5b, Cavallini et al., 2014), and (v) other structural genes with no physical proximity in the grape genome and encoding conserved enzymes for the synthesis of flavonoid precursors (chalcone synthases, flavanone 3-hydroxylases, flavonoid 3'-hydroxylase, flavonoid 3',5'-hydroxylase, dihydroflavonol reductase, leucoanthocyanidin dioxygenase) or for vacuolar delivery of the end products (VIT 16s0050g00910, AM3, and VIT 04s0079g00690, GST4, Gomez et al., 2011). The second set included 42 expressed genes listed in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Pathway Database (Kanehisa and Goto, 2000; Kanehisa et al., 2017; Kanehisa et al., 2019) for lysine biosynthesis with no physical proximity in the grape genome. Gene expression variation in all pairwise comparisons of 10 varieties showed higher absolute values of log<sub>2</sub> ratio than the average in secondary metabolism and lower values than the average in primary metabolism (Figure S8). The predominant effect of *cis*acting genetic differences that was apparent at the whole genome level (Figure S8a) remained significant for genes involved in the lysine biosynthetic pathway (Figure S8c), while it was nullified, presumably by stronger *trans*-acting effects, for genes involved in anthocyanin synthesis and transport, resulting into non-significantly different distributions of log<sub>2</sub> ratios under the conditions of IBS0, IBS1, and IBS2 (Figure S8b).

#### Signatures of selection, expression similarity and haplotype sharing

We looked at sequence evolution and namely at selective constraints on protein sequence evolution by computing the ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous nucleotide diversity  $(\theta_N/\theta_S)$  for each gene as a proxy for the strength of purifying selection. A strong monotonic relationship was detected in all four stages of berry development between the strength of purifying selection and the expression similarity, no matter which of the three measures was used (expression correlation, absolute value of  $\log_2$  ratio, proportion of differentially expressed genes), Figure 4, with genes experiencing stronger purifying selection being significantly more similar in expression. When we looked at haplotype sharing levels in relation to different purifying selection levels as expressed by the  $\theta_N/\theta_S$  ratio, we observed that the monotonic relationship was present and significant for each category of haplotype sharing (IBD0, IBD1 and IBD2), even though with decreasing strength as we moved from IBD0 to IBD2 (Figure 5a, 5b, 5c). A direct comparison of expression similarity, as estimated by the Spearman correlation coefficient, among the three categories of haplotype sharing, for each class of  $\theta_N/\theta_S$  ratio reveals that, no matter how strong the purifying selection is, the similarity increases significantly in an additive manner going from IBD0 to IBD2 with increasing differences being observed as purifying selection intensity decreases (Figure 5d).

#### Spatial analysis of gene expression variation

We also considered whether gene expression variation is influenced by physical proximity of genes by looking either at pairs of consecutive genes or at all pairs of nearby genes and their correlation in gene expression among varieties and developmental stages. We sorted gene pairs in 1 Kb bins, based on the distance between their transcription start sites (TSS) (from 1

to 30 Kb for consecutive genes and 50 Kb for all genes). We found that the correlation of transcript abundances between consecutive gene pairs decreases as their inter-TSS distance increases (Figure S9a and S9b). According to a Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test, the expression correlations for gene pairs with inter-TSS distance longer than 2 Kb were significantly lower than for gene pairs with shorter inter-TSS distance. Part of this correlation may be due to bidirectional promoters (Li et al., 2006; Chen et al., 2010) coupling the transcription of head-to-head gene pairs – or to the presence of enhancers that coregulate multiple neighbouring genes. The separate analysis for head-to-head (i.e. those including pairs of genes under bidirectional promoters) gene pairs does not show a larger contribution of this group of genes to the overall short distance expression correlation in comparison to head-to-tail and tail-to-tail genes (Figure S10 and S11). Gene expression is also affected in *cis* by the intrachromosomal variation in chromatin domains (Razin *et al.*, 2007) and by the presence of structural units of higher order chromatin organization such as topologically associating domains (TADs, Dixon et al., 2012). We thus tested pairwise correlation of transcript abundances between physically adjacent genes (up to 30 Kb in distance) and between gene pairs in which the members were randomly sampled from different chromosomes. Consecutive genes showed significantly higher correlation of expression up to an inter-TSS distance of 27 Kb. When all gene pairs were considered (Figure S9c and S9d) the decreasing trend of expression correlation with increasing physical distances is even more visible at least up to a distance of approximately 20 Kb and the higher correlation in comparison to unlinked genes is significant visible even up to 48 Kb distances. To make sure that this result was not influenced by the level of haplotype sharing we checked for each distance bin the distribution of haplotype sharing categories for the

pairs of genes and, as expected, the level of haplotype sharing was similar for all distance bins as well as for the unlinked genes.

# Associations between genetic and epigenetic factors and gene expression variation

We used a generalized linear model (GLM) analysis to identify different genomic properties, either of the individual gene or of the genomic region in which it resides, that are associated with the extent of gene expression variation among individuals. We considered the coefficient of variation among expression levels in the 10 varieties (FPKM) as dependent variable (following  $\log_{10}$  transformation) and eight independent variables. Six of these were attributes of the gene (mean FPKM, level of GbM, TE density in the 5' intergenic region, TE density in the intronic space, nucleotide diversity in the exons and introns of the gene, and  $\theta_N/\theta_S$ ) and two of them were attributes of the 100 Kb window in which each gene resides (gene density, structural diversity). We also found that additional regional properties such as TE density and cytosine methylation in the CG context are highly correlated with one another and with transcript density (r>0.6) (Figure S12). We therefore decided to use only transcript density in the model to recapitulate a number of regional properties. The results for each of the four developmental stages of the berry are shown in Table 1. Results of the analysis were highly concordant among stages in terms of significance as well as direction of the association. To test the significance of the association, we performed a simulation by randomly assigning to each gene dependent variable (coefficient of variation of expression) the eight independent variables. Probability values obtained from 10,000 iterations of the simulation were in very good concordance with those observed (Table S2). The variables with the most significant effects on gene expression variation were the mean level of

expression and the level of gene body methylation (GbM), both negatively correlated with expression variation. Nucleotide and structural diversity and ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous nucleotide diversity ( $\theta_N/\theta_s$ ) were the only positively associated variables, while the density of transposable elements in the 5' intergenic region was the only variable that did not show a significant association in any of the four stages of development. Both presence of TEs in the introns and gene density, which is a proxy for a number of other regional properties as reported above, were also negatively associated with expression variation. The model was highly significant in all four stages and it explained a considerable fraction of the variance observed in gene expression variation as estimated by the  $r^2$ coefficient, ranging from a maximum of 42 % for the pre-veraison stage to a minimum of 36 % for the harvest stage (Figure S13). The decrease in predictive power of the model based on genic and genomic properties with the progression in berry development may reflect a greater contribution of environmental variation among genotypes and/or asynchronous berry development that may also be responsible for the previously reported lower overall correlations in expression among varieties.

# Ripening-related gene coexpression networks and switch genes

We generated a coexpression network in each variety, using a set of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between green (P and PV stages) and ripening (EV and H stages) berries, hereafter referred to as ripening-related genes. A topological role was assigned to each node based on three components: degree of interaction within the module ( $z_g$ ), degree of interaction among modules ( $K_{\pi}$ ), and average Pearson correlation coefficient (APCC) between the expression profiles of each node and the nearest neighbours (Palumbo *et al.*,

2014). The heat cartography maps show the topological properties of the coexpression network in each variety (Figure S14). The plane of the cartography map is defined by  $K_{\pi}$  and  $z_a$  axes and is arbitrarily divided into seven regions, defining different topological roles. In each map, we identified a topologically isolated group of nodes in the R4 region of the  $K_{\pi}z_a$ plane, corresponding to switch genes (Figure S15). This group comprised on average 176 nodes per network (Table S3). Switch genes correspond to nodes that have few interactions in their own module and show many interactions outside their module, with a negative average correlation with their interactors, and are a class of hubs that should include genes that induce major transcriptome changes during berry ripening (Palumbo et al., 2014). Our 10 varieties shared a large proportion of the switch genes (Table S4). A subset of varieties that comprised all white-skinned varieties and the red grape 'Sangiovese' shared a number of switch genes greater than the average of all pairwise comparisons involving each of these varieties (Figure S16). Switch genes appear to be under weaker purifying selection when compared to other topologically defined categories of genes (R1, ultraperipheral nodes; R2, peripheral nodes; R3, non-hub connectors; R4, non-hub kinless nodes), Figure S15.

# Discussion

In this study, we analysed gene expression variation among 10 grape varieties during berry development and identified associations between properties of the genes and/or of the genomic regions they reside in and variation in expression levels on one hand and topology of gene coexpression networks on the other hand. We used haplotype sharing between genealogically related individuals, which extended from the transcriptional unit into the *cis*-regulatory region of each gene, as a proxy for *cis*-sequence variation. We found that

similarity in gene expression across synchronized berries of different grape varieties is highly correlated with haplotype sharing at *cis* loci. At all analysed stages of berry development, the effects of *trans*-acting regulation and environmental variation on berry transcriptomes never masked the predominant effect of *cis*-regulation. Median values of Spearman correlation decreased for all levels of haplotype sharing as berry development progressed, likely resulting from an increasing effect of environmental factors and asynchronies in berry development. Median values of Spearman correlation showed a nearly perfectly additive behaviour of gene expression as haplotype sharing increased, with similarity in expression between varieties for genes in regions with partial haplotype sharing (IBD1) being intermediate between that for genes located in genetically identical regions (complete haplotype sharing, IBD2) and for genes in regions with no haplotype sharing (IBD0). An indirect confirmation of the relative importance of *cis*-acting factor can also be derived from the results of the GLM analysis, where only *cis*-acting factors are accounted for and where the model explains a considerable fraction of the variation in expression variation.

In addition to haplotype sharing we have shown that also the strength of purifying selection acting on a gene is significantly correlated with expression variation. Evidences are accumulating that lend support to the view that genes with regulatory variation are also subject to weaker purifying selection (Pophaly and Tellier, 2015; Steige *et al.*, 2017; Mähler *et al.*, 2017; Lin *et al.*, 2018). Our observations fully support this view. Interestingly, not only genes showing *cis*-regulatory variation are under weaker purifying selection (Steige *et al.*, 2017), but also *trans*-regulatory variation is more frequently observed in genes that evolve under less selective constraints. We found significant differences in expression similarity as purifying selection levels vary for genes that are only subject to *trans*-acting effects (IBD2 genes, Figure 5c). The simultaneous comparison of expression similarity among genes that

differ in haplotype sharing as well as in purifying selection strength reveals that both *cis*and *trans*-acting effects on regulatory variation are increasing as purifying selection is relaxed, highlighting a tight interrelationship between purifying selection acting on coding regions and on noncoding ones affecting expression variation.

We have observed a significant effect of physical distance on the extent of covariation of gene expression of neighbouring genes. Genes that are physically very close to one another (less than 2-3 Kb) are more correlated in their expression than genes that are further apart. This may be largely due to the presence of pairs of head-to-head genes that share so-called bidirectional promoters (Trinklein et al., 2004; Dhadi et al., 2009) or to tandemly arranged and recently duplicated genes. The observation that consecutive genes within 25-27 Kb and pairs of genes within 48 Kb distance are significantly more correlated in their expression than unlinked genes may point to either the existence of local chromatin domains that can be altered by genetic variation, possibly affecting multiple genes, or of *cis*-regulatory elements that can interact locally with multiple genes. Neighbouring genes on chromosomes are more similar in their expression patterns than unlinked gene pairs in a range of eukaryotic species (Hurst *et al.*, 2004). Expression piggybacking (Ghanbarian and Hurst, 2015), i.e. the tendency of adjacent genes to be coexpressed, has recently also been reported to be widespread in an eQTL study in maize (Wang et al., 2018). Clusters of coregulated genes could arise as a result of different underlying molecular mechanisms. The attempts at identifying in plants higher order chromatin structures such as TADs that have been successfully identified in Drosophila, mouse and humans, among the others (Sexton et al., 2012; Nora et al., 2012; Dixon et al., 2012), where clusters of coregulated genes have often been observed (Nora et al., 2012), have so far not been very successful (Wang et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2017; Dong et al., 2017). Also examples of

individual enhancers (Jin *et al.*, 2013; Lower *et al.*, 2009) or superenhancers (Novo *et al.*, 2018) that can exert their effects over several genes have not been reported for plants. While most studies here mentioned have focused on coexpression across tissues or environmental conditions, we show that the same relationship between physical distance and coexpression is also observed across genotypes. This observation lends support to the hypothesis that regulatory variation does not act on individual genes but can exert its effects over neighbouring genes.

Our GLM analysis has shown strong associations between the extent of expression variation among varieties, and a number of genetic and epigenetic properties either of the gene itself or of the genomic region in which it is located. The strongest associations, both negative, are observed with the level of gene expression and with the level of GbM. Highly expressed genes have previously been shown to be less prone to gene expression variation in mammals (Liao and Zhang, 2006) and plants (Xu et al., 2016). GbM is pervasive in Angiosperms and consists of high levels of methylated CG in the transcribed portion of the gene accompanied by low levels at the transcription start and transcription termination sites (Bewick and Schmitz, 2017). Evidences collected so far lend support to some type of association between transcription and GbM, however its precise function still remains elusive (Zilberman, 2017) in consideration of the fact that some plant species lack it entirely (Bewick and Schmitz, 2017). GbM is evolutionarily conserved and affects genes that are frequently housekeeping, constitutively expressed and slowly evolving. A strong association between GbM and cis-regulatory variation has been observed in Capsella grandiflora (Steige et al., 2017). In light of the positive correlation between nucleotide diversity of the gene and expression variation, the negative association between GbM levels and expression variation may result from lower nucleotide diversity as a consequence of slow evolution rates of

these genes. The correlation between the two factors is, however, slightly negative (-0.05), thus ruling out this simple explanation. A higher correlation is observed between GbM and the density of transposable elements in the genes (r=0.25). Grapevine is characterized by unusually long introns and a high frequency of LINE elements as compared to other plant species (Jaillon *et al.*, 2007). A closer inspection of GbM data shows that most LINE elements are present within introns of genes with high levels of GbM and this may at least partly explain the negative association we detected. Finally, significant positive associations with expression variation were observed for both nucleotide diversity and the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous nucleotide diversity, as previously reported for a range of organisms including Arabidopsis (Kliebenstein *et al.*, 2006), Capsella (Steige *et al.*, 2017) and aspen (Mähler *et al.*, 2017; Lin *et al.*, 2018).

As to the regional properties we tested in the GLM model, gene density recapitulates a number of other properties since it is highly correlated with transposon density (negatively) and cytosine methylation in all contexts (negatively). Our model shows that genes located in gene-rich, transposon-poor, open chromatin (A-type chromatin compartments) and methylation poor regions are less variable in their expression than those in regions with opposing features. A positive association is observed with regional structural diversity that in grapevine is mostly due to presence/absence polymorphisms, as a consequence of recent TE insertions. A positive association between expression variation and TE insertions has been reported for Drosophila (Osada *et al.*, 2017). TE insertions can affect expression of flanking genes in a variety of ways, either by disrupting or providing regulatory elements such as promoters and enhancers (Dubin *et al.*, 2018) or by affecting the epigenetic status of the local chromatin region (Song and Cao, 2017).

Genetic variants that impact coexpression networks by modifying coexpression relationships have been identified in humans as coexpression QTLs (Van Der Wijst *et al.*, 2018). We show here that switch genes, i.e. hub genes with a role as master regulators during berry ripening, are largely shared among the 10 varieties, showing the robustness of the network topology for this category of genes, even though they appear to be under weaker purifying selection than any of the other topologically defined categories.

In this study, we combined the analysis of the grape berry transcriptome in a set of 10 highly heterozygous varieties with a range of different data sets, including sequence and structural diversity, epigenetic modifications and chromosome structure to shed light on the factors that influence the extent of expression variation, as well as of coexpression network variation. While the importance of the role of *cis*-acting regulatory variation in determining gene expression variation confirms previous observations in both plant as well as animal systems, our analysis of expression variation highlights: (i) relationships between genetic, epigenetic and genomic factors and the levels and patterns of expression variation among individuals, (ii) relevant roles for gene body methylation, expression levels, structural and nucleotide diversity, gene richness, and local spatial effects on gene coexpression, and (iii) a tight relationship between the strength of purifying selection acting on coding regions and the extent of both *cis*- and *trans*-acting regulatory variation.

#### **Experimental procedures**

#### **DNA sequencing and SNP calling**

Genomic DNA from each cultivar was sheared by sonication and 2 × 100 bp paired-end libraries (sample preparation guide, Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) were sequenced on Illumina HiSeq2500 sequencing apparatus. The raw sequences of 'Sangiovese' and 'Vermentino' are deposited in NCBI Short Read Archive (SRA) under the BioProject no. SRP106422 and SRP111078, respectively. The raw sequences of the remaining eight varieties are deposited under the BioProject no. SRP119718. Raw reads were trimmed using erne-filter (ERNE, RRID:SCR 010912) (Del Fabbro et al., 2013) and adapters were removed with cutadapt (cutadapt, RRID:SCR 011841) (Martin, 2011). Reads were aligned to the Vitis vinifera 12Xv0 reference genome (Jaillon et al., 2007) of the strain PN40024 (GCA 000003745.2) using BWA (BWA, RRID:SCR 010910, Li and Durbin, 2009) with default parameters The coverage of the uniquely mapped paired-end reads ranged between 9.7 to 89.8 (average 32) among varieties. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were called using GATK Unified Genotyper variant discovery (GATK, RRID:SCR 001876, McKenna et al., 2010; DePristo *et al.*, 2011). SNPs with Phred scaled quality score  $\geq$  50 were called in informative sites with minimum coverage  $\geq$  5 reads or read coverage  $\geq$  0.5 × or  $\leq$  1.5 × the modal coverage. Heterozygous genotypes were called when the reference/alternate allele ratio was  $\geq 0.25$  and  $\leq 0.75$ . We removed variant sites in repetitive DNA, which included high copy number sequences identified by ReAS (Li et al., 2005), transposable elements annotated in Repbase (Bao et al., 2015), and microsatellites ± 10 bp identified using the software Sputnik (Abajian, 1994). We identified and used for subsequent analyses a total of 4,373,518 high-quality variant sites in 10 varieties, with an average of 1,469,301 variant sites

across an average of 165.8 M informative nucleotides per variety. The SNP analysis was restricted to the scaffolds anchored to the 19 grape chromosomes, amounting to 426.2 Mb in the 12Xv0 version of the reference genome.

# **Genetic relationships**

Genotypic distance (*D*) was calculated using the following formula: genD = ((IBS1\*0.5) + IBS0) / (IBS0 + IBS1 + IBS2 + 0). Sites were classified as IBS0, IBS1, IBS2 as described by (Wu *et al.*, 2014). Sites where both individuals showed identical homozygous genotype were classified as "0". For the calculation of haplotypic distance (hapD), we defined 2,367 genome windows, each 100 Kb in length of single-copy DNA, by masking repetitive DNA using ReAS annotations (Li *et al.*, 2005). The IBD level in each genome window was calculated with a slightly modified version of the identity-by-state ratio (IBSRH) method used in citrus (Wu *et al.*, 2014) with the following thresholds: IBD = 0 (IBD0) if IBSRH < 0.95 and genotypic distance (D) > 0.025; IBD = 1 (IBD1) if IBSRH  $\ge$  0.95 and D > 0.025; IBD = 2 (IBD2) if IBSRH  $\ge$  0.95 and D  $\le$  0.025. IBSRH was calculated using the following formula: IBSRH = (IBS2 + IBS1)/(IBS2 + IBS1 + IBS0). Then, hapD was expressed using the cumulative genome length (bp) of IBD0, IBD1, and IBD2 windows in the following formula: hapD = ((IBD1 \* 0.5) + IBD0) / (IBD0 + IBD1 + IBD2). Aggregate length of shared haplotypes was obtained as sum of nucleotides in windows with IBD1 and twice the nucleotides in windows with IBD2.

#### Gene expression analysis

Experimental conditions for gene expression analyses are described in Massonnet et al., 2017. RNA-seq raw reads are stored in NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under the accession numbers GSE62744 and GSE62745. Transcript abundance was analysed at four developmental stages of the berry: pea size (P), pre-veraison (PV), end of veraison (EV), and harvest (H). Berries at P and PV are in the phase of green growth. Berries at EV and H are in the phase of ripening. Berries at EV where collected at an average of 12.7° Brix of soluble sugar concentration, approximately 10 days after softening, which marks the onset of berry ripening. Berries at H where collected at an average of 19.7° Brix. Transcript levels were expressed as FPKM. Three biological replicates were analysed with Cuffdiff (Cuffdiff, RRID:SCR 001647, Trapnell et al., 2012) for the identification of significant changes in expression level between genotypes. Genes were considered differentially expressed if p was greater than the FDR after the Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple-testing. Mean values of three biological replicates were used in all other analyses. Correlation of gene expression was calculated using the Spearman or Pearson (R) coefficient in pairwise comparisons between varieties. Transcript abundances refer to 29,770 gene models predicted by the V1 gene annotation (http://genomes.cribi.unipd.it/DATA/V1/). A Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test was used for assessing significant differences between distributions of values. For all subsequent analysis we retained only transcribed genes with FPKM greater than 1 in at least one variety.

#### Metabolic pathway analysis

From a set of 41 genes putatively involved in flavonoid biosynthesis, we selected a subset of 26 genes more specifically involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis among those showing highest correlation between expression level and berry anthocyanin content (Falginella *et al.*, 2012). We retained one copy of tandemly arrayed genes for *CHS*, *F3'H*, *F3'5'H* by selecting the copy with highest correlation between expression level and anthocyanin content in each gene family. We finally analysed expression variation among genotypes using 18 anthocyanin transcribed genes with FPKM greater than 1 in at least one variety.

From a set of 47 genes listed in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Pathway Database for their involvement in lysine biosynthesis (organism identifier vvin, pathway identifier 00300), we retained one copy of tandemly arrayed genes for *AGD2aminotransferase* (VIT\_03s0088g01040) and *cystathionine beta-lyase* (VIT\_12s0028g03200) by selecting the copy with highest average expression in the 10 varieties of this study. We finally analysed expression variation among genotypes using 42 lysine transcribed genes with FPKM greater than 1 in at least one variety.

### **Detection of selection signatures**

We used the ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous diversity ( $\theta_N/\theta_S$ ) to assess the level of constraint in the coding portion of each gene.  $\theta_N/\theta_S$  was calculated using all variant sites identified in an extended set of 126 accessions of *Vitis vinifera* (Gabriele Magris, Michele Morgante, personal communication) including the 10 analyzed in this paper to have a more

accurate estimate of selective constraints than the one we could have obtained from 10 varieties only.

#### Spatial analysis of gene expression

We computed Pearson and Spearman correlation of gene expression for genes in physical proximity using FPKM values in all 10 varieties and in all developmental stages. We selected all pairs of consecutive genes with an inter-TSS distance < 30 Kb in the reference genome, (i) no matter the orientation of the members of the pair, (ii) with head-to-head, (iii) tail-tohead or head-to-tail, (iv) tail-to-tail orientation. We also selected all pairs of genes with an inter-TSS distance < 50 Kb in the reference genome, no matter the orientation of the members of the pair.

# Generalized linear model

A linear model was formulated in order to analyse how regional and genic factors correlate with the variation in expression estimated by computing the coefficient of variation of FPKM. We used the Im function implemented in R (R Project for Statistical Computing, RRID:SCR\_001905, R Core Team, 2013). The coefficient of variation of expression measured as FPKM was computed across the 10 varieties separately for each developmental stage. Genes with an average FPKM level across the 10 varieties below 0.5 were removed from the analysis. The log<sub>10</sub> transformation of the coefficient of variation was used as dependent variable of the linear model.

Intergenic TE density was assessed in the intergenic space upstream the TSS of each gene. ReAS annotations (Li *et al.*, 2005) were used to identify repetitive regions in the reference genome (Jaillon *et al.*, 2007). Similarly, the genic TE density was estimated using the ReAS annotations across the entire gene sequence (including both exons and introns).

Nucleotide diversity was estimated using Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms identified in each gene across the 10 varieties.

For the analysis of gene body methylation (GbM), DNA methylation estimates at singlecytosine resolution were generated by whole genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) of purified nuclear DNA from expanded leaf tissue. Leaves were collected from three independent plants of Vitis vinifera 'Pinot Noir', grown under field conditions. The three WGBS libraries were constructed either using NuGEN Ovation<sup>®</sup> Ultralow Methyl-Seq Library Systems in combination with Qiagen EpiTect<sup>®</sup> Fast Bisulfite Kit (first replicate) or Illumina TruSeq DNA Methylation Kit after processing with Zymo Research EZ DNA Methylation-Gold<sup>™</sup> kit (second and third replicate) following manual protocols. A spike-in of Lambda phage unmethylated DNA was added to the libraries to estimate bisulfite conversion rates (threshold for acceptance, 98 %). WGBS libraries were sequenced on Illumina HiSeq2000/2500 sequencers yielding approximately 318 million paired-end 100/125 bp reads per library. Raw reads were quality- and adapter-trimmed, aligned to the Vitis vinifera 12Xv0 reference genome of the strain PN40024 and converted into single cytosine methylation percent levels using the ERNE DNA alignment package v.1.4.5 including the ERNE-BS5 module for DNA methylation analysis (Prezza et al., 2012). GbM analysis was performed by taking into account the transcribed regions of the predicted genes from the putative transcription start site to the end of the annotated 3'-UTR, when present. The

distribution of DNA methylation average levels across gene bodies was determined by a meta-analysis of DNA methylation levels across exon-only gene body regions normalized in length. The average level of methylation in the 50-65<sup>th</sup> percentile of gene body length was used to assign the GbM value to the gene.

For regional properties, the reference genome was divided in windows of 100 Kb. Gene density was estimated as a proportion of transcript base pairs per window. In addition, the nucleotide diversity of structural variants was measured across the same windows. Structural variants ranging in size between 1 Kb and 25 Kb were identified using the paired end mapping information derived from the alignment of short reads to the reference genome. Deletions were identified using DELLY (DELLY, RRID:SCR\_004603, Rausch *et al.*, 2012) and GASV (GASV, RRID:SCR\_000061, Sindi *et al.*, 2009), while insertions were identified using a pipeline described in Pinosio *et al.*, 2016. For each SV, the genotype was called in each variety as described in Pinosio *et al.*, 2016. For deletions, ratios were defined as follows: SVs with a frequency below 0.25 were classified as homozygous reference; SVs with a ratio between 0.25 and 0.8 were considered heterozygous; lastly, SVs with a frequency above 0.8 were identified as homozygous reference <0.2; heterozygous between 0.2 and 0.75; homozygous for alternate allele >0.75.

#### Among varieties variation in ripening-related gene coexpression networks

Coexpression networks were generated as described in Palumbo *et al.*, 2014. DEGs had to meet the following criteria for being included in the network: genes were consistently up- or down-regulated in both ripening stages (EV and H stages) compared to both green stages (P

and PV stages); FPKM fold change was higher than 4.5; Pearson correlation was higher than 0.97. Following these criteria, we obtained from each variety a set of DEGs of manageable size for performing network analyses, ranging from 1,386 to 2,080 (Table S4). Coexpression networks consisted on average of 1,594 nodes and 269,773 edges. Ripening-related genes were assigned to one of the following regions: R1, ultraperipheral nodes; R2, peripheral nodes; R3, non-hub connectors; R4, non-hub kinless nodes. Switch genes were identified using SWIM as described in Paci *et al.*, 2017.
