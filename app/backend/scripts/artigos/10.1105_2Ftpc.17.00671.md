## 19 ABSTRACT

20 Ethylene plays pleotropic roles in plant growth, plant development, and stress responses. 21 Although the effects of ethylene on plants are well documented, little is known about molecular-22 level events that result in transcriptional repression during the ethylene response. In this study, 23 we found that two histone deacetylases, SRT1 and SRT2, interact with ENAP1, which 24 associates with EIN2 in the nucleus. Genetic and transcriptome analyses revealed that SRT1 25 and SRT2 are required for negative regulation of certain ethylene-responsive genes. The 26 acetylation of HISTONE 3 at K9 (H3K9Ac) is specifically regulated by SRT1 and SRT2 in 27 ethylene-repressed genes. In addition, the srt1 srt2 double mutation in Arabidopsis thaliana 28 suppresses both the ENAP1ox and the EIN3ox constitutive ethylene-response phenotypes, and 29 the ethylene-induced transcriptional repression observed in EIN30x plants is de-repressed in 30 the EIN3ox/srt1 srt2 mutant. SRT2 and ENAP1 both bind to promoter regions of genes 31 negatively regulated by ethylene reducing H3K9Ac levels and resulting in transcriptional 32 repression. This work establishes a mechanism by which histone deacetylases SRT1 and SRT2 33 interact with ENAP1 to mediate transcriptional repression by regulating the levels of H3K9 34 acetvlation in the ethylene signaling.

#### **INTRODUCTION**

 Ethylene is a gaseous plant hormone that acts at trace levels to regulate a variety of processes including plant growth, ripening of fruit and shedding of leaves. Plants also produce ethylene in response to wounding, pathogen attack, or exposure to environmental stresses such as extreme temperatures or drought (Achard et al., 2007; Bailey-Serres et al., 2012; Kazan, 2015). Ethylene is perceived by a family of receptors bound to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane (Chang et al., 1993; Bleecker et al., 1998; Hua and Meyerowitz, 1998; Hua et al., 1998). Each receptor binds ethylene via a copper cofactor, potentially provided by the copper transporter RESPONSIVE-TO-ANTAGONIST 1 (RAN1) (Hirayama et al., 1999). In the absence of ethylene, ethylene receptor ETHYLENE RESPONSE 1 (ETR1) interacts with CONSTITUTIVE TRIPLE RESPONSE 1 (CTR1), a downstream negative regulator of ethylene signaling (Chang et al., 1993; Kieber et al., 1993; Gao et al., 2003; Shakeel et al., 2015). CTR1 is a Raf-like protein that phosphorylates ETHYLENE INSENSITIVE 2 (EIN2), the key positive regulator of ethylene signaling (Alonso et al., 1999; Ju et al., 2012), preventing the ethylene response. In addition, EIN2 protein levels are regulated by EIN2 TARGETING PROTEIN1/2 (ETP1/2) F-box proteins via ubiquitin/proteasome-mediated degradation (Qiao et al., 2009).

 In the presence of ethylene, both ethylene receptors and CTR1 are inactivated, and the C- terminal end of EIN2 is dephosphorylated and cleaved by unknown mechanisms. The cleaved C-terminal end of EIN2 translocates to the nucleus (Qiao et al., 2012; Wen et al., 2012; Ju et al., 2015) where it regulates the acetylation of HISTONE 3 at K14 and K23 (H3K14 and H3K23, respectively) to integrate transcriptional regulation by transcription factors EIN3 and EIN3-LIKE 1 (EIL1) (Zhang et al., 2016a). The cleaved EIN2 C-terminal end also translocates into the Processing-body (P-body), where it represses the translation of EIN3-BINDING F BOX PROTEIN 1/2 (EBF1/2) (Guo and Ecker, 2003), which in turn stabilize EIN3 and EIL1, resulting in activation of EIN3- and EIL1-dependent transcription and the activation of an ethylene response (Li et al., 2015; Merchante et al., 2015).

 The dynamic physiological response to the presence of ethylene is a rapid growth inhibition independent of the master transcriptional regulator EIN3, followed by an EIN3-dependent sustained growth inhibition (Binder et al., 2004). Both genetic and molecular studies have demonstrated that EIN3 and EIL1 are the positive regulators that are necessary and sufficient for the ethylene response (Chao et al., 1997; Guo and Ecker, 2003; Chang et al., 2013) and that accumulate upon exogenous ethylene gas treatment (Guo and Ecker, 2003). Although many  studies have focused on ethylene-activated genes, a recent transcriptome analysis showed that nearly 50% of ethylene-altered genes are down regulated and that a subset of ethylene- repressed genes are bound by EIN3 (Chang et al., 2013). The factors involved in the negative regulation of gene expression in the ethylene response and the functions of ethylene-repressed genes are largely unknown.

 In this study, we identified two histone deacetylases (HDACs), SIRTUIN 1 (SRT1) and SRT2, that interact with the protein EIN2 NUCLEAR ASSOCIATED PROTEIN 1 (ENAP1). ENAP1 is known to associate with EIN2 in the nucleus (Zhang et al., 2016a; Zhang et al., 2017), and it contains a SANT domain, which is conserved in many chromatin remodeling enzymes including histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and HDACs (Boyer et al., 2004; Horton et al., 2007). The SANT domain in ENAP1 is important for its interaction with histone tails (Zhang et al., 2016a). Genetic and transcriptome analyses of *Arabidopsis* demonstrated that SRT1 and SRT2 are required for a negative regulation of a subset of ethylene-responsive genes. Acetylation of H3K9 is specifically regulated by SRT1 and SRT2 over ethylene-repressed genes. In addition, the *srt1 srt2* double mutation suppressed the constitutive ethylene-response phenotypes of both *ENAP1ox and EIN3ox* mutant plants, and the ethylene-induced transcriptional repression in *EIN3ox* plants was de-repressed in *EIN3ox/srt1 srt2* plants. Thus, we established the molecular mechanism responsible for repression of certain genes in the presence of ethylene: Binding of SRT2 and ENAP1 to promoter regions inhibits acetylation of HISTONE 3 at K9 (H3K9Ac) to inhibit transcription in the ethylene signaling.

### **RESULTS**

## **ENAP1 interacts with SRT1 and SRT2 both** *in vitro* **and** *in vivo*

 To identify HDACs involved in histone acetylation mediated by EIN2 and ENAP1, we cloned the cDNAs of 18 HDACs previously identified in *Arabidopsis* (Hollender and Liu, 2008) and conducted yeast two-hybrid assays using ENAP1 or EIN2-C as prey (Supplemental Figure 1A). SRT1 and SRT2, which are closely related according to phylogenetic analysis (Hollender and Liu, 2008), were shown to interact with ENAP1, and no histone deacetylases were identified that interacted with EIN2-C (Figure 1A). Given that ENAP1 interacts with EIN3 and SRT1 and SRT2, we wondered whether SRT1 and SRT2 interact with EIN3. In a yeast two-hybrid assay, no interactions were detected between EIN3 and SRT1 or SRT2 (Supplemental Figure 1B), and no interaction between SRT1 and SRT2 was detected either (Supplemental Figure 1C). We then conducted yeast two hybrid assay to further examine whether SRT1 and SRT2 interact with any

 other HDACs. No obvious interaction between SRT1 or STR2 with other HDACs was detected in yeast (Supplemental Figure 1D).

 To confirm the interaction between ENAP1 and SRT1 or SRT2, we conducted *in vitro* pull-down assays using the proteins purified from *E. coli*. Consistent with the result from the yeast two- hybrid assay, both SRT1 and SRT2 strongly interacted with ENAP1 *in vitro* (Figures 1B and 1C). To examine the interaction between ENAP1 and SRT1 and SRT2 *in vivo*, we introduced FLAG- tagged SRT1 and SRT2 into *ENAP1ox* plants to generate *ENAP1-YFP-HA/SRT1/2-FLAG* plants. In an *in vivo* immunoprecipitation assay, both SRT1 and SRT2 interacted with ENAP1 (Figures 1D and 1E). Together, these data show that SRT1 and SRT2 interact with ENAP1 both *in vitro* and *in vivo.* To further examine whether the interaction between ENAP1 and SRT1 or SRT2 is ethylene dependent, we carried out the co-immunoprecipitation assay using the plants treated with or without ethylene. Although ENAP1 interacted with SRT1 and SRT2 both with and without ethylene treatments (Supplemental Figures 1E and 1F), the interaction was enhanced in the presence of ethylene (Supplemental Figures 1E and 1F).

# *srt1 srt2* **double mutation and SRT2 overexpression results in weak ethylene-responsive phenotype**

 To evaluate the function of SRT1 and SRT2 in the ethylene response, we first examined the gene expression of *SRT1* and *SRT2* in whole plant using eFP [\(http://bar.utoronto.ca/efp2/Arabidopsis/Arabidopsis\\_eFPBrowser2.html\).](http://bar.utoronto.ca/efp2/Arabidopsis/Arabidopsis_eFPBrowser2.html)) Both genes are expressed in all the tissues (Supplemental Figure 2) (Wang et al., 2010). We also examined their gene expressions in response to ethylene and found that neither was regulated by ethylene (Supplemental Figure 3A). We then obtained T-DNA insertion plants *srt1-1*, *srt1-2*, *srt1-3*, *srt2-1*, and *srt2-2* (Supplemental Figures 3B and 3C)*,* and performed an ethylene response assay. Interestingly, the expression levels of *SRT1* in *srt2* mutants and *SRT2* in *srt1* mutants were all increased than in wildtype, which indicate a compensate effect (Supplemental Figure 3C). No obvious ethylene-responsive phenotypes were observed for *srt1* and *srt2* single mutants (Figures 2A-2C). We then generated double mutants in different combinations: *srt1-1 srt2-1*, *srt1-1 srt2-2*, *srt1-2 srt2-1*, *srt1-2 srt2-2*, *srt1-3 srt2-1*, and *srt1-3 srt2-2.* All double mutants displayed weak ethylene-insensitive phenotypes in roots when the plants were treated with a low concentration of 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC), but no obvious hypocotyl phenotypes were observed (Figures 2A-2C). To further confirm these results, we constructed plants in which *SRT1* and *SRT2* were overexpressed (*SRT1ox* and *SRT2ox*,

- respectively). *SRT2ox,* but not *SRT1ox*, plants displayed a weak hypersensitive ethylene- response phenotype in a low concentration of ACC (Figures 2D-2F). The phenotype correlated with levels of SRT2 (Supplemental Figure 3D), and the protein levels of SRT1 and SRT2 were not regulated by ethylene (Supplemental Figures 3E and 3F).

# **SRT1 and SRT2 are required for the transcriptional repression of certain ethylene-responsive genes**

- To investigate the molecular function of SRT1 and SRT2 in the ethylene response, we conducted a transcriptome analysis of *srt1-1 srt2-1* plants (Supplemental Figures 4A and 4B and Supplemental Table 1). About 35% (443/1292) of genes that were differentially expressed in wild-type plants grown with and without ethylene overlapped with the genes with significant differences in expression when the transcriptome of *srt1-1 srt2-1* double mutant plants grown in air was compared to that of the wild-type plants grown in air (Figure 3A, Supplemental Datasets 1, 2, and 3). We performed a similar analysis in *ein2-5* and *ein3-1 eil1-1* mutants and found that 36% (459/1292) and 38% (487/1292), respectively, of ethylene-regulated genes in wild-type plants overlapped with genes differentially expressed when the transcriptomes of *ein2-5* and *ein3-1eil1-1* mutant and wild-type plants grown in air were compared (Figure 3A and Supplemental Datasets 4, 5, 6 and 7). The patterns of expression of genes regulated by ethylene in wild-type plants were similar in *srt1-1 srt2-1*, *ein2-5*, and *ein3-1 eil1-1* mutants and were anti-correlated with levels in Col-0 treated with ethylene (Figure 3B).

 Next, we compared the expression levels of these anti-correlated genes in *srt1-1 srt2-1*, *ein2-5* and *ein3-1 eil1-1* mutants treated with air and treated with ethylene. As expected, few genes responded to ethylene treatment in *ein2-5* and *ein3-1 eil1-1* mutants (Figure 3C). Interestingly, the ethylene up-regulated genes in Col-0 were still up regulated by ethylene in the *str1-1 srt2-1*  mutant. About 50% of genes repressed by ethylene in wild-type plants did not respond to ethylene treatment in the *str1-1 srt2-1* mutant (Figure 3C and Supplemental Datasets 8 and 9)*,*  suggesting that ethylene-induced transcriptional repression of these genes is dependent on SRT1 and SRT2. Reverse transcription- quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) validation further confirmed this result (Figure 4A and Supplemental Figure 4C).

 We found that the expression level of the SRT1- and SRT2-dependent ethylene-repressed genes were reduced in *SRT2ox* plants compared to levels in wild-type plants grown in air (Figure 4B). Gene ontology (GO) analysis showed that the GO term "ethylene response" was

 enriched among the ethylene-activated genes that did not depend on SRT1 or SRT2 (Supplemental Figure 4D). SRT1- and SRT2-dependent ethylene-repressed genes were enriched in GO terms "lipid transport", "lignin biosynthesis", and "stress responses" (Figure 4C). These results suggest that SRT1 and SRT2 are involved in the regulation of genes repressed when plants are grown in ethylene; these genes potentially regulate lipid and lignin metabolism or stress responses.

 Because ENAP1 interacts with SRT2 both *in vivo* and *in vitro*, and they regulate ethylene- repressed genes, we decided to examine whether ENAP1 and SRT2 bind to the same target genes, and whether the binding is influenced by ethylene. We performed Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR assays in *pENAP1:ENAP1-YFP* and *SRT2ox* plants treated with or without ethylene. ENAP1 and SRT2 bound to the promoter regions of the ethylene- repressed genes both with and without ethylene treatments in these plants, and SRT2 binding was slightly elevated by ethylene (Figure 4D).

 To further examine the connection between ENAP1 and SRT1 and SRT2, we generated *ENAP1ox/srt1-3 srt2-2* plants. The ethylene hypersensitive phenotype of *ENAP1ox* was not observed in *ENAP1ox/srt1-3 srt2-2* plants (Figures 5A-5C) even though both the *ENAP1* gene expression and its protein levels were similar in *ENAP1ox/srt1-3 srt2-2* plants and *ENAP1ox* plants (Supplemental Figures 5A and 5B). We then compared the expression of genes that are activated by ethylene in wild-type plants in *ENAP1ox* and in *ENAP1ox/srt1-3 srt2-2* plants by RT-qPCR. Levels of these genes were slightly reduced in *ENAP1ox/srt1-3 srt2-2* plants compared with those in *ENAP1ox* plants both with and without ethylene treatments (Supplemental Figure 5C), and ethylene responsiveness was maintained. By contrast, the levels of these ethylene-repressed genes were significantly elevated in both *ENAP1ox/srt1-3 srt2-2* and *srt1-3 srt2-2* plants, and no differences in transcript levels were detected when ethylene- treated and air-treated plant data were compared (Figure 5D). Notably, the levels of these genes were slightly elevated in *ENAP1ox/srt1-3 srt2-2* compared to *srt1-3 srt2-2* plants both with and without ethylene treatments*.* These data suggest that de-repression of ethylene- responsive genes due to the absence of SRT proteins leads to a suppression of the *ENAP1ox*  hypersensitive ethylene response phenotype.

#### **SRT2 restores yeast** *sir2* **mutant phenotype and regulates H3K9Ac in** *Arabidopsis*

 SRT1 and SRT2 are annotated as HDACs, and SRT2 is predicted to be a homolog of the yeast HDAC Silent information regulator 2 (SIR2) (Arima et al., 2010; Konig et al., 2014); however, the exact biochemical function of SRT2 in *Arabidopsis* is unknown. To evaluate SRT2 function, we expressed *SRT1* and *SRT2* in a yeast *sir2* mutant (Supplemental Figure 6A), which does not survive on medium containing a high concentration of salt (Kang et al., 2015). SRT2, but not SRT1, rescued the yeast *sir2* mutant salt-sensitive phenotype (Figure 6A), indicating that SRT2 from *Arabidopsis* has a function similar to that of SIR2 from yeast. We then examined levels of H3K9Ac, H3K14Ac, and H3K23Ac. In the *srt1-1 srt2*-*1* mutant, the H3K9Ac levels were significantly increased compare to that in Col-0 (Figure 6B), whereas no significant difference was detected between Col-0 and *srt1-1 srt2*-*1* in the levels of H3K14Ac and H3K23Ac (Figure 6B). This demonstrates that SRT1 and SRT2 regulate levels of H3K9Ac.

 Since *srt1-3 srt2-2* suppresses the *ENAP1ox* phenotype, we quantified H3K9Ac, H3K14Ac and H3K23Ac in *ENPA1ox/srt1-3 srt2-2* plants. The H3K9Ac levels were upregulated in *ENPA1ox/srt1-3 srt2-2* compared to in *srt1-3 srt2-2* plants (Figure 6C)*,* whereas H3K14Ac and H3K23Ac levels were not altered significantly (Figure 6C). We then performed a ChIP assay to examine H3K9Ac at the molecular level. The H3K9Ac levels in the promoter regions of ethylene-repressed genes were significantly elevated in both *srt1-3 srt2-2* and *ENPA1ox/srt1-3 srt2-2* plants compared to that in Col-0 plants (Figure 6D), but no significant changes were detected for H3K14Ac and H3K23Ac (Supplemental Figures 6B and 6C). In contrast to the *srt1- 3 srt2-*2 mutant, the H3K9Ac levels were reduced in *SRT2ox* (Figure 6E). Furthermore, the H3K9Ac levels were not subject to ethylene regulation in any of the plants examined (Figures 6B-6E). Notably, the levels of H3K9Ac were slightly higher in *ENPA1ox/srt1-3 srt2-2* plants than in *srt1-3 srt2-2* plants. We speculate that the function of SRTs is to maintain the low levels of H3K9Ac in a subset of ethylene-repressed genes, which is necessary for the transcriptional regulation. Taken together, these data suggest that SRT2 is a histone deacetylase in *Arabidopsis* and that SRT1 and SRT2 regulate acetylation of H3 on K9.

# *srt1 srt2* **suppresses the** *EIN3ox* **phenotype and elevates the expression of SRT1- and SRT2-dependent ethylene-repressed genes**

 To further examine the functions of SRT1 and SRT2 in ethylene signaling, we introduced *SRT2ox* into *ein2-5* and *ein3-1 eil1-1* plants to obtain *SRT2ox/ein2-5* and *SRT2ox/ein3-1 eil1-1*  plants*.* In addition, *EIN3ox* was introduced into *srt1-1 srt2-1* to generate *EIN3ox/srt1-1 srt2-1*  plants. By examining ethylene response phenotypes of these plants, we found that the function

 of SRT2 was both EIN2 and EIN3 dependent (Figure 7A and Supplemental Figures 7A and 7B). Interestingly, the constitutive ethylene-response phenotype of *EIN3ox* was restored to that of Col-0 plants by expression of *srt1-1 srt2-1* both with and without ethylene treatments (Figure 7B and Supplemental Figures 7C and 7D). By contrast, the *EIN3* gene expression and EIN3 protein levels were not altered in *EIN3ox/srt1-1 srt2-1* plants compared to that in *EIN3ox* (Supplemental Figures 7E and 7F). RT-qPCR quantification showed that the expression of SRT1- and SRT2- dependent ethylene-repressed genes was significantly higher in *EIN3ox/srt1-1 srt2-1* than in *EIN3ox* both with and without ethylene treatments (Figure 7C). Interestingly, the expression of ethylene-activated genes was similar in *EIN3ox* and *EIN3ox/srt1-1 srt2-1* plants (Supplemental Figure 7G), showing that de-repression of ethylene-repressed genes in *EIN3ox/srt1-1 srt2-1* leads to suppression of the *EIN3ox* phenotype. These data further support our hypothesis that SRT1- and SRT2-mediated transcriptional repression is involved in the ethylene response.

#### **DISCUSSION**

 Histone acetylation and deacetylation switches on and off gene expression. In general, acetylation neutralizes the positive charges of lysine residues and decreases the interaction between histone and DNA, leading to a more relaxed chromatin structure, which is associated with transcriptional activation (Struhl, 1998; Eberharter and Becker, 2002; Verdin and Ott, 2015). Acetylation can also activate genes by recruiting other transcription regulators such as TFIID and SWI/SNF (Mizzen et al., 1996; Hassan et al., 2001). By contrast, deacetylation induces a more compact chromatin structure that is associated with transcriptional repression. Histone acetylation mediates the effects of various plant hormones (Pandey et al., 2002; Liu et al., 2012; Yamamuro et al., 2016), and several histone deacetylases are known to be involved in the regulation of gene expression in plants (Zhou et al., 2005; Zhu et al., 2011; Perrella et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2013). Little is known about histone acetyltransferases or HDACs in ethylene signaling, however. Using a yeast two-hybrid screen, we identified SRT1 and SRT2 as potential HDACs that interact with ENAP1. We also provide evidence that ENAP1 interacts with SRT1 and SRT2 both *in vivo* and *in vitro*.

 SRT1 and SRT2 contain histone deacetylase activity domains (Hollender and Liu, 2008), and *SRT2* is reportedly the homolog of yeast *sir2* (Arima et al., 2010; Konig et al., 2014). *OsSRT1* from rice is the homolog of *sir2* and potentially regulates the levels of H3K9Ac (Huang et al., 2007). Here we provide evidence showing that SRT2, but not SRT1, is able to complement the high salt sensitivity phenotype of yeast *sir2* (Guarente, 2000). An immunoblot analysis showed  that H3K9Ac levels were significantly increased in *srt1 srt2* mutant plants; however, the levels of H3K14Ac and H3K23Ac were not altered in the *srt1 srt2* mutant compared to Col-0 plants. Additionally, a ChIP-qPCR analysis showed that H3K9Ac levels were significantly elevated in the promoter regions of genes bound by SRT2. These results demonstrate that SRT2 is a histone deacetylase and that SRT1 and SRT2 specifically regulate histone acetylation at H3K9 in *Arabidopsis*.

 Deacetylation induces a more compact chromatin structure and is associated with transcriptional repression. Histone deacetylases are involved in transcriptional repression during plant hormone signaling. For example, HDA19 is required for the repression of gene expression in brassinosteroid signaling (Oh et al., 2014), and JAZ proteins recruit HDA6 as a co-repressor to modulate histone acetylation to inhibit jasmonate signaling (Wu et al., 2008). Here we provide multiple lines of evidence that SRT2 regulates transcriptional repression in the ethylene response: First, the *srt1 str2* double mutant had a weak ethylene-insensitive phenotype and *srt1-1 srt2-1* suppressed the ethylene hypersensitivity phenotype of *ENAP1ox*, suggesting that SRT1 and SRT2 negatively regulate the ethylene response. Second, by comparing RNA seq data from the *srt1 srt2*, *ein2-5*, and *ein3-1 eil1-1* plants with or without ethylene treatment, we found that certain ethylene-repressed genes are SRT1 and SRT2 dependent (Figure 3). By contrast, core ethylene-activated genes are not altered in the *srt1 srt2* double mutant relative to wild-type plants. In addition, a ChIP assay showed that H3K9Ac levels over ethylene-repressed genes that are dependent on SRT1 and SRT2 were elevated in the *srt1 srt2* mutant. Third, we found that mutations in *srt1* and *srt2* rescued the constitutive ethylene response phenotype of *ENAP1ox* and *EIN3ox* plants and that the expression of ethylene-repressed genes, but not ethylene-activated genes, was restored in *ENAP1ox/srt1 srt2* and *EIN3ox/srt1 srt2*. Finally, a ChIP assay showed that SRT2 binds to the promoter regions of genes repressed in the presence of ethylene in a manner dependent on SRT1 and SRT2. These results suggest that SRT1 and SRT2 regulate ethylene-induced transcriptional repression.

 Most plant hormones including ethylene activate and repress about equal numbers of genes, but the mechanisms for gene activation are known understood than those for gene repression. The mechanism for how the transcriptional activation and repression occur in the same response is not well established. We found that ENAP1 is involved in both acetylation and deacetylation associated with gene activation and gene repression in response to ethylene. Our prior study showed that the levels of H3K14Ac and H3K23Ac are upregulated by ethylene and

 that levels of H3K9Ac are not (Zhang et al., 2016a). Here we found that H3K9Ac levels are specifically regulated by SRT1 and SRT2. This discovery suggests a connection between the levels of H3K9Ac and ethylene-induced regulation of gene expression. We found that the levels of H3K9Ac over ethylene up-regulated genes were significantly higher than over ethylene down- regulated genes (Wang et al, 2017). Our data thus suggest that the levels of H3K9Ac are important for the ethylene response and that low levels of H3K9Ac, which is required for transcriptional repression, are maintained by SRT1 and SRT2. Here, we propose a model that in the presence of ethylene, ENAP1 interacts with SRT1 and SRT2 and recruits them to a subset of ethylene-repressed genes to maintain a low level of H3K9Ac, resulting in transcriptional repression (Figure 7D). We showed that SRT1 and SRT2 interact with ENAP1, which binds to both ethylene-activated and ethylene-repressed genes. The questions of how ENAP1 is recruited to both activated genes and repressed genes, whether ENAP1 is required for the function of SRT1 and SRT2 in the transcriptional repression, and how SRT1 and SRT2 are targeted to the particular set of ethylene-repressed genes remain to be addressed. Whether the recruitment of SRT1 and SRT2 is determined by *cis* elements in promoter regions and/or by *trans*-acting factors will be determined in future studies. Our data elucidate a link between chromatin status and hormone signaling to explain how plants respond to signals for their growth and development.

#### **MATERIALS AND METHODS**

#### **Plant materials and growth conditions**

 All mutants were in the Columbia-0 (Col-0) background. *srt1-1* (SALK\_086287), *srt1-2* (SALK\_001493), *srt1-3* (SALKseq\_034794), *srt2-1* (SALK\_131994), and *srt2-2* (SALK\_149295) were obtained from ABRC. *ENAP1ox* and *pENAP1:ENAP1:YFP-HA* plants have been described (Zhang et al., 2016a). Seeds were surface sterilized with 4% bleach and washed three times with sterilized water and then sown on MS medium. Plants were grown in long day conditions (16 h light/8 h dark) at 22 ˚C in soil. The light intensity is about 100 µmol m-2 s-1 with white fluorescence tube bulbs (F32T8/SPP50/ECO, Ecolux Starcoat, USA). The length of hypocotyls and roots were measured using Image J software (NIH).

### **Plasmid and Transgenic Plant Construction**

 To construct vectors for yeast two-hybrid, the CDS of *HDAC*s, *EIN2-C*, *ENAP1* and *EIN3* was amplified using the Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB). The PCR products were ligated into pDBLeu (Invitrogen) and pEXP-AD502 (Invitrogen) vectors to give rise to BD-

 HDACs, AD-EIN2-C, AD-ENAP1, AD-EIN3 and AD-SRT1/SRT2. To construct vectors for pull- down, the CDS of *SRT1*, *SRT2* and *ENAP1* was amplified using the Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase. The PCR products were ligated into pGEX-KG and pVP13 vectors to give rise to GST-SRT1, GST-SRT2 and His-ENAP1. To construct SRT1/SRT2 overexpression vectors, the CDS of *SRT1* and *SRT2* were amplified using the Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase. The PCR products were ligated into the pCHF3 vector to give rise to 35S:SRT1-Flag, 35S:SRT2- Flag, 35S:SRT1-YFP and 35S:SRT2-YFP. All the sequences above were verified by sequencing and primers are listed in Supplemental Table 2. The binary constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium strain GV3101 and then transformed into Col-0 or *ENAP1ox* by the floral dip method (Clough and Bent, 1998). Transgenic plants were screened on MS plates with antibiotic selection. For each construct, multiple independent lines were examined with similar results, and as least one representative line was shown. The *srt1 srt2* double mutants, *ENAP1ox/srt1-3 srt2-2* line, *SRT2ox/ein2-5* line, *SRT2ox/ein3-1 eil1-1* line and *EIN3ox/srt1-1 srt2-1* plants were made by crossing.

#### **Yeast two-hybrid assay**

 The yeast two-hybrid assay was performed according to the instructions provided with the ProQuest™ Two-Hybrid System (Invitrogen) and were previously published (Zhang et al., 2016a; Zhang et al., 2016b). In briefly, BD-HDACs and AD-EIN2-C, AD-ENAP1, AD-EIN3 or AD- SRT1/SRT2 were co-transformed into the yeast strain AH109 (Clontech). The transformants were grown on SD/-Trp-Leu or SD/-Trp-Leu-His dropout medium. The transformants growing on SD/-Trp-Leu-His dropout medium indicates interaction between corresponding proteins.

## **GST pull-down assay**

 The GST pull-down assay was performed as previously described (Zhang et al., 2016a). In brief, GST, GST-SRT1/SRT2, and His-ENAP1 proteins were purified using glutathione sepharose 4B (GE Healthcare) and Ni-NTA agarose (Qiagen). Equal amounts of His-ENAP1 and GST or GST-SRT1/SRT2 were incubated with glutathione sepharose 4B beads at 4 C for 1 h. After washing five times, precipitated beads were collected and resuspended in protein extraction buffer for immunoblot analysis.

### **Co-immunoprecipitation assay**

 The co-immunoprecipitation assay was performed as previously described (Zhang et al., 2016a; Pedmale et al., 2016). In brief, 3-day-old etiolated seedlings were harvested. Proteins were  extracted and then combined with anti-HA antibody or anti-Flag antibody and incubated overnight at 4 C. DynabeadsTM Protein G (Invitrogen) were added, and samples were incubated for 1 hour at 4 C. After washing five times, Protein G beads were collected and then resuspended in protein extraction buffer for immunoblot analysis.

### **Ethylene treatment, RNA extraction, and RT-quantitative PCR analysis**

*Arabidopsis* seeds were grown on MS medium in air-tight containers in a 22 ˚C chamber in the dark supplied with a flow of hydrocarbon-free air (Zero grade air, AirGas) for 3 days. The etiolated seedlings were treated with a continuous flow of hydrocarbon-free air or hydrocarbon- free air with 10 parts per million (ppm) ethylene gas for 4 h as previously described prior to harvest (Zhang et al., 2016a). Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plant Kit (Qiagen). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using Superscript III First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen). RT-quantitative PCR was performed as previously described (Zhang et al., 2016a). The expression level was normalized to *UBIQUITIN 10* (*UBQ10)* as an internal control.

### **RNA library construction and deep sequencing**

 The RNA library construction was performed as previously described (Zhang et al., 2016b). Briefly, total RNA was isolated from 3-day-old *srt1-1 srt2-1* double-mutant etiolated seedlings treated with air or ethylene gas for 4 h prior to harvest using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). The mRNA was purified by NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module and the libraries were synthesized using the NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. The PCR reactions were performed using different index primers (NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina). The PCR products were size selected by Agencourt AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter). The quality of the libraries was assessed by Bioanalyzer (Agilent High Sensitivity Chip). The libraries were sequenced on NextSeq 500 Systems (Illumina).

### **RNA-seq data analysis**

 The RNA-seq data analysis was performed as previously described (Qiao et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2016a; Wang et al., 2017). Raw reads were aligned to the TAIR10 genome using TopHat version 2.0.9 with default parameters. Differential expressed genes were identified using Cufflinks version 2.2.1 following the workflow with default parameters (Trapnell et al., 2012). Gene expression levels (RPKM, Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads) in air and ethylene conditions were generated from the output files of cuffdiff (Trapnell et al. 2012). Differentially expressed genes were those for which relative fold change values of greater than

 2 and reads per kilobase per million mapped reads values greater than 1 were observed (Bai et al., 2012). Generation of Venn diagrams and heat maps was performed using R (version 3.2.2).

#### **Yeast complementation**

 Yeast complementation was performed as previously described (Kang et al., 2015). Briefly, the wild-type and *sir2*Δ mutant strains were in the BY4741 background. The pRS415 plasmid containing the GPD promoter, the entire *SIR2/SRT1/SRT2* coding region, and the CYC terminator was introduced into a *sir2* Δ mutant strain. The empty pRS415 plasmid was introduced into a *sir2*Δ mutant strain as a negative control. Yeast cells were grown in YPD medium to log phase, collected, and spotted onto YPD or YPD containing 0.8 M NaCl with different tilters. The plates were photographed after 2 or 3 days.

#### **Histone extraction and immunoblot analysis**

 Histone extraction was performed as previously described (Zhang et al., 2016a). In brief, 3-day- old etiolated seedlings were treated with air or ethylene gas for 4 h and were harvested. Proteins were extracted, and the pellet was resuspended in 0.4 M H2SO4 and incubated for at least 2 h at 4 ˚C. After precipitation by addition of trichloroacetic acid, the pellet was washed with acetone and resuspended in protein extraction buffer for immunoblot analysis.

### **ChIP assay**

 ChIP was performed as previously described (Zhang et al., 2016a; Song et al., 2016). Briefly, 3- day-old etiolated seedlings treated with air or ethylene gas for 4 h were harvested and then crosslinked in 1% formaldehyde. After the chromatin was isolated, antibody and Dynabeads or Protein G were added to the sonicated chromatin followed by incubation overnight at 4 ˚C to precipitate antibody-bound DNA fragments. DNA was eluted and detected by qPCR.
