## 13 Abstract

14 Pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) recognition occurs by plasma membrane 15 located receptors that induce among other processes nuclear gene expression. However, 16 signaling to the nuclear compartment is restricted by the nuclear envelope and nuclear pore 17 complexes (NPCs). We show here that among the four Arabidopsis lamin homologs 18 LITTLE NUCLEI/CROWDED NUCLEI (LINC/CRWN), LINC1 plays an important role 19 in PTI and jasmonic acid signaling. We show that *linc1* knock out mutants affect PAMP-20 triggered MAPK activation and growth inhibition, but not reactive oxygen species (ROS) 21 or callose accumulation. We also demonstrate that *linc1* mutants are compromised in 22 regulating PAMP-triggered pathogen-related genes, in particular encoding factors involved 23 in jasmonic acid (JA) signaling and responses. Expression of a number of JAZ domain 24 proteins, the key JA-related transcription factor MYC2 as well as key MYB transcription 25 factors and biosynthesis genes of both the indole and aliphatic glucosinolate (GS) pathways 26 are changed in *linc1* mutants. Moreover, PAMP triggers JA and JA-Ile accumulation in 27 *linc1* mutants, whereas salicylic acid (SA) levels are unchanged. Despite impairment in 28 PAMP-triggered immunity, *linc1* mutants still show basal immunity towards *Pseudomonas* 29 syringae DC3000 strains. High JA levels usually render plants resistant to necrotrophic 30 pathogen. Thus, *linc1* mutants show enhanced resistance to *Botrytis cinerea* infection. In 31 accordance with a general role of LINC1 in JA signaling, *linc1* mutants are hypersensitive 32 to growth inhibition to external JA. In summary, our findings show that the lamin-like 33 LINC1 protein plays a key role in jasmonic acid signaling and regulation of PTI responses 34 in Arabidopsis.

# 39 Introduction

Plants are constantly under attack from potentially pathogenic microbes. The 40 41 perception of a pathogen attack by the plant triggers the activation of immune signaling 42 events and defense responses. Components of plant innate immunity have been categorized 43 into two general lines of defense (Jones and Dangl 2006), with pattern-triggered immunity 44 (PTI) through the perception of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) by 45 membrane bound pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) representing the first (Schwessinger 46 and Ronald 2012; Macho and Zipfel 2014). Early defense responses include a rise in the 47 intracellular calcium concentration (Liese and Romeis 2013), the production of reactive 48 oxygen species (Torres et al. 2006), the activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase 49 (MAPK) cascades and the expression of pathogen-related proteins (Ahuja et al. 2012; 50 Nurnberger et al. 2004) and the biosynthesis of stress-related hormones such as salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), and ethylene (Tsuda et al. 2013; Grant and Jones 2009). 51 52 These early events after PAMP perception lead to defense responses mediated by 53 transcriptional reprogramming (Mur et al. 2008), stomatal closure, production of 54 antimicrobial compounds (Dixon 2001), and the deposition of callose (Gomez-Gomez et 55 al. 1999). The second tier of the plant defense system elicits a more robust and long-term 56 response and is termed ETI (effector-triggered immunity). ETI is prompted when effectors 57 transferred by host-adapted pathogens in order to interrupt signaling steps of the PTI 58 response are recognized by Resistance proteins of the host cells (Dodds and Rathjen 2010; Tsuda and Katagiri 2010; Spoel and Dong 2012; Nicaise et al. 2009; Jones and Dangl 2006; 59 60 Takken and Goverse 2012; Collier and Moffett 2009; Hann et al. 2010).

61 Jasmonic acid (JA) not only regulates plant development, but also plays a pivotal 62 role in local and systemic pathogen resistance responses. JA-Ile is the bio-active form of 63 JA, it induces the formation of a receptor complex that leads to the ubiquitination and 64 degradation of the JAZ (JASMONATE ZIM-DOMAIN) transcriptional repressors 65 (Browse 2009; Fonseca et al. 2009; Koo et al. 2009; Yan et al. 2016; Thines et al. 2007). 66 In the absence of pathogen challenge, low levels of JA-Ile allow JAZ accumulation and 67 repression of JAZ target genes (Pauwels and Goossens 2011; Zhang et al. 2015). Upon pathogen-induced JA-Ile accumulation, JAZs interact with the F-box CORONATINE 68 69 INSENSITIVE 1 (COI1), forming a co-receptor complex leading to JAZ degradation and 70 transcriptional induction (Pauwels and Goossens 2011).

Signal transduction induced by extracellular factors such as PAMPs ultimately 71 72 results in modulation of gene expression in the nuclear compartment. However, access to 73 the genomic DNA embedded in chromatin is restricted by the nuclear envelope and 74 transport through nuclear pore complexes (NPCs). NPCs, which are embedded in the 75 nuclear envelope, are surrounded by inner and outer nuclear membrane proteins. These NPCs include the SUN (Sad1p, UNC-84) and the membrane-spanning KASH (Klarsicht, 76 77 ANC-1, Syne Homology domain) proteins (Poulet et al. 2017). A series of plant-specific 78 WPP domain proteins play an analogous role to the metazoan KASH proteins including 79 the anchoring of the RanGAP GTPase to the outer nuclear membrane (Xu et al. 2007; Zhao 80 et al. 2008; Zhou et al. 2012). The protein complex that is formed by the KASH and SUN 81 proteins is proposed to link the cytoskeleton to the nucleoskeleton. SUN1 and SUN2 82 interact with the LITTLE NUCLEI1 (LINC1)/CROWDED NUCLEUS1 (CRWN1) protein 83 that lies at the periphery of the nucleoplasm, providing shape and size to the nucleus (Graumann 2014). On the other hand, SUN proteins also interact with the WPPINTERACTING PROTEIN (WIP1) protein that lies on the outer nuclear membrane (Zhou
et al. 2012). WIP1 is closely related to WIT proteins, which link the nuclear envelope to
actin filaments via the plant-specific KAKU1 myosin-like protein (Tamura et al. 2013).

88 In Arabidopsis, the LITTLE NUCLEI/CROWDED NUCLEI gene family has four 89 members, *LINC1* to *LINC4*. While *linc1* and *linc4* single mutants are affected in nuclear 90 size, nuclear shape, and chromatin organization, some double and triple mutants also show 91 a dwarf phenotype (Dittmer et al. 2007; Wang et al. 2013). A recent study reported that 92 LINC1 and LINC3 are involved in seed germination by regulating the degradation of ABA-93 INSENSITIVE5 (ABI5) protein (Zhao et al. 2016). Furthermore, LINC1 also interacts with 94 the NAC transcription factor NTL9 and plays a role in suppressing the plant immune 95 response towards the virulent pathogen *Pseudomonas syringae* pv. maculicola (Psm) 96 ES4326 (Guo et al. 2017). Guo et al. also reported a role for pathogens and SA in inducing 97 LINC1 degradation, which interacts with NTL9 and SNI1 to inhibit *PR1* transcription (Guo 98 et al. 2017).

99 Here, we demonstrate that LINC1 plays a role in regulating PTI responses. By 100 characterizing PTI in *linc1* knock out mutants, we show that LINC1 influences several 101 components of the JA and glucosinolate (GS) biosynthetic and signaling pathways. The 102 induction of the immune response in the *linc1* mutant plants is affected and leads to 103 induction in JA accumulation and transcriptional reprogramming. Furthermore, both 104 tryptophan-derived indole glucosinolate (IGS) and methionine-derived aliphatic 105 glucosinolate (AGS) signaling pathways are affected in *linc1* mutants in PTI. The data 106 reported in this study establish that LINC1 is a positive regulator of PTI responses and 107 plays a role in JA signaling and GS biosynthesis.

#### 108 **Results**

#### 109 LINC1 positively regulates early and late PTI responses

110 LINCs contain a tripartite structure with a central coiled coil domain as well as 111 nuclear localization signals and are proposed to be the best candidates of lamins in 112 Arabidopsis (Guo and Fang 2014). They have been shown to participate in functions such 113 as in regulating chromatin organization and nuclear shape and size that are controlled by 114 nuclear lamins in organisms other than plants. Arabidopsis quadruple linc mutants are not 115 viable (Dittmer et al. 2007; Wang et al. 2013; Sakamoto and Takagi 2013), but mutations 116 in both *LINC1* and *LINC4*, but not in *LINC2* and *LINC3*, can change nuclear size and the 117 shape of differentiated cells (Dittmer et al. 2007; Dittmer and Richards 2008). However, 118 except for recent reports on the role of *LINC1* and *LINC3* in seed germination (Zhao et al. 119 2016), and enhanced disease phenotype in the *linc1 linc2* double mutant (Guo et al. 2017), 120 the functions of LINC proteins have not been further characterized.

121 To elucidate the specific role of *LINC1* in the PTI stress response, we investigated 122 whether LINC1 is involved in flg22-induced PTI responses. We obtained T-DNA insertion 123 lines *linc1-1* (Dittmer et al. 2007) and *linc1-2* (Sakamoto and Takagi 2013) and confirmed 124 by quantitative RT-PCR analysis (qRT-PCR) that both are knock out mutants (Figures S1A) 125 and S1B). Nevertheless, as reported before, both had no visible developmental or growth 126 phenotype (Figure 1a). In order to generate complementation lines, we introduced a GFP-127 tagged *LINC1*-fusion construct under the control of a constitutive ubiquitin gene-derived 128 promoter into the *linc1-1* mutant line by *Agrobacterium*-mediated transformation. Two of 129 the obtained transgenic lines, ProUbi:LINC1-GFP C#2 and ProUbi:LINC1-GFP C#5, showed similar *LINC1* transcript levels as wild type plants (Figure S2) and exhibited no
discernable growth or developmental phenotypes (Figure 1A). We also acquired a strong *LINC1* overexpression line, *Pro35S:LINC1*-GFP OE (Sakamoto and Takagi 2013), this
line showed 20-fold increased *LINC1* transcript levels and the plants were phenotypically
slightly larger in size when compared to wild type (Figure 1A and S1).

135 We then proceeded to examine known early and late PTI responses in the *linc1* 136 mutants. PAMP-induced ROS production is one of the well-documented early immune 137 responses, therefore we measured ROS levels upon flg22 treatment in our selected lines. 138 linc1-1 and linc1-2 mutant plants showed slightly lower flg22-induced ROS levels, 139 whereas the OE, C#2 and C#5 plants showed similar ROS levels as wild type plants (Figures 1B and 1C). Another well documented early PTI response which is the activation 140 141 of the immune MAPKs MPK3, MPK4, and MPK6 in response to flg22, which we checked 142 by testing for activation-specific protein phosphorylation. *linc1-1* and *linc1-2* mutants 143 displayed a delayed peak of activation of the MAPKs when compared to wild type (Figure 144 1D).

145 Furthermore, *linc1-1* and *linc1-2* mutants were completely compromised in the 146 flg22-induced expression of the PTI marker genes *FLS2* and *FRK1* (Figure 1E). To confirm 147 that the compromised FLS2 and FRK1 expression in these lines was due to the lack of 148 functional LINC1 we examined C#2 and C#5 lines which had been obtained by 149 transforming the *linc1-1* mutant. Expression of *LINC1-GFP* restored flg22-mediated 150 induction of *FLS2* and *FRK1* expression to normal levels (Figure 1F). We also examined 151 the LINC1 OE line for FLS2 and FRK1 expression and found that it was compromised in 152 flg22-mediated *FLS2* and *FRK1* induction (Figure 1F).

153 One of the later plant responses to PAMP perception is increased callose deposition 154 (Zhang et al. 2007). Therefore, *linc1-1* and *linc1-2* mutant plants exhibited resistance to 155 growth inhibition by flg22 compared to wild type plants, while LINC1 OE and both 156 complementation lines C#2 and C#5 exhibited normal growth-inhibition responses (Figure 157 1G). In addition, we measured callose accumulation in our mutant plants as well as in the OE line 24 hours after flg22 treatment. However, no significant changes in callose 158 159 accumulation levels were observed in any of the lines when compared to wild type plants 160 (Figure 1H).

161 Finally, we tested if *LINC1* plays a role during PTI. We infected both *linc1-1* and 162 *linc1-2* plants by spray-inoculation with the virulent bacterium *Pseudomonas syringae* pv. 163 tomato DC3000 (Pst DC3000), and the non-virulent Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato 164 DC3000 hrcC<sup>-</sup> (Pst hrcC<sup>-</sup>). The Pst hrcC<sup>-</sup> strain is characterized by attenuated virulence 165 due to a lack of a functional type III-secretion system and therefore can be considered as a 166 PTI-specific strain. We observed no difference between bacterial levels within leaf tissue 167 in wild type, *linc1-1*, and *linc1-2* plants after spray-inoculation with *Pst* DC3000 at 3h or 168 72 hours post-inoculation (hpi) (Figure 11), indicating that *LINC1* does not play an essential 169 role in basal resistance. However, a reduction in resistance was evident in *linc1-1* and *linc1-*170 2 after 72 hours post-inoculation with Pst hrcC<sup>-</sup> (Figure 1J). Since no differences in 171 bacterial levels were observed at 3 h, we could exclude an effect at the level of bacterial 172 access through stomata. The enhanced susceptibility to infection in the *LINC1* knock out 173 mutants was largely restored to wild type levels in the complementation lines C#2 and C#5 174 (Figure 1K). To assess the specificity of the enhanced plant resistance detected in *linc1* 175 mutants, we tested whether a similar phenotype could be observed in T-DNA knockout

176 mutant lines of other members of the LINC gene family (Wang et al. 2013). We did not 177 find significant changes in response to *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> infection in *linc2*, *linc3*, *linc4*, *linc1/linc4* 178 or *linc2/linc3* plants at 72 hpi (Figure S3). Interestingly, although we did not see major 179 effects of the OE lines in PTI markers, these plants showed lower infection levels than in 180 wild type plants. Moreover, since Guo et al. reported that among the single and double *linc* 181 mutant lines tested, they observed a slightly enhanced pathogen resistance phenotype to 182 the virulent *Psm* ES4326 pathogen in the single *linc1-1* mutant, we decided to investigate 183 whether a similar phenotype could be observed in the *linc1* mutants plants used in this 184 study. We observed no significant changes in response to *Psm* ES4326 pathogen infection 185 in *linc1-1*, *linc1-2*, *OE*, *C#2* and *C#5* plants after 72 hpi (Figure S4).

Taken together, our data demonstrate that the disease resistance to *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> is strongly affected in *linc1* mutant plants. Moreover, these results also indicate that *LINC1* plays a significant role in the regulation of PAMP-induced MAPK activation and defense gene expression.

#### 190 *LINC1* regulates PAMP-triggered gene expression

191 In order to identify genes dependent on *LINC1* in their regulation after *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> 192 treatment, we performed a global transcriptome analysis on samples from three independent repeats of 14 day old seedlings of wild type and *linc1-1* mutant plants (Figure 193 194 2). The plants were harvested at 24 hpi of *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup>. Of the 24,911 transcripts that were 195 represented in the data, 1618 genes showed differential expression (fold change > 1.5, p 196 value < 0.05). Comparing data from mock treated *linc1-1* to mock treated wild type plants, 197 we found 420 genes that were upregulated and 912 genes that were down regulated 198 according to the above criteria. Comparing mock treated to *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> treated samples, we found 786 upregulated and 832 downregulated genes in wild type compared to 865 upregulated and 456 downregulated genes in *linc1-1* (fold change > 1.5, P value < 0.05). When we compared the differentially regulated genes (DEGs) in *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> treated *linc1-1* and *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> treated wild type plants, we found that 55 % of *linc1-1 Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> DEGs are downregulated (585 genes), whereas 45 % are up-regulated (385 genes) (Figure S5A).

GO analysis of genes down regulated in *linc1-1* inoculated with *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> in comparison to wild type inoculated with *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> showed a clear enrichment in genes involved in defense response, response to hormones, and hormone mediated signaling pathways (Figure S5B). In contrast, up-regulated genes were enriched in cell cycle-, GS biosynthesis- and metabolism-related functions (Figure S5C).

To gain a deeper understanding of the role of *LINC1*, we carried out hierarchical 209 210 clustering of *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> induced genes in *linc1-1*, which were then separated into 15 clusters 211 and examined by GO enrichment (Figure 2A). Clusters 9, 13, and 14 contained the largest 212 numbers of DEGs with 388, 529 and 263 genes, respectively, revealing that in *linc1-1* 213 mutants, immune-related genes are repressed when compared to the situation of Pst hrcC-214 induced genes in wild type (Figure 2B). A number of GO categories that are all associated 215 with defense processes, including systemic acquired resistance, and the key defense 216 hormones ethylene (ET), salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), were significantly 217 compromised in *linc1-1* mutant plants upon *Pst hrcC*- treatment. Interestingly, a strong 218 upregulation of secondary metabolite and GS biosynthesis and metabolism was also 219 observed in *Pst hrcC*- challenged *linc1-1* mutant plants (Figure 2C).

#### 220 *LINC1* modulates defense and hormone related genes

221 To further examine the mechanisms by which *LINC1* regulates hormone-related 222 genes, we performed qRT-PCR for SA-, ET-, and JA-related genes upon Pst hrcC-223 treatment. QRT-PCR confirmed the RNA-seq results indicating a repression in the 224 expression of the SA-related genes PR1, PR2, AIG1, and NIMIN1 in the Pst hrcC<sup>-</sup> treated 225 *linc1-1* mutant when compared to *Pst hrcC*<sup> $\cdot$ </sup> treated wild type plants (Figure 3A). Similarly, 226 the ET-related genes ACS8, ACS11, ERF2 and ERF6 also exhibited repressed gene 227 expression in the *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> treated *linc1-1* mutant when compared to *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> treated wild 228 type plants (Figure 3B). On the other hand, the JA-related genes MYC2, PDF1.2b, VSP1, 229 and VSP2 showed a marked increase in the expression in both the mock and Pst hrcC<sup>-</sup> 230 treated plants when compared with wild type plants (Figure 3C). These results confirm the 231 transcriptome data and point to an important role for *LINC1* in modulating the expression 232 of defense-related hormone genes. We were able to see recovery of the expression of all of 233 these genes to wild type levels in the C#2, C#5 and OE lines (Figures S6, S7 and S8).

234 Effect of *LINC1* on JA accumulation and signal transduction

235 Both the transcriptome data as well as the qRT-PCR results indicated that a 236 substantial number of JA-related genes are significantly affected in the *linc1-1* mutant. 237 Since these changes in JA-related gene expression could affect endogenous JA and JA-Ile 238 levels, we quantified their levels in the LINC1 knock out and the OE lines. We found a 239 significant increase in JA and JA-Ile levels in the *linc1-1* mutant compared with wild type 240 plants following inoculation with Pst hrcC<sup>-</sup> at 24 hpi, while JA-Ile levels exhibited a 241 reduction in the OE line (Figure 4A). High JA levels are known to boost resistance to 242 necrotrophic pathogens. Therefore, we examined the *linc1* mutant plants as well as the OE line for sensitivity to the necrotrophic fungal pathogen *Botrytis cinerea*. The *linc1* mutants
were more resistant against *Botrytis cinerea*, while the OE line exhibited a wild type-like
response (Figure S9A). Furthermore, we found no changes in SA levels in the *linc1-1*mutant and the OE line compared with wild type plants following inoculation with *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> at 24 hpi (Figure S9B).

Recent studies highlighted a transcriptionally activated JA-Ile catabolic pathway, 248 249 which is mediated through three endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-localized cytochrome P450 250 oxidases, CYP94B1, CYP94B3, and CYP94C1, that induce JA break-down (Heitz et al. 251 2012; Koo et al. 2014). From our transcriptome data, we found that the expression of CYP94B1 and CYP94C1 was significantly inhibited following *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> treatment. In 252 order to confirm this observation, we performed qRT-PCR analysis on CYP94B1 and 253 254 CYP94C1 expression levels. *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> induced expression of CYP94B1 and CYP94C1 255 was compromised in the linc1-1 mutant when compared to WT (Figure 4B). In contrast, 256 recovery of the induction of these two genes to wild type levels was observed in C#2, C#3 257 and OE lines (Figure S10).

A newly characterized set of enzymes termed JAOs (JASMONIC ACID 258 259 OXIDASES) encompassing four members, JAO1, JAO2, JAO3, and JAO4, has been 260 demonstrated to inactivate JA by hydroxylation (Smirnova et al. 2017). The expression 261 levels of JAO2, JAO3 and JAO4 were affected in our transcriptome analysis. qRT-PCR 262 analysis confirmed that *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> induced expression of *JAO2*, *JAO3* and *JAO4* is 263 significantly compromised in *linc1-1* mutant plants when compared to wild type (Figure 264 4C). We also examined expression in the C#2, C#5 and OE lines and found that their 265 induction levels are comparable to wild type plants (Figure S11).

To further validate the accumulation of JA and JA-Ile in *linc1-1* mutant on the observed gene expression pattern, we used qRT-PCR to examine the expression levels of several *JAZ* genes with a known role in repressing JA signaling. We found that *JAZ1, JAZ3, JAZ5, JAZ6* and *JAZ7* are all significantly compromised in *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> induction in *linc1-1* mutants (Figure 4d), but expression of these *JAZ* genes was found at comparable levels to wild-type in C#2, C#5 and OE lines (Figure S12).

272 The increased expression of several JA responsive genes and the inhibition of 273 several JA-repressor genes suggested that the response to JA might be altered in *LINC1* 274 mutant plants. Since MeJA inhibition of root growth is a classical JA assay, we tested the 275 effect of MeJA treatment on the roots of *linc1-1* mutant and *LINC1* OE line. We observed that following MeJA treatment of the *linc1-1* mutant, root growth was much more inhibited 276 277 than in wild type plants (Figure 4e). Surprisingly, *LINC1* OE also resulted in enhanced root 278 growth inhibition, suggesting that appropriate LINC1 protein levels are necessary for JA 279 signaling. Taken together, our expression analysis and measurement of JA and JA-Ile 280 levels, indicate that *LINC1* is a positive regulator of JA biosynthesis and signaling.

#### 281 *LINC1* regulates GS biosynthetic and metabolic pathways

Another transcriptome segment revealed that transcript levels of several members of the glucosinolate biosynthetic pathways are enhanced in *linc1* mutants upon *Pst hrcC* infection (Figure 2, Cluster 14). Glucosinolates (GS) are a large group of secondary metabolites in *Brassicaceae* that are involved in plant defense against insects as well as pathogens (Brader et al. 2006; Hopkins et al. 2009). GS are amino acid-derived compounds and comprise the tryptophan-derived indole glucosinolates (IGS) and methionine-derived aliphatic glucosinolates (AGS). Several R2R3-MYB transcription factors initiate the expression of GS biosynthetic genes. *MYB28, MYB29,* and *MYB76* drive AGS biosynthesis
(Sonderby et al. 2007; Sonderby et al. 2010a; Li et al. 2013), while *MYB34, MYB51,* and *MYB122* transcriptionally regulate IGS biosynthesis genes (Frerigmann and Gigolashvili
2014).

293 IGS and AGS synthesis is mostly JA-induced (Sonderby et al. 2010b). The increased 294 accumulation of JA and JA-Ile levels in *linc1-1* mutants led us to explore JA-induced GS 295 synthesis. Before and after *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> treatment, the expression of MYB28, MYB29, MYB76, 296 MYB34, MYB51 and MYB122, was significantly upregulated in *linc1-1* mutants when 297 compared with wild type plants (Figure 5A). Enhanced *MYB* transcription factor mRNA 298 levels also correlated with upregulated expression of key biosynthetic genes, such as 299 CYP79B2, CYP79B3 and CYP83B1 which are related to IGS synthesis, and CYP79F1, 300 CYP79F2 and CYP83A1, which are involved in AGS biosynthesis (Figure 5B). The 301 expression of GS genes was also assessed in our C#2, C#5 and OE lines, showing 302 comparable levels to wild type (Figures S13 and S14). These results demonstrate that the 303 expression of all the tested IGS and AGS biosynthetic genes is affected in *linc1-1* mutants 304 with and without *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> treatment.

### 306 **Discussion**

The *Arabidopsis* genome contains four *LINC* genes which have been extensively documented regarding their roles in regulating nuclear structure, chromatin organization, and nuclear size (Dittmer et al. 2007; Sakamoto and Takagi 2013; Wang et al. 2013; Guo and Fang 2014; Grob et al. 2014). Recent work addressed the role of the *LINC* gene family in biological processes such as seed germination (Zhao et al. 2016) and plant immunity (Zhao et al. 2016; Guo et al. 2017; Chao et al., 2019). In the latter study, Guo et al. reported 313 that among the single and double *linc* mutant lines tested, they observed a slightly enhanced 314 pathogen resistance phenotype in the single *linc1-1* mutant against infection by the virulent 315 *Psm* ES4326 pathogen, but a much stronger resistance phenotype in the *linc1 linc2* double 316 mutant. Choi et al. (2019) recently also investigated the role of *LINC* genes in immunity. 317 Upon *Pst* DC3000 infection, they found no difference in *linc1*, and 4 single mutants but 318 enhanced resistance in linc1/2 and linc1/4 double mutants. Guo et al (2017) were able to 319 observe lower SA levels in the *linc1 linc2* double mutant than in wild-type plants after 320 inoculation with Psm ES4326, whereas Choi et al. (2019) found elevated SA levels only in 321 linc1/2 double mutants. In the linc1/2 and linc1/4 double mutants, the same authors 322 reported enhanced levels of cell death and SID2, PR1, 2 and 5 mRNA amounts (Choi et al., 2019). These observations strongly indicate that members of the *LINC* gene family play 323 324 a significant role in the plant immune response that necessitates further examination.

325 We examined if there is a specialized role for the *LINC* genes in the PTI response using inoculation with the PTI specific bacterial strain *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> as a model system. Out of 326 327 four single and two double mutant *linc* lines examined, only *linc1* single mutants exhibited 328 enhanced susceptibility to Pst hrcC<sup>-</sup> infection. Additionally, our data further show that 329 *linc1* knock out mutants are compromised in the PAMP-induced activation of MPK3, 330 MPK4 and MPK6 as well as the expression of PTI marker genes. These results corroborate 331 the enhanced susceptibility phenotype of *linc1* mutants to infection by non-virulent *Pst* 332 *hrcC*<sup>-</sup> indicating that *LINC1* plays a role in the transcriptional regulation of defense-related 333 genes and is a positive regulator of PTI. We did not observe any changes in SA levels upon 334 *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> infection, but significant changes in JA and JA-IIe levels and observed that *linc1* 335 mutants show enhanced resistance to the nectrotrophic fungal pathogen *Botrytis cinerea*.

336 Since the single *linc1* mutants already show a role for *LINC1* in JA-related immune 337 responses, we believe that this is the true function of LINC1, whereas double mutant 338 combinations of several pathways might generate synthetic phenotypes.

339 Phytohormones play a key role in plant disease resistance, whereby pathogen 340 infection-induced hormone level changes can result in pronounced effects on defense gene 341 expression (Durrant and Dong 2004). This prompted us to examine the global gene 342 expression profile of a *linc1-1* knock out mutant in response to *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> infection. The 343 data suggest that *LINC1* controls defense and hormone signaling genes in response to *Pst* 344 *hrcC*<sup>-</sup> infection. Furthermore, in response to infection, SA- and ET-related genes become 345 significantly suppressed while JA response genes are strongly induced. These changes in 346 the expression levels of hormone and immune related genes in response to Pst hrcC-347 infection further support a pivotal role for *LINC1* in regulating PTI.

348 The substantial increase in the expression levels of the JA response genes in *linc1* 349 mutant plants might result in an increase in endogenous JA and JA-Ile levels. Indeed, *linc1* 350 knock out plants showed enhanced, whereas *LINC1* OE resulted in reduced JA and JA-Ile 351 levels. Furthermore, our analysis of the LINC1 KO and OE lines revealed no changes in 352 SA levels when compared to wild-type after inoculation with *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup>. The increased JA 353 levels together with the unchanged SA levels could explain the enhanced susceptibility of 354 *linc1* mutant plants to *Pst hrcC* infection, while at the same time exhibiting a resistant 355 phenotype toward *Botrytis cinerea*.

Key components in JA signaling are the JAZ proteins, which repress the activity of
 transcription factors in the absence of JA-Ile. Upon JA-Ile perception, JAZs dissociate from

358 their TFs to form co-receptor complexes with COI1 before their subsequent degradation. 359 The JAZ-released TFs can then activate JA-responsive genes and related defense (Thines 360 et al. 2007; Pauwels and Goossens 2011). We therefore investigated whether the observed 361 increased JA accumulation leads to changes in the expression of JAZ repressors. Compared to wild type, we detected reduced expression of JAZ1, JAZ3, JAZ5, JAZ6 and JAZ7 in Pst 362 hrcC-untreated and treated LINC1 KO plants. To further investigate the effect of an 363 364 increased expression of several JA-responsive genes along with the decreased expression 365 of several JA-repressor genes on JA responses in the *LINC1* lines, we performed a MeJA 366 root growth inhibition assays. The inhibitory effect of MeJA on root growth was more 367 pronounced in *LINC1* KO and OE plants. This result supports the idea that LINC1 protein 368 levels might also control JA signaling in a developmental context, but needs further studies.

Glucosinolates as well as their hydrolysis products have been demonstrated to play 369 370 a role in the resistance to biotic stresses (Brader et al. 2006; Schlaeppi et al. 2010). 371 Characterization of the components of the GS biosynthetic pathway have been carried out 372 and both transcription factors controlling GS-related genes as well as core biosynthetic 373 genes were identified (Halkier and Gershenzon 2006; Brader et al. 2006). Positive 374 regulation of several GS transcription factors and biosynthetic genes has been shown to be 375 JA driven (Doughty et al. 1995; Brader et al. 2001; Mikkelsen et al. 2003). The increased 376 JA and JA-Ile levels in *linc1* mutant plants prompted us to examine if there is an effect on 377 the GS biosynthetic pathways. We found that the expression of *MYB* transcription factors 378 involved in regulation of tryptophan-derived indole glucosinolate (IGS) and methionine-379 derived aliphatic glucosinolate (AGS) biosynthesis, including MYB28, MYB29, MYB76, 380 MYB34, MYB51 and MYB122, was significantly upregulated in both Pst hrcC<sup>-</sup> treated and untreated *linc1* plants when compared with wild-type. Examination of the expression
pattern of several IGS and AGS biosynthetic genes, such as *CYP79B2*, *CYP79B3*, *CYP83B1*, *CYP79F1*, *CYP79F2* and *CYP83A1* under *Pst hrcC*<sup>-</sup> treated and untreated
conditions reveled that they were also highly induced in *linc1* mutant plants. In summary,
the identification of a function of *LINC1* in both JA signaling and JA-Ile accumulation
reveals a new role for this protein in the regulation of JA-related processes including
immunity.

### **Bant material and growth conditions**

390 All Arabidopsis thaliana lines used in this study were in the Columbia-0 (Col-0) 391 background which was used as wild-type plant. Arabidopsis plants were grown in Jiffy pots 397 with a photoperiod of 16/8 hours and at 22°C and 60% humidity, or on plates containing 398 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>2</sub> Murashige-Skoog (MS) medium with a photoperiod of 16/8 hours at 22°C. The 399 Nicotiana benthamiana plants were soil-grown under a photoperiod of 16/8 hours and at 400 28°C for 4 weeks and used for Agrobacterium infiltration for transient expression studies.

#### **PAMP-induced** oxidative burst

452 Twelve leaf discs (4 mm in diameter) of four 3-4 week old plants per genotype were 453 collected. Each leaf disc was floated adaxial-side-up in an individual well of a 96-well microtiter plate (Costar, Fisher Scientific) containing 150 µl distilled H<sub>2</sub>O and then 454 455 incubated overnight at 22°C in continuous light to reduce the immediate wounding 456 response. Immediately prior to elicitation, the incubating medium was carefully removed 457 from each well avoiding any tissue damage or desiccation. Using a multichannel pipet, then 458 100 µl of the elicitation solution [10 mg/ml horseradish peroxidase (HRP, Sigma-Aldrich), 0.2 µM Luminol (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 µL flg22 (1mM)] was quickly added to each well 459 460 containing leaf disc for the treated samples and without the 5 µL flg22 for control samples. 461 Luminescence was measured every 1 min after the addition of elicitation solution for a 462 period of 40 min using TECAN Infinite 200 PRO microplate reader and signal integration 463 time was 0.5s. ROS measurements were expressed as means of RLU (Relative Light Units).

Seedling growth inhibition

The seedlings were then weighed at 10 days after treatment in three independent experiments. 469

#### **PAMP-induced callose deposition** 470

471 6 days old seedlings (6 independent plants per treatment) grown in  $\frac{1}{2}$  MS medium 472 containing 1% sucrose in a 12 well plate were treated with 1 µm of flg22 or water for 24 473 hours. After 24 hours, plants are placed in acetic acid:ethanol (1:3) over night to de-stain 474 the chlorophyll from the leaf. Following the de-staining step plants were rehydrated with 475 successive additions of ethanol (50% v/v) for 1 h, then 5ml ethanol (30% v/v) for 1 h, and 476 finally water for 2 h with two changes for washing. The plants were then incubated in the 477 dark over night with 0.01% aniline blue in 150 mM K<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub>. The following day individual 478 leaves were mounted on slides in 50% glycerol, and observed via epifluorescence 479 microscopy under UV illumination with a DAPI filter (Nikon). Pictures from three 480 independent experiments was collected and the number of callose deposited calculated using the automated cell counting in ImageJ and results were given in number of cells per 481 482 square pixel.

# 487 **Transcriptome analysis**

Total RNA was prepared from two-week-old plants. Total RNA was extracted with
NucleoSpin<sup>®</sup> RNA Plant extraction kit (Macherey-Nagel) according to the manufacturer's
instructions. RNA was eluted in 60 µl RNase-free water. For RNA-seq, mRNA sequencing
libraries were prepared from 1 µg of total RNA using TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library
Prep Kit (Illumina). The libraries were pooled together and sequenced on a HiSeq4000

493 (Illumina) system. Three biological replicates were analyzed for each condition. Paired-494 end sequencing of RNA-Seq samples was performed using Illumina GAIIx with a read 495 length of 100 bp. Reads FASTQC were quality-controlled using 496 (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/ fastqc/). Trimmomatic was used for 497 trimming of adaptor sequences. Parameters for read quality filtering were set as follows: 498 Minimum length of 36 bp; Mean Phred quality score greater than 30; Leading and trailing 499 bases removal with base quality below 3; Sliding window of 4:15. TopHat v2.0.9 was used 500 for alignment of short reads to the A. thaliana genome TAIR10, Cufflinks v2.2.0 for 501 transcript assembly and differential expression. To identify differentially expressed genes, 502 specific parameters (*p*-value: 0.05; statistical correction: Benjamini Hochberg; FDR: 0.05) in cuffdiff were used. Post-processing and visualization of differential expression were 503 504 done using cummeRbund v2.0.0. Genes were considered as regulated if fold change was 505 more than 1.5 and p-value < 0.05 compared to mock condition. Identified de-regulated 506 genes were used to generate HCL tree using Multi Experiment Viewer (MeV 4.9.0 version, 507 TM4, https://sourceforge.net/projects/mev-tm4/files/mev-tm4/MeV% 204.9.0/). The 508 normalization for the raw data was carried out for every gene and hierarchical clustering 509 was performed under Euclidian distances, average linkage and leaf order optimization. 510 Gene enrichment analyses performed using AgriGO were 511 (http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/agriGOv2/).

512 **Quantitative RT-PCR** 

513 The quantitative PCR was carried out using SsoAdvanced<sup>™</sup> Universal SYBR<sup>®</sup>
514 Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) in the CFX96 Touch<sup>™</sup> Real-Time PCR Detection System
515 (Bio-Rad). The relative expression values were determined using *ACTIN2*

516 (At3g18780) and *UBIQUITIN10* (At4g05320) as reference genes and comparative 517 analysis was carried out by Bio-Rad CFX manager software with the Ct method (2-518  $\Delta\Delta$ Ct). Normalized gene expression was expressed relative to wild-type controls in each 519 experiment.

#### 520 **Phytohormone measurements**

521 For phytohormones quantification, the leaves from 4-weeks old (wild-type, linc1-522 I and OE) plants were collected after inoculated as described above. 24 hours after 523 treatment, the plants were harvested, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, freeze-dried 524 overnight in a Liophylazer and grinded in a Gino grinder (for 2 cycles of 45 seconds each, 525 at 1150rpm). They were then aliquoted in 1.5 mL tubes (around 10 mg) and extracted with 526 1.0 mL of 70% methanol containing the respective phytohormones internal standards (d6-527 JA and d5-SA). The measurements were performed on an Agilent 1100 HPLC system 528 (Agilent Technologies, Böblingen, Germany) connected to a LTQ Iontrap mass 529 spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Bremen, Germany), and the quantification was based on 530 a calibration curve using original SA, JA and JA-Ile standards (Almeida Trapp et al. 2014).
