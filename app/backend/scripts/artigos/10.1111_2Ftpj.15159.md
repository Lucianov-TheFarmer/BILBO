# SUMMARY

SPLAYED (SYD) is a SWItch/Sucrose Non-Fermentable (SWI/SNF)-type chromatin remodeler identified in Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis). It is believed to play both redundant and differential roles with its closest homolog BRAHMA (BRM) in diverse plant growth and development processes. To better understand how SYD functions, we profiled the genome-wide occupancy of SYD and its impact on the global transcriptome and trimethylation of histone H3 on lysine 27 (H3K27me3). To map the global occupancy of SYD, we generated a GFP-tagged transgenic line and used it for chromatin immunoprecipitation experiments followed by next-generation sequencing, by which more than 6000 SYD target genes were identified. Through integrating SYD occupancy and transcriptome profiles, we found that SYD preferentially targets to nucleosome-free regions of expressed genes. Further analysis revealed that SYD occupancy peaks exhibit five distinct patterns, which were also shared by BRM and BAF60, a conserved SWI/SNF complex component, indicating the common target sites of these SWI/SNF chromatin remodelers and the functional relevance of such distinct patterns. To investigate the interplay between SYD and Polycomb-group (PcG) proteins, we performed a genome-wide analysis of H3K27me3 in syd-5. We observed both increases and decreases in H3K27me3 levels at a few hundred genes in syd-5 compared to wild type. Our results imply that SYD can act antagonistically or synergistically with PcG at specific genes. Together, our SYD genome-wide occupancy data and the transcriptome and H3K27me3 profiles provide a much-needed resource for dissecting SYD's crucial roles in the regulation of plant growth and development.

Keywords: Arabidopsis thaliana, SWItch/Sucrose Non-Fermentable chromatin remodeler, SPLAYED, BRAHMA, Polycomb-group protein, trimethylation of histone H3 on lysine 27.

# INTRODUCTION

In eukaryotes, chromatin is mainly composed of repeating units called nucleosome cores, which consist of 146 bp of DNA wrapped around a central histone octamer (two copies of each core histone H2A, H2B, H3, and H4) (Luger et al., 1997). Chromatin remodeling plays crucial roles in the regulation of gene expression through dynamic modification of chromatin architecture. There are two kinds of key chromatin-remodeling players. One utilizes the energy derived from ATP hydrolysis to alter DNA–histone interactions (Clapier and Cairns, 2009; Hargreaves and Crabtree, 2011; Han et al., 2015). The second involves enzymes that specifically add or remove covalent modifications on DNA and histone residues (Li et al., 2007; Jones, 2012).

The SWItch/Sucrose Non-Fermentable (SWI/SNF) ATPdependent chromatin-remodeling complexes, initially identified in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, have been extensively studied (Neigeborn and Carlson, 1984; Peterson and Herskowitz, 1992; Saha et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2007; Clapier and Cairns, 2009; Kobayashi and Kurumizaka, 2019). They are evolutionally conserved from yeast to mammals and plants (Martens and Winston, 2003; Sif, 2004; Flaus et al., 2006; Euskirchen et al., 2012; Dutta et al., 2017). A number of SWI/SNF homologs have been identified in Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis) (Verbsky and Richards, 2001; Knizewski et al., 2008), which include four SWI2/ SNF2 ATP-driven chromatin remodelers: SPLAYED (SYD), BRAHMA (BRM), MINUSULE 1 (MINU1), and MINU2 (Wagner and Meyerowitz, 2002; Farrona et al., 2004; Mlynarov a et al., 2007; Tang et al., 2008; Sang et al., 2012).

The Arabidopsis SWI2/SNF2 ATPase SYD plays pivotal roles in the regulation of several facets of development, including floral homeotic gene expression, stem cell pool maintenance, and maintenance of boundary cells during embryogenesis (Wagner and Meyerowitz, 2002; Kwon et al., 2005; Kwon et al., 2006). Moreover, SYD acts in specific biotic stress signaling pathways, e.g., negatively regulating SUPPRESSOR OF NPR1, CONSTITUTIVE 1 (SNC1)-mediated immunity (Walley et al., 2008; Johnson et al., 2015). The Arabidopsis SYD and BRM proteins share several conserved domains, of which the ATPase domain is the most conserved with nearly 50% identity (Wagner and Meyerowitz, 2002; Farrona et al., 2004). Unlike BRM, which harbors a C-terminal bromodomain, SYD contains a large unique C-terminal extension with two repetitive regions (Farrona et al., 2004; Su et al., 2006). Furthermore, a SYD truncated version lacking the C-terminal extension was shown to be able to rescue the syd null mutant (Su et al., 2006). SYD and BRM have redundant and differential functions in the regulation of plant development, resulting in null mutants that exhibit similar but distinct defects (Bezhani et al., 2007). For example, both syd and brm mutants are dwarfed and slow-growing, and have curling leaves (Wagner and Meyerowitz, 2002; Farrona et al., 2004; Hurtado et al., 2006; Tang et al., 2008).

In Drosophila, BRM has been identified as a Trithoraxgroup (TrxG) protein, based on genetic evidence that mutations of BRM suppress the mutations in Polycombgroup (PcG) genes through homeotic gene regulation (Tamkun et al., 1992; Schuettengruber et al., 2017). Likewise, Arabidopsis BRM appears to antagonize PcG activity at key developmental loci (Wu et al., 2012; Li et al., 2015; Li et al., 2016). For example, it was shown that BRM promotes vegetative development by suppressing PcG-mediated H3K27me3 deposition at the flowering repressor gene SHORT VEGETATIVE PHASE (SVP), thereby upregulating its expression (Li et al., 2015). As SYD is the closest homolog of BRM, it is also proposed to be a TrxG member. However, it is still not clear whether SYD functions similarly to BRM in antagonizing PcG-mediated repression.

Recently, the genome-wide occupancy of BRM has been profiled (Li et al., 2016; Archacki et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2020), which aids in speeding up the decipherment of BRM's important roles in plant developmental processes. Although a few direct targets of SYD have already been identified by chromatin immunoprecipitation combined with quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR) during the past decade (Kwon et al., 2005; Walley et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2015), the global occupancy of SYD has yet to be revealed.

To determine the functions of SYD, we used ChIP followed by next-generation sequencing (ChIP-seq) and transcriptome profiling to reveal the range of activities and transcriptional changes effected by SYD during vegetative development. It was uncovered that SYD directly targets to nearly a quarter of protein-coding genes in the Arabidopsis genome, being mainly present at developmental and stress-responsive genes. We also found that SYD preferentially occupies nucleosome-free regions of expressed genes genome-wide. Further, to understand the interplay between SYD and PcG proteins, we performed a genomewide analysis of H3K27me3 in a syd mutant, which revealed several hundred genes that show altered H3K27me3 levels upon loss of SYD activity.

## RESULTS

#### A transgenic line expressing GFP-tagged SYD

To profile the genome-wide occupancy of SYD, we generated a transgenic Arabidopsis line expressing the fulllength SYD driven by its native regulatory region (an approximately 18-kb genomic fragment in total; pSYD:: SYD-GFP syd-5; Figure S1). We then attempted to use this line for ChIP-seq, but were unsuccessful, likely due to the extraordinarily large size of the full-length SYD (approximately 400 kDa). Because the N-terminal ATPase AT-hookcontaining region of SYD has been shown to be sufficient for its biological activity (Su et al., 2006), we thus generated another Arabidopsis line expressing the GFP-tagged SYD with its C-terminal domain excluded (SYDDC) (Figure 1a). Indeed, the pSYD::SYDDC-GFP transgene could fully rescue the syd-5 phenotype (Figure 1b). Clear GFP signals were observed in the nuclei of root tip cells by confocal microscopy (Figure 1c). Furthermore, a Western blot experiment was conducted to examine the size and level of the SYDDC protein; as shown in Figure 1(d), the transgene was expressed as expected in vivo. Altogether, these data indicate that the truncated version of SYD is fully functional in vivo.

The ChIP-seq results showed that SYD occupies 4865 sites in total, corresponding to 6714 genes in the Arabidopsis genome (Datas S1 and S2). The majority of the identified SYD peaks were located in the promoter regions, nearly a quarter in intergenic regions, and a small proportion (9.5%) in gene bodies (Figure 2a). We observed two major types of SYD-occupied sites defined by either sharp, narrow peaks or broad peaks (Figure 2b), similar to the BRM occupancy patterns observed previously (Li et al., 2016). Furthermore, we analyzed the distribution of SYD peaks along each chromosome and found that, in general, SYD occupancy is predominantly located in euchromatic regions (Figure 2c). We also examined whether any particular sequence motifs are overrepresented in SYD-occupied genomic regions by performing a stringent motif search through the web-based MEME-ChIP platform (Machanick and Bailey, 2011). Three putative motifs were identified that are significantly enriched in SYD peaks (Figure S3). In addition, an inspection of SYD occupancy at several known direct SYD targets confirmed the published results (Figure 2d), for example, the MYB DOMAIN PROTEIN 2 (MYB2) and TAR-GET OF MONOPTEROS 3 (TMO3) loci, which have been shown to be direct SYD targets by ChIP-qPCR (Walley et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2015).

Intriguingly, Gene Ontology (GO) term analysis revealed that genes involved in responses to biotic and abiotic stimuli are highly enriched in SYD target genes (Figure 2e), implying that SYD may play an essential role in the response to stimuli. Thus, we investigated whether the expression of SYD could be stimulated by different stimuli, including biotic/abiotic stresses and plant hormones. Quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR) experiments were conducted to examine the expression levels of SYD using wild-type (WT) seedlings that were mock-treated or treated with cold, indoleacetic acid (IAA), methyl jasmonate (MeJA), abscisic acid (ABA), flg22, or benzothiadiazole (BTH). Cold stress is one of the major environmental factors that limit the productivity of plants (Shi et al., 2014). The phytohormones IAA, JA, and ABA play critical roles in plant growth, development, and defense (Song et al., 2014; Vissenberg et al., 2020). Flg22 is a synthesized bacterial flagellin peptide that induces many defense-responsive genes (Chinchilla et al., 2006). BTH is a salicylic acid synthetic analogue that can provide resistance to diseases (Huot et al., 2017). Strikingly, we found that, except MeJA treatment, which had no effect on SYD expression, the expression levels of SYD were significantly decreased upon cold, IAA, ABA, and BTH treatments and increased upon flg22 treatment (Figure S4). These observations indicate that SYD not only preferentially targets to stimulus-responsive genes during normal growth conditions but also responds to various stimuli by adjusting its own expression at the transcription level.

## SYD occupies nucleosome-free regions of expressed genes

To further examine SYD occupancy at the genome-wide scale, we mapped SYD occupancy signals over a gene model. Through this method it was revealed that SYD is centered near transcription start sites (TSSs), with very weak signals near transcription termination sites (TTSs) (Figure 3a), sharing a similar binding pattern with BRM (Li et al., 2016; Archacki et al., 2017). Because SYD is a SWI2/ SNF2-type chromatin remodeler, we sought to examine the relationship between nucleosome distribution density and SYD enrichment patterns. By taking advantage of the published data obtained with the assay for transposaseaccessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) on WT plants (Harris et al., 2018; Potok et al., 2019), we uncovered that SYD is highly enriched over nucleosome-free regions of hundreds of genes, predominately around TSSs (Figure 3a), suggesting a potentially direct effect of SYD on local transcriptional control.

To decipher the role of SYD in the regulation of gene expression, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) experiments to identify transcriptome changes in the syd-5 mutants as compared with those in WT. It was found that 601 and 1535 genes were upregulated and downregulated compared to WT, respectively (Figure 3b; Data S3). Next, we compared the SYD occupancy profile with the transcriptome data. In WT plants, we observed that the global expression level of SYD-occupied genes is higher than the average expression level of all the genes in the Arabidopsis genome (Figure 3c). To further test how SYD enrichment relates to mRNA levels, the relationship between SYD occupancy intensity and mRNA levels was examined and a positive correlation was revealed (Figure 3d). Besides, approximately 11% (744 out of 6714) of the genes occupied by SYD exhibited significant misexpression in syd-5 compared to WT. The majority of the direct targets were downregulated (517 out of 744) (Figure 3e; Data S4), suggesting that SYD has a major role in transcriptional activation of its targets. Taken together, these data imply that SYD preferentially targets to nucleosome-free regions of highly expressed genes.

## SYD occupancy peaks form clusters of distinct patterns

Having shown the strong genome-wide enrichment of SYD over TSSs (Figure 3a), we asked whether those peaks display certain patterns over target genes. To answer this question, we further analyzed SYD peaks over a gene model and found that SYD occupancy peaks form five distinct clusters (Figure 4a). The cluster 1 peaks are quite broad, displaying enrichment over gene bodies. The peaks in clusters 2 and 4 display opposite patterns, with cluster 2 peaks displaying enrichment over TTSs toward 2 kb downstream and cluster 4 over TSSs toward 2 kb upstream. Approximately half of the SYD peaks belong to cluster 3, which show strong enrichment over TSSs; the cluster 5 peaks, on the contrary, show strong enrichment over TTSs (Figure 4a). Next, we categorized all SYD target genes into those five clusters based on their peak patterns. First, we investigated the expression levels of the genes in each of the clusters in WT and syd-5. In general, the expression level of cluster 1 genes is higher than that of the genes in all other clusters; and in agreement with SYD's role in gene activation, the cluster 1 genes are also significantly downregulated in syd-5 compared to WT (Figure 4b). Second, we found that the average length of cluster 1 genes is shorter than the length of those in other clusters, and the percentage of exon coverage of genes in cluster 1 is dramatically higher than that of those genes in clusters 2–5 (Figure 4b). ## SYD and BRM co-occupy a common set of genes

It was shown previously, largely based on DNA microarray and mutant analysis data, that Arabidopsis SWI2/SNF2 chromatin remodelers SYD and BRM exhibit redundant and differential roles in regulating plant growth and development (Kwon *et al.*, 2006; Bezhani *et al.*, 2007). Furthermore, it was shown in a recent study that BRM can physically associate with SYD (Li *et al.*, 2016). Based on these earlier observations, we reasoned that SYD and BRM might directly target to a common set of genes, thereby providing an explanation for their genetic redundancy. To test this hypothesis, we plotted all the uniquely mapped reads of BRM ChIP-seq over those five clusters of genes and found that BRM largely targets to SYD-occupied genes (Figure 5a; Li *et al.*, 2016). Similarly, we also plotted the published ChIP-seq reads of BAF60, a subunit of the SWI/ SNF chromatin-remodeling complex (Jégu *et al.*, 2017). As shown in Figure 5(a), BRM and BAF60 together with SYD target to a common set of genes with similar occupancy patterns, indicating a concerted genomic targeting of these chromatin-remodeling ATPases and associated players.

## DISCUSSION

In this study, we employed global approaches to understand the functions of the SWI2/SNF2 chromatin remodeler SYD. We showed that a truncated version of SYD, lacking the large C-terminal domain, can fully rescue the syd-5 null mutant, which is consistent with a previous report (Su et al., 2006). Hence, it appears that the truncated version is sufficient for its biological activity. However, it was shown by Su et al. (2006) that the ratio of the full-length and truncated SYD protein changes dynamically during development, indicating a potential developmentally regulated process involved in generating the truncated SYD. It would be of great interest to investigate whether the full-length SYD is functionally active and what the differences are between the two forms of SYD. For that, future efforts are needed to profile the global occupancy of the full-length SYD.

Through comparing ChIP-seq data for SYD and BRM, we found that these two close homologs share similar occupancy patterns (Figure 5), suggesting a concerted genomic targeting of these SWI2/SNF2-type chromatin remodelers. This finding also implies that SYD and BRM may play redundant roles on their co-targets at the genome scale. It would be of importance to investigate whether SYD and BRM affect each other's occupancy at their co-targets and on which specific genes they play unique, shared, or redundant roles. To that end, future ChIP-seq experiments to profile the genome-wide occupancy of SYD in a BRM null mutant background, and vice versa, would shed light on their complex actions at specific loci genome-wide. Further, GO term analysis indicated that the co-targets of SYD and BRM are enriched in biotic/abiotic stress responses and other diverse cellular processes, suggesting that SYD and BRM are redundantly involved in many biological processes. SYD also appears to regulate ABA and drought stress responses, a function previously reported for BRM (Han et al., 2012; Peirats-Llobet et al., 2016). Interestingly, SYD is likely to positively regulate the salicylic acid response, while BRM was reported to negatively regulate this response (Archacki et al., 2017), implying that SYD and BRM may play opposing roles in regulating salicylic acid signaling. Increasing evidence suggests that the interplay between SYD and BRM is a complex network, with the two proteins showing both redundant and differential functions in Arabidopsis. Thus, the genome-wide profiles of SYD/ BRM, together with the transcriptome data and the transgenic materials, would be an asset for the plant biology community to further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the diverse biological processes regulated by the two SWI2/SNF2 chromatin remodelers in the future.

Early studies in Drosophila foreshadowed the antagonistic functions of TrxG and PcG proteins that are required for activating and repressing targets, respectively (Brock and Fisher, 2005; Kadoch and Crabtree, 2015; Schuettengruber et al., 2017). To date, several putative TrxG proteins have been identified in Arabidopsis, including SYD and BRM (Wu et al., 2012; Li et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2016; Shu et al., 2020), the chromatin-remodeling ATPases PICKLE (PKL) and PICKLE RELATED 2 (PKR2) (Aichinger et al., 2009; Aichinger et al., 2011), the SAND domain protein ULTRA-PETALA 1 (ULT1) (Carles and Fletcher, 2009), the H3K4 methyltransferase ARABIDOPSIS TRITHORAX 1 (ATX1) (Alvarez-Venegas et al., 2003), and the H3K27 demethylase RELATIVE OF EARLY FLOWERING 6 (REF6) (Lu et al., 2011; Cui et al., 2016; Li et al., 2016). However, the detailed functions of these putative TrxG proteins in antagonizing PcG are generally not well understood. Our previous genomewide analysis of H3K27me3 in brm mutants suggested both antagonistic and cooperative roles between BRM and PcG at specific loci during plant development. For example, at the SVP locus, it was shown that BRM directly activates its expression by preventing the occupancy of CLF/ SWN. At the WRKY23 locus, on the contrary, BRM is likely to cooperate with CLF/SWN (Li et al., 2015). In this study, we compared the genome-wide occupancy of SYD with those of PcG catalytic subunits CLF and SWN. It was shown that the occupancies of SYD and CLF/SWN are largely mutually exclusive throughout the genome (Figure 6a), which fits into the general model that TrxG and PcG function antagonistically. However, we also identified a small number of genes that are co-targeted by SYD and CLF/SWN, although the peak summits on individual genes do not perfectly overlap (Figure 6b,c). Next, by comparing the global distribution of H3K27me3 in WT and syd mutants, we found several hundred genes showing altered levels of H3K27me3, of which almost half displayed increased levels of H3K27me3 (Figure 7), indicating that SYD not only antagonizes PcG function but also cooperates with PcG at some genes. Our results are consistent with an earlier study on Brahma Related Gene 1 (BRG1), a BRM homolog in mammalian cells, which was shown to regulate the activity of PcG in both antagonistic and synergistic modes across the genome (Ho et al., 2011).

More interestingly, our results also uncovered complex interplays among SYD, BRM, and PcG proteins at individual loci during vegetative development. For instance, we found that SYD and CLF both target to BELL1 (BEL1), which encodes a homeodomain protein required for ovule identity, and loss of SYD activity led to a gain of H3K27me3 at this locus (Figure 7e), suggesting that SYD functions antagonistically with PcG at this locus. In our previous work, similarly, it was revealed that BRM also antagonizes PcG repression through physically preventing PcG's occupancy at this locus (Li et al., 2015). Thus, the BEL1 locus is an example where SYD and BRM act together to antagonize the activity of PcG proteins. In contrast, the miR156 locus is an example showing that SYD and BRM play opposite roles in regulating PcG activity. In plants, it has been shown that miR156 acts as a master regulator of the juvenile-to-adult phase transition (Wu et al., 2009). Here we showed that loss-of-function of SYD results in a decrease in H3K27me3 levels, and thereby upregulation of gene expression, at the miR156A/C loci (Figure 7e; Data S3), implying that SYD functions synergistically with PcG to regulate the juvenile-to-adult transition in plants. BRM, on the other hand, has been shown to actually antagonize PcG at the miR156A locus to regulate vegetative phase change (Xu et al., 2016). Therefore, it is tempting to speculate that SYD and BRM may function oppositely, instead of redundantly or synergistically, to fine-tune the expression of the master vegetative phase transition regulator miR156.

It is expected that, with the genome-wide resources available, the plant biology community can mine the data and determine the functional interplay of SYD and BRM with PcG at specific loci involved in various development or stress response pathways. However, it is necessary to emphasize that the current ChIP-seq profile is a snapshot of the occupancy at a particular time of development, i.e., seedling stage in this study. It would be important to profile the dynamic occupancy of SYD throughout an Arabidopsis plant's life in the future.

## EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

## Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis WT and mutants syd-5 (SALK\_023209; Wu et al., 2015) and brm-3 (SALK\_088462; Farrona et al., 2007) used in this study were all in a Columbia (Col-0) background. Seeds were stratified in the dark at 4°C for 3 days and then sown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) basal medium plates (2.22 g L-<sup>1</sup> MS mix [Cat# M519, PhytoTech Labs, Lenexa, KS, USA], 1.5% sucrose, 0.8% agar, pH 5.7) or soil. Plants were grown in long-day photoperiod conditions (16 h light/8 h dark cycles) with the temperature set to 22°C. PCR-based genotyping was performed to identify homozygous transfer DNA (T-DNA) insertion mutants which were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC). Primers used in this study are listed in Data S9.

#### Plasmid construction for plant transformation

The bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) clone T3B23 that harbors the SYD locus was obtained from ABRC. An approximately 12-kb genomic fragment that contains the SYD regulatory region was amplified from the T3B23 plasmid, which was isolated using the QIAGEN Large-Construct Kit. The PCR products were then purified (GenepHlowTM Gel/PCR Kit, Geneaid) and inserted into pMDC107 to generate pMDC107-gSYDDC. The pMDC107-gSYDDC construct was then sequenced and transferred into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101, which was used to transform into the syd-5 heterozygous plants (Clough and Bent, 1998). GFP-tagged proteins were detected using T3 homozygous transgenic lines.

#### Western blot

For total protein extraction, 1 g of seedlings was ground into fine powder in liquid nitrogen and resuspended in a twofold volume of lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 300 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM EDTA, 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, protease inhibitor cocktail). Then the debris was removed by centrifuging the crude lysate at 16 000 g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were mixed with 59 SDS sample buffer and loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels. The proteins were detected by antibodies raised against GFP (Cat# ab290, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or actin (Cat# ab1801, Abcam, Cambridge, UK).

## ChIP assay

ChIP was performed as previously described (Li et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2017; Shu et al., 2019). Briefly, 10 g of seedlings was fixed for 20 min in 1% formaldehyde. The chromatin was isolated and sonicated. Then the sonicated chromatin was pre-cleaned followed by incubation with anti-GFP (Cat# ab290, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or anti-H3K27me3 (Cat# 07-449, Millipore, Oakville, ON, Canada) overnight at 4°C with gentle shaking, and the remaining chromatin was kept as input control. ChIP DNA was purified using the MinElute PCR Purification Kit (Cat# 28004, Qiagen, Toronto, ON, Ontario). The libraries were prepared using the NEBNext UltraTM DNA Library Prep Kit (E7370S) and subsequently used for Illumina single-end sequencing. Two independent biological replicates were conducted for each ChIP-seq, and three biological replicates were performed for ChIP-qPCR. The ChIP-qPCR primers used in this study are listed in Data S9.

## ChIP-seq data analysis

ChIP-seq data were analyzed as previously described (Chen et al., 2019; Shu et al., 2019). Briefly, the sequenced reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis genome (TAIR10) by Bowtie mapper with default settings (Langmead et al., 2009; Lamesch et al., 2012). The uniquely mapped reads were kept for further analysis (Table S1). To identify reads-enriched regions (peaks), peak calling was performed using the MACS2 program with default settings (Zhang et al., 2008). Highly confident peaks were generated by using the IDR with a 1% threshold (Landt et al., 2012).

## RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA extraction, library preparation, and data analysis were carried out as previously described (Shu et al., 2019). Three biological replicates of each sample were sequenced separately. The summary of read numbers is shown in Table S2. Genes with at least a twofold change in expression (FDR ≤ 0.05) were considered to be expressed differentially among these samples in this study.
