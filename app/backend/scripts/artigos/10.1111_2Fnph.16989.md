# Summary

- Phosphate (Pi) is a critical macronutrient for the biochemical and molecular functions of cells. Under Pi limitation, plants manifest adaptative strategies to increase Pi scavenging. However, how low Pi sensing links to the transcriptional machinery remains unknown.
- The role of the MEDIATOR (MED) transcriptional co-activator, through its MED16 subunit in Arabidopsis root system architecture remodeling in response to Pi limitation was assessed. Its critical function acting over SENSITIVE TO PROTON RHIZOTOXICITY1 (STOP1)-ALUMINUM ACTIVATED MALATE TRANSPORT1

(ALMT1) signaling module was tested through a combination of genetic, biochemical, and genome-wide transcriptomic approaches.

- Root system configuration in response to phosphate scarcity involved MED16 functioning, which modulates the expression of a large set of low-Pi-induced genes that respond to local and systemic signals in the Arabidopsis root tip, including those directly activated by STOP1. BiFC analysis suggests that MED16 is required for the transcriptional activation of STOP1 targets including the membrane permease ALMT1, to increase malate exudation in response to low Pi.
- Our results unveil the function of a critical transcriptional component MED16, in the root adaptive responses to a scarce plant macronutrient, which helps understanding how plant cells orchestrate root morphogenesis to gene expression with the STOP1-ALMT1 module.

**Key words:** *Arabidopsis thaliana,* malate, Mediator complex, phosphate starvation, plant nutrition, root system architecture.

### Introduction

In their natural environment, plant roots encounter organic and/or inorganic Pi pools, which need to be solubilized for transport and redistribution into the plant. To improve productivity in agricultural ecosystems, Pi fertilization is generally required to maximize fruit and grain yields, but at undesirable economic and ecological costs (López-Arredondo *et al.*, 2014). Thus, management of Pi nutrition is critical in the search of a more efficient crop production. Under Pi limitation, plants manifest systemic and local Pi-starvation responses (López-Bucio *et al.*, 2002; Wu *et al.*, 2003; Sánchez-Calderón *et al.*, 2005; Cruz-Ramírez *et al.*, 2006; Morcuende *et al.*, 2007; Thibaud *et al.*, 2010). Systemic response activation depends upon

the Pi status inside cells, whereas the local response, regulated by the external concentration of Pi, is perceived at the root tip to influence cell fate decisions (Thibaud *et al.,* 2010). Pi concentrations below a minimal threshold activate a root determinate developmental program that arrests cell division and triggers differentiation of root meristematic cells (López-Bucio *et al.,* 2003; Bustos *et al.,* 2010; Thibaud *et al.,* 2010; Ruiz-Herrera *et al.,* 2015).

Root architecture remodeling in response to low Pi availability is related to cellular homeostasis of other nutrient ions. For instance, the transition from indeterminate to determinate primary root growth in response to low Pi, is tightly linked to iron (Fe) uptake and accumulation within the meristematic and cell elongation zones. This in turn triggers the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that modulate stem cells (Ward *et al.*, 2008). This pathway is controlled by the transcription factor STOP1 and its downstream component ALMT1, which encodes a membrane protein responsible for malate exudation (Balzergue *et al.*, 2017; Mora-Macias *et al.*, 2017; Godon *et al.*, 2019). Malate is believed to play two important adaptive functions in the rhizosphere, i) it interacts with Fe and other cations making more Pi available for root acquisition, and ii) it diminishes toxic Fe<sup>3+</sup> and Al<sup>3+</sup> levels via chelation (Balzergue *et al.*, 2017).

The MEDIATOR (MED) complex is a multi-protein assembly that acts as a transcriptional coactivator controlling gene expression through interaction with enhancers, *cis*-regulatory elements and the basal RNA polymerase II (Pol II) transcription machinery (Björklund and Gustafsson, 2005). MEDIATOR 16 (MED16) subunit regulates plant development and multiple biotic and abiotic stress, including Fe uptake and signaling (Yang *et al.*, 2014; Zhang *et al.*, 2014), but its role in phosphate scarcity is currently unclear. Considering the tight link between local Pi starvation response and Fe, as well as the involvement of Mediator as a transcriptional activator, it is possible that MED16 plays a role in sensing mechanisms that control root foraging to adapt to local low Pi. To address this question, the possible role of MED16 for plant adaptation to phosphate deprivation was assessed through molecular characterization of related loss-of-function Arabidopsis mutants, genome wide transcriptomic analysis and protein-protein interactions, which revealed the critical function of MED16 in plant low Pi tolerance, acting in the STOP-ALMT1 signaling pathway.

### **Materials and Methods**

# Plant material and growth conditions

*Arabidopsis thaliana* wild-type (WT; Col-0 and Ws), transgenic lines *CycB1:uidA* (Colón-Carmona *et al.*, 1999), *DR5:GFP* (Ottenschläger *et al.*, 2003), *pWOX5:GFP* (Sarkar *et al.*, 2007), *pPIN1::PIN1-GFP* (Benková *et al.*, 2003), *pPIN2::PIN2-GFP* (Blilou *et al.*, 2005), *pPIN3::PIN3-GFP* (Blilou *et al.*, 2005), *pCLE14:GFP* (Gutiérrez-Alanís *et al.*, 2017), *pSTOP1:GFP* (Mora-Macias *et al.*, 2017), *pALMT1:GFP* (Mora-Macias *et al.*, 2017) and the mutant lines *med16-2* (SALK\_048091; Zhang *et al.*, 2014), *med16-3* (CS859103; Zhang *et al.*, 2014), *med16-4* (Zhang *et al.*, 2014), *stop1* (SALK\_114108; Mora-Macias *et al.*, 2017) and *almt1* (SALK\_009629; Mora-Macias *et al.*, 2017) were used for the experiments reported here. Seeds were disinfected, germinated and grown on 1% agar plates containing 0.1x Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium (López-Bucio *et al.*, 2002). KH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub> was used as the main phosphate source for the different concentrations, 1 mM KH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub> (high Pi; +Pi), 5  $\mu$ M (low Pi; -Pi). For optimal growth, plates were placed in a Percival AR95L chamber at 22 °C, with 16 h/ 8 h light/darkness photoperiod and >200  $\mu$ mol/m<sup>-2</sup>/s<sup>-1</sup> luminous intensity.

# Microscopy

For GUS staining, *Arabidopsis* seedlings were incubated in a GUS reaction buffer (0.5 mg mL<sup>-1</sup> 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl- $\beta$ -D-glucuronide in 100 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7), then cleared and fixed as described (Malamy and Benfey, 1997).

The histochemical Fe staining analysis was performed according to Muller et al. (2015). Plant roots were analyzed by differential interference contrast microscopy (DIC), Leica and laser-scanning microscope for histochemical staining.

The GFP detection was performed in roots treated with propidium iodide (PI) 10 mg ml<sup>-1</sup> solution for 3 minutes, after three washes with sterilized distilled water, plants were mounted

in 50% glycerol on microscope slides. Samples were analyzed using an inverted Zeiss LSM510 confocal laser-scanning microscope at wavelengths specific to both PI fluorescence with a 568-nm excitation line and emission window of 585–610 nm, and GFP emission with 500- to 523-nm emission filter (488-nm excitation line).

### Analysis of growth

The root system architecture was analyzed with a stereoscopic microscope (Olympus SZH10). For lateral root density, emerged lateral roots were counted for each plant and their number was divided by the primary root length. Primary root length was measured using a ruler. Meristem length was measured taking into account the distance between QC and the most distal *CycB1* expressing cell. Cortical cells were analyzed from the mature zone of the primary root. To establish statistically significant differences among means, univariate and multivariate analyses with Tukey's post-hoc test were applied and different letters were used to indicate means that differ significantly (P<0.05).

### Preparation of mRNA-seq libraries from root tip sections

mRNA-seq libraries from sections of root rips was performed according to Mora-Macias et al. (2017). Root tip sections of approximately 2-3 mm in length were sectioned from approximately 500 seedlings (4 days after germination) per treatment. Harvested root tips were frozen and powdered using liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was isolated from frozen root tip using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. mRNA-seq libraries were generated from 5  $\mu$ g of total RNA and prepared using the TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

### RNA-seq high-throughput sequencing and data analysis

RNA-seq and data analysis were performed as previously described (Yong-Villalobos *et al.,* 2015). FastQC software (version 0.11.4) was used for quality assessment of Illumina reads (.fastq format files) and Trimmomatic software (version 0.35; Bolger *et al.,* 2014) used to remove reads that contained adapter sequences and low quality reads. Paired-end reads

were aligned to the Arabidopsis TAIR10 reference genome using TopHat2 software (version 2.0.9; Kim *et al.*, 2013) and to estimate raw counts per gene HTSeq (version 0.6.0; Anders *et al.*, 2015) was used. Differential gene expression analysis was carried out using the edgeR package (version 3.12.0; Robinson *et al.*, 2010). Analysis of Gene Ontology categories enrichment was performed using g:Profiler (Raudvere et al. 2019).

# **Results and Discussion**

# Phosphate starvation sensing operates via MED16

Low phosphate availability triggers the consumption of the root meristem and promotes the formation of branches that increases the absorptive capacity (López-Bucio et al., 2002; Svistoonoff et al., 2007). Accordingly, when WT root tips sense Pi scarcity (<10 µM Pi [-Pi]), primary root growth is inhibited, whereas lateral root and root hair formation close to the root tip is strongly stimulated, evidenced by high root hair density, primary root shortening, as well as reduction of cortical cell size (Fig. 1a-d, i; Figs. S1, S2). In contrast, in low Pi medium, *med16-2*, *med16-3*, and *med16-4* Arabidopsis seedlings showed longer primary roots with meristem lengths similar to the WT grown in Pi sufficient conditions with high cell proliferation activity and reduced lateral root formation (Fig. 1a-e; Figs. S1, S2). Loss of MED16 thus renders a low Pi-root resistant phenotype in Arabidopsis seedlings. To assess whether *MED16* expression is regulated by Pi limitation, we quantified *MED16* mRNA in the root apex of 4d-old WT and *med16-2* seedlings using qRT-PCR (Fig. S3). We found that MED16 expression was not significantly affected by Pi availability, suggesting a nontranscriptional regulation. Then, the effects of low Pi on MED16 protein was examined using Arabidopsis seedlings carrying the 35S:MED16-GFP gene construction (Zhang et al., 2014). As shown in Figure S3, the green fluorescence signal of MED16-GFP protein was enhanced up to 3-fold in the nucleus of root cells under Pi limitation, whereas in seedlings harboring *35S:GUS-GFP* gene construct *GUS-GFP* expression was not affected by Pi status. These results suggest that low Pi availability increases the level of MED16 at a non transcriptional level, probably by modulating its stability.

The inhibition of primary root growth in response to low-Pi in the WT is associated with the arrest of cell division and cell elongation, as well as changes in auxin transport within the root tip (López-Bucio *et al.*, 2019). These responses can be monitored via expression of the mitotic gene marker *CycB1:uidA*, the quiescent center reporter construct pWOX5:GFP and auxin-related gene markers (Colón-Carmona *et al.*, 1999; Sarkar *et al.*, 2007). In low Pi conditions, as expected by the differentiation of meristematic cells, the *CycB1:uidA* and pWOX5:GFP reporter signals became undetectable in WT plants, accompanied with a drastic reduction of cell size. On the other hand, in *med16-2* seedlings that have an active primary root meristem, both markers were normally expressed (Fig. 1e-i). In fact, MED16 loss-of-function leads to longer root meristem, cortical cells, and higher *CycB1* exression in RAM than WT seedlings grown in +Pi conditions. This correlates with the negative role of MED16 on endoreduplication and cell growth in Arabidopsis (Liu et al., 2019) (Fig. 1e-i). Auxin transport and response in Pi-deprived *med16-2* root tips was similar to that observed in WT seedlings grown under sufficient Pi conditions (Fig. S4), indicating that MED16 orchestrates low Pi sensing and root meristem functioning.

# MED16 triggers changes in global gene expression underlying low Pi adaptation

To understand the mechanism by which *MED16* modulates the transcriptional activation of low-Pi responsive genes, we performed RNA-seq analysis of RNA extracted from root tips of WT and *med16-2* seedlings grown in +Pi (1 mM Pi) and -Pi (5  $\mu$ M Pi) conditions (Fig. 2a). We generated four RNA-seq libraries named according to the genotype and conditions tested: WT\_+Pi, WT\_-Pi, *med16-2\_*+Pi and *med16-2\_*-Pi. We performed pairwise comparisons between libraries to determine statistically significant changes in transcript abundance induced by low Pi (-Pi/+Pi) in both WT and *med16-2\_*+Pi/WT\_+Pi) (Fig. 2a). A

threshold limit of -0.5 ≤logFC≥ 0.5 and false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 was used to determine differentially expressed genes (genes for which the transcript level is different in pairwise comparisons). A list of the differentially expressed genes and the logFC for each pairwise comparison is included in Table S1. To identify low Pi-responsive genes whose expression is affected in *med16-2*, we performed a Venn analysis between the genes whose transcripts are increased or decreased by low Pi in the WT and compared to those that increase or decrease by low Pi in med16-2 (Fig. 2b). We identified 329 genes whose transcripts significantly increase in both the WT and *med16-2*, whereas 815 genes are upregulated by low Pi in the WT and no longer up-regulated in *med16-2* (Fig. 2b). Similarly, we found 562 genes whose transcript levels decrease in response to low Pi in the WT and are no longer responsive in *med16-2* and 112 genes that are down-regulated in both WT and *med16-2* (Fig. 2b). These results show that approximately 25% (29% up-regulated; 21%) down-regulated) of the WT transcriptional response to low Pi is activated in med16-2 seedlings, suggesting that MED16 plays an important role in the activation of low-Pi responsive genes. Interestingly, we also found a set of 358 genes that were upregulated by low Pi in *med16-2*, which appear not to be responsive in the WT (Fig. 2b). The fact that transcripts are up-regulated/down-regulated when *MED16* is defective suggests a direct role for MED16 in the activation/repression of these genes or compensation for the absence of cellular processes that are activated by MED16 in the root tip in response to low Pi conditions.

Since the MEDIATOR complex plays a key role in the general activation of gene expression, we examined the effect of the lack of *MED16* on general basal transcription by comparing transcript levels between the WT and *med16-2* under control (+Pi) conditions (Fig. 2a; *med16-2\_+Pi/WT\_+Pi*). This analysis showed that the expression of 2215 genes (including low-Pi responsive and non-responsive genes) is down-regulated in *med16-2* mutants (Fig. 2a). Among these genes we found a subset of 272 low-Pi responsive genes, of which 113 were no longer responsive to low Pi in *med16-2* and 159 had a lower basal level of expression in *med16-2* than in the WT but were still responsive to low Pi (Fig. 2c). Therefore, the transcript level of 974 low-Pi-responsive genes is altered in *med16-2*, 815

that fail to respond to low Pi and 159 that have reduced basal expression, hence, the number of genes with affected expression in Pi-deprived *med16-2* seedlings accounts for 85% of the 1144 low-Pi responsive transcripts in WT. The effect of the *med16-2* mutation on the transcriptional landscape of low-Pi responsive transcripts can be observed in Figure 2d. These data indicate a major role of *MED16* in the activation of gene expression in the Arabidopsis root tip under low Pi conditions.

Two major sets of genes that respond to low Pi have been identified, those that respond to systemic signals generated in response to the internal Pi status of the plant and the other that responds to local signals determined by the external concentration of Pi (Thibaud *et al.*, 2010). To further dissect the effect of *med16-2* mutation on the response to low Pi, we examined whether MED16 had a similar or different effect on the expression of genes that respond to local or systemic low Pi signals. We first selected transcripts from Thibaud *et al.* (2010) that are activated in response to low Pi in the root tip of WT seedlings and then examined their transcript level levels in *med16-2*. We found that only 27 (31%) out of 83 locally up-regulated genes in the WT were significantly induced (logFC > 0.5; FDR<0.05) in *med16-2* (Fig. 2e), while 39 (67% of WT) out of 58 systemically up-regulated genes were induced in *med16-2*, corroborating a major role of *MED16* in the regulation of gene expression under low Pi conditions, however, local transcriptional responses to low Pi were the most affected in *med16-2* root tips. The full list of genes and their respective logFC and FDR values are included in Table S1.

To investigate whether specific biological processes were affected in *med16-2* in response to low Pi, we carried out a comparative gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Table S1). For this purpose, we used the previously defined gene sets in Figure 2c, to be able to dissect enriched categories within the previously defined class of genes which expression requires *MED16*. GO enrichment analysis of the set of 702 low-Pi responsive genes that are not responsive in *med16-2* revealed that this gene set is enriched in categories related to secondary metabolism and defense response (Table S1). We have previously reported that these processes are activated in Pi-deprived seedlings that develop a short-root phenotype

and absent in long-root Pi-insensitive mutants, which agrees with the long root phenotype of Pi-deprived *med16-2* seedlings. GO analysis of the 113 low-Pi-responsive genes that are not responsive to low Pi and have reduced basal expression in *med16-2*, revealed that this gene set is enriched in categories related with overall stress responses and hormone signaling, indicating a role for these MED16-regulated genes in signaling under low Pi conditions. Analysis of the 170 genes that are responsive to low Pi in both WT and *med16-2*, showed enrichment in categories related to cellular response to Pi starvation and response to low nutrient levels (Table S1). This was expected because within this gene set there are several PHR1-targets (Bustos et al., 2010) related to Pi transport (PHT1;4), Pi recycling (SQD1, SQD2, DGD2, MGD3) and Pi scavenging (PAP12). This indicates that the PHR1-SPX1 regulatory node that underlies systemic responses to Pi limitation (Bustos *et al.*, 2010; Puga et al., 2014; Thibaud et al., 2010) remains functional in med16-2. Finally, we performed GO enrichment analysis for the set of 159 genes that are responsive to low Pi, but have downregulated basal expression in *med16-2*; we found that enriched categories related to the response to Fe, metal handling, cellular response to Pi starvation and response to ROS were enriched in this gene set (Table S1). Because local signaling in response to low Pi conditions depends on Fe and ROS signaling, these results support the notion that MED16 plays an important role in the modulating the local transcriptional response to low Pi in the Arabidopsis root tip.

As the low Pi-resistant root phenotype of *med16* seedlings is similar to that reported for *stop1* (Mora-Macías *et al.*, 2017; Balzergue *et al.*, 2017), we asked whether *MED16* and *STOP1* could co-regulate a common set of genes. Therefore, we analyzed the expression of a set of STOP1-targets that were previously identified using DAP-seq (O'Malley *et al.*, 2016). We found that 65 STOP1 targets were up-regulated in response to low Pi conditions in the root tip of WT seedlings (Fig. 2f, g), of which only 18 were significantly up-regulated (logFC > 0.5; FDR<0.05) in *med16-2* root tips (Fig. 2f, g). A list of gene identifiers of the STOP1-targets and their expression in WT and *med16-2* is included in Table S1. Overall, the basal expression and responsiveness to low Pi of low-Pi responsive STOP1-targets is affected in *med16-2* relative to the WT (Fig. 2f). These data indicate that STOP1 and MED16 co-

regulate a common set of genes in response to low Pi conditions. STOP1 activates *ALMT1* expression in response to low Pi conditions to increase malate exudation and trigger the inhibition of primary root growth (Mora-Macías *et al.*, 2017; Balzergue *et al.*, 2017), because *med16-2* seedlings are defective in this response we analyzed *ALMT1* expression in WT and *med16-2* using our transcriptomic data (Fig. 2e). We found that *ALMT1* belongs to the 159-gene set of genes that are responsive to low Pi but have down-regulated basal expression in *med16-2* (Fig. 2h; Table S1). Our data indicate that even though *ALMT1* remains inducible in *med16-2* not tips, this change of expression does not reach the threshold of *ALMT1* expression that is necessary for the inhibition of primary root growth and, therefore, that the *med16-2* long root phenotype could be explained because it has down-regulated *ALMT1* expression. The effect of mutations of MED16 on the basal expression of ALMT1was corroborated by qRT-PCR analysis of RNA extracted from the root tip of *med16-2* seedlings grown in +Pi and -Pi media (Fig. S6).

### MED16 links the STOP1-ALMT1 node for low Pi-induced root architecture remodeling

To directly test whether MED16 is required for the activation of *ALMT1* in response to low Pi, we introduced a *pALMT1:GFP* reporter gene construct into the *med16-2* mutants. In contrast to Pi-deprived WT seedlings that show a strong induction of *pALMT1:GFP* expression in the meristematic and elongation zones of the primary root, only a modest but reproducible, patchy induction pattern was observed in the *med16-2* root tip (Fig. 3a; Fig. S5). We then asked whether a functional *MED16* is required for the expression of STOP1, by introducing a *pSTOP1:GFP* construct in the *med16-2* mutant. As previously reported (Mora-Macias *et al.*, 2017), Pi external levels did not modify the *STOP1* expression pattern in WT seedlings. Indeed, the level and pattern of *STOP1* expression was not altered by the lack of a functional MED16, indicating that regulation of *STOP1:GFP*, we quantified the level of *ALMT1* and *STOP1* transcripts by qRT-PCR (Fig. S6). We did not find differences in *STOP1* transcript level between -Pi and +Pi seedlings in both WT and *med16-2*. In the case of

*ALMT1*, transcript level was reduced between 60-80% in *med16-2* respect to the WT in both -Pi and +Pi seedlings, however, its transcript level was still responsive to low Pi in *med16-2* (Fig. S6). These results corroborate the RNA-seq data presented in Figure 2h and suggest that the level of expression of *ALMT1* expression directly depends on MED16 functioning during adaptation to Pi scarcity.

Because ALMT1 is involved in malate exudation and the low-Pi-resistant root phenotype of *almt1* and *stop1* is rescued by malate treatments during the primary root response to low Pi (Mora-Macias *et al.*, 2017), we hypothesized that *med16* could be deficient in malate exudation due to the reduced expression of ALMT1 in this mutant. To test this possibility, the effect of malate supplementation on root growth of WT and *med16-2*, *stop1* and *almt1* mutants was investigated in +Pi and -Pi conditions (Fig. 3c-e). In +Pi treatment, malate application had no detectable effects on primary root growth of all tested genotypes (Fig. 3c-e). However, in -Pi conditions, malate halted primary root growth of *med16-2*, *stop1* and *almt1* seedlings, leading to meristem exhaustion, as evidenced by full epidermal cell differentiation and lack of *pWOX5:GFP* expression in the quiescent center (Fig. 3f). These results confirm that MED16 is required for the proper expression of ALMT1 and that the expression of ALMT1 in *med16* seedlings does not reach the level necessary to excrete sufficient malate to trigger root meristem exhaustion in response to low Pi.

MED16 controls the low Pi/Fe redox balance responsible of root meristem exhaustion

Increased malate excretion in the root tip of seedlings exposed to low Pi media, was previously reported to cause an accumulation/mobilization of Fe in the transition, elongation and differentiation zones of the primary root, which cause meristem exhaustion (Müller *et al.*, 2015; Gutiérrez-Alanís *et al.*, 2017; 2018; Mora-Macias *et al.*, 2017). This accumulation/mobilization of Fe was not observed in the root tip of *med16-2* seedlings exposed to low Pi (Fig. 4a). The accumulation/mobilization of Fe in the root tip has been proposed to cause an alteration in the redox balance that triggers meristem exhaustion via a CLAVATA3/ENDOSPERM SURROUNDING REGION 14 (CLE14)-dependent mechanism (Gutiérrez-Alanís *et al.*, 2017; 2018). We therefore analyzed whether the exogenous

application of synthetic CLE14 peptide influences the *med16* long root phenotype in low Pi. Interestingly, CLE14 peptide restored the short-root phenotype and caused meristem exhaustion in *med16-2* seedlings grown in low Pi, suggesting that MED16 acts upstream of CLE14 (Fig. 4b; Fig. S7). To investigate if CLE14 is genetically downstream of MED16 in the signaling pathway that activates the primary root response to low Pi, we crossed the *med16-2* mutant with the *pCLE14:GFP* transgenic line. The GFP signal of *pCLE14:GFP* induced by low Pi in the RAM was lost in the *med16-2* background (Fig. 4c). These results demonstrate that MED16 acts upstream of CLE14 signaling.

Altogether, our results suggest that MED16 modulates the local transcriptional response to low Pi probably by directly affecting the expression of STOP1 target genes, including ALMT1. A Biomolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BiFC) assay was performed to assess whether MED16 could directly interact with STOP1 to modulate the expression of its target genes. Transient expression analyses in onion epidermal cells of a MED16-nYFP gene fusion with STOP1-cYFP gene fusions were performed. Since MED16 is known to interact with FER-LIKE IRON DEFICIENCY-INDUCED TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR (FIT) to regulate iron uptake/homeostasis (Zhang et al., 2014), MED16-FIT1 gene fusions were also included in the transient expression assays as a positive control (Fig. 4d). A strong and specific fluorescence was observed in nuclei of cells transfected with the MED16-nYFP and STOP1-cYFP or MED16-nYFP and FIT-cYFP, but not in cells co-expressing the C- and Nhalves of YFP. MED16 therefore most likely interacts with STOP1 (Fig. 4d). This hypothesis is strongly supported by distinct evidence: (i) The expression of 72% of the STOP1 target genes expressed in the root tip in response to low Pi is affected in *med16-2*; (ii) MED16 and STOP1 proteins show an overlapping accumulation pattern, preferentially on the outer cell layers of root tips (iii) *MED16* and *STOP1* regulation is post-transcriptional and Pi limitation stabilizes MED16 and STOP1 proteins in the nucleus (Fig. S3; Balzergue et al., 2017); (iv) BiFC analysis suggest that MED16 and STOP1 physically interact to regulate gene expression.

In conclusion, several lines of evidence are provided which support a critical function of MED16 in low Pi adaptive responses of *Arabidopsis* roots. Our results show that: (i) MED16

modulates root system configuration in response to Pi scarcity; (ii) the expression of 85% low-Pi responsive genes is affected in *med16-2*; (iii) MED16 regulates the expression of large subset of STOP1 target genes that are responsive to low Pi in the root tip, possibly via protein interaction between STOP1 and MED16 under low Pi conditions; and (iv) MED16 acts upstream of CLE14 under Pi deficiency. A model is provided to update the signaling pathway that activates low Pi induced root architecture adjustments in Arabidopsis (Fig. 4e). As previously proposed (Gutiérrez-Alanís et al., 2018), when the root tip perceives a soil region deprived of Pi a number of genes are induced, which orchestrate the so-called low Pi rescue strategy, aimed at improving Pi uptake and mobilization, but at the same time leads to Fe<sup>3+</sup> accumulation in the root growth zones. At the rhizosphere and/or root apoplast, Fe<sup>3+</sup> forms a complex with malate, an organic acid commonly derived from the Krebs cycle, exuded by the ALMT1 transporter (Mora-Macias et al., 2017). In the model we propose that the MED complex via MED16 interacts with STOP1, and due to its predicted molecular function, it will help positioning STOP1 onto the promoter of a subset of low-Pi-responsive genes, to up-regulate their expression via the association of the co-activator complex with RNA Pol II. Subsequently, malate-Fe<sup>3+</sup> complexes accumulate in the apoplast of root tip cells, where a Fe redox cycle, with participation of LOW PHOSPHATE ROOT 1 and 2 (LPR1/2) ferroxidases, causes up-regulation of peroxidase genes leading to a ROS burst in root tips of Pi-deprived plants. This, in turn, via a still unknown mechanism, activates expression of CLE14. Finally, this signaling peptide is recognized by CLV2 and PEPR2 receptors to repress auxin signaling, SHORT ROOT/SCARECROW (SHR/SCR) expression and promotes quiescent center cell division (Gutiérrez-Alanís et al., 2017; 2018). Thus, MED16 concertedly integrates both phosphate and iron nutrition in plants, and links the MED roles to basic cellular programs, as CLE14 restored root growth of *med16-2* mutants in low Pi conditions and CLE14 expression in med16-2 seedlings remain minimal under low-Pi (Fig. 4b, c; Fig. S7).

A recent report (Zheng *et al.*, 2019) suggests that primary root growth inhibition in response to Pi-limiting conditions is a phenotype that results from blue-light triggered chemical reactions. Our findings about the role of *MED16* in the inhibition of primary root growth

provide additional evidence that there is a genetic basis to this response rather than it being a purely chemical phenomenon. Zheng et al. (2019) reported that the blue-light triggered chemical reactions require an induction in ALMT1 expression to occur, thus ALMT1 induction is clearly a cellular response to low Pi because ALMT1 is induced by STOP1 under low Pi conditions (Mora-Macías et al., 2017; Balzergue et al., 2017) and STOP1 is activated under these conditions either by reducing its degradation (Godon et al., 2019) or by promoting its movement from the cytoplasm to the nucleus (Balzergue et al., 2017). Nonetheless, we believe that the blue light triggered chemical reactions could influence root development by providing the yet unknown reducing potential to complete the redox cycle initiated by STOP1-ALMT1 and in which the ferroxidases LPR1/2 play a key role (Müller et al., 2015), a phenomenon that could be important for adaptation to low Pi conditions in the early stages of root system development when the root is most likely exposed to light. The fact that ALMT1 is down-regulated in med16-2 suggests that MED16 acts upstream of the Fe-dependent that lead to the inhibition of primary root growth and corroborates an essential role for the induction of malate exudation in the modification of root growth in response to low Pi availability.

Although, there are still several missing gaps to be filled in the signaling pathways that activate root system responses to Pi deprivation, our results unraveled an important step in the STOP1-mediated transcriptional activation of genes involved in root system remodeling. Whether the mediator complex assist STOP1 in binding to its cognate binding site or promotes chromatin remodeling to activate transcription remains untested. Nevertheless, the identification of MED16 as an important component that modulates the low Pi adaptive response, reveals how cells orchestrate overall transcriptional programs to specific transcriptional factors and physiological responses enabling an efficient plant nutrition.
