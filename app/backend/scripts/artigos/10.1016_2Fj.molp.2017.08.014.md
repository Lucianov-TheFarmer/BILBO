## 1 Abstract

2

3 Temperature influences the distribution, range and phenology of plants. The key 4 transcriptional activators of the heat shock response in eukaryotes, the heat shock factors (HSFs), have undergone a large-scale gene amplification in plants. While 5 6 HSFs are central in heat stress responses, their role in ambient temperature changes is less well understood. We show here that the warm ambient 7 8 temperature transcriptome is dependent upon the HSFA1 clade of Arabidopsis 9 HSFs, which cause a rapid and dynamic eviction of H2A.Z-nucleosomes at target genes. A transcriptional cascade results in the activation of multiple downstream 10 11 stress responsive transcription factors, triggering large-scale changes to the 12 transcriptome in response to elevated temperature. H2A.Z-nucleosomes are 13 enriched at temperature responsive genes at non-inducible temperature, and thus 14 likely confer inducibility of gene expression and higher responsive dynamics. We 15 propose that the antagonistic effects of H2A.Z and HSF1 provide a mechanism to 16 activate gene expression rapidly and precisely in response to temperature, while 17 preventing leaky transcription in the absence of an activation signal. 18

## 1 Introduction

2 Warm temperature is an important cue for plants, which must be able to adapt to their environment (Wigge, 2013). In Arabidopsis, many of the growth and 3 4 developmental responses to temperature are mediated by the bHLH transcription factor PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR4 (Koini et al., 2009; Kumar et 5 6 al., 2012), which is controlled by the thermosensor phyB (Jung et al., 2016; Legris et al., 2016). The phyB temperature perception mechanism relies on dark 7 8 reversion, and consequently expression of the warm temperature transcriptome 9 controlling development occurs at night (Jung et al., 2016). Plants are most likely to encounter higher temperatures in sunlight however, suggesting an additional 10 11 mechanism to sense temperature during the day. Consistent with this, genes 12 involved in response to heat stress are predominantly expressed in the light (Jung 13 et al., 2016).

14

15 Thermal stress is a major threat to the cell, causing protein denaturation and 16 compromising membrane integrity. Rising global temperature is estimated to 17 reduce crop yields by 2.5 to 16% for every additional 1°C of warming during hot 18 summers (Battisti and Naylor, 2009). It is therefore important to understand the 19 pathways and mechanisms by which warm temperature influences the cell. Work 20 in yeast, Drosophila, plants and mammalian cells has led to a widely established 21 model that genes encoding heat shock proteins (HSPs) are transcriptionally 22 induced by heat shock factor (HSF)-class transcription factors (TFs) upon 23 activation by heat stress (Jacob et al., 2017; Shivaswamy and Iyer, 2008; Zobeck 24 et al., 2010). A genetic screen in plants revealed that in addition to HSFs (Miozzo et al., 2015), the chromatin state also influences the expression of warm 25 temperature induced genes (Kumar and Wigge, 2010). Mutants deficient in the 26 27 incorporation of H2A.Z-nucleosomes show a higher HSP70 expression and many 28 phenotypes associated with warm temperature growth. Moreover it was shown 29 that H2A.Z-nucleosome occupancy decreases in response to temperature at 30 HSP70 and a few other genes (Kumar and Wigge, 2010), and heat stress results 31 in a global increase in chromatin accessibility at responsive loci (Sullivan et al., 32 2014). It is unclear however, whether these dynamics of H2A.Z-nucleosomes 33 reflect a passive process by which the stability of H2A.Z-nucleosomes responds

directly to temperature, or result from increased transcriptional responsiveness of
 the loci, or a combination of these mechanisms.

3

The transcriptional activation of heat stress genes in plants is potentially 4 5 complicated by the high degree of gene duplication. While yeast has a single HSF, Arabidopsis for example has 21 HSF family members (von Koskull-D<sup>^</sup>ring et al., 6 7 2007). One clade in particular, the HSFA1 group, appears to be important for the early responses to heat stress (Yoshida et al., 2011). Activation of the HSF 8 9 pathway in Arabidopsis is complex, with multiple downstream TFs being involved 10 (Schramm et al., 2008). While the role of HSFA1 TFs in the response to heat 11 stress is well established, it is still not clear if these factors are involved in the transcriptional response to warm temperature in the ambient range. While H2A.Z-12 13 nucleosomes have been implicated in regulating the warm temperature 14 transcriptome, how they interact with TFs and other *cis*-acting factors is not clear.

15

16 In this study, we use genome-wide datasets to investigate the dynamics of both 17 nucleosome and TF behavior to determine their contributions to the temperature 18 transcriptome. We find that the day-time warm ambient temperature transcriptome 19 is dependent on HSFA1 TFs and these are rapidly and robustly recruited to the 20 promoters of responsive genes, activating their transcription. Moreover, we show 21 that HSFA1a TFs are essential for H2A.Z eviction occurring in response to warm 22 temperature at these genes. Activation of downstream TFs by the HSFA1-class 23 results in a transcriptional cascade that can account for a large proportion of the 24 day-time warm temperature transcriptome. Genes responding rapidly to warmer 25 temperature display distinctive promoter architecture of heat shock elements 26 (HSEs) and nucleosome positions. We propose that both HSFA1 class TFs and 27 H2A.Z-nucleosomes enables a dynamic transcriptional response system to be 28 activated upon passing a threshold temperature.

#### 3 A warm temperature transcriptome defined by the HSFA1 TFs

4 To analyze the day-time temperature response, we measured the temperature 5 transcriptomes of plants shifted 1 hour after dawn from 17 to 27°C for 0.25, 1 and 6 4 h. (Supplemental Figure 1A). A total of 1,035 transcripts show significant changes in these conditions (see Methods), and we refer to these as the 7 8 "temperature responsive" transcripts (Supplemental Figure 1B). Hierarchical 9 clustering of these temperature responsive genes reveals six major patterns of 10 transcriptional response to warmer temperature (Figure 1A, Supplementary Figure 11 2A, Supplemental Dataset 1).

12

13 **Cluster 1** (350 genes, grev sidebar in Figure 1A) contains the genes that are 14 predominantly repressed over time, and is enriched in genes involved in metabolic 15 processes (based on a Gene Ontology, GO, enrichment analysis) (Eden et al., 16 2009), which are likely to be transcribed at a lower rate under stresses (more 17 details on GO term analyses are in Supplemental Table 1). Cluster 2 (193 genes, blue) shows partial up-regulation after 1 and 4 h at 27°C, but the patterns are 18 19 similar to the 17°C control samples at these time-points, suggesting the 20 transcriptional dynamics may be endogenous to the experimental setup, and/or 21 related to the circadian rhythm. The transcriptional activation of cluster 2 genes 22 appears to be more rapid at 27°C than 17°C after 1 h (Supplemental Figure 2B). 23 **Cluster 3** (110 genes, cyan) demonstrates partial transcriptional repression but is 24 not significantly enriched for any functional GO term. **Cluster 4** (130 genes, green) 25 demonstrates rapid and transient transcriptional activation at both  $17^{\circ}$  and  $27^{\circ}$ , followed by repression after 4 h, and is enriched in genes that are involved in 26 27 stress and defence responses. Both transcriptional activation and repression 28 seems to be faster at 27°C for the genes in this cluster (Supplemental Figure 2B). 29 **Cluster 5** (105 genes, pink) is the smallest cluster with a slight increase in 30 transcriptional level at 27°C, but not enriched in a GO term. Cluster 6 (147 genes, 31 red) genes are termed "rapidly temperature responsive", as they show maximal expression within 15 min of 27°C treatment. The peak of expression at 15 min is 32 33 transient, as the expression of these genes returns to near basal levels after 4 h.

These genes are highly enriched in biological process GO terms of heat and light 1 2 responses (Supplemental Table 1), and include genes involved in the response to 3 heat stress such as HSP70 and other HSPs, HSFA7A and DREB2A (Supplemental Dataset 1). The role of higher temperature in activating these 4 5 genes is particularly clear when gene expression at 27°C is normalized to that at 17°C (Supplementary Figure 2B). To confirm these results are a consequence of 6 7 rapid transcriptional activation, we investigated RNA Pol II occupancy by ChIPseq. We observe a corresponding increase in the relative amount of Pol II in the 8 9 gene bodies of the cluster 6 genes in response to ambient temperature increase, 10 which is absent in the control set of all genes (Figure 1B).

11

The Arabidopsis genome encodes 21 HSFs and members of the HSFA1 class are 12 13 of particular importance in the early responses to heat since the hsfa1abde quadruple (QK) mutant is more sensitive to mild heat stress (Liu et al., 2011; 14 15 Yoshida et al., 2011). We therefore investigated whether the QK mutant alters the warm temperature transcriptome. Consistent with a major role for this HSF clade, 16 17 there is a global reduction in temperature responsiveness across all clusters in our 18 experiment (Figure 1C and E, Supplemental Figure 2C), particularly clearly evident after 15 min of shifting to 27°C. Clusters 2, 4 and 6 are most strongly perturbed. 19 20 with many transcripts showing little or no temperature responsiveness. 21 Interestingly, more than half the genes in cluster 6 (83/147 ~56%) lose 22 temperature responsiveness (same criteria used to extract temperature responsive transcript, see Methods) after 15 min temperature shift in the QK mutant, as 23 24 compared to its corresponding TK wild-type, which has the same background as 25 QK but with HSFA1ABDE activity (Figure 1D). Lower proportions of genes in 26 clusters 2 (59/193 ~31%) and 4 (21/130 ~16%) become unresponsive to 27 temperature in QK.

## 1 HSFA1a binds to rapidly responsive genes and initiates a

#### 2 transcriptional cascade in response to warm temperature

To determine if the effect of HSFA1a, a representative of the HSFA1 family TFs. 3 on the warm ambient temperature transcriptome is direct, we performed ChIP-seq 4 5 of HSFA1a on seedlings shifted from 17 to 27°C for 15 min, and in controls kept at 17°C (Supplemental Figure 1A). We identified 1,371 genes that are directly bound 6 7 by HSFA1a within 15 min of 27°C (Supplemental Dataset 2). Specifically, ~46% of 8 cluster 6 genes are directly bound by HSFA1a, and in clusters 1-5, 17% of genes 9 are bound by HSFA1a, marked in red on the second sidebar of Figure 1A (Fisher's exact P-value of the overlaps < 2.2e-16 for both groups) (Supplemental Figure 10 11 3A). By comparison, HSFA1a binds to only ~5% of the genes in Arabidopsis. 12 Consistent with HSFA1a binding the promoters of temperature responsive genes, 13 we observe strong enrichment for predicted heat shock elements (HSEs) in 14 clusters 2 and 6 (Supplemental Table 2). For cluster 6 genes we found predicted HSEs within 44/84 (~52%) and 17/39 (~45%) of HSFA1a ChIP-seq peaks at 27 15 16 and 17°C, respectively.

17

While HSFA1a targets are significantly enriched in clusters 1, 2, 4 and 6 (Table 1), 18 19 HSFA1a signal increases specifically for cluster 6 genes after 15 min of shift from 17°C to 27°C, (Figure 2A, Wilcoxon test P-value < 2.2e-16 when comparing cluster 20 21 6 genes to genes in clusters 1-5). For rapidly temperature responsive genes (cluster 6), there is significant signal for HSFA1a at the non-inductive temperature 22 23 of 17°C at HSEs and over the gene body, and this markedly increases at 27°C 24 (Figure 2B). Increase in HSFA1a occupancy upon temperature increase can be observed in most of cluster 6 genes (Figure 2C, red dots represent predicted 25 26 HSEs). This increase in HSFA1a occupancy with temperature is not seen in other 27 clusters, including cluster 2, which is also enriched for predicted HSEs 28 (Supplemental Figure 3B-E).

Consistent with the central role of HSFA1a in shaping the rapid transcriptional responses to warm temperature, there is a positive correlation between changes in transcription and HSFA1a binding occupancy in response to shifting at 27°C only for cluster 6 genes (P-value from linear model fitting = 1.5e-11, Figure 2D), in line

1 with a previous study showing the major role of HSFA1a in regulating the 2 response to heat stress (Liu et al., 2011). Within cluster 6, ~56% (83/147) of the 3 genes become unresponsive to temperature in the QK mutant. Interestingly, we observe direct binding of HSFA1a to ~58% of these (48/83, Fisher's exact test P-4 5 value < 2.2e-16). HSFA1a binding also occurs at  $\sim$ 32% (35/111, P-value =2.6e-15) 6 of the other temperature-responsive genes that lose responsiveness in the 7 hsfa1abde background in clusters 1-5 (Figure 2E). Despite HSEs being found in 8 multiple clusters, the rapid increase in transcript abundance of the cluster 6 genes, 9 accompanied by increased HSFA1a occupancy, is specific for this group of genes. 10 Taken together, these results indicate that the presence of an HSE alone is 11 insufficient to predict rapid gene induction by temperature. We note that the HSFA1a target genes identified here might be under-estimated, as the ChIP was 12 13 performed using a HSFA1a-tagged line in a wild-type background, and thus might 14 be subjected to interference by the native HSFA1a that could potentially decrease 15 ChIP signal. We nonetheless observe a strong overlap of ~43% (83/194) between 16 identified HSFA1a targets in all temperature-responsive genes (cluster 1-6), and 17 the genes becoming unresponsive to temperature in the QK mutant.

19 While HSFA1a only binds directly to ~17% of the temperature responsive genes 20 outside of cluster 6, we observe that the expression of up to 32% of these genes is perturbed in *hsfa1abde* (Figure 2E). This could be because some targets require 21 22 HSFA1 b, d or e and cannot be bound by HSFA1a. We think this is unlikely 23 however, since the HSFA1 clade has a highly conserved DNA binding domain and 24 the HSFA1A knockout mutants are highly redundant (Liu et al., 2011; Yoshida et 25 al., 2011). Furthermore, many of the temperature responsive genes outside of 26 cluster 6 do not have a clearly identifiable HSE in their promoters. As we show, 27 several of the direct targets of HSFA1a are themselves TFs, it is plausible that 28 these are part of a transcriptional cascade that activates a broader range of 29 transcriptional targets. We identified 9 TFs in cluster 6 that are directly bound by 30 HSFA1a, and have previously been implicated in transcriptional responses. 31 particularly to stresses. These HSFA1a targets are two members of the HFSB2 32 family (HSFB2A and HFSB2B), two members of the HSFA7 family (HSFA7A and 33 HSFA7B), two closely related myb homeodomain TFs (EARLY PHYTOCHROME 34 RESPONSE1 (EPR1) and At3g10113), and genes encoding the stress responsive

1 TFs bZIP28, RAP2.4 and DREB2A. We were able to identify potential targets for 6 2 of these TFs in all temperature responsive clusters (HSFB2A, HFSB2B, EPR1, 3 At3g10113, bZIP28 and DREB2A), using data generated by DNA affinity purification coupled with sequencing (DAP-seq) (O'Malley et al., 2016). Our 4 5 analysis reveals a cascade by which these intermediate TFs are able to transmit 6 the warm temperature signal to genes in other clusters. This cascade can 7 therefore account for a large proportion of the temperature transcriptome, and likely contributes to the temporal variation in the responses we see (Figure 2F, 8 9 Supplemental Table 3).

# H2A.Z-nucleosomes signal transiently decreases at rapidly responsive genes at 27°C

13 As H2A.Z-nucleosomes at the +1 nucleosome of HSP70 can be evicted in 14 response to warm temperature (Kumar and Wigge, 2010) we used ChIP-seq of 15 FLAG tagged HTA11, a broadly expressed H2A.Z gene, to determine if this is a 16 global response of temperature responsive genes. We observe that cluster 6 17 genes show a rapid loss of HTA11 ChIP signal at the transcriptional start site 18 (TSS) in response to 27°C (Figure 3A). We were able to confirm this dynamic behavior for the occupancy of HTA9, a separate H2A.Z protein, using anti-HTA9 19 antibodies (Yelagandula et al., 2014), at HSP70 (Figure 6B and Supplemental 20 21 Figure 8B), indicating that HTA11 ChIP signal is representative of H2A.Z-22 nucleosome occupancy for temperature responsive genes. The decrease of 23 HTA11 ChIP signal on temperature responsive promoters is dynamic, since we 24 observe signal returning after 1 h, and by 4 h the HTA11 ChIP signal is comparable to the starting levels at 17°C. This depletion of H2A.Z-nucleosome 25 26 occupancy is not confined to the +1 nucleosome, but also occurs in the gene body and the region surrounding the transcriptional termination site (TTS) (Figure 3B, 27 28 Supplemental Figure 4A). We also sought to determine whether temperature 29 responsiveness is a general property of H2A.Z-nucleosomes or whether this 30 occurs only at specific loci. Interestingly, we see that H2A.Z-nucleosome eviction 31 is only clearly apparent at cluster 6, and not at the other clusters (Figure 3C, 32 Wilcoxon test P-value < 2.2e-16 when comparing cluster 6 genes to genes in 33 clusters 1-5, and Supplemental Figure 4D). We also observed a strong negative

correlation between transcription and HTA11 occupancy in response to shifting to
27°C for 15 min (Figure 3D and Supplemental Figure 4B). This negative
correlation is specific to cluster 6, as it is not observed elsewhere, even in clusters
2 and 4 (Supplemental Figure 5) where partial increases of transcription levels are
observed, together with occurrence of predicted HSEs and HSFA1a binding.

# H2A.Z loss at temperature-responsive genes is associated with increased chromatin accessibility

9 A key question is the extent to which H2A.Z-nucleosome eviction is required for 10 transcriptional activation or a consequence of it. The occupancy of HTA11 at 11 cluster 6 genes (solid lines, Figure 3E and Supplemental Figure 4C) is markedly 12 higher than that of the genome-wide average at 17°C (dotted lines), but within 15 13 min at 27°C this drops below the average. Again, this high baseline HTA11 ChIP signal at 17°C for the +1 nucleosome is not observed in clusters 2 and 4 14 15 (Supplemental Figure 5C-F). This trend is also apparent within cluster 6, as we 16 can identify two subgroups based on HTA11 dynamics: 6A, which shows robust 17 decreases in HTA11 ChIP signal, and 6B, where the change in HTA11 is less 18 clear (Figure 3F). Interestingly, genes in 6A are also characterized by higher baseline HTA11 ChIP signal at 17°C compared to both the genome average and 19 20 subgroup 6B (Figure 3G). While transcripts in both 6A and 6B are activated within 21 15 min at 27°C, 6A genes show a greater dynamic range with little or no 22 expression at 17°C compared to 6B genes that show moderate expression at this 23 temperature (Figure 3H). These observations suggest that H2A.Z-nucleosomes 24 may act to enhance the transcriptional dynamic range by keeping genes 25 transcriptionally repressed under non-inductive conditions by restricting promoter 26 and gene accessibility.

27

We also assessed the influence of H2A.Z-nucleosomes on the temperature transcriptome by comparing transcriptional responses of WT plants shifted to 27°C for 15 min, with *arp6-1*, which is deficient in H2A.Z incorporation. Interestingly, transcriptional changes due to temperature shift and in *arp6-1* appear to be more correlated in cluster 6, as compared to genome-wide (Pearson correlations, 0.24 for cluster 6 versus 0.12 genome-wide, Supplemental Figure 5G), and to any other

cluster (Pearson correlations of 0.004, -0.01, -0.04, 0.05 and -0.06 for clusters 1 to
 5, respectively). This further supports a model where H2A.Z-nucleosomes create a
 local chromatin environment refractory to transcription at 17°C for genes in cluster
 6, which is diminished when shifted to 27°C or in *arp6-1*.

6 To measure chromatin accessibility, we investigated the accessibility of chromatin 7 to micrococcal nuclease (MNase) genome-wide using sequencing (MNase-seg) on seedlings subjected to the same temperature shift regime as described above. 8 9 Since nucleosomes protect DNA from MNase cleavage, this provides a robust 10 assay of nucleosome accessibility. Genes within cluster 6 are enriched for MNase-11 protected sequences at 17°C, but these become accessible after shifting to 27°C (Supplemental Figure 6A). This higher protection of the +1 nucleosome at 17°C is 12 13 less distinct for genes in clusters 2 or 4 (Supplemental Figure 6B). Loss of HTA11 ChIP signal correlates with loss of MNase-seq signals at the +1 nucleosome 14 position for cluster 6 genes (Supplemental Figure 6C, red dots), when shifted to 15 27°C. This suggests that the genomic DNA at these genes becomes more 16 17 accessible when H2A.Z is evicted compared with the other temperature 18 responsive genes (Supplemental Figure 6C, black dots). To investigate whether 19 the changes in DNA accessibility are specific to H2A.Z-containing nucleosomes, or 20 whether they reflect broader changes in nucleosome dynamics, we investigated 21 how other histones changed under the same temperature shift. H3 occupancy is 22 indistinguishable between cluster 6 genes and the rest of the genome at 17°C and 23 27°C (Supplemental Figure 6D-E) or genes in clusters 2 and 4 (Supplemental 24 Figure 6F). This suggests that the decrease in H2A.Z signal in response to heat 25 does not reflect a general depletion of nucleosomes in response to greater 26 transcription of these loci, but rather the specific exchange of H2A.Z-nucleosomes 27 for H2A.

# 29 Promoter architecture of genes responding rapidly to 30 temperature may facilitate transcriptional activation

We have seen that HSFA1a binds to and activates transcription of a large proportion of the cluster 6 genes, and consistent with this we observe 130 instances of predicted HSEs among these genes, statistically greater than

1 expected by chance (Figure 4A, Fisher's exact test P-value < 2.2e-16). We do not 2 observe this degree of enrichment for the other clusters, highlighting the 3 importance of the HSF class of TF in mediating this response. Furthermore, in the promoters of cluster 6 genes, HSEs appear to be strongly positioned within 200 bp 4 5 upstream of the TSS, adjacent to the +1 nucleosome that exhibits loss of HTA11 6 signal after the temperature shift (Figure 4B). This is also true for predicted HSEs 7 in cluster 6 genes with bound HSFA1a (ChIP-filtered HSEs), based on our ChIPseq experiments. ## 23 The transcriptional response of the warm temperature genes is

#### 24 triggered by absolute temperature

25 The transcriptional changes we observe could reflect a response to a relative 26 change in temperature or be triggered by crossing an absolute temperature 27 threshold. To distinguish between these possibilities. we generated 28 transcriptomes, in conjunction with HTA11 and HSFA1a ChIP-seq datasets, for 29 seedlings shifted from 17 to 22°C, from 22 to 27°C, as well as from 17 to 37°C. 30 While shifting seedlings from 22 to 27°C elicits a robust induction of the warm 31 temperature response (Figure 5A), comparable to the previous shift from 17 to 32 27°C, only a very mild change in gene expression is seen for the 17 to 22°C shift 33 (Supplemental Figure 7A). This indicates that absolute temperature plays a pivotal

1 role in determining the transcriptional response, since both these shifts were of the 2 same magnitude (5°C change), but had very different transcriptional outcomes. 3 These results are supported by the HTA11 occupancy behavior, where shifting to 27°C had a strong effect, but 22°C did not (Supplemental Figure 7A-B). 4 5 Furthermore, increasing the temperature to 37°C causes a strong induction of the cluster 6 genes (Figure 5B and Supplementary Figure 7C), concomitant with a 6 7 strong increase in HSFA1a signal at the responsive genes (Figure 5C), and a greater loss of HTA11 and MNase signals that are maintained over 4 h (Figure 5D 8 9 and Supplemental Figure 7D-E). Interestingly, the transcriptional induction of HSP70 in plants shifted from 17°C to 27°C was observed only during the day but 10 11 not in the dark, whereas HSP70 induction occurs in both light and dark when shifted to 37°C (Supplemental Figure 7F). Taken together, these results indicate 12 that there is a key threshold temperature between 22°C and 27°C that must be 13 passed for the induction of cluster 6 genes during the day, and once this is 14 activated, further increasing temperature results in a quantitative increase in gene 15 16 activation.

# HSFA1a binding at warm temperature is necessary to promote H2A.Z loss and transcriptional activation

20 To understand the relationship between HTA11, gene expression and HSFA1a 21 more clearly, we asked if HTA11 and/or HSFA1a occupancy are good predictors 22 of transcriptional level among the cluster 6 genes. We see a strong positive 23 correlation between transcription and HSFA1a occupancy (P-value = 1.5e-11 for 24 linear model fitting), and a negative relationship between transcription and HTA11 signal (Supplemental Table 4) (P-value = 1.1e-9), indicating that both HTA11 and 25 26 HSFA1a can predict the transcriptional response to 27°C to a certain extent. 27 Interestingly, combining HSFA1a and HTA11 occupancies provides a prediction of 28 gene expression upon shifting from 17°C to 27°C with higher confidence, based on 29 a linear model fitted using transcriptional rate as a response (P-value = 8.5e-15, 30 Figure 6A, Supplementary Table 4). Within cluster 6, the sub-class 6A genes (red 31 dots) demonstrate a greater reduction of H2A.Z signal, gain of HSFA1a, and 32 transcriptional induction, as compared to the sub-class 6B (orange). Such a 33 correlation is not observed in randomly selected non-temperature-responsive

genes (P-value = 0.38, Supplemental Figure 8A, Supplemental Table 4). These
results show that both HSFA1 class TFs and H2A.Z-nucleosomes are necessary
for rapid transcriptional responses to warmer temperatures, but how they influence
each other is not clear.

#### 2 A dynamic transcriptional cascade protects the cell from warm temperatures

We first observe a rapid induction of a core set of transcripts highly enriched for 9 genes encoding cellular protective proteins such as chaperones within 15 min shift 10 from 17 to 27°C. The activation of these genes is transient as they show a 11 reduction of transcriptional levels after 1 h, and return to the baseline by 4 h. The warm temperature cellular protection pathway is therefore under tight 12 13 transcriptional control and is rapidly attenuated when seedlings are maintained at 14 warm temperatures, consistent with previous observations for heat stress (Ohama 15 et al., 2016). Since chaperones function as ATPases, it may be important to control their expression levels for energy homeostasis within the cell. In 16 17 Drosophila, maintaining flies at higher ambient temperature causes a depletion of 18 body fat stores and a decline in fitness (Klepsatel et al., 2016). HSFA1a directly 19 activates multiple activating TFs in cluster 6 genes, as well as the transcriptional 20 repressors HSFB2A and HSFB2. These TFs may act in a negative feedback loop 21 to fine tune the response to warm temperature, and genes up-regulated in hsfb1-22 1/hsfb2b (Ikeda et al., 2011) are highly enriched in cluster 6 (Fisher's exact test pvalue < 2.2e-16, Supplemental Table 3). This indicates that HSFA1a and 23 24 HSFB2A/B may act in an incoherent feed-forward loop, a common regulatory motif in biology (Milo et al., 2002). 25

26

In addition to HSFB2A and HSFB2B, we show that HSFA1a also directly regulates multiple key TFs involved in stress response signaling, in response to warm temperature: HSFA7A, DREB2A, RAP2.4, EPR1 and BZIP28 (Gao et al., 2008; Larkindale and Vierling, 2008; Lin et al., 2008; Ohama et al., 2017; Sakuma et al., 2006; Yoshida et al., 2011) (Figure 2F). This is well in line with earlier studies showing that the HSFA1 family are key regulators of genes involving in responses to heat as well as other abiotic stresses such as oxidative, osmotic and salt

stresses (Liu et al., 2011). Strikingly, these TFs that are HSFA1 targets bind to the 1 2 promoters of multiple genes that are differentially expressed upon the shift to 3 27°C. It therefore appears that there is a transcriptional cascade from HSFA1a via these additional TFs, and this can partly account for differentially transcribed 4 genes after shifting to 27°C, as previously described for heat stress (Ohama et al., 5 6 2016). Consistent with this model, *hsfa1abde* shows a greatly attenuated 7 transcriptional response to 27°C, even for genes that are responsive to temperature but not bound by HSFA1a directly. This transcriptional cascade can 8 9 help account for the diverse transcriptional responses to day-time warm 10 temperature of genes in cluster 1-5 (Figure 1A) with both activation as well as 11 repression of genes, occurring over different timescales. To this end, the direct control of a key set of TFs, including HSFB2A, HSFB2A, EPR1, At3g10113 and 12 13 bZIP28 enables a complex transcriptional outcome in response to a single rapid stimulus. This role of HSFA1 TFs as master regulators of key TFs coordinating 14 15 responses to heat stress and drought resembles the sequential waves of transcriptional activation observed in responding to the environment (Murray et al., 16 17 2004) and during cell differentiation (Boyd et al., 2014).

## 19 The heat protection response has an activation threshold and increases20 quantitatively above that

21 Temperature has diverse influences upon plant growth, development and survival. 22 An open question is the extent to which these response pathways share common 23 or separate initial temperature sensing events and downstream transcriptional 24 cascades. In this study we have focused on the response to 27°C during the day, 25 typically not regarded as a heat stress (Balasubramanian et al., 2006). 26 Interestingly however, much of the transcriptomic response appears to be 27 conserved with higher temperature heat stress responses, suggesting that even 27°C is sufficient to activate these pathways. The key regulators we observe 28 (HSFA1, DREB2A, HSFA7, RAP2.4, BZIP28, MBF1C and HSFB2) have also 29 30 previously been shown to be central in high temperature stress responses (Gao et 31 al., 2008; Ikeda et al., 2011; Larkindale and Vierling, 2008; Lin et al., 2008; Liu et 32 al., 2013; Schramm et al., 2008). The HSFA1 class TFs appear to activate both 33 heat stress and warm ambient temperature pathways during the day, in agreement 34 with the previous observation that *hsfa1abde* is hypersensitive to prolonged

exposure to 27°C (Liu and Charng, 2013). In contrast to the heat stress pathway, growth and developmental responses to warm temperature, mediated via the evening complex (Box et al., 2015; Mizuno et al., 2014; Raschke et al., 2015) or thermosensory phytochromes (Jung et al., 2016; Legris et al., 2016), occur predominantly at night and control different target genes (Ezer et al., 2017; Jung et al., 2016).

7

8 Our results indicate that an absolute temperature, rather than relative temperature 9 is important for cluster 6 genes activation and suggest the presence of a threshold 10 between 22 and 27°C for transcriptional induction of HSFA1a targets. Further 11 experiments would be required to define the exact threshold temperature for cluster 6 genes activation, and this may vary from gene to gene depending on the 12 13 promoter architecture. Above this activation threshold temperature, there is a quantitative increase in transcriptional induction with temperature, as seen in 14 15 further activation of temperature responsive genes in plants shifted to 37°C. Since 16 HSFA1a is expressed constitutively in the plant, the temperature perception event 17 in this pathway may occur at the level of the HSFA1 protein, consistent with the 18 extensive post-translational modifications of HSFs in response to heat stress in 19 plants (Ohama et al., 2016). HSFA1a binding signal is detectable by ChIP at a 20 number of temperature rapid-response genes at 17℃ (Figure 2B and 21 Supplemental Figure 3D), suggesting that the TF may maintain genes in a poised 22 state, ready for activation in response to warm temperature via the recruitment of 23 additional HSFs and/or other components of transcriptional machinery complexes, 24 a scenario which has been previously observed in yeast (Shivaswamy and lyer, 25 2008; Zanton and Pugh, 2006).

## A combinatorial role for H2A.Z and HSF1 to mediate temperature responsive gene expression

The day-time warm temperature transcriptome is important for cellular survival and is able to be rapidly activated and shutdown. While HSFA1 TFs are essential for this response, many genes with HSEs in their promoters are not rapidly activated by temperature, indicating additional factors might be required to confer rapid and robust temperature responsive gene expression. Nucleosome architecture at promoters can contribute to the transcriptional responsiveness of gene expression

(Lam et al., 2008; Weber et al., 2014), and in this study we observe a strong 1 2 tendency for temperature responsive promoters to have well-positioned H2A.Z-3 nucleosomes just downstream of HSEs (Figure 4B). Furthermore, we observe a specific temperature dependent depletion of H2A.Z-nucleosome signal at these 4 5 loci at higher temperature. This drop is also associated with an increase in nucleosome accessibility as shown by a reduction of signal in the MNase-seq. 6 7 However, this may not simply reflect a removal of nucleosomes by the 8 transcriptional machinery upon the activation of transcription, because a 9 concomitant drop in H3 is not observed. This is in agreement with a previous 10 observation of an increase in nucleosome accessibility without reduction in nucleosome occupancy during rapid transcriptional activation (Mueller et al., 11 2017). This specific depletion of H2A.Z-nucleosomes in response to temperature 12 13 requires HSFA1a activity, and it does not occur on chromatin in vitro or on reconstituted nucleosomes. Further experiments on H2A.Z occupancy changes in 14 response to temperature using drugs blocking transcription, may reveal if H2A.Z 15 16 eviction is indeed mediated by HSFA1a, or is a consequence of transcriptional 17 activation. Here, we also observe strong H2A.Z eviction at genes transcriptionally 18 activated in response to warm temperature, whereas a previous study suggested that H2A.Z eviction might be independent of expression changes (Kumar and 19 20 Wigge, 2010). This discrepancy could potentially have arisen from differences in temperature shift regimes (shifting from 12°C to 27°C for 2 or 24 h, as opposed to 21 22 17℃ to 27℃ for 15 min in day-time), and a more re stricted set of genes being 23 analyzed by ChIP-gPCR (6 genes that showed a decreased or flat transcriptional 24 response to temperature showed some level of H2A.Z decline by ChIP-gPCR).

26 Being refractory to transcription in vitro (Park et al., 2004; Thakar et al., 2010), 27 H2A.Z-nucleosomes are well suited for creating inducible transcriptional switches 28 (Figure 6C). At non-permissive conditions, loci can be maintained in a fully 29 repressed state, but upon H2A.Z-nucleosome depletion, loci are fully accessible 30 for rapid expression. In this way, the specific replacement of the H2A.Z-H2B dimer 31 from nucleosomes may provide a particularly rapid and reversible transcriptional activation switch, and may also account for the paradoxical observation that 32 33 H2A.Z-nucleosomes anti-correlate with H3-H4 turnover (Weber et al., 2014). This 34 depletion of H2A.Z at transcriptionally activated genes in response to elevated

temperature is in agreement with a recent study demonstrating depletion of H2A.Z at genes transcriptionally activated in response to drought stress and an overrepresentation of genes affected by drought stress among the genes misregulated in a H2A.Z-depleted mutant (Sura et al., 2017). This together with our results suggest a repressive role of H2A.Z at genes responding to different environmental stresses, possibly by creating a closed chromatin environment refractory to transcription.

26 C) A model of transcriptional regulation of rapidly temperature responsive 27 genes (cluster 6). At a low ambient growth temperature (17°C), HSFA1a is 28 detectable at the promoters of the rapidly temperature responsive genes at a low 29 level and at a small fraction of its target genes, insufficient to trigger transcriptional 30 activation in Col-0 WT. Tightly bound H2A.Z-nucleosome, especially at the +1 31 position of these genes, may play a role to help restrict transcriptional activation at 32 lower temperatures. At elevated ambient temperature  $(27^{\circ})$ , HSF1 is transiently 33 further recruited to HSEs around the TSS as well as to the gene bodies of the 34 targets, an event concomitant with depletion of H2A.Z from these regions and this

is accompanied by robust transcriptional activation. The strong correlation between transcriptional activation, H2A.Z eviction, and HSF1 binding is also observed to a greater extent and for a longer period of time, when plants are subjected to heat stress (37°C).
