#### **1. Introduction**

Rice (*Oryza sativa* L.) is one of the most important staple cereal crops consumed by more than half the population in the world, and utilization of rice heterosis is very important for improving rice yield, which, in turn, supports international food security [\[1\]](#page-8-0). Heterosis or hybrid vigor refers to the biological phenomenon wherein F1 hybrids, compared to any of their parents, exhibit superior performance [\[2](#page-8-0)–4], such as greater biomass, faster growth speed, and stronger stress tolerance.

Through genomics approaches, some heterosis-related loci, such as *DTH8/Ghd8/LHD1* and *OsHD3A*, have been identified in rice to increase yield [[5](#page-9-0)], and the contribution of *DTH8/Ghd8/LHD1* genes to heterosis has been further analyzed using multiple omics data [\[4\]](#page-9-0). However, more heterosis-involved genes must be further explored on a genomic scale. In the last decade, significant progress has been made in depicting the mechanism of heterosis development using omics technologies [\[3,5](#page-9-0)–9]. Analysis of gene expression on a genomic scale between hybrids and their parents has been performed in different tissues and during different developmental stages using transcriptomic approaches [[10,11\]](#page-9-0). According to the evidence obtained from microarray data, both additive

and dominant effects contributed to heterosis in *Arabidopsis thaliana* [[6](#page-9-0)], and additive or non-additive expression of genes involved in the photosynthesis and carbon-fixation pathways might be related to hybrid vigor in rice [[7,8\]](#page-9-0). Recently, transcriptomic data analysis of coffee revealed that an altered circadian clock coupled with higher photosynthesis efficiency resulted in a better agronomic performance by three time points [\[9\]](#page-9-0).

At an epigenetic level, histone modification of circadian clockrelated genes can alter plant growth; Ni et al. (2009) proposed a model of the epigenetic changes in circadian-related genes that contribute to growth vigor in *Arabidopsis* hybrids and allopolyploids [[12\]](#page-9-0). It was also found that the difference in the methylation level of the promoter of the parental *CCA1* allele led to biomass heterosis in *Arabidopsis* hybrids [[13\]](#page-9-0). Ko et al. (2016) revealed that early activation of CCA1-binding targets in maize hybrids promoted photosynthesis and biomass heterosis through ChIP-seq at three time points [\[14](#page-9-0)].

Most previous studies on the molecular mechanism underlying rice heterosis have been based on single-time-point experiments [\[7,8,10,11](#page-9-0)]; some studies performed multiple-time-point experiments, but they only investigated a certain aspect of the biological processes related to

#### **2. Materials and methods**

### *2.1. Plant materials and growth conditions*

The super-hybrid rice Liang-You 2186 (LY2186) (provided by Prof. Feng Wang) with superior growth and yield vigor and its parental lines [[8](#page-9-0),[15\]](#page-9-0), sterile line SE21s and restorer line Ming-Hui 86 (MH86), were planted in a rice field under natural conditions in Beijing, China. LY2186 surpassed both of its parents in biomass and harvest index [[8](#page-9-0)]. The flowering time of LY2186 and MH86 is earlier than that of SE21s [\[15](#page-9-0)]. To ensure that all three lines were harvested during the same developmental stage, the sterile line was sowed a week earlier than the other two lines. Before sampling, the rice plants were transferred to an artificial climate chamber for recovery and adaptation under conditions of a 12-h light/dark cycle with 28/25 ◦C, respectively, and 70% relative humidity for a week. At the grain-filling stage, rice flag leaves were harvested at 3-h intervals during 2 diurnal cycles. Each sample included three biological replicates, with six to eight flag leaves per plant. All plant tissues from each sample were pooled, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 ◦C.

#### *2.2. Strand-specific RNA sequencing (ssRNA-seq) and data processing*

Total RNA was extracted from the collected plant samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). ssRNA-seq libraries were constructed using TruSeq Stranded RNA LT Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's protocol. The quality of the sequencing libraries was regulated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen). The resulting libraries were sequenced on the HiSeq 2500 System using HiSeq v4 reagents (Illumina), and paired-end reads of 125 basepair nucleotides were generated. The adaptor sequences and low-quality raw reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic [[16\]](#page-9-0).

### *2.3. Expression profiling of rice genes*

The clean sequencing reads were mapped onto the Rice Genome Annotation Release 7 [[17\]](#page-9-0) using TopHat 2 [\[18](#page-9-0)]. The gene expression levels were quantified by calculating the expected number of fragments per kilobase of transcript per millions fragments sequenced (FPKM) [\[19](#page-9-0)] using Cuffdiff 2 [[20\]](#page-9-0). A gene expressed with an FPKM value ≥0.1 in any of the 51 samples was considered an expressed gene. Genes with rhythmic expression profiles were identified as rhythmic genes (RGs) using the ARSER [[21\]](#page-9-0) and RAIN [[22\]](#page-9-0) algorithms at a false discovery rate (FDR) *<*0.05. DEGs were defined as the sum of significant differences in the gene expression levels between the hybrid and its maternal or paternal parent at each of the 17 time points. The significant differences in gene expression were calculated using edgeR [\[23](#page-9-0)] at the significance threshold values of FDR ≤0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥1.

#### *2.4. In silico analyses of RDGs*

The overlapping genes between RGs and DEGs were defined as RDGs. These intersecting RDGs were subjected to clustering by running a selforganizing map (SOM) algorithm in R using the Kohonen package [\[24](#page-9-0)]. We defined 4 xdim and 4 ydim dimensions manually and obtained 16 clusters. Further, these 16 clusters were divided into 2 groups (day and night) according to the peak time of gene expression.

*2.5. Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)* 

Total RNA was used as the template for reverse transcription and cDNA synthesis using the Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche, Switzerland). cDNA was then amplified using 2 × Taq PCR StarMix with Loading Dye (GenStar, China). qRT-PCR of diluted cDNA in 96-well plates was performed on the LightCycler 480 II instrument (Roche) using LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master (Roche), according to the manufacturer's instructions. *Ubiquitin 5* (LOC\_Os01g22490) was used as an internal reference for qRT-PCR with three biological replicates. The relative expression levels of genes in different samples were quantified by the 2<sup>−</sup> ΔΔCT method [[30\]](#page-9-0). All primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

# **3. Results**

# *3.1. Profiling and identification of RDGs*

To elucidate the complex mechanism associated with the circadian rhythm in hybrid rice, we constructed 51 ssRNA-seq libraries of the flag leaves of super-hybrid rice LY2186 at 17 consecutive time points during 2 day/night cycles. After sequencing and filtering the low quality reads, we obtained an average of 55,553,499 clean reads per library and a Q30 ratio above 90% (Supplementary Table 2). The clean reads were mapped onto the rice genome, and the percentage of total mapped clean reads in 51 libraries ranged from 79.51% to 93.84% (Supplementary Table 2).

Totally, we obtained 24,892, 26,286, and 25,569 expressed genes from SE21s, LY2186, and MH86, respectively ([Fig. 1](#page-1-0)a). Then, among the 28,190 expressed genes between the hybrid and each of its parents at all 17 time points, 3714 (13.17%) DEGs were obtained using edgeR [\[23](#page-9-0)] (Supplementary Table 3). Meanwhile, we screened out the circadianrelated RGs from the total expressed genes in each sample using the ARSER algorithm [\[21](#page-9-0)], and 14,260, 14,736, and 15,405 (RGs) were identified in the SE21s, LY2186, and MH86 samples, respectively (Supplementary Table 4). The Venn diagram indicated that 10,329 and 11,941 RGs were identified using the ARSER and RAIN algorithms [\[22](#page-9-0)], respectively [\(Fig. 1b](#page-1-0), Supplementary Table 4). The identified RGs included the following core circadian clock-related genes: *OsLHY*  (LOC\_Os08g06110), *OsGI* (LOC\_Os01g08700), and *OsTOC1*  (LOC\_Os02g40510) (Supplementary Table 5). Comparison of the qRT-PCR-obtained relative expression level of these genes and the ssRNAseq transcriptional profiles between the hybrid and its parents displayed similar patterns in the hybrid rice combination, indicating that our ssRNA-seq data were validated (Supplementary Fig. 1). In this study, 1552 RDGs, obtained from the intersection of 3714 DEGs and 12,661 RGs between the hybrid and its parents, were selected for further study ([Fig. 1c](#page-1-0)).

Clustering analysis of the 1552 RDGs was performed by running a SOM algorithm in R using the Kohonen package [\[24](#page-9-0)]. Among these RDGs, 987 were day-phased, whereas 565 were night-phased (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table 5). We further analyzed the expression patterns of these RDGs, and the results indicated obvious oscillating expression (Fig. 2b). According to the phase distribution analysis, we found that genes phased to every time point of sampling, and regardless of the genotype, the number of day-phased RDGs is more than that of nightphased RDGs (Fig. 2c).

#### *3.3. RDGs in the photosynthesis process*

According to the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, 27 day-phased RDGs were involved in the most significantly enriched category, the photosynthesis process (FDR = 2.00E-13, Supplementary Table 6). These RDGs included 14 genes that encoded antenna proteins (Supplementary Table 7). #### *3.4. Regulatory networks of RDGs*

To illuminate the relationship among the RDGs and analyze their functions, we constructed the regulatory networks of RDGs using Pathway Studio software [\[28\]](#page-9-0). In the network of day-phased RDGs, phytochrome B (OsPHYB) regulated chlorophyll *a*/b-binding proteins (OsCab1R, LOC\_Os03g39610) and Mg-chelatase H subunit (OsChlH) ([Fig. 5](#page-5-0)a), thereby establishing a link between the central transcriptional

loop of the circadian clock and the photosynthesis system. Additionally, we found that the key molecule OsLHY regulated the two-component response regulator OsRR2 [\(Fig. 5a](#page-5-0)), which is an important gene involved in ethylene signal transduction, in this network [[33](#page-9-0)]. In the network of night-phased RDGs, phytochrome A (OsPHYA) regulated an auxin-responsive protein 4 (OsIAA4) ([Fig. 5b](#page-5-0)). These results indicated a close relationship between the circadian rhythm pathway and plant hormone signal transduction.

### **4. Discussion**

Previous omics studies on heterosis mechanism have mainly focused on analysis of data at a single time point [\[6](#page-9-0)–8[,10](#page-9-0),[11\]](#page-9-0); however, most genes involved in heterosis are rhythmically expressed. Recently, some studies have attempted to explore this finding through multiple-timepoint analysis [[9,14](#page-9-0)]. In this study, we collected flag leaf tissue samples from the super-hybrid rice combination at the grain-filling stage at 17 time points during 2 day/night cycles and systematically investigated the dynamic expression patterns of RDGs between the hybrid and its parents through comparative transcriptomic analysis. The rice flag leaf plays an important role in providing photosynthates during grain filling, and the grain-filling stage is crucial for good yield [[35\]](#page-9-0). A multiple-timepoint comparative transcriptome study on flag leaves obtained during the filling stage will provide better understanding of rice heterosis and a new angle for the investigation of the mechanism underlying heterosis.

The Q30 sequencing ratio was over 90% and the genome mapping ratio was *>*80% (Supplementary Table 2), implying that the ssRNA-seq datasets were accurate and saturated for further informatics analysis. Functional enrichment analysis showed that the day-phased RDGs were significantly enriched in photosynthesis (FDR = 2.00E-13, [Fig. 3](#page-3-0)a, Supplementary Table 6), and this was consistent with our previous results [\[7,8,10\]](#page-9-0). According to the GO analysis results, 14 of the 27 genes that were enriched in photosynthesis encoded antenna proteins, accounting for 93.3% (14/15) of the total antenna protein-encoding genes in our libraries (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). These antenna proteincoding genes were highly expressed during daytime and poorly expressed at night (Supplementary Fig. 2); however, they were highly expressed in the maternal line SE21s, which showed a better photosynthesis performance than the paternal line MH86 [[8](#page-9-0)]. Additionally, the circadian patterns of these genes in super-hybrid rice LY2186 were more similar to those in SE21s. Antenna proteins are key components of the photosynthesis system and are involved in light absorption and transportation of absorbed energy [[36\]](#page-9-0). They also play a certain role in photoprotection [\[37](#page-9-0)]. The expression patterns of these genes in the super-hybrid might promote photosynthetic efficiency. Additionally, GO analysis also indicated that the day-phased RDGs, *OsPsbR1*  (LOC\_Os07g05360) and *OsPsbR2* (LOC\_Os07g05365), were significantly enriched in the photosynthetic membrane component (FDR = 0.0054, Supplementary Table 6), which is an important component of photosystem II [[31\]](#page-9-0). Moreover, we performed a functional analysis of all these genes in association with the rice mutant database [\[32](#page-9-0)], which includes

published phenotypic data. Insertion mutants of nine photosynthesisrelated genes were associated with the observed phenotypes (Supplementary Table 10). These mutants showed negative phenotypes in growth vigor and/or fertility. The day-phased RDGs were significantly enriched in these pathways, suggesting that they might be closely related to the high-light-efficiency phenotype of hybrid rice.

According to the KEGG pathway analysis showed that 2 day-phased RDGs encoding malate dehydrogenase (MDH) and fructose-1,6 bisphosphatase were involved in the carbon fixation pathway (Supplementary Fig. 3). MDH catalyzes the reaction involving the reversible conversion of oxaloacetate and malate and reduction or oxidation of the NAD pool [[38\]](#page-9-0). Fructose-1,6-phosphatase cleaves to yield fructose-6 bisphosphate in the Calvin cycle, and the resultant product branches out into the regenerative phase of the Calvin cycle and starch biosynthesis [\[39](#page-9-0)]. These results suggested that the altered expression of these genes, which are involved in the dark reaction of photosynthesis, might be related to high assimilation efficiency, i.e. high yield, of hybrid rice.

Unexpectedly, KEGG analysis revealed that both day- and nightphased RDGs as well as some DEGs related to the circadian clock were not significantly enriched in the circadian rhythm pathway. We then searched the updated KEGG database [\[40](#page-9-0)] and published data [\[41](#page-9-0)] and identified 35 circadian clock-related genes in rice. We found that all 35 genes could be detected in our ssRNA-seq libraries. Notably, 5 (14.29%) (*OsLHY*, *OsFTL2*, *OsHD3B/FTL3*, *OsELF3-2*, and *OsLUX/PCL1*) out of these 35 genes between the hybrid LY2186 and its parents were circadian DEGs (CDGs) (Supplementary Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 5). Interestingly, the ratio of CDGs to the total number of expressed circadian clock-related genes in rice (14.29%, 5/35) was significantly higher than that of RDGs to the total number of expressed genes in rice (4.5%, 1552/34,653). This considerably high proportion of CDGs suggested that the circadian rhythm played an important role in hybrid rice heterosis. We further investigated the transcript abundance of the five CDGs and found that CDG expression patterns were significantly different between the maternal and paternal parents (Supplementary Fig. 4a) and higher in the hybrid than in the parents. Four of these CDGs (except *OsLHY*, which exhibited a high maternal parent-like expression pattern) exhibited high paternal parent-like expression patterns (Supplementary Fig. 4a). As expected, the expression patterns of five non-CDGs randomly selected from the sequencing database were similar between the hybrid and its patents (Supplementary Fig. 4b).

To investigate the interactions and regulatory relationships among the RDGs, we constructed regulatory networks of the day- and nightphased RDGs. The results showed that there were multiple links among the circadian clock loop, the photosynthesis system, carbohydrate metabolism, and plant hormone signal transduction and the circadian clock-related RDGs played a critical regulatory role in the regulatory networks of both types of RDGs [\(Fig. 5\)](#page-5-0), further providing favorable evidence to support the important role of the circadian clock in heterosis development. <span id="page-8-0"></span>day-phased RDG, OsPHYB, a primary red light photoreceptor that regulates the circadian clock [[42\]](#page-9-0), regulated 2 photosynthesis-related proteins, the Mg-chelatase H subunit OsChlH and chlorophyll *a*/bbinding protein OsCab1R [\(Fig. 5a](#page-5-0)). CHlH is involved in the biosynthesis of chlorophyll, a basic unit of the plant energy system of photosynthesis [[43\]](#page-9-0), and Cab1R is the major light-harvesting antenna involved in the photosynthesis process [[44\]](#page-9-0). The rhythmic expression of *OsPHYB*, an important photoreceptor of the circadian input pathway [\[45\]](#page-9-0), could contribute to the circadian regulation of photosynthesis by affecting the expression of some related genes [[46,47\]](#page-9-0), such as *OsChlH* and *OsCab1R*  ([Fig. 5a](#page-5-0)). In contrast, circadian genes, such as *LHY/CCA1* and *ELF3*, affected *PHYB* expression [\[48](#page-9-0)], thereby establishing a link between the circadian clock and the photosynthesis system. Some other circadian output responses, such as photoperiodic flowering, could also be regulated by altering the expression of *OsHD3A* ([Fig. 5a](#page-5-0)), a rice ortholog of *FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT)*, and this was consistent with a previous report [[49\]](#page-9-0). Additionally, day-phased RDG *OsLHY* regulated the twocomponent response regulator OsRR2 [\(Fig. 5a](#page-5-0)), which is an important gene involved in ethylene signal transduction, in its regulatory network [[33\]](#page-9-0). Night-phased RDG phytochrome A (OsPHYA) regulated an auxinresponsive protein OsIAA4 in its regulatory network [\[50](#page-9-0)] ([Fig. 5b](#page-5-0)), implying a close relationship between the circadian rhythm pathway and plant hormone signal transduction.

LATE ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL (LHY) is a core component of the circadian clock, and altering its expression disrupts the circadian rhythm, strongly affecting plant and/or hybrid growth and development [[12,42](#page-9-0)]. For example, the *Arabidopsis CCA1/LHY* genes are epigenetically repressed in hybrids during the day, thereby mediating the changes in downstream gene expression and the circadian rhythm. As a result, growth vigor is increased in hybrids [\[12](#page-9-0)]. In the present study, we analyzed *OsLHY* expression pattern and found that the time points of the initial and peak values of the expression levels were delayed by 3 h in the hybrid, compared with in the high maternal parent, but the declining trends were similar (Supplementary Fig. 4a). This 3-h delay in expression requires further research. Generally, *LHY* negatively regulates downstream genes that contain the evening element [\[51](#page-9-0)]. In the present study, we detected 501 targeted genes of *OsLHY* from the 34,653 expressed genes in our libraries, as previously reported. Among these 501 targeted genes, 64 could be classified into various KEGG pathways and 35 were annotated as metabolism- and biosynthesis-related genes (Supplementary Table 13), but the function of most genes remained unknown. Taken together with our GO and KEGG analysis results, the *OsLHY* expression changes further confirmed the important role of the circadian clock in heterosis development.

To correlate RDGs and their expression with the phenotype for complex traits, we mapped the RDGs onto rice QTL. The result showed that 282 RDGs could be mapped onto QTL of small intervals spanning ≤100 genes with significant agronomic traits [\(Table 1\)](#page-6-0), accounting for 18.2% (282/1552) of the total mapped RDGs. For example, the chlorophyllide oxygenase-encoding RDG (LOC\_Os10g41780) was mapped onto AQEY022 and AQAB013 (Supplementary Table 11), which were related to the traits "1000 seed weight" and "spikelet number," respectively. Interestingly, LOC\_Os10g41550 encoding beta-amylase, which was classified into starch and sucrose metabolism, was also mapped onto AQEY022 and AQAB013 (Supplementary Table 11). Further analysis revealed that 136, 97, and 91 RDGs could be significantly mapped onto the yield-, vigor-, and anatomy-related QTL of small intervals, respectively ([Table 1\)](#page-6-0), accounting for 48.2%, 34.4%, and 32.3% of the 282 RDGs, respectively. Totally, up to 72.3% (204/282) of the RDGs were mapped onto the QTL of three categories closely related to heterosis (yield, vigor, and anatomy) ([Table 1\)](#page-6-0). The high correlation between RDGs and the dominant phenotypes of hybrid rice indicated that the methodology used in this study was reliable and credible.

In this study, we systematically analyzed the changes in the dynamic gene expression patterns in hybrid rice at the transcriptional level during 2 complete day/night cycles. However, to elucidate the underlying mechanism of heterosis in detail, a systematic biological study with epigenomics and proteomics analyses, along with transcriptome analysis, is necessary. Overall, this research provides a large amount of valuable information to achieve this goal and substantial guidance for crop breeding practices.

## **5. Conclusion**

Through transcriptome analysis of the dynamic gene expression patterns of hybrid rice at 17 time points during 2 complete day/night cycles, we concluded that day-phased RDGs were associated with photosynthesis and the circadian clock and night-phased RDGs were involved in stress response, which was probably the major contributor to LY2186 heterosis. The RDGs that were mapped onto yield-related QTL can be considered candidate genes and provide valuable information for the cloning and functional investigation of these yield-related QTL.
