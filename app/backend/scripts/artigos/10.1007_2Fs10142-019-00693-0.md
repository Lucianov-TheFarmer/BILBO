## Insights into transcriptional characteristics and homoeolog expression bias of embryo and de-embryonated kernels in developing grain through RNA-Seq and Iso-Seq

Jun Wei1,2 & Hong Cao<sup>1</sup> & Jing-dong Liu<sup>1</sup> & Jing-hong Zuo1,2 & Yu Fang1,2 & Chih-Ta Lin<sup>3</sup> & Run-ze Sun<sup>3</sup> & Wen-long Li1,4 & Yong-xiu Liu<sup>1</sup>

Received: 23 September 2018 / Revised: 21 March 2019 /Accepted: 17 May 2019 # Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2019

#### Abstract

Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is an allohexaploid, and the transcriptional characteristics of the wheat embryo and endosperm during grain development remain unclear. To analyze the transcriptome, we performed isoform sequencing (Iso-Seq) for wheat grain and RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) for the embryo and de-embryonated kernels. The differential regulation between the embryo and deembryonated kernels was found to be greater than the difference between the two time points for each tissue. Exactly 2264 and 4790 tissue-specific genes were found at 14 days post-anthesis (DPA), while 5166 and 3784 genes were found at 25 DPA in the embryo and de-embryonated kernels, respectively. Genes expressed in the embryo were more likely to be related to nucleic acid and enzyme regulation. In de-embryonated kernels, genes were rich in substance metabolism and enzyme activity functions. Moreover, 4351, 4641, 4516, and 4453 genes with the A, B, and D homoeoloci were detected for each of the four tissues. Expression characteristics suggested that the D genome may be the largest contributor to the transcriptome in developing grain. Among these, 48, 66, and 38 silenced genes emerged in the A, B, and D genomes, respectively. Gene ontology analysis showed that silenced genes could be inclined to different functions in different genomes. Our study provided specific gene pools of the embryo and de-embryonated kernels and a homoeolog expression bias model on a large scale. This is helpful for providing new insights into the molecular physiology of wheat.

International Agricultural Research (CGIAR) predicts that by 2050, consumers will require 60% more wheat than they do today ([http://wheat.org/wheat-in-the-world/\)](http://wheat.org/wheat-in-the-world/). Wheat grains are mainly composed of an embryo and endosperm. The embryo contains most of the genetic information that controls plant development, including endosperm energy metabolism, while the endosperm can secrete signals to control embryo growth (Lee et al. [2010](#page-12-0)). The bidirectional interactions between the embryo and endosperm are essential for the normal growth and development of plants. The molecular basis of wheat embryo and endosperm development, and the differences between them, are still unknown. However, research on seed germination has been done by means of proteomics and metabolomics (He et al. [2015;](#page-12-0) Cao et al. [2016;](#page-12-0) Han et al. [2017](#page-12-0)).

Grain development is fundamental to the yield of wheat. Previous studies have mainly focused on physiochemistry, such as chromosome organization (Wegel and Shaw [2005\)](#page-13-0), nutrient transport and storage (Zhou et al. [2009](#page-13-0); Yu et al. [2015a\)](#page-13-0), reproductive development (Fábián et al. [2011;](#page-12-0) Okada et al. [2018](#page-13-0)), and aleurone cell differentiation and function (Zheng and Wang [2014](#page-13-0)). However, due to the polyploidization that contributes to the complexity of gene expression, molecular physiology research on wheat grain development is typically slow.

Developing omics research, such as proteomics and transcriptomics, has provided insights into the mechanisms of wheat grain development (Guo et al. [2012;](#page-12-0) Tasleem-Tahir et al. [2012](#page-13-0); Kang et al. [2013](#page-12-0); Ma et al. [2014](#page-12-0); Zhang et al. [2014](#page-13-0); Nadaud et al. [2015;](#page-12-0) Cao et al. [2016;](#page-12-0) Brinton et al. [2018](#page-12-0)). Starch biosynthesis, storage proteins, and defense/ stress-related proteins accumulate at the late stages of grain development, while synthesis-, assembly-, degradation-, and photosynthesis-related proteins show the opposite pattern (Ma et al. [2014;](#page-12-0) Cao et al. [2016\)](#page-12-0). Various accessions show a significantly different proteome, which may be associated with superior gluten quality (Guo et al. [2012](#page-12-0)). In addition, proteins mainly involved in starch biosynthesis and stress/defense mechanisms are phosphorylated in developing grains. This implies that phosphorylation may have an important role in the development of wheat grain (Guo et al. [2012;](#page-12-0) Ma et al. [2014;](#page-12-0) Zhang et al. [2014](#page-13-0)). Although proteomics significantly improves our understanding of grain development regulation, it is insufficient due to the low percentage of proteins detected. In contrast, transcriptomics can, to some extent, provide more details through lower consumption.

Bread wheat is an allopolyploid and genomic shock, caused by heterozygosity and polyploidy, can trigger rapid genome alterations (Zhao et al. [2011](#page-13-0); Feldman and Levy [2012](#page-12-0)). Bread wheat often has changes in its genomic components and gene expression in the early generations (Zhang et al. [2013](#page-13-0)), which induces aneuploidy of the chromosome. Aneuploidy is closely associated with regulation of gene expression (Zhang et al. [2017](#page-13-0)). Furthermore, duplication of the genome may result in genome asymmetry, as homoeologs may be lost, silenced, or show expression bias (Bottley et al. [2006;](#page-12-0) Bottley and Koebner [2008](#page-12-0); Qi et al. [2012;](#page-13-0) Leach et al. [2014\)](#page-12-0).

For bread wheat, 21 pairs of homologous chromosomes fall into seven homoeologous groups. In each group, homoeologous chromosomes still share a high degree of gene synteny and DNA sequence homology (Feldman and Levy [2012\)](#page-12-0). However, they differ from one another by a number of non-coding and highly repetitious DNA sequences, in addition to many functional gene complexes (Feldman and Levy [2012](#page-12-0)). The high homology of the subgenomes causes the functions of many genes to be redundant. However, differences owing to different progenitors make its transcription relatively independent. Recently, transcriptomics has indicated that three subgenomes of wheat show a high degree of cell type– and stage–dependent expression dominance. This implies that specification, asymmetry, and interplay occur among the three subgenomes at the lever of transcriptional regulation (Zhang et al. [2017\)](#page-13-0). However, the molecular bases of these particular expression patterns are still unclear.

In the present study, we conducted an investigation on the transcriptome characterization of developing wheat grains at 14 and 25 days post-anthesis (DPA). This was done using RNA and isoform sequencing, and revealed the regulatory differences between embryo and de-embryonated kernels. In addition, the expression characteristics of A, B, and D homologue genes have been surveyed. This study provided a comprehensive view of the characterization of the embryo and deembryonated kernel transcriptome, and homologue transcripts in developing grain.

## Materials and methods

#### Plant material and sequencing

Zhou 8425B, an elite Chinese bread wheat cultivar with high yield and superior quality (Gao et al. [2015\)](#page-12-0), was used in this experiment. Field cultivation was done in Beijing from 2016 to 2017. Both embryo and de-embryonated kernels were collected at 14 and 25 DPA, respectively (Fig. [1\)](#page-2-0). Samples collected from three biological replicates were stored at − 80 °C before RNA isolation. Each sample was obtained randomly from different plants.

Total RNA was extracted using a Plant Total RNA Purification Kit (GeneMark). The Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA) and the Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) were used to detect RNA quality. The purified RNAs were used to construct libraries and sequencing on an Illumina HiSeqTM 4000 platform. Isoform sequencing (Iso-Seq) by PacBio was used to obtain fulllength transcripts. For Iso-Seq, total RNA was extracted from mixed whole grains at 14 and 25 DPA. The RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) data reported in this paper has been submitted to GEO of NCBI with accession GSE118474.

#### Read mapping, gene expression analysis, and alternative splicing analysis

Raw reads were examined using fastQC to inspect read qualities and the extent of adapter sequence contamination. Adapter sequences were clipped and reads with qualities of less than 20 were trimmed. Reads from a pair where only one read passed the filtering criteria were also trimmed. After preprocessing, the clean reads were aligned to the Chinese Spring (CS) genome (TGACv1, release-36) using TopHat (Trapnell et al. [2012\)](#page-13-0), allowing a maximum of five mismatches. We calculated the number of uniquely mapped reads for each gene model in the CS genome by parsing the alignment output files from TopHat, and then normalized the resulting read counts by reads per kilobase million (RPKM) to measure the gene

expression level. We calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient between biological replicates with R software. Heml tools (Deng et al. [2014\)](#page-12-0) were used for hierarchical clustering. Alternative splicing (AS) analysis was performed using TopHat ([http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/tophat/index.shtml\)](http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/tophat/index.shtml).

#### Identification of differential expression and tissue-specific genes

RPKM was used to estimate gene expression, and differential expression analysis (|log2(Fold Change)|≥1) was performed using the DESeq R package. The resulting P values were adjusted using Benjamini-Hochberg's approach for controlling the false discovery rate (FDR). Transcripts with FDR < 0.001 found by DESeq were deemed to have differential expression. To identify tissue-specific genes, we selected the genes with RPKM > 0.05 and created Venn diagram for the four tissues. Gene annotation was based on the Non-Redundant Protein Sequence Database (Nr), KEGG orthology (KO), and the Gene ontology (GO) database. Enrichment analysis for GO was executed by the topGO R package, and GO classification was carried out with WEGO 2.0 [\(wego.](http://wego.genomics.org.cn) [genomics.org.cn/](http://wego.genomics.org.cn)). Differential expression and tissue-specific gene identification were based on a non-redundant reference gene pool, constructed by the genome CDS and a full-length sequence from Iso-Seq (Supplementary Fig. 1).

#### Identification of homoeologous genes in A, B, and D homoeoloci

To identify homoeologous genes in the A, B, and D homoeoloci, we screened out wheat CDS sequences with mapping rate great than 95%, as detected by Blat. We then filtered out redundant sequences and created homoeologous CDS list. Based on genomic annotation files and a single gene library, the homoeologous CDS ID in the A, B, and D homoeoloci was converted into a gene ID, and a list of homoeologous genes was acquired (Supplementary Fig. 1). Homoeologous genes with RPKM < 0.1 in all of the A, B, and D homoeoloci were filtered out. Pairwise comparisons among the A, B, and D homoeoloci with |log2(Fold Change)|≥1 were deemed to be differentially expressed homoeologous genes. For differentially expressed homoeologous genes, if RPKM = 0 in the A, B, or D homoeoloci, it was considered to have been silenced. A Venn diagram of the GO items of the silenced genes in the A, B, and D homoeoloci was created using GOView [\(www.](http://www.webgestalt.org/GOView/) [webgestalt.org/GOView/](http://www.webgestalt.org/GOView/)).

#### Quantitative RT-PCR

RNA samples used for quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) were isolated from the embryo or de-embryonated kernels in 14- or 25-DPA wheat grains. Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 software and are listed in Supplementary Table 9. The first-strand cDNA was synthesized by using a FastQuant RT Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Amplification via PCR was done following the instructions of the KAPA SYBR® FAST qPCR Kit (Sigma, USA), performed following the fluorescent quantitative PCR amplification instrument (Eppendorf Master™ ep realplex, Germany). TaGAPDH was used as a reference gene for normalizing expression levels. Non-specific products were identified by melting curve analysis.

#### Full-length transcript library constructed by Iso-Seq

To gain better insight into the transcriptional regulation of grains, Iso-Seq by PacBio was used to obtain full-length transcripts. Iso-Seq generated 67,313 high-quality isoforms with a length of 0.5–3 kb (Supplementary Fig. 4). Among these, 61,930 (92.0%) transcripts could translate protein, and 52,668 (78.2%) could align to the A (14,930), B (15,560), or D (22,178) genomes. All transcripts were annotated based on Nr, Clusters of Orthologous Groups of proteins (COG), Swiss-Prot, KO, and GO database. Only 3373 (5.0%) of the transcripts did not have any annotation. We combined RNA-Seq with Iso-Seq for differential expression analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1).

#### Alternative splicing is dynamically regulated

To investigate the role of AS in wheat grain development, we conducted a survey of transcript isoforms across the four tissues. We investigated four main classes of AS events: intron retention (IR), exon skipping (ES), the alternative 5′ splice site donor (A5′SS), and the alternative 3′ splice site acceptor (A3′ SS). Among the 82,914 genes that were expressed, we detected AS within 49,187 genes. This was 59% of the expressed transcripts.

We documented four types of AS in Fig. [2a](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Table 1. The most prevalent AS was IR (74.1%), followed by A3′SS (14.0%), A5′SS (8.6%), and ES (3.3%). Only a small fraction of the genes had all four classes of AS. For example, the four classes of AS were present in 755 genes (~ 2.3%) in G-EB1. It is possible that AS may be dynamically regulated. A primary example of this is isoamylase 3 (TRIAE\_CS42\_5AL\_TGACv1\_375782\_AA1227100), which is necessary for starch metabolism (Fig. [2b, c](#page-4-0)). At 14 DPA in the embryo, isoamylase 3 possessed four classes of AS, of which A3′SS was the most frequent. This was followed by A5′SS, IR, and ES. In deembryonated kernels, IR was the most frequent, with ES, A5′SS, and A3′SS present in lower number. At 25 DPA, isoamylase 3 in the embryo also had four classes of AS, but only A3′SS was present in the deembryonated kernels (Fig. [2b](#page-4-0); Supplementary Table 2).

#### Homoeologous gene expression patterns revealed subgenome gene bias and silencing

Bread wheat has a complex hexaploid genome. Each wheat gene potentially contains three alleles in the A, B, and D homoeoloci, each of which may contribute differentially to wheat phenotypes. To reveal the expression characteristics of homoeologous genes in the A, B, and D homoeoloci during grain development, we constructed homoeologous gene libraries of embryos and deembryonated kernels at 14 and 25 DPA (Supplementary Tables 4, 5, 6, and 7). Overall, 4351, 4641, 4516, and 4453 genes with A, B, and D homoeoloci were detected in G-EB, G-ES, M-EB, and M-ES, respectively. These genes belonged to 14,356 single genes. Of the 77.9–87.1% of genes expressed in the A, B, and D homoeoloci, the D homoeoloci had the most expression, while B had the least (Fig. [7a\)](#page-8-0).

There were 2425 (55.7%), 2545 (54.8%), 2611 (57.8%), and 2633 (59.1%) homoeologous genes differentially expressed in G-EB, G-ES, M-EB, and M-ES, respectively (Fig. [7b](#page-8-0)). In G-EB, Cysteine proteinase inhibitor (Supplementary Table 4, number 273) was highly expressed in the B homoeoloci (average RPKM, 169.8), while its expression in the A and D homoeoloci was low (average RPKM, about 22.5). In M-EB, Ribosomal protein S20 (Supplementary Table 6, numbers 18 and 25) was highly expressed in the A homoeoloci, with an average RPKM of about 500. However, its expression in the B and D homoeoloci was low. These characteristics expressed by homoeologous genes in the A, B, and D homoeoloci may be conserved in different tissues. As an example, Ribosomal protein S20 had similar expression patterns in G-ES, M-EB, and M-ES. It was highly expressed in the A homoeoloci and had low expression in the B and D homoeoloci. The homoeologous gene of Cysteine proteinase inhibitor also showed a similar expression pattern in G-EB, G-ES, and M-EB.

To infer whether homoeologous genes in the A, B, and D homoeoloci have differing regulation in the embryo and endosperm, we selected tissue-specific homoeologous genes of the embryo and de-embryonated kernels. The results showed that the B homoeoloci was the most frequent, followed by the D and A homoeoloci (Supplementary Fig. 8). Furthermore, many tissue-specific homoeologous genes in G-ES showed higher expression compared with the others.

Homoeologous genes not only had expression bias, but also had gene silencing in the A, B, or D homoeoloci. # Function of phytohormones during grain development

In grain development, phytohormone regulation is complex and diverse. Before grain filling, the interaction of CYT and AUX largely promotes the division and enlargement of embryo and endosperm cells, increasing the storage of grain (Kong et al. 2015). During grain filling, ABA and gibberellic acid (GA) play major roles in regulating the accumulation of nutrients such as starch (Kong et al. 2015). ABA and GA have antagonistic roles in the regulation of signaling molecules and gene expression, contributing to concentrations of calcium,  $\alpha$ -amylase, MAP kinase, and PH (Bethke et al. 1997). ABA and ETH can interact to regulate the rate of grain filling (Kong et al. 2015). However, in this study, genes related to BR were the most frequent (Fig. 4). Transcription levels were insufficient for reflecting the changes in protein levels, but these implied that BR may play an important role in wheat grain filling. Previous studies on rice have shown that GW5 can interact with GSK2 (Arabidopsis GSK3/SHAGGY-like kinase BIN2), a negative regulator of BR signaling (Song 2017). Overexpression of OsmiR397, which targets OsLAC, resulted in longer grains

and made plants more sensitive to exogenously applied BR (Song 2017). Exogenously applying BR can have both beneficial and non-beneficial effects during cultivation. The appropriate concentration can enlarge grains (Gao et al. 2017), but also increases plant height (Liu et al. 2015; Yuan et al. 2017), reducing lodging resistance. Many DEGs related to AUX and CYT were enriched (Fig. 4). AUX and CYT could play a major role before filling (Kong et al. 2015). This may be largely due to differences in embryo and de-embryonated kernel regulation, rather than differences before and after filling.

#### Transcription factor–, nucleic acid–, or protein modification–related genes revealed developmental differences between embryo and de-embryonated kernels

Based on the number of tissue-specific genes, deembryonated kernel metabolism appeared to be more exuberant than that of the embryo at 14 DPA, while the reverse was true at 25 DPA (Fig. 5). This was consistent with the results of 2,3,5-triphenyl-2H-tetrazolium chloride staining (Yu et al. 2015a). These results implied that the development of the endosperm may occur prior to or faster than that of the embryo. Fertilization initiates the development of the embryo and endosperm (Ohad et al. [1999\)](#page-13-0). However, it is worth considering whether or not embryo and endosperm development is synchronous. Previous studies have showed that after fertilization, the primary endosperm nucleus divides before division of the zygote nucleus (Algan and Bakar [1996;](#page-11-0) Riahi et al. [2003\)](#page-13-0). In some Arabidopsis mutants, the endosperm can develop independently and without fertilization (Kiyosue et al. [1999;](#page-12-0) Ohad et al. [1999;](#page-13-0) Kohler et al. [2003](#page-12-0)). These suggest that embryo and endosperm development may not be synchronized. Thus, we hypothesized that in order to better provide nutrients for offspring survival after fertilization, the plant first initiates nutrient accumulation (endosperm development), followed by filial generation development (embryo development).

For tissue-specific transcription factor genes, WOX (TRIAE\_CS42\_5DS\_TGACv1\_456589\_AA1474690; WOX5) was highly expressed in de-embryonated kernels (Fig. [5a](#page-7-0)). The WOX family has important functions in embryo formation and maintaining stem cell stability (Drisch and Stahl [2015;](#page-12-0) Zhang et al. [2015](#page-13-0); Zhou et al. [2018\)](#page-13-0). In Arabidopsis, WOX5 is expressed in the quiescent center of root apical, preventing cell division and establishing quiescence in embryonic root (Zhang et al. [2015\)](#page-13-0). Previous studies have shown that WOX5 is expressed in the wheat endosperm, aleurone layer, and pericarp ([http://plants.ensembl.org/Triticum\\_](http://plants.ensembl.org/Triticum_aestivum/Info/Index) [aestivum/Info/Index\)](http://plants.ensembl.org/Triticum_aestivum/Info/Index), confirming the accuracy of our data. However, the function of WOX5 in the endosperm needs to be investigated further.

Protein modification is a part of transcriptional and metabolic regulation. In developing grains, E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase (TRIAE\_CS42\_3DS\_TGACv1\_272575\_AA0922570) and SKP1-like (TRIAE\_CS42\_7AL\_TGACv1 \_563322\_ AA1803790) are highly expressed in the de-embryonated kernels. The E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase contains a RING-type zinc finger domain and a substrate-binding domain (SBD). The SBD is related to the TRAF family [\(http://www.uniprot.org/uniprot/](http://www.uniprot.org/uniprot/A0A1D5WUN0) [A0A1D5WUN0\)](http://www.uniprot.org/uniprot/A0A1D5WUN0). Studies have shown that the TRAF protein plays an important role in the immune response (Huang et al. [2016](#page-12-0)). These suggested that E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase may have a function in pathogen resistance during grain development through the ubiquitin pathway. SKP1 is a subunit of the SKP1- CUL1-F-box protein. SKP1 connects F-box and Cullin-1 protein. Various E3 ubiquitin ligases mediate the ubiquitylation of different substrates (Zheng et al. [2002\)](#page-13-0). However, the reason for the specifically high expression of SKP1-like in de-embryonated kernels is worthy of further study.

Histone acetylation and de-acetylation are important means of gene regulation. The GO annotation indicated that TRIAE\_CS42\_5BL\_TGACv1\_406409\_AA1345240 and TRIAE\_CS42\_5BL\_TGACv1\_407635\_AA1358520, two de-embryonated kernel-specific genes, are related to histone acetylation. Sequence alignment showed that they have an amino acid sequence of up to 88.1%, and they have high homology with the C-terminal region of methyl-CpGbinding domain–containing protein 9 (XP\_020184167.1, MeCP9) (Supplementary Fig. 9). MeCP9 contains a PHD1\_Lid2p\_like, WHIM1, PHD, WSD super family and an FYRN super family domain. It plays an important role in DNA methylation and histone acetylation.

Histone H2A (TRIAE\_CS42\_1AS\_TGACv1\_018984\_ AA0057270) was specifically highly expressed at 14 DPA, especially in the embryo. GO annotation indicated that it was related to DNA methylation, temperature response, flower organ development, and pathogen resistance. Previous studies have shown that the H2A message is abundant in cycling cells such as apices and early developing fruit, and is very low in mature tissue (Ozga et al. [2002\)](#page-13-0). Therefore, expression of this histone is replication-dependent, and accumulation of its mRNA is useful as a marker for cell division (Ozga et al. [2002\)](#page-13-0). These results imply that the division of grain cells is faster at 14 DPA.

#### C4 photosynthesis in grain pericarp

The photosynthesis antenna protein pathway was notably enriched in the G-EB-VS-G-ES, M-EB-VS-G-EB, and M-ES-VS-G-ES groups (Fig. [3b](#page-4-0); Supplementary Fig. 6). It is well known that seeds cannot perform photosynthesis. However, de-embryonated wheat kernels with pericarps, which have chloroplasts in the early stages (Yu et al. [2015b\)](#page-13-0), may perform photosynthesis. Although many reports have suggested that the wheat pericarp can accumulate nutrients (Zhou et al. [2009](#page-13-0); Yu et al. [2015a;](#page-13-0) Yu et al. [2015b\)](#page-13-0), it is not clear whether the nutrients are from the mother or independent production. Recently, research has shown that the wheat pericarp has different photosynthetic mechanisms to leaves. Although wheat is a typical C3 plant, the pericarp has the potential to perform C4 photosynthesis (Rangan et al. [2016\)](#page-13-0). Rangan et al. [\(2016\)](#page-13-0) found that genes specifically related to NAD-ME-type C4 photosynthesis are present and are expressed in grains, but are not present or are only expressed in low amounts in leaves. We also identified many key enzyme genes of C4 photosynthesis, including some C4-type genes identified by Rangan (Supplementary Table 8). These C4-type genes were expressed in all of four tissues, but were highly expressed in de-embryonated kernels for carbonic anhydrase, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase, aspartate aminotransferase, and alanine transaminase (Supplementary Fig. 10). This implied the wheat pericarp does indeed have the potential for C4 photosynthesis. Furthermore, we identified some new copies of key photosynthesis genes based on blast Nr, and these were compared with the result of Rangan (2016) et al. However, these genes were C3-type or C4-type and need to be verified. During grain filling, more than 41% of the <span id="page-11-0"></span>nutrients come from C3 photosynthesis of flag leaves (Han et al. [2014\)](#page-12-0). However, the discovery of the potential for C4 photosynthesis highlights the positive and effective evolution of wheat.

#### Gene bias and silencing of A, B, and D homoeoloci in hexaploid wheat

Allopolyploidization is a driving force in plant evolution. The formation of bread wheat represents a classic example of successful speciation via allopolyploidy. Allopolyploidy can cause DNA elimination, and different genomes may not be equally affected (Zhang et al. [2013;](#page-13-0) Li et al. [2015\)](#page-12-0). The contribution of wheat A, B, and D genomes to the transcriptional pool or phenotypes is still unknown. Each trio of A, B, and D homoeoloci in hexaploid wheat originates from a common ancestral gene. Hence, a simple null hypothesis would be that each homoeolocus of A, B, and D equally expresses and contributes to mRNA transcripts ('A=B=D') (Leach et al. [2014\)](#page-12-0). However, in polyploids, homologous gene bias is a common phenomenon. Leach et al. [\(2014\)](#page-12-0) discussed that the evolutionary divergence of homoeoloci may cause changes in relative expression, from slight changes to the physical loss of a homoeolocus or the complex successive hybridization and domestication processes. This can result in homoeolocus divergence and relative homoeolocus expression levels.

A regulation study of the A, B, and D genomes is difficult due to the high similarity among them. In allohexaploid wheat, early attempts to analyze homoeolog contribution to gene expression levels mostly used low-throughput techniques, such as expressed sequence tags and single-strand confirmation polymorphism analyses (Mochida et al. [2004](#page-12-0); Bottley et al. [2006;](#page-12-0) Bottley et al. [2008;](#page-12-0) Bottley and Koebner [2008;](#page-12-0) Leach et al. [2014](#page-12-0)). However, only a few genes were identified in these studies. Stamati (2009) found that the D genome contributes more significantly using type I thionins as an example. With the development of high-throughput sequencing technology, it is feasible in wheat to comprehensively analyze homologous gene bias. RNA-Seq technology measures homoeolog expression, using reads tagged with parental sequence single nucleotide polymorphisms. This suggested higher expression of A and B homoeologs than D homoeologs in nascent allohexaploid wheat (Li et al. [2014](#page-12-0)). However, due to the lack of a high-quality reference genome for wheat, there were some limitations. Recently, the fine mapping of the wheat genome has been published (Avni et al. 2017; Luo et al. [2017](#page-12-0); Zhao et al. [2017;](#page-13-0) Zimin et al. [2017\)](#page-13-0), which can promote wheat research. Furthermore, the identification and localization of A, B, and D homologue genes will also become more accurate due to this development.

In this study, according to the CS genome, A, B, and D homologue gene classification was conducted based on sequence similarity. Although there were limitations due to the existence of multiple copy genes on the same genome, such as TRIAE\_CS42\_2AS\_TGACv1\_114934\_AA0370010 and TRIAE\_CS42\_3AL\_TGACv1\_196278\_AA0659020, our data could comprehensively represent the expression pattern of homologue genes among the A, B, and D genomes. The results suggested that the D genome contributes more significantly than the A and B genomes in grain, not only based on gene counts but also on gene expression levels (Supplementary Fig. 11). This was consistent with previous studies (Stamati et al. [2009\)](#page-13-0). This may be due to the fact that the D genome maintains high stability during evolution (Zhang et al. [2013](#page-13-0)). However, the high contribution of the D genome to transcripts is not constant. Leach et al. ([2014](#page-12-0)) found that the B genome contributes more significantly in nullisomic-tetrasomic lines. In nascent allohexaploid wheat, A and B homoeologs were more highly expressed than D homoeologs (Li et al. [2014](#page-12-0)). Thus, wheat A, B, and D homoeolog expression bias or silencing could be regulated not only by genomic stability, but also by epigenetics. In this study, A, B, and D genomes had 48, 66, and 38 silenced genes, respectively. Although there were few genes undergoing annotation, GO classification of these genes implied that these may be functionally biased, which hence requires further verification.
