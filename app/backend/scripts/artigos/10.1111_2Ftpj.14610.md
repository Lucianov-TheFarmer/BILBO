Running head: Complex phenotype of a frd3 mutant in Arabidopsis

## Summary

FRD3 (FERRIC REDUCTASE DEFECTIVE 3) plays a major role in iron (Fe) and zinc (Zn) homeostasis in Arabidopsis. It transports citrate, which enables metal distribution in the plant. A *frd3* mutant is dwarf and chlorotic and displays constitutive Fe deficiency response and strongly altered metal distribution in tissues. Here, we have examined the interaction between Fe and Zn homeostasis in a *frd3* mutant exposed to varying Zn supply. Detailed phenotyping using transcriptomic, ionomic, histochemical and spectroscopic approaches revealed the full complexity of the *frd3* mutant phenotype, which resulted from altered transition metal homeostasis, manganese toxicity, oxidative and biotic stress responses. The cell wall played a key role in these processes, as a site for Fe and hydrogen peroxide accumulation, and displayed modified structure in the mutant. Finally, we showed that Zn excess interfered with these mechanisms and partially restored root growth of the mutant, without reverting the Fe deficiency response. In conclusion, the *frd3* mutant molecular phenotype is more complex than previously described, and illustrates how the response to metal imbalance depends on multiple signaling pathways.

### Introduction

Iron (Fe) is an essential micronutrient for plant growth and quality (Briat et al., 2015) and for human health (WHO; http://www.who.int/nutrition/en/). Fe is one of the five most abundant metals in soils but it is hardly accessible to plants as it is highly insoluble, especially in alkaline soils (Marschner and Marschner, 2012). As plants are at the base of the food pyramid, Fe deficiency is the most common human nutritional disorder, with approximatively 30% of the world population affected by anemia (WHO; http://www.who.int/nutrition/en/).

In plant cells, Fe is involved in numerous redox processes. As a component of heme and Fe-sulfur clusters, it is a major cofactor for both the respiratory electron transfer chain (Palmer and Guerinot, 2009; Nouet et al., 2011) and photosynthesis (Hanikenne et al., 2014). It is for instance required for chlorophyll synthesis (Kobayashi and Nishizawa, 2012). Fe also participates to several important processes, such as root morphology, flower and pollen development, nutrient assimilation (e.g. sulfate) and hormone biosynthesis (e.g. ethylene) (Bouzayen et al., 1991; Schmidt, 1999; Takahashi et al., 2003). In addition to these essential roles, Fe can also be cytotoxic. As redox active metal, excess Fe can trigger the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Stohs and Bagchi, 1995; Marschner and Marschner, 2012).

Plants have developed mechanisms to efficiently take up Fe from soil (Walker and Connolly, 2008). Dicotyledonous and non-graminaceous monocotyledonous plants use an Fe uptake machinery, called strategy I, based on the reduction of Fe<sup>3+</sup> in Fe<sup>2+</sup> (Römheld, 1987). In contrast, grasses use the strategy II based on Fe<sup>3+</sup> chelation and uptake from soil (Schmidt, 2003). In Arabidopsis, the ARABIDOPSIS H<sup>+</sup>-ATPase 2 (AHA2) is responsible for the first step of strategy I consisting in proton pumping into the rhizosphere (Santi and Schmidt, 2009), resulting in a local acidification to increase Fe<sup>3+</sup> solubility (Guerinot and Yi, 1994). Fe<sup>3+</sup> is then reduced in Fe<sup>2+</sup> by the plasma membrane-bound enzyme FERRIC REDUCTASE-OXIDASE 2 (FRO2) (Robinson et al., 1999). Fe<sup>2+</sup> is finally taken up at the plasmamembrane of root epidermis cells by IRON-REGULATED TRANSPORTER 1 (IRT1), a member of the ZRT-IRT LIKE PROTEIN (ZIP) family. IRT1 has a high affinity for Fe<sup>2+</sup> but also for a range of divalent metal cations, including Zn<sup>2+</sup>, Mn<sup>2+</sup>, Ni<sup>2+</sup> and also Cd<sup>2+</sup> (Eide et al., 1996; Vert et al., 2002). Moreover, the secretion of phenolic compounds was recently shown to increase Fe<sup>3+</sup> availability. The F6'-H1 (a coumarin biosynthesis enzyme) and ATP-BINDING CASSETTE G37 [ABCG37, also called

PLEIOTROPIC DRUG RESISTANCE 9 (PDR9)] proteins are involved in phenolics synthesis and secretion by roots, respectively (Fourcroy et al., 2014; Schmidt et al., 2014).

The regulation of Fe uptake and the Fe deficiency response in Arabidopsis is complex and notably controlled by the transcription factor FER-LIKE IRON-DEFICIENCY-INDUCED TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR (FIT), which is required for the up-regulation of expression of, among others, the AHA2, FRO2, IRT1, F6'H1 and ABCG37 genes under Fe deficiency (Colangelo and Guerinot, 2004; Yuan et al., 2005; Ivanov et al., 2012; Rodríguez-Celma et al., 2013; Schmidt et al., 2014). FIT is a BASIC HELIX-LOOP-HELIX (bHLH) protein, and needs interaction with an additional bHLH protein (bHLH038, 039, 100 or 101) to activate the strategy I response (Yuan et al., 2008). BRUTUS (BTS), a E3-ligase, is also able to interact with different bHLH (bHLH034, 104 and 105) to control the transcription of the bHLH038, 039, 100 and 101 genes in response to Fe availability, whereas two BTS-like proteins were recently shown to directly interact with and regulate FIT (Rodríguez-Celma et al., 2019). The BTS-bHLH complex also regulates POPEYE (PYE) which negatively controls genes involved in iron mobility between roots and shoots (Long et al., 2010; Li et al., 2016). Two proteins of the MYELOBLASTOSIS (MYB) transcription factors family (MYB10 and MYB72) are also involved in the Fe deficiency response, and are regulating NICOTINAMINE SYNTHASE 4 (NAS4) (Sivitz et al., 2012; Palmer et al., 2013; Brumbarova et al., 2015).

Once inside the roots, Fe is transported symplastically, mostly chelated by nicotianamine (NA), and apoplastically to vascular tissues, where it is possibly loaded into the xylem by FERROPORTIN 1 [FPN1; also called IRON REGULATED 1 (IREG1)] for root to shoot translocation (Morrissey et al., 2009). In the xylem, Fe is unable to move freely and is chelated essentially by citrate (Rellán-Álvarez et al., 2010). Citrate, an organic acid, is essential for the proper mobility and distribution of Fe in roots, shoots, anthers, pollen and seeds (Roschzttardtz et al., 2011). *FERRIC REDUCTASE DEFFECTIVE 3 (FRD3)* encodes a plasma membrane transporter expressed in the root pericycle in Arabidopsis and involved in citrate efflux into the xylem. FRD3 plays a key role in Fe homeostasis (Rogers and Guerinot, 2002; Green and Rogers, 2004; Durrett et al., 2007). In a *frd3* mutant [allelic to *MANGANESE ACCUMULATOR 1 (man-1)* (Delhaize, 1996)], 40% reduction of citrate levels in the xylem sap is associated to leaf chlorosis, plant growth reduction, and Fe accumulation in the root vascular tissues (Rogers and Guerinot, 2002; Green and Rogers, 2004; Durrett et al., 2004; Durrett et al., 2004; Durrett et al., 2004; Durrett et al., 2007). Furthermore, *frd3* plants express

constitutively the Fe deficiency response, including *IRT1*, and consequently accumulate large amounts of manganese (Mn), zinc (Zn) and Fe in roots and shoots (Delhaize, 1996; Rogers and Guerinot, 2002; Green and Rogers, 2004).

Secondary Fe deficiency is typically a major component of Zn excess toxicity. Increased Fe supply indeed alleviates Zn toxicity symptoms in Arabidopsis (Shanmugam et al., 2011). In addition to its major role in Fe homeostasis, FRD3 also contributes to this interaction that exists between Fe and Zn homeostasis. A quantitative trait locus analysis identified *FRD3* as a determinant of Zn tolerance variation among Arabidopsis ecotypes. Higher expression of *FRD3* in response to Zn excess was co-segregating with higher Zn tolerance and reduced impact of Zn excess on Fe homeostasis (Pineau et al., 2012). Moreover, the two *FRD3* transcript variants undergo differential expression and translation efficiencies upon Zn excess (Charlier et al., 2015).

In this study, we assessed the response of an Arabidopsis *frd3* mutant (*frd3*-7) to varying Zn supply to examine the connection between Fe and Zn homeostasis. We show that the dwarfed phenotype of the mutant in normal growth conditions results from multiple alterations in Fe, Mn and Zn homeostasis as well as in cell walls and in oxidative and biotic stress responses. Exposure to higher Zn concentrations reverted most of these phenotypes in roots. Our results highlight that the response to metal homeostasis imbalance is intertwined with multiple signaling pathways.

#### Results

### Altered Zn phenotype of the frd3 mutant

When grown hydroponically in control condition (1 $\mu$ M Zn), the *frd3* mutant was small and chlorotic compared to the wild-type (WT, Col-0) (Figure 1 and S1) as described (Rogers and Guerinot, 2002). The root growth phenotype was partially rescued when Zn supply was increased to 5 $\mu$ M and 20  $\mu$ M (Figure 1A and B). When exposed to 20 $\mu$ M Zn, root growth was more than doubled compared to the control although shoots remained small and chlorotic compared to the WT (Figure 1C and 1D, Figure S1). Similar results were obtained with seedlings grown *in vitro* (Figure S2). The *frd3* mutant displayed a 40% root growth reduction compared to the WT in control condition. Upon exposure to high Zn (75 $\mu$ M Zn), root growth of the WT was reduced by 30%, whereas it slightly, but significantly, increased in the *frd3* mutant. At high Zn, there were no more differences between root growth in the WT and *frd3* mutant (Figure S2A). The *frd3* shoots

remained smaller than in the WT at high Zn, similar to observations in hydropony (Figure 1D and S2B).

### Impact of Zn on the mobilization of Fe and Zn in the frd3 mutant

Next, the impact of high Zn exposure on the typical metal accumulation phenotypes of the *frd3* mutant was examined. When grown hydroponically in control condition  $(1\mu M Zn)$ , the *frd3* mutant accumulated more Fe, Zn and Mn (6-, 7- and 22-fold, respectively) in roots than the WT (Figure 2A). The mutant also accumulated more Zn and Mn in shoots (3.5- and 4-fold, respectively) (Figure S3), as described (Delhaize, 1996; Rogers and Guerinot, 2002). Upon high Zn exposure ( $20\mu M Zn$ ), the Zn concentrations were similar in roots and shoots of both mutant and WT, resulting from increased Zn accumulation in the WT. The impact on Fe accumulation in the mutant was limited, with a slight decrease of Fe concentrations in both roots and shoots. In contrast, Zn exposure drastically reduced Mn accumulation in the mutant (by ~97% in roots and ~69% in shoots). The ionomic profile of the plants exposed to 5µM Zn was more or less intermediate between the control and 20µM Zn conditions (Figure 2A and Figure S3).

A typical phenotype of the *frd3* mutant is  $Fe^{3+}$  accumulation in the root vascular tissues, which can be visualized by Perls staining and enhanced by diaminobenzidine (DAB) (Green and Rogers, 2004; Roschzttardtz et al., 2011). Accordingly, a positive staining was observed in roots and root sections of the *frd3* mutant in control condition (Figure 2B). At 20µM Zn, the staining was no longer observed in *frd3* roots, suggesting competition between Fe and Zn in roots upon high Zn exposure (Figure 2B). No staining was observed in mutant and WT shoots. Note that high Zn exposure (75µM Zn) had similar effects on both the ionome and Fe accumulation in vascular tissues of seedlings grown *in vitro* (Figure S4).

Finally, Zn distribution in WT and *frd3* roots was imaged using the Zn fluorophore Zinpyr-1 (Sinclair et al., 2007) (Figure 2C). In WT roots, the Zn-associated green fluorescence was localized in the vascular bundle. In the *frd3* mutant, massive Zn accumulation was observed in some endodermis cells in addition to vascular tissues. Upon high Zn exposure, the fluorescence of the Zn-Zinpyr-1 complex was only observed in the root vascular tissues of both genotypes with a higher intensity compared to the control condition. Metal distribution in the *frd3* root was dependent of the Fe and Zn concentration ratio in the medium (Figure S5). When the Fe/Zn concentration ratio in the medium was lower or equal to 1, typical *frd3* phenotypes were no longer observed, with no Fe<sup>3+</sup> staining in the mutant root, as described in Figure 2. Conversely, when this ratio was higher than 1, Fe<sup>3+</sup> staining was observed in *frd3* roots.

### *Mn toxicity in the frd3 mutant*

A particularly striking phenotype of the *frd3* mutant is the massive Mn accumulation in root and shoot tissues in control condition and its almost complete reversal upon high Zn exposure (Figure 2A and Figure S3). Next was examined whether Mn accumulation contributed to the *frd3* mutant phenotype. Both WT and mutant were grown in control condition ( $5\mu$ M Mn) and at Mn deficiency ( $0\mu$ M Mn) (Figure 3). The treatment abolished Mn accumulation in root and shoot tissues of the *frd3* mutant (Figure S6A and 6B). Mn deficiency slightly decreased root growth of the WT, whereas in contrast, it increased root growth of the *frd3* mutant (~33%) compared to control condition. Consequently no growth difference was observed between WT and mutant at Mn deficiency (Figure 3A). This condition had however no influence on Fe<sup>3+</sup> accumulation in the mutant root vascular tissues (Figure 3B).

Combined Mn deficiency (0 $\mu$ M Mn) and high Zn exposure (75 $\mu$ M Zn) restored root growth of the *frd3* mutant, similar to Mn deficiency alone (Figure 3A), and resulted in competition between Fe and Zn in mutant root, similar to Zn excess alone (Figure 3B). Note that Mn deficiency had a limited impact on *frd3* shoot (Figure S6C). Altogether, these observations suggested that Mn accumulation resulted in toxicity in the *frd3* mutant and was at least partly responsible for its altered root growth, but did not influence the Fe-related phenotypes (*e.g.* root Fe<sup>3+</sup> accumulation) of the mutant.

#### Transcriptome profiling of the frd3 mutant

To shed light on the molecular mechanisms underlying the complex interplay between Fe, Zn and Mn observed in the *frd3* mutant roots, a transcriptomic profiling of root tissues of WT and *frd3* plants grown in control (1µM Zn) and Zn excess (20µM Zn) was conducted using RNA-Seq. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the PC1 (explaining 61% of the gene expression variance) separated the plant genotypes whereas the PC2 (explaining 21% of the gene expression variance) separated the growth conditions (Figure 4A). The PCA also showed that, in control conditions, the WT and *frd3* mutant had highly diverging gene expression phenotypes in roots, whereas upon high Zn exposure, both profiles were almost similar. In agreement with these observations, differential gene expression analysis (log<sub>2</sub> fold change > 1 or < -1; FDR < 0.05) identified 490 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between WT and mutant roots in control conditions and only 5 DEGs in high Zn condition (Figure 4B, and Table S1).

Comparison of gene expression in the *frd3* mutant and WT in control condition revealed the extent of molecular alteration in the *frd3* mutant roots, which involved several processes beyond metal homeostasis. In fact, gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Figure 4C, Table S2) revealed that a large majority of the 368 genes more highly expressed in *frd3* in control condition were involved in biotic stress (98 genes) hormonal (59 genes) and oxidative stress (43 genes) responses. Metal homeostasis only ranked fourth with 37 genes more highly expressed in *frd3*, among which 26 Fe, 9 Zn and 6 Mn homeostasis related genes, respectively (Figure 4C and 4D). Finally, 14 genes were involved in the nitric oxide (NO) response (Figure 4C). In this list of 368 genes, a number of genes involved in cell wall (CW) modifications were also found (Figure 4E and Table S1), including CW restructuring, CW enzymatic activity regulation or lignin biosynthesis. Although no GO enrichment was observed among the 122 genes less expressed in *frd3* compared to the WT in control condition, this set included a number of genes of interest, such as *FPN1/IREG1* and again genes involved in cell wall modifications (Figure 4D and Table S1).

Upon Zn treatment, the number of DEGs between the WT and the *frd3* mutant strikingly decreased to 5 genes (Figure 4A and 4B). Analysis of genes more highly expressed upon Zn exposure in the *frd3* mutant (139 genes) or the WT (69 genes) compared to the WT in control condition revealed a similar GO enrichment pattern, highlighting a convergence of gene expression profiles in the two genotypes in high Zn condition (Figure 4C). This convergence was achieved by (i) increased expression of Fe and Zn homeostasis genes in the WT, to reach levels similar to the *frd3* mutant (Figure 4C and 4D and Figure S7) and (ii) decreased expression in the *frd3* mutant of all or most genes involved in biotic stress, oxidative stress, hormonal response or Mn homeostasis, respectively (Figure 4C and 4E and Figure S7). The latter group of genes largely

correlated with the 72 down-regulated genes in the *frd3* mutant upon zinc exposure. Altogether, Zn exposure almost totally abolished the secondary gene expression phenotypes of the *frd3* mutant, but the constitutive expression of the Fe deficiency response was maintained.

## Overexpression of the Fe uptake strategy I

RNA-Seq data and GO enrichment analysis showed that many genes involved in the Fe deficiency response were constitutively over-expressed in frd3 roots in both control and high Zn conditions, whereas these genes were induced by high Zn in the WT (Figure 4C and 4D and Table S2). These data were confirmed by RT-qPCR for three key genes of the Fe deficiency response in Arabidopsis: *FIT*, *FRO2* and *IRT1*, with 3-, 26- and 30-fold higher expression in the mutant compared to the WT in control condition, respectively (Figure 5A, 5B and 5C). In high Zn condition, the expression level of three genes was high in both WT and *frd3*. Acidification of the medium, ferric chelate reductase activity and accumulation of the IRT1 protein followed the same pattern (Figure 5D and 5E and Figure S8). Altogether, these data confirmed that the constitutive expression of the Fe deficiency response in the *frd3* mutant was not reversed by high Zn exposure.

#### Oxidative stress in the frd3 mutant roots

Among DEGs, an enrichment of GO terms related to ROS production (43 up-regulated genes) and the NO response (14 up-regulated genes) in the comparison of *frd3* and WT in control condition (Figure 4C and 4E and Table S2) suggested that the mutant roots experienced oxidative stress. This response was partially reversed by high Zn in the mutant, with only 14 and 8 up-regulated genes for ROS production and NO response in mutant exposed to high Zn (Figure 4C, *frd3-*Zn/WT-ctrl comparison). In agreement,  $H_2O_2$  accumulation was observed in the root vascular tissues, but not in the WT, in control conditions (Figure S9A) and was suppressed by exposure to high Zn, but not by Mn deficiency (Figure S9A and 9B). Fe (Figure 2 and Figure 3) and  $H_2O_2$  (Figure S9) accumulation in the *frd3* root vascular tissues thus correlated, suggesting that Fe

accumulation was responsible for oxidative stress. Coherently, no  $H_2O_2$  accumulation (and no Fe accumulation) was observed in *frd3* roots upon Fe deficiency (Figure S5 and Figure S9C).

#### Cell wall analysis by FT-IR

The RNA-Seq data analysis also showed that a number of genes encoding proteins involved in CW modifications were differentially expressed in the *frd3* mutant compared to the WT in control conditions. Those genes were down-regulated or not influenced by high Zn in the mutant (Figure 4E and Table S1-2). To further examine this response, the CW composition in root tissues of WT and *frd3* plants exposed to control and high Zn conditions was analyzed by FT-IR spectroscopy. The spectra were then submitted to a PCA (Figure 6 and Figure S10).

For the 1800-1500 cm<sup>-1</sup> and 1500 and 1300 cm<sup>-1</sup> regions of the FT-IR spectrum (Figure 6A and 6C), the PC1s (86.2% and 85.4% of the total variance, respectively) separated the samples according to the genotype and to a lower extant the condition (Figure 6B and 6D), and were associated with positive (1600 cm<sup>-1</sup>) and negative (1420 cm<sup>-1</sup>) peaks corresponding to the COO<sup>-</sup> antisymmetric and symmetric stretchings, respectively (Sene et al., 1994; Jones et al., 2005), which correlate with unesterified pectins (Jones et al., 2005; Alonso-Simón et al., 2011; Meyer et al., 2015). These observations were indicative of a modification of CW structure in the *frd3* mutant in control condition, corresponding to a lower degree of pectin methylesterification compared to the WT. This is agreement with the lower expression of a pectin methylesterase inhibitor gene (At5g62360) in the mutant compared to the WT in control conditions (Table S1). Upon high Zn exposure, the differences in methylesterification of pectins in the mutant and the WT were attenuated (Figure 6B and 6D).

Moreover, for the 1800-1500 cm<sup>-1</sup> region of the FT-IR spectrum (Figure 6A and 6B), the PC2 (9.52% of the variance) separated the two conditions and can be connected to the amide I and the amide II [respectively the C=O vibration (1650 cm<sup>-1</sup>) and the N-H vibration (1510-1580cm<sup>-1</sup>)]

with the described intensity ratio of 2/1 (Goormaghtigh et al., 1994; Sene et al., 1994) (Figure 6A and 6B), which correlate with proteins (Alonso-Simón et al., 2011). For the 1500-1300 cm<sup>-1</sup> region of the FT-IR spectrum, the PC2 did not clearly separate the samples (Figure 6D). Finally, for the 1300-900 cm<sup>-1</sup> region of the FT-IR spectrum [associated with polysaccharides (Kačuráková et al., 2000)], there was no clear differences between WT and mutant (Figure S10).

#### Metabolite analysis

As the *frd3* mutant phenotype is characterized by altered Fe mobility, which is at least partially reversed upon high Zn exposure, the levels of organic acid (*e.g.* citrate and malate) and nicotianamine (NA) were determined using mass spectrometry in root tissues of WT and *frd3* seedlings exposed to control and high Zn conditions. These metabolites are metal chelators and are involved in metal mobility and sequestration within the plant (Stephan and Scholz, 1993; von Wirén et al., 1999; Haydon and Cobbett, 2007, 2007).

In control condition, the *frd3* mutant accumulated more citrate (2.6-fold), malate (1.6-fold) and NA (7.5-fold) in roots compared to the WT (Figure 7). Upon high Zn exposure, citrate and malate levels remained stable in the mutant but increased to reach similar or higher levels in the WT, respectively (Figure 7A and 7B). In contrast, NA levels were low and stable in the WT and sharply decreased in the mutant exposed to high Zn (Figure 7C). This observation correlated with root transcript levels of *NICOTIANAMINE SYNTHASE 2* (*NAS2*), encoding one of the major form of NA synthase in roots (Klatte et al., 2009): *NAS2* was highly expressed in *frd3* compared to WT in control conditions and was strongly down-regulated in the mutant upon high Zn exposure to low levels similar to the WT (Figure S11).

#### Discussion

The phenotype of a *frd3* mutant in Arabidopsis has been extensively described as a small and chlorotic plant which accumulates Mn, Fe and Zn in tissues and overexpresses the Fe uptake machinery (Delhaize, 1996; Rogers and Guerinot, 2002; Green and Rogers, 2004; Durrett et al.,

2007). It has been attributed to a defect in citrate transport into the xylem in roots and, as a consequence, to the inability of the mutant to translocate Fe to shoot tissues which are iron starved. This phenotype can be rescued by Fe supplementation in the medium. Here, we showed that the *frd3* mutant phenotype not only results from Fe deficiency, but also from multiple consequences of Fe immobilization in roots. Our data also highlighted the tight interconnection between Fe, Zn, Mn homeostasis, oxidative stress and biotic stress responses (Figure 8A).

### Biotic stress signaling and Fe accumulation in the cell wall

We showed that Fe accumulation in root vascular tissues, typical of the frd3 mutant, resulted in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> accumulation (Figure S9 and Figure 8A), which is consistent with the high expression of a large set of oxidative stress genes in frd3 (Figure 4 and Table S2). Fe is highly redox active and known to induce oxidative stress (Stohs and Bagchi, 1995; Pierre and Fontecave, 1999; Marschner and Marschner, 2012). Our data also suggests that Fe and H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> accumulation in the mutant hitchhikes the biotic stress signaling pathway (Figure 8A), as biotic stress response is the largest functional category among genes overexpressed in the mutant compared to the WT in control conditions (Figure 4). As a matter of fact, both Fe and H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> accumulation have been linked to pathogen response. Upon pathogen attack, the role of ROS, including H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, as signals for the induction of defense genes and as weapon for pathogen elimination is well documented (Levine et al., 1994; Torres et al., 2006; Piterková et al., 2013; Aznar et al., 2014; Kadota et al., 2015; Suzuki and Katano, 2018). In addition, several genes involved in Fe or other metal homeostasis, which are highly expressed in the *frd3* mutant (Figure 4D), directly contribute (e.g. MYB72, BGLU42) (Palmer et al., 2013; Zamioudis et al., 2014; Zamioudis et al., 2015) or have been linked (e.g. NRAMP3 and 4, YSL3) (Segond et al., 2009; Chen et al., 2014) to pathogen resistance or plant-microbe beneficial interactions (Verbon et al. 2017; Romera et al. 2019). Several studies also suggested that the Fe status in the plant influences the pathogen virulence (Kieu et al., 2012; Koen et al., 2014). Moreover, it was shown in wheat that a pathogen attack induces a cellular remobilization and accumulation of Fe in the leaf apoplast, causing a local Fe deficiency in the cytosol and a production of ROS resulting in an oxidative burst in the apoplast. Those events trigger the transcriptional activation of pathogen response genes (Liu et al., 2007).

As suggested for NA (Schuler et al., 2011), it thus appears that the defect in citrate transport from the pericycle in the *frd3* mutant, and the resulting Fe deficiency response and accumulation of Fe in the root cell walls, mimics the initial steps of a biotic stress response (Figure 8A), which possibly contributes to the dwarfed phenotype of the mutant.

## Fe and citrate xylem loading coordination

The ferroportin FPN1/IREG1 is suggested to act in Fe loading into the xylem (Morrissey et al., 2009). The down regulation of the *FPN1/IREG1* gene in the roots of the *frd3* mutant compared to the WT is interesting to note (Figure 4D and Table S1). It suggests that Fe translocation to the shoot may be repressed in the *frd3* mutant and that citrate and Fe transport, by FRD3 and FPN1/IREG1, respectively, may be coordinated. How such a coordination can be achieved is unclear. Indeed, in contrast to *FPN2* (Schaaf et al. 2006; Morrissey et al., 2009), *FPN1* and *FRD3* are not part of the FIT regulon (Colangelo et al. 2004; Mai et al. 2016) and are therefore not corregulated with *IRT1*, *FRO2* and others in response to Fe deficiency (Figure 4D and Figure 6). Furthermore, *FRD3* expression, but not *FPN1*, expression is altered in the *pye* mutant (Long et al. 2010).

#### The super toxic Mn

The strong activation of the Fe uptake system in the *frd3* mutant also results in massive Mn accumulation (Delhaize, 1996), essentially in CW (Green and Rogers, 2004), and Mn toxicity (Figure 2 and Figure 3, Figure 8A). Mn is in fact the second most phytotoxic metal (Millaleo et al., 2013) and an excess of Mn was shown to cause growth reduction (Lei et al., 2007; Millaleo et al., 2013), for instance. In the *frd3* mutant, Mn toxicity did not result from  $H_2O_2$  accumulation (Figure S9) (Demirevska-Kepova et al., 2004; Lei et al., 2007).

### CW modification and metal binding

Both FT-IR and RNA-Seq data suggested that the CW structure is modified in the *frd3* mutant, possibly resulting in a lower degree of pectin methylesterification (Figure 6). The CW acts as a barrier preventing metal entry into plant cells, and as an extracellular storage compartment limiting toxic effects of excess metals (Krzesłowska, 2011). The methylesterification degree of pectins is important for the metal tolerance. Indeed, pectins with a low degree of methylation are

rich in free carboxyl groups that can bind di- and trivalent metals. A low degree of pectin methylations has been associated to Fe accumulation in the CW (Ye et al., 2015).

Moreover, high expression of CW biosynthesis genes (*e.g. EXPA1, 4CL1, PRXCA and PRXCB, PAL1 and PAL2*; Figure 4E and Table S1 and 2) was also observed in the mutant (Figure 8A). Several of these genes belong to the phenylpropanoid pathway, which is important for the production of many secondary plant metabolites (e.g. flavonoids), but also for lignin biosynthesis (Le Gall et al., 2015). Metal stress is known to increase lignin biosynthesis (Moura et al., 2010). For instance, lignin biosynthesis is induced during copper (Cu) stress (Ali et al., 2006; Lequeux et al., 2010) or aluminum (Al) exposure (Chandran et al., 2008). Furthermore, the level of ROS (mostly  $H_2O_2$ ) and cell wall peroxidases are important for lignin polymerization and cross-linking of proteins and polysaccharides in the CW (Ranieri et al., 2003).

#### Zn to the rescue

Exposure to high Zn had a strong impact on the *frd3* mutant, with unexpectedly improved root growth compared to control conditions (Figure 1). It reduced or abolished many phenotypes observed in the mutant (Figure 8B): Mn accumulation and toxicity, ROS and Fe accumulation in the vascular tissues, biotic stress response and CW modifications. As highlighted by the PCA of both RNA-Seq and FT-IR data (Figure 4A, Figure 6B and 6D), the molecular phenotypes of the WT and the *frd3* mutant largely converged at high Zn exposure.

The one phenotype that Zn did not alleviate was the constitutive expression Fe uptake mechanisms, which is shared by both WT and mutant at high Zn (Figure 4 and Figure 5). It is well documented that Zn excess causes Fe deficiency in Arabidopsis (Fukao et al., 2011; Shanmugam et al., 2011; Pineau et al., 2012; Shanmugam et al., 2012; Mendoza-Cózatl et al., 2014). In the *frd3* mutant, high expression of the Fe uptake system might be triggered by a similar response or by a shoot-born Fe deficiency signal or both (Figure 4, Figure 5, Figure 8B and Figure S7 and Figure 7).

The reversal of the other *frd3* phenotypes by high Zn possibly results from competition between Zn, Fe and Mn for transport and/or binding (Figure 8B). Indeed, according to the Irving Williams series (Pd > Cu > Ni > Co > Zn > Cd > Fe > Mn > Mg), each metal presents a different affinity for potential binding sites and therefore metal replacements can occur (Irving and Williams, 1953; Krämer et al., 2007). For instance, in the presence of equimolar concentrations of Zn, Cu and Fe, Cu and Zn are coupled to ligands to the detriment of Fe (Gayomba et al., 2015). It is also admitted for a long time that the CW is a major actor for Zn tolerance by governing the cellular pool of free Zn in plants (Turner, 1970; Turner and Marshall, 1972), with the root apoplast being a major Zn storage compartment (Schmid et al., 1965; Jiang and Wang, 2008).

In the *frd3* mutant at high Zn exposure, we suggest several hypothesis. First, Zn may displace Fe from its binding sites in the CW. As Zn is a non-redox active metal, contrary to Fe (Broadley et al., 2007), its interactions with thiol groups in the CW would prevent oxidative damages, abolishing the oxidative stress response and hence the biotic stress response observed in the mutant in control conditions (Figure 8B). It was shown that the Fe deficiency response and the induced systemic resistance (ISR) in the presence of beneficial microbes share ~20% of differentially expressed genes, suggesting that the Fe deficiency response is part of ISR (Zamioudis et al. 2015). As it is abolished by high Zn, the biotic response in the frd3 mutant clearly results from Fe, and consequently  $H_2O_2$ , accumulation in the CW, and is not triggered by Fe deficiency, which persists in the mutant. Moreover, the CW modifications observed upon high Zn in the mutant (Figure 6) can also be explained by reduced oxidative burden, which is, among others, important for lignin polymerization (Ranieri et al., 2003), and by increased methylesterification of pectins triggered by Zn in the CW (Muschitz et al., 2015). Alternatively, Zn may also alter intracellular trafficking of Fe, via its strong effect on NA root content (see below and Figure 7C) (Klatte et al., 2009; Haydon et al., 2012), to limit Fe access to the CW (Figure 8B). It is also possible that Zn acts both in the apoplast and in the cytosol (Figure 8B).

High Zn exposure has a dramatic effect on Mn accumulation in the *frd3* mutant (Figure 2 and Figure S3), most likely resulting from competition for uptake between Mn and Zn, possibly via ZIP transporters that are highly expressed in the mutant (Figure 4D and Tables S1 and S2). As Mn accumulation is toxic for the *frd3* mutant (Figure 3), its reduction is certainly contributing to improved growth of the mutant at high Zn (Figure 8B).

Finally, as part of the Fe deficiency response (Brouquisse et al., 1986; de Vos et al., 1986; Abadía et al., 2002; Rogers and Guerinot, 2002; Li et al., 2008), citrate, malate and NA accumulated in the roots of the *frd3* mutant in control condition (Figure 7 and Figure 8A). Citrate accumulation occurred in the mutant, despite a 40% reduction in citrate level in the xylem sap

(Rogers and Guerinot, 2002). NA accumulation correlated with high expression of *NAS2* (Figure S11), which is known to be induced by Fe deficiency in roots (Klatte et al., 2009). At high Zn, the organic acid concentrations remained high in the *frd3* mutant and increased in the WT, in agreement with the observed positive correlation between organic acids and Zn tolerance (Godbold et al., 1983; Godbold et al., 1984) and their accumulation at Fe deficiency (Brouquisse et al., 1986; de Vos et al., 1986; Abadía et al., 2002; Li et al., 2008). In contrast, NA levels, and *NAS2* expression (Figure 7C and Figure S11), were strongly reduced in the *frd3* mutant upon high Zn exposure, suggesting that a hierarchy exists between the activating and repressive effects of Fe (Klatte et al., 2009) and Zn (Talke et al., 2006) on *NAS2* expression, respectively (Figure 8B).

#### Conclusion

We reported here that the *frd3* mutant phenotype results from mechanisms more complex than Fe mislocalization and deficiency. Indeed, we showed that Mn toxicity significantly contributes to the root growth defect in the mutant and that Fe mislocalization in CW triggers a biotic stress response through ROS production. We also showed that Zn, by effectively competing with Fe in the CW, can interfere with these mechanisms and restore root growth in the mutant. Altogether, our data shed light on the interaction that exist between Fe, Zn and Mn homeostasis and the oxidative and biotic stress responses, and the key role of the CW in these processes.

#### **Experimental procedures**

#### Plant material and growth conditions

All experiments were conducted with *Arabidopsis thaliana* [Arabidopsis, accession Columbia-0 (Col-0)] as Wild-Type and the Arabidopsis *frd3-7* mutant (Col-0 background) (Roschzttardtz et al., 2011). Seeds were surface-sterilized and germinated on 1/2 MS medium (Duchefa Biochimie) supplemented with sucrose (1% w/v, Duchefa Biochimie) and agar (0.8% w/v Select Agar, Sigma-Aldrich). For hydroponics experiments, the seedlings were transferred 2 weeks after germination in hydroponic trays (Araponics), grown for 3 weeks in control Hoagland medium, then submitted to experimental conditions for an additional 3 weeks. The nutrient solution was exchanged with

fresh medium once a week and last 3 days before harvest. For plate experiments, seedlings were transferred 5 days after germination on solid Hoagland (0.8% w/v Select Agar in square plastic Petri plates, Greiner Bio-One). The control Hoagland medium was modified as in (Talke et al., 2006; Hanikenne et al., 2008) and included 1 $\mu$ M Zn (ZnSO<sub>4</sub>.H<sub>2</sub>O), 5 $\mu$ M Mn (MnSO<sub>4</sub>.H<sub>2</sub>O) and 10 $\mu$ M FeIII-HBED [N, N' – di (2-hydroxybenzyl) ethylenediamine N, N' – diacetic acid monohydrochloride]. Zn, Mn and/or Fe were omitted from the medium for deficiency experiments and were added as indicated for excess experiments. All experiments were realized under 8h light (100  $\mu$ mol photon m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>, 22°C)/16h dark (20°C) in a climate-controlled growth chamber. Unless otherwise stated, all experiments were conducted three times independently. Details on experimental replication are provided in figure legends.

#### Chlorophyll content

Two to three leaves per plant were weighed, then incubated in the dark during 40h in ethanol (95%, VWR). Discolored leaves were removed and the solution was then analyzed using a spectrophotometer (Genesys20; Thermo Scientific), measuring absorbance at 649nm and 665nm. Total amounts of chlorophyll were calculated using the equation: chla+b = [6,1\*(A665) +20,4\*(A649)]/fresh weight (in mg) (Wintermans et De Mots, 1965; Lichtenthaler et Buschmann, 2001).

#### Analysis of metal contents

Root and shoot tissues were harvested separately. Root tissues were desorbed and washed as described (Talke et al., 2006), and shoot tissues were rinsed in distilled water. After drying at 60°C for 3 days, 10-30mg of tissue were digested on a DigiPrep Graphite Block Digestion System (SCP Science) with 3mL HNO<sub>3</sub> ( $\geq$ 65% v/v; Sigma-Aldrich) in DigiPrep tubes. The digestion program was: 15 min. at 45°C, 15 min. at 65°C, and 90 min. at 105°C. After digestion, volumes were adjusted to 10 mL with distilled water and 200 µL of HNO<sub>3</sub> ( $\geq$ 65% v/v; Sigma-Aldrich). Element concentrations were determined by inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectroscopy (Vista AX, Varian).

## Fe and hydrogen peroxide staining

For Fe Perls staining, root samples were vacuum infiltrated with HCl (4% v/v) and K-Ferrocyanide (II) (4% w/v; Sigma-Aldrich) at equal volume (1/1) for 10 min. and incubated for 30min. at room temperature. The reaction was stopped by substituting the solution with distilled water (Roschzttardtz et al., 2009; Roschzttardtz et al., 2011). Images were taken using a binocular (SMZ1500; Nikon) equipped with a camera (Digital Sight DS-5M; Nikon). For In Situ Perls/DAB/H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> intensification, root tissues were vacuum infiltrated with paraformaldehyde (2% w/v), glutaraldehyde (1% v/v), and caffeine (1% w/v) in phosphate buffer (0.1 M; pH 7) for 30 min. and incubated overnight in the solution (Roschzttardtz et al., 2009; Roschzttardtz et al., 2011). The tissues were then washed 3 times in phosphate buffer (0.1M, pH7.4) and dehydrated in successive bathes for 2h each (50%, 70%, 90%, 95%, 100% ethanol, butanol/ethanol (1/1), 100% butanol). Technovit 7100 resin (Kulzer) was used to embed tissues and 3 $\mu$ m sections were realized and deposited on glass slides. The slides were then incubated for 45 min. in perls staining solution (as above), followed by DAB intensification as described (Roschzttardtz et al., 2009; Roschzttardtz et al., 2011). Observation was realized using a binocular (AZ100; Nikon) equipped with a camera (Digital Sight DS-Ri1; Nikon).

Detection of  $H_2O_2$  in root tissues was carried out according to (Baliardini et al., 2015). Briefly, root samples were vacuum infiltrated 15 min. with 3-3'-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (1.25mg/mL; DAB; Sigma-Aldrich), tween-20 (0.05% v/v) and Na<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub> (200mM) and incubated 45 min. in the same solution. Then the roots were bleached in acetic-acid/glycerol/ethanol (1/1/3) during 5 min. at 100°C, and then stored in glycerol/ethanol (1/4) before observation. Observation was performed using a binocular (SMZ1500; Nikon) equipped with a camera (Digital Sight DS-5M; Nikon).

## Zinc imaging

Root tissues were washed 3 times 5 min. in distilled water then 5 min. in EDTA (10mM) to remove metal ions attached to the root surface. Plants were transferred in Zinpyr-1 (10 $\mu$ M, in water, Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 hours in the dark then washed in distilled water (Sinclair et al., 2007;

Hanikenne et al., 2008). To visualize root cell wall, tissues were bathed in propidium iodide  $(33\mu g/mL)$  for 1 min. Images were collected using a SP2 confocal microscope (Leica, Mannheim, Germany) as described (Tillemans et al., 2006). Zinpyr-1 was visualized by using an Argon/Ion laser ( $\lambda$ ex: 488 nm;  $\lambda$ em: 500-535 nm) and a Helium/Neon laser ( $\lambda$ ex: 543 nm;  $\lambda$ em: 600-730 nm) was used for propidium iodide.

## RNA-Seq and RT-qPCR analysis

Upon harvesting, plant root tissues were blotted dry, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C. Total RNAs were prepared using 100 mg of homogenized tissues and RNeasy Plant Mini kit with on-column DNase treatment (Qiagen). Libraries for RNA-Seq were prepared from 1 µg of total RNAs with the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, CA, USA), multiplexed and sequenced in two runs with an Illumina NextSeq500 device (high throughput mode, 75 base single-end reads) at the GIGA-R Sequencing platform (University of Liège), yielding on average ~22 million reads per sample. Read quality was assessed using FastQC (v0.10.1, http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Quality trimming and removal of adapters were conducted using Trimmomatic (v0.32, Bolger et al., 2014), with the following parameters: trim bases with quality score lower than Q26 in 5' and 3' of reads; remove any reads with O<26 in any sliding window of 10 bases; crop 1 base in 3' of all reads, and discard reads shorter than 70 bases. Overall quality filtering discarded between 7 and 9% of the raw reads. The Arabidopsis reference genome sequence (TAIR10) and annotation (201606 version) files were downloaded from Araport on Sept 16, 2016 (www.araport.org). Read mapping on the genome was achieved using TopHat (v2.1.1), with the following parameters: --read-mismatches 2; --min-intron-length 40; --max-intron-length 2000; 2 --report-secondary-alignments; --no-noveljuncs and providing an indexed genome annotation file. Raw read counts were obtained using htseq-count (v0.6.1p1) and differentially expressed genes were identified by pairwise comparisons with the DESeq2 package (v1.12.3, Love et al., 2014). Genes were retained as differentially expressed when the  $\log_2$  fold-change (FC) was > 1 or < -1, with a false discovery rate (FDR, Benjamini-Hochberg) adjusted *p*-value of < 0.05. Principal Component Analysis plots (PCA) were created with the *PlotPCA* function from R using rlog transformed data (Beginner's guide, DESeq2) package, Mav 13. 2014.

http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/2.14/bioc/vignettes/DESeq2/inst/doc/beginner.pdf). GO enrichment analyses were conducted using the Thalemine tool on Araport (http://www.araport.org). The heatmaps were constructed using the heatmap.2 function of the gplots R package.

For RT-qPCR, cDNAs were synthesized with the RevertAid H Minus First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Fisher Scientific) using Oligo(dT) and 1 µg of total RNAs. Quantitative PCR were performed in an ABI Prism 7900HT system (Applied Biosystems) using a 384-well plates and Mesa Green qPCR MasterMix (Eurogentec) as described (Nouet et al., 2015). Primer reaction efficiencies [LinRegPCR software (v2016.1) (Ruijter et al., 2009); Table S3; (Nouet et al., 2015)] were used to calculate relative gene expression levels by normalization using the *EF1* $\alpha$  and *UBQ10* reference genes with the qBase software [Biogazelle; (Hellemans et al., 2007)]. Their adequacy to normalize gene expression in the experimental conditions was verified using the geNorm software in qBase (Vandesompele et al., 2002).

## IRT1 western blot

Total proteins were extracted from 100mg of frozen root tissues as described (Séguéla et al., 2008). The western blot was conducted using an IRT1 antibody, as described (Schvartzman et al., 2018).

#### Acidification and FeIII chelate reductase activity assays

The FeIII chelate reductase activity and acidification assays were conducted as described (Yi and Guerinot, 1996). For the acidification assay, seedlings were transferred after 3 days of treatment on Bromocresol media [Bromocresol Purple (0.006% w/v; Roth); CaSO<sub>4</sub> (0.2mM); agar (Agar Select 0.8% w/v; Sigma-Aldrich); pH6.5] for 18h. For the FeIII chelate reductase activity assays, seedlings were pooled (5 by 5) and roots were immerged in the reductase solution [FeIII-EDTA (0.1mM; Roth); FerroZine (0.3mM; Acros Organics)] for 20 min. in the dark. The absorbance of FeII- FerroZine complex formed in the solution was measured at 562nm. The root weight of

sample and the molar extinction coefficient of the complex (28.6mM<sup>-1</sup>.cm<sup>-1</sup>) were used for the final calculation.

### FT-IR analysis

Cell walls (CW) extraction and FT-IR analysis from roots and shoots was performed according to (Zornoza et al., 2002) and (Meyer et al., 2015). Root samples (100 mg dry material) were washed consecutively 3 times in ethanol (80% v/v), once in chloroform/methanol (2/1 v/v) and 3 times in acetone. For each washing step, samples were vortexed, agitated (30 min.) and centrifuged (1200g; 5 min.) before going to the next step. Final pellets of CW were dried overnight ( $67^{\circ}$ C).

All measurement was performed according to (Meyer et al., 2015), with a Bruker Equinox 55 FT-IR spectrometer (Bruker) equipped with a liquid  $N_2$  refrigerated mercury cadmium Telluride detector as described (Goormaghtigh et al., 1999; Meyer et al., 2015). To detect CW modification, the FT-IR measurements were recorded between 1800 cm<sup>-1</sup> and 800 cm<sup>-1</sup>. Each spectrum was obtained by averaging 128 scans recorded at a resolution of 2 cm<sup>-1</sup>. FT-IR data were analyzed (substraction of water vapor contribution, baseline correction and normalization of the spectra) as described (Meyer et al., 2015). To identify differences among populations, PCA was used to reveal spectral contributions that explained most of the variance present in the data set (Johnson and Wichern, 2002).

### Mass spectrometry

Samples were prepared based as described (Klatte et al., 2009; Haydon et al., 2012). Frozen plant tissues (60 mg) were grinded to fine powder in a tissue grinding mill (1 min., 25Hz, cold with liquid nitrogen, MM200, Retsch) and extracted in distilled water at 80°C (500  $\mu$ L; pre-warmed at 60°C). After a shaking (500 rpm for 15 min.), both extraction step and shaking were repeated. Cell debris were removed from the samples by two centrifugation (15000g, 20 min.). At first, a mass spectrometry analysis of standard solutions of malate (1mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), citrate (1 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) and nicotianamine (1mg/mL; Carbosynth) was performed to determine each m/z and make a calibration curve. To quantify malate, citrate and nicotianamine, each sample

was analyzed using the standard addition method. All measurements were performed on the SYNAPT G2 HDMS spectrometer (Waters, Manchester, UK) fitted with an electrospray ionization source used in negative mode. The instrument has been extensively described previously (Giles et al., 2004; Pringle et al., 2007; Shvartsburg, 2008; Shvartsburg and Smith, 2008). A weighed standard tartaric acid (Sigma-Aldrich) solution was prepared with Milli-Q water (Millipore) at an exact concentration is 2.35 10<sup>-4</sup> M and used as standard as it is absent in Arabidopsis (DeBolt et al., 2006). Each sample was spiked with the standard solution as following: approximately 150 mg of the sample were accurately weighed on an analytical balance (ML 54; Mettler Toledo) in which about 10 mg of tartaric acid and 100 mg of acetonitrile were added by analytical weighing.

#### Statistical analysis

All data evaluation and statistics were done using GraphPad Prism 5 (GraphPad Software; version 5.03).
