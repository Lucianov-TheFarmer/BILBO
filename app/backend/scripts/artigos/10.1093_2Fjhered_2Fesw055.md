## Abstract

Spinach (Spinacia oleracea, 2n = 12) and sugar beet (Beta vulgaris, 2n = 18) are important crop members of the family Chenopodiaceae s.s. Sugar beet has a basic chromosome number of nine and a cosexual breeding system, as do most members of the Chenopodiaceae s.s. family. By contrast, spinach has a basic chromosome number of six and, although certain cultivars and genotypes produce monoecious plants, is considered to be a dioecious species. The loci determining male and monoecious sexual expression were mapped to different loci on the spinach sex chromosomes. In this study, a linkage map with 46 mapped protein-coding sequences was constructed for the spinach sex chromosomes. Comparison of the linkage map with a reference genome sequence of sugar beet revealed that the spinach sex chromosomes exhibited extensive synteny with sugar beet chromosomes 4 and 9. Tightly-linked protein-coding genes linked to the male-determining locus in spinach corresponded to genes located in or around the putative pericentromeric and centromeric regions of sugar beet chromosomes 4 and 9, supporting the observation that recombination rates were low in the vicinity of the male-determining locus. The locus for monoecism was confined to a

chromosome 9, which may facilitate future positional cloning of the locus.

## Introduction

Spinach (Spinacia oleracea, 2n = 2x = 12), which belongs to the subfamily Chenopodioideae within the goosefoot family (Chenopodiaceae s.s.), is a highly nutritious leafy vegetable crop that is cultivated worldwide. Members of the Spinacia genus are commonly dioecious, but certain spinach cultivars and genotypes produce monoecious plants (Onodera et al. 2008). Dioecism and monoecism are of relevance in commercial hybrid seed production, and elucidation of the mechanisms underlying sex determination is valuable for spinach breeding programs (Onodera et al. 2011). Self-pollinated progeny plants from male Long Standing Bloomsdale plants, which occasionally produced seed, segregated as males and females, suggesting that spinach had a male-heterogametic (XX/XY) sex determination system (Janick and Stevenson 1954). Sexual dimorphism in dioecious spinach lines was therefore interpreted to be controlled by an allelic pair termed 'X' and 'Y', and the male-determining locus (Y) was mapped to the largest chromosome (hereafter referred to as the sex chromosome). The sex chromosomes containing 'X' and 'Y', namely X and Y, do not differ from each other morphologically (Janick and Stevenson 1955; Janick et al. 1959; Ellis and Janick 1960).

Our previous research identified a locus, termed M/m, that is responsible for the monoecious condition in spinach line 03-336 and linked to the X/Y locus at a distance of approximately 13 cM (Yamamoto et al. 2014). Female plants of the dioecious line 03-009 pollinated by the monoecious line 03-336 produced monoecious progeny with the proportion of female flowers approximately intermediate between those of the parental values, suggesting that the allele (M) determining the monoecious condition in line 03-336 would be incompletely dominant to the allele (m) in line 03-009 (Onodera et al. 2008, 2011). Furthermore, genetic analysis suggested that Y and M would be epistatic to M and X, respectively: XXmm is female, XYMM, XYMm and XYmm are male, and XXMM and XXMm produce a monoecious phenotype (Onodera et al. 2011; Yamamoto et al. 2014). However, the genes determining male and monoecious sexual expression have not yet been isolated or characterized.

Although the basic chromosome number x = 9 is prevalent in *Chenopodiaceae* s.s., a recent molecular phylogenetic analysis suggests independent dysploid chromosome loss in two lineages in the subfamily *Chenopodioideae*: one is found in *Spinacia* (2n = 2x = 12) (Kühn *et al.* 1993; Fuentes-Bazan *et al.* 2012a). Cosexual conditions (e.g.,

monoecious and hermaphrodite) are predominant breeding systems in *Chenopodiaceae* s.s., and the cosexual genus *Blitum* (x = 9) is sister to *Spinacia* within the tribe *Anserineae* of *Chenopodioideae*, suggesting that spinach sex-determining genes evolved recently from autosomal gene(s) in an ancestral cosexual species, and that descending dysploidy occurred during *Spinacia* evolution (Kühn *et al.* 1993; Fuentes-Bazan *et al.* 2012a; Fuentes-Bazan *et al.* 2012b). According to another recent molecular phylogenetic analysis, which was based on chloroplast sequence variation (*rbcL* gene, *atpB-rbcL* spacer), the divergence time between *Spinacia* and *Blitum* (*Chenopodium* s.l.) is estimated to be ~15.4 (based on *rbcL*) and ~17.4 (*atpB-rbcL*) million years ago

The sugar beet genome is estimated to be 714–758 megabases (Mb) in size. Its genetic and physical maps, which cover all nine chromosomes, and a reference genome sequence comprising 567 Mb (N50 length = 2.0 Mb) were recently generated (Dohm, *et al.* 2012; Dohm *et al.* 2014). Here, we determine the regions of the spinach sex chromosome that control dioecism and monoecism. Molecular markers were designed in protein-coding genes from regions of predicted synteny between sugar beet autosomes and the spinach sex chromosomes, based on preliminary genetic mapping. ## Material and methods

## Plant materials

*Spinacia* plants for total RNA preparation were cultivated as follows. Plants from a dioecious line, 03-009, and a monoecious line, 03-336, (Onodera *et al.* 2008) were grown in a growth chamber (LH-350S, Nippon Medical & Chemical Instruments Co. Ltd, Osaka, Japan) at 20°C under a 12 h photoperiod until 6–7 true leaves had emerged (within the initial 20 days for 03-009, and the initial 28 days for 03-336), and then were grown under a 20 h photoperiod for the following 3 days.

A *Spinacia* population (S<sub>2</sub>BC<sub>2</sub>F<sub>1</sub> of the 03–009 × 03–336 cross) was developed by twice selfing a monoecious selection from 'Test-cross population B' (BC<sub>2</sub>F<sub>1</sub> of the 03– 009 × 03–336 cross) in Yamamoto *et al.* (2014) and was used to locate the locus responsible for monoecious expression (Supplementary Figure S1). Plants from this population were grown in a growth chamber (LH-350S, Nippon Medical & Chemical Instruments Co. Ltd, Osaka, Japan) at 20°C with an 8 h photoperiod for the first 3 weeks, at 25°C with an 8 h photoperiod during the next 3 weeks, and subsequently at 25°C with a 24 h photoperiod. In this study, plants bearing more than 20 flower clusters, each of which consisted of 5–10 flowers, were classified for femaleness (expressed as an index based on the percentage of female flowers per plant). Plants with femaleness ratings of >95–100% were classified as female. Plants in the >0–95% femaleness classes were considered monoecious.

Database searches

Sugar beet gene sequence and chromosomal data were obtained from Dohm *et al.* (2014). To identify corresponding spinach homologs for sugar beet genes, the scaffold dataset for the spinach draft genome sequence (Spinach-1.0.1) and the spinach transcript sequence set (SpiSet-1) (Dohm *et al.* 2014 [http://bvseq.molgen.mpg.de]) were searched using the Blastn program implemented in GENETIX-MAC ver. 16 (GENETYX CORPORATION, Tokyo, Japan), using an e-value threshold of 1e-100. Converse searches were performed using the reference genome sequences for the sugar beet

genome (RefBeet-1.1 and -1.2) (Dohm et al. 2014 [http://bvseq.molgen.mpg.de]).

Identification of variants in the spinach genome

Sequence variations between spinach lines 03-336 and 03-009 (single nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs] and a microsatellite variant) were identified in protein-coding genes. Variants were identified through screening of genomic clones isolated from 03-336 and 03-009 BAC libraries (unpublished) with primers complementary to the protein-coding genes. Genomic PCR fragments generated using primers designed against DNA sequences of the spinach BAC clones or scaffold sequences for the draft spinach genome (Dohm *et al.* 2014) were also searched for allelic differences between spinach line 03-009 and lines 03-336 or 03-259.

For efficient identification of SNPs, RNA-seq analysis of spinach lines 03-336 and 03-009 was also performed. Total RNA was extracted from whole aerial plant parts using an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit combined with an RNase-Free DNase Set (QIAGEN, Venlo Netherlands). Equal amounts of RNA isolated from five 03-009 individuals or from four 03-336 individuals were used for cDNA library preparation with a SureSelect Strand Specific RNA Library Preparation Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Libraries were sequenced using a HiSeq 2500 Instrument (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA) configured to generate 100 bp paired-end reads. Approximately 3.2 Gb and 5.2 Gb of raw RNA-seq reads were obtained from spinach lines 03-009 and 03-336, respectively. Reads were mapped to the spinach draft genome sequence (Spinach-1.0.1, http://bvseq.molgen.mpg.de/index.shtml) using the TopHat program. Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) software (Robinson *et al.* 2011) was used to visualize the aligned reads and SNPs. SNP alleles from lines 03-259, 03-336, and 03-009 were validated using DNA markers developed in the present study.

DNA marker development and genotyping

With microsatellite the exception of a variant in the gene encoding phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (ppc), all variants used as markers in this study were SNPs (Supplementary Tables S1, S2 and S3). Cleaved Amplified Polymorphic Sequence (CAPS) and derived Cleaved Amplified Polymorphic Sequence (dCAPS) marker techniques (Neff et al. 2002), and an allele-specific amplification technique employing a 3'-terminal mismatch primer (Fukuoka et al. 2008), were used for genotyping SNPs.

Previously developed expressed sequence tag (EST)/gene-derived microsatellite markers SO1, SO3, SO6, SO7, SO9, SO10, SO13, SO14, SO17, SO19, SO22, SO28, SO29, SO32, SO35, SO42, SO48, and SO51, which were developed by Groben and Wricke (1998) and Khattak *et al.* (2007), were also used in this study. Amplification parameters and marker detection were performed as described therein.

Linkage analysis and genetic map construction

Two spinach populations, 03-259 × 03-009 BC<sub>1</sub>F<sub>1</sub> (n = 121) and 03-009 × 03-336 F<sub>2</sub> (n = 70), which were used for mapping the male determining locus (*Y*) and the monoecious gene (*M*), respectively, in our previous studies (see Onodera *et al.* 2011 and Yamamoto *et al.* 2014 for details), were subjected to linkage analysis. Linkage analysis and genetic map-construction were performed as described by Yamamoto *et al.* (2014). The previously developed microsatellite markers (described above) and the newly developed markers were assessed for linkage to the spinach sex chromosomes (or sex determining genes) using genotypic data for pre-existing sex-linked markers and phenotypic data for the mapping populations (Onodera *et al.* 2011 and Yamamoto *et al.* 2014). MergeMap

Spinacia species are placed as a sister lineage to the taxon comprising polygamo-monoecious (plants with bisexual flowers and unisexual flowers) species (Kadereit et al., 2010). Given that monoecy (or polygamo-monoecious) is the ancestral sex form in the taxon, and that in the early evolution of dioecy the male-sterile and female-suppressing mutations produced 'initial males' with a monoecious condition to some degree, an additional mutation(s) in the vicinity of a proto-male-determining locus could have produced an allele with sexually antagonistic effects that increased the proportion invested in male flowers and decreased the proportion invested in female flowers, resulting in the initial males having a fully- (or almost fully) male phenotype (Charlesworth 2015). As our previous studies suggested that an increase (or decrease) in the dose of *M* allele tended to result in a shift toward a lower (or higher) proportion of female flowers in monoecious plants (Onodera et al. 2008; Onodera et al. 2011), it seems likely that M controls the proportion of female and male flowers and corresponds to the allele with sexually antagonistic effects. Recent studies have reported that the pseudo-autosomal region of the Silene latifolia sex chromosome pair has expanded via translocations or fusions involving autosomal regions (Bergero et. 2013; Qiu et al. 2015). As mentioned in the Introduction section, other than *Spinacia*, descending dysploidy (x = 8) was found in the genus Dysphania (Chenopodium s.l.) of the subfamily Chenopodioideae, whose members are cosexual. Therefore, unlike Silene latifolia, the chromosome rearrangement events found in the subfamily, which may involve chromosomal fusions, may not necessarily be related to sex chromosome evolution. Our recent work showed that a wild relative of spinach, Spinacia tetrandra, possesses a heteromorphic sex chromosome pair (XY), and that the Spinacia tetrandra sex chromosomes are homologs of spinach sex chromosomes, on which homologs of sugar beet chromosomes 4 and 9 genes (nir and *clc-e*) are located (Fujito *et al.* 2015). This suggests that the chromosomal fusion event occurred prior to the divergence of the species and is not related to the predicted evolutionary transition from homomorphic to heteromorphic sex chromosomes.
