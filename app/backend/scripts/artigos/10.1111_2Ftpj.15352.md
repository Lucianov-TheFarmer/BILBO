High-light (HL) stress enhances the production of H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> from the photosynthetic electron transport chain in chloroplasts, potentially causing photooxidative damage. Although stromal and thylakoid membrane-bound ascorbate peroxidases (sAPX and tAPX, respectively) are major H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> scavenging enzymes in chloroplasts, their knockout mutants do not exhibit a visible phenotype under HL stress. There are trans-thylakoid  $\Delta pH$ -dependent mechanisms for controlling H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> production from photosynthesis, such as thermal dissipation of light energy and down-regulation of electron transfer between photosystem II and I, which may compensate for the lack of APXs. To test this hypothesis, we focused on a proton gradient regulation 5 (pgr5) mutant, wherein both  $\Delta p$ H-dependent mechanisms are impaired, and an *Arabidopsis sapx tapx* double mutant was crossed with the pgr5 single mutant. The sapx tapx pgr5 triple mutant exhibited extreme sensitivity to HL compared to its parental lines. This phenotype was consistent with cellular redox perturbations and enhanced expression of many oxidative stress-responsive genes. These findings demonstrate that the PGR5-dependent mechanisms compensate for chloroplast APXs and vice versa. An intriguing finding was that the failure of non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) induction in pgr5 (because of the limitation in  $\Delta pH$  formation) was partially recovered in sapx tapx pgr5. Further genetic studies suggested that this recovery was dependent on the NADH dehydrogenase-like complex (NDH)-dependent pathway for cyclic electron flow around photosystem I. Together with data from the sapx tapx npq4 mutant, we discuss the interrelationship between APXs and  $\Delta p$ H-dependent mechanisms under HL stress.

### SIGNIFICANCE STATEMENT:

Chloroplast ascorbate peroxidases (APXs) and proton gradient regulation 5 (PGR5) restrict  $H_2O_2$ levels under high light by scavenging and by limiting production, respectively, but their functional relationship is largely unknown. Our current genetic study using Arabidopsis plants clearly shows that cooperation of APXs and PGR5 plays a critical role in photoprotection.

#### INTRODUCTION

Photosynthesis can be a significant source of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in illuminated leaves, such as hydrogen peroxide ( $H_2O_2$ ), superoxide radical ( $O_2^-$ ), singlet oxygen, and hydroxyl radical (Asada, 1999). These reactive molecules can potentially oxidize and destroy cellular components, such as proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, which ultimately lead to cell death (Apel and Hirt, 2004). In contrast to their cytotoxic effects, ROS have another function as signaling molecules that can activate defense pathways to cope with environmental stresses (Mignolet-Spruyt *et al.*, 2016; Mittler, 2017; He *et al.*, 2018). The signaling functions of ROS are also associated with the regulation of plant growth, development, metabolism, and programmed cell death (Mittler *et al.*, 2011; Mhamdi and Van Breusegem, 2018). Plant stress acclimation requires a tight regulation of the balance between their production and scavenging.

In the linear electron flow, electrons excised from water splitting at photosystem II (PSII) are transferred to NADP<sup>+</sup> through PSI, which results in the formation of NADPH as a reducing equivalent (Asada, 1999). This flow also generates a proton gradient ( $\Delta$ pH) across the

thylakoid membrane to drive ATP synthesis. The NADPH and ATP produced are then used in the Calvin-Benson cycle to fix CO<sub>2</sub>. Under high-light (HL) stress, excess excitation energy facilitates the rate of NADPH production, which often exceeds its consumption rate, thereby leading to a decrease in the availability of NADP<sup>+</sup> as a terminal electron acceptor. This situation can also occur even under low-light (LL) conditions if other abiotic stresses inhibit the Calvin-Benson cycle (e.g., drought, salinity, and low temperature). The resulting over-reduction of the photosynthetic electron transport chain facilitates electron transfer from PSI to molecular oxygen, generating  $O_2^{+}$  through the Mehler reaction (Asada, 1999). The  $O_2^{+}$  radicals are then disproportionated into  $O_2$  and  $H_2O_2$  spontaneously or enzymatically by the reaction of superoxide dismutases (SODs). Two isoforms of ascorbate peroxidases (APXs), stromal sAPX and thylakoid membrane-bound tAPX, catalyze the reduction of  $H_2O_2$  into water using ascorbate as an electron donor (Asada, 1999; Shigeoka *et al.*, 2002; Maruta *et al.*, 2016). This ROS metabolism begins from water splitting at PSII and ends with water formation around PSI, the so-called water-water cycle (Asada, 1999).

In terrestrial plants, both sAPX and tAPX are highly conserved and have been considered to be the bottleneck enzymes in photooxidative stress tolerance (Maruta *et al.*, 2016). This idea was primarily based on an intriguing feature of chloroplast APXs; that is, these enzymes are highly sensitive to  $H_2O_2$  under low ascorbate levels (Chen and Asada, 1989; Miyake and Asada, 1996). When ascorbate concentration is deficient (less than 20  $\mu$ M), chloroplast APXs are immediately inactivated by  $H_2O_2$  within 15 s, while cytosolic and peroxisomal isoforms are much more stable. The inactivation of chloroplast APXs actually occurs *in vivo* under photooxidative stress (Yoshimura *et al.*, 2000; Miyake *et al.*, 2006). Attempts to reinforce the fragile nature of chloroplast APXs have been made by overexpressing  $H_2O_2$ -scavenging enzymes within

chloroplasts and have succeeded in improving the stress resistance of several plant species (e.g., Shikanai et al., 1998; Yabuta et al., 2002). Thus, it was expected that the loss-of-function of chloroplastic APXs results in severe growth defects, light sensitivity, or even lethality in plants (Yabuta *et al.*, 2002; Rizhsky *et al.*, 2003). However, *Arabidopsis* and rice mutants that either lack or underexpress chloroplastic APXs have been found to not exhibit any stress-sensitive phenotypes under laboratory conditions (Giacomelli et al., 2007; Kangasjarvi et al., 2008; Maruta et al., 2010; Caverzan et al., 2014). Therefore, the role of chloroplast APXs as antioxidative defense enzymes remains unclear. In addition to APXs, Arabidopsis chloroplasts employ six thiol peroxidases, namely two 2-Cys peroxiredoxins (2CPA and 2CPB), one peroxiredoxin Q (PrxQ), one type II peroxiredoxin (PrxIIE), and two glutathione peroxidase-like proteins (GpxL1 and GpxL7) (Chang et al., 2009; Dietz, 2016). It is easy to imagine that these thiol-based enzymes may compensate for the lack of chloroplast APXs. Indeed, a severe oxidative damage phenotype was recently observed in an Arabidopsis 2cpa 2cpb tapx triple mutant compared to 2cpa 2cpb double and *tapx* single mutants under HL (Awad *et al.*, 2015), thereby indicating the overlapping role of APXs and 2CPs.

An alternative explanation for the negligible phenotype of apx mutants may be provided by the existence of  $\Delta p$ H-dependent mechanisms that prevent the overreduction of PSII and PSI. One such mechanism involves the xanthophyll cycle that contributes to dissipate excess light energy as heat, which is a major component of non-photochemical quenching (NPQ), a chlorophyll fluorescence parameter (Li *et al.*, 2000). The absence of the PsbS protein encoded by the *NPQ4* gene prevents NPQ induction and accelerates PSII photoinhibition under HL (Li *et al.*, 2002). Another mechanism is the down-regulation of electron transfer from PSII to PSI at the

cytochrome  $b_6/f$  (Cyt  $b_6/f$ ) complex, known as photosynthetic control, which plays a crucial role in prevention of PSI photoinhibition (Tikhonov, 2014; Tikkanen et al., 2015). Since the Mehler reaction is active when PSI over-reduction occurs, photosynthetic control is critical for prevention of ROS production by keeping the reaction center chlorophyll (P700) in PSI oxidized. Proton gradient regulation 5 (PGR5) is a small chloroplast protein essential for  $\Delta pH$  formation (Munekage et al., 2002), and this protein is generally considered to be involved in the major pathway for cyclic electron flow (CEF) around PSI (Munekage et al., 2002; DalCorso et al., 2008; Yamori and Shikanai, 2016). There are several chloroplast K<sup>+</sup>/H<sup>+</sup> transporters that also regulate ΔpH (Kunz et al., 2014; Armbruster et al., 2017; Aranda-Sicilia et al., 2020). The Arabidopsis *pgr5* mutant cannot generate enough  $\Delta pH$  across the thylakoid membrane and, therefore, fails to fully activate NPQ and photosynthetic control (Suorsa et al., 2012; Yamamoto and Shikanai, 2019). The PSI of the pgr5 mutant is highly sensitive to light stress conditions, especially fluctuating light (Suorsa et al., 2012; Kono et al., 2014). Thus, the PGR5- and ApH-dependent mechanisms can tightly reduce the probability of the Mehler reaction under stressful conditions. However, it has not been addressed whether these mechanisms can compensate for the lack of chloroplast APXs.

The aim of this study was to clarify the physiological role of chloroplast APXs as 'antioxidant enzymes,' particularly when  $\Delta p$ H-dependent mechanisms are compromised. Therefore, we investigated the combined effects of APXs and other regulatory proteins, such as PGR5 and PsbS, on HL stress tolerance.

## The extreme sensitivity of the sapx tapx pgr5 triple mutant to high-light stress

Wild-type and mutant plants were grown under LL ( $40 - 60 \mu mol photons m^{-2} s^{-1}$ ) conditions for 18 days, and their growth was found to be indistinguishable from each other. Plants were then exposed to HL (1,300-1,500 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) for 24 h. Marked damage symptoms (e.g., photo-bleaching) were not found in the wild-type plants and *sapx tapx* during the stress treatment (**Figure 2**), which was consistent with findings of previous reports (Giacomelli *et al.*,

2007; Kangasjarvi *et al.*, 2008; Maruta *et al.*, 2010). In the *pgr5* single mutant, a slight photo-bleaching was observed in their old leaves at 24 h. The double mutants, *sapx pgr5* and *tapx pgr5*, exhibited a phenotype comparable to that of the *pgr5* single mutants (**Figure S1**). Thus, the lack of sAPX or tAPX alone did not stimulate the HL stress sensitivity of the *pgr5* mutant. However, compared to these genotypes, the *sapx tapx pgr5* triple mutant had a more prominent photo-bleaching phenotype. As shown in **Figure 2**, clear photo-bleaching occurred after 12 h of HL exposure in almost expanded leaves of *sapx tapx pgr5*, but not in the other mutants, and the visible damage was further enhanced at 24 h. We also checked the sensitivity of *sapx tapx pgr5* to short-term HL exposure. For this, plants were exposed to HL for 6 h and then transferred to LL conditions. Only triple mutants showed a very clear cell death phenotype at 24 and 48 h after the short-term stress application (**Figure S2**).

We measured the maximum quantum yield of PSII ( $F_v/F_m$ ), an indicator of intactness of PSII, to evaluate photooxidative damage under HL stress. In all genotypes, the value was decreased by HL exposure in a time-dependent manner (**Figure 3a**). There was no significant difference between  $F_v/F_m$  of wild-type plants and *sapx tapx*. Compared to these genotypes, the decrease in the value was significantly promoted in *pgr5* single mutants (**Figure 3a**). At any period, the values in this mutant were not distinguishable from those in *sapx pgr5* and *tapx pgr5* double mutants (**Figure 3b**). Further significant PSII photoinhibition was observed in the *sapx tapx pgr5* triple mutants (**Figure 3a**). These data were clearly consistent with the phenotype of the wild-type and mutant plants under HL (**Figures 2 and S1**), thus demonstrating that sAPX and tAPX play a crucial role in photo-protection in the *pgr5*-background, and chloroplast APXs partially complement defects in PGR5-dependent photo-protection. Additionally, PSI

photoinhibition was evaluated as the ratio of total oxidizable P700 ( $P_{\rm m}$ ) measured before and after HL exposure (**Figure 3c**). As PSI of the *pgr5* mutant is known to be highly sensitive to HL (Munekage *et al.*, 2002), leaves adapted to the dark for 30 min were exposed to HL (1,600 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) for only 10 min. Although this treatment did not considerably affect the oxidizable  $P_{\rm m}$  ratio in wild type and *sapx tapx*, this value decreased by approximately 54% in *pgr5* (**Figure 3c**). The decrease in  $P_{\rm m}$  ratio tended to be promoted in the triple mutant (approximately 45%), but there was no statistical difference between *pgr5* and *sapx tapx pgr5*. In contrast to the severe PSI photoinhibition in the genotypes lacking PGR5, the difference between  $F_{\rm v}/F_{\rm m}$  ratio before and after 10 min of HL exposure was slight (**Figure 3d**).

### Cellular redox perturbations in *sapx tapx pgr5* triple mutants

Considering the enzymatic function of chloroplast APXs, the extreme sensitivity of the *sapx tapx pgr5* mutant to HL stress might be explained by enhanced  $H_2O_2$  accumulation and subsequent oxidative damage. However, as discussed in previous literatures (Queval *et al.*, 2008; Winterbourn, 2014; Noctor *et al.*, 2015; Noctor *et al.*, 2016), difficulties are currently associated with quantitative measurements of  $H_2O_2$ . We attempted to measure  $H_2O_2$  in wild-type and mutant plants using a peroxidase-based assay reported by Noctor *et al.* (2016), but unfortunately, the results were neither reproducible nor reliable. Then, we tried to measure  $H_2O_2$  using Amplex Red (Chakraborty *et al.*, 2016) and obtained somewhat reproducible data, which suggested that the *sapx tapx pgr5* mutant highly accumulated  $H_2O_2$  compared to the wild-type plants (**Figure S3**).

To investigate redox perturbations in *sapx tapx pgr5* mutants in more detail, we measured glutathione and ascorbate (**Figure 4**). The wild-type and mutant plants were exposed to

HL for 12 h. Before HL stress, there was no difference in glutathione profiles among the genotypes. Increases in total glutathione level (reduced form [GSH] + oxidized form [GSSG]) were found in the wild-type plants and *sapx tapx* mutants after 6 h of HL exposure (**Figure 4a**), while the glutathione redox state (GSH/total  $\times$  100) was hardly affected in these genotypes (**Figure 4b**). In *pgr5* single mutant, total glutathione levels were enhanced after 6 h of HL exposure but decreased after 12 h, although glutathione redox state was not changed. Thus, the *pgr5* mutation had a negative impact on glutathione pool size under HL stress. This was more obvious in the *sapx tapx pgr5*, in which the HL-induced increase in total glutathione level was almost completely inhibited (**Figure 4a**). Furthermore, the glutathione redox state was decreased only in the *sapx tapx pgr5* under HL stress (**Figure 4b**). In all genotypes, except for the *sapx tapx pgr5* mutant, total ascorbate content (reduced form [ASC] + oxidized form [dehydroascorbate, DHA]) was increased by HL stress, but there was no difference in the values between these genotypes. In HL-exposed *sapx tapx pgr5*, total ascorbate pool was not increased but significantly decreased, although the ascorbate redox state (ASC/total  $\times$  100) did not change (**Figure 4d**).

The decrease in glutathione redox state and total ascorbate content are two representative redox perturbations under severe oxidative stress conditions, as observed in the catalase 2-deficient *(cat2)* mutant (e.g., Han *et al.*, 2013; Kerchev *et al.*, 2016), which is the best-characterized mutant as an oxidative stress model in plants. These data clearly suggest that the *sapx tapx pgr5* triple mutant was severely damaged by oxidative stress in HL.

# Impact of photosynthetic redox perturbations on the transcriptome under HL

In the last decade, transcriptomic studies have delivered extensive inventories of

transcriptome-wide changes induced by oxidative stress in plants (Gadjev *et al.*, 2006; Willems *et al.*, 2016). To further investigate the impact of lack of chloroplast APXs and PGR5 on oxidative stress in HL, RNA-seq analysis was performed with Col-0, *sapx tapx, pgr5*, and *sapx tapx pgr5*. Prior to this assay, we checked the expression of the representative HL-responsive gene, *APX2* (Karpinski *et al.*, 1997), in the wild-type plants exposed to HL, and the preliminary data suggested that *APX2* induction peaked at 90 min after HL (**Figure S4**). Therefore, wild-type and mutant plants exposed to HL for 90 min were used for RNA-seq analysis.

From the triplicate data, we selected the genes whose expression was significantly induced (fold change > 2, FDR < 0.01) in mutants compared to the wild-type plants. Our results indicated that only 11 genes were up-regulated in *sapx tapx* compared to those in the wild-type plants, although there were 113 and 408 genes whose expressions were enhanced in *pgr5* and *sapx tapx pgr5*, respectively (Figure 5a and Table S1). A total of 93 genes were common among both genotypes. Scatter plot analysis of these 93 genes revealed that the slope of the trend line in the plot comparing the log<sub>2</sub> fold changes in triple/Col-0 and pgr5/Col-0 was approximately 1.39 (Figure 5b), suggesting that the expression of these genes in the triple mutant was higher than in pgr5. Furthermore, there were only 20 genes up-regulated specifically in pgr5, although much more genes (313) were up-regulated only in sapx tapx pgr5. We also found that 78 and 112 genes were down-regulated in pgr5 and sapx tapx pgr5, respectively, and a total of 41 genes were common among both genotypes (Figure S5). In contrast, only six genes, including sAPX and *tAPX*, were down-regulated in *sapx tapx*. Thus, the lack of chloroplast APXs and PGR5 together had a more prominent impact on nuclear gene expression compared to that observed with the dearth of the two individually.

It was unclear if the transcriptomic changes between the wild-type and mutant plants were associated with HL stress response, because we did not include non-stressed plants (before HL) in the RNA-seq assay. Recent bioinformatics analysis identified 442 core genes for early HL stress response (Willems *et al.*, 2016). Among them, 80 genes were included in the 408 genes that were up-regulated in *sapx tapx pgr5* (**Table S2**). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that the GO terms 'response to high light' and 'response to light intensities' were enriched in the up-regulated genes of the triple mutant (**Figure 5c**). These data indicate that the lack of both chloroplast APXs and PGR5 facilitates the transcriptomic response to HL stress.

The GO terms associated with oxidative stress, reactive oxygen species, and hydrogen peroxide were also enriched (**Figure 5c**). Our RNA-seq data were further compared with three previous reports showing transcriptional responses to  $H_2O_2$ . We found that among the 408 genes up-regulated in *sapx tapx pgr5*, 208 genes were previously reported to be induced by oxidative stress caused by the *cat2* mutation (plus photorespiratory stress for 3 h, Willems *et al.*, 2016), 20 mM  $H_2O_2$  treatment for 1 h (Davletova *et al.*, 2005b), and/or 50 µM methyl viologen treatment for 2 h (Scarpeci *et al.*, 2008) (**Table S3**). These included representative oxidative stress-induced genes, such as zinc finger and WRKY transcription factors (*ZAT7* [AT3G46090], *ZAT12* [AT5G59820], and *WRKY25* [AT2G30250]; Rizhsky *et al.*, 2004), a multiprotein bridging factor c1 (*MBF1C* [AT3G24500], Suzuki *et al.*, 2010). In addition, the GO terms associated with other abiotic stresses, especially heat stress, were also enriched in the up-regulated genes of the triple mutant. To investigate if the lack of both APXs and PGR5 had an impact on leaf temperature, we measured it after 90 min HL exposure using a thermography. The leaf

temperatures of the wild type and mutants were ranging from 25.0 to 26.2 °C, and there was no significant difference among the genotypes. Since heat shock transcription factors (Hsfs) that govern heat shock responses are candidates for redox sensors (Davletova *et al.*, 2005a; Jung *et al.*, 2013), the activation of the Hsf pathway could reflect cellular redox perturbations. Together with phenotypic and biochemical data, these findings indicate a severe oxidative damage in the *sapx tapx pgr5* triple mutant under HL stress.

# Partial recovery of NPQ in *sapx tapx pgr5* triple mutant

The *pgr5* could not sufficiently induce NPQ because of its low capacity for trans-thylakoid  $\Delta pH$  formation (Munekage *et al.*, 2002). As shown in **Figure 6**, NPQ induction upon the onset of actinic light (90 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) was largely inhibited in *pgr5*. Understandably, lower NPQ induction was also observed in *sapx tapx pgr5* than that observed in wild type and *sapx tapx*. However, when compared to the *pgr5* single mutant, the triple mutant was found to show a higher NPQ induction. The difference was slight but statistically significant (**Figure 6a and Table S6**). Thus, the failure of NPQ induction in *pgr5* was partially recovered in *sapx tapx pgr5*. The lack of chloroplast APXs alone did not affect NPQ induction.

Since the xanthophyll cycle and PsbS function are important for energy-dependent quenching (qE), the major component of NPQ in C<sub>3</sub> plants (Li *et al.*, 2002; Kulheim *et al.*, 2002; Niyogi *et al.*, 2005), the partial recovery of NPQ induction suggests that the lack of chloroplast APXs activated the xanthophyll cycle in the *pgr5*-background. Therefore, we measured xanthophyll cycle pigments, such as violaxanthin (V), antheraxanthin (A), and zeaxanthin (Z) (**Figure S6**), to address the xanthophyll cycle in more detail. In response to light, violaxanthin

de-epoxidase is activated upon the acidification of thylakoid lumen, and violaxanthin undergoes de-epoxidation to antheraxanthin and finally to zeaxanthin. The formation of zeaxanthin, together with the function of PsbS protein, is responsible for the thermal dissipation of excess excitation energy (Müller *et al.*, 2001). Accordingly, the de-epoxidation state [(0.5A + Z)/(V + A + Z)] of xanthophyll pigments reflects the xanthophyll cycle activity (Thayer and Björkman, 1990). After 5 min of exposure to HL, the de-epoxidation state increased from 0.05 to 0.33 in wild type Col-0 (**Figure 6b**), which was very similar to the cases in wild type Col-*gl1* and *sapx tapx*. De-epoxidation of xanthophylls by HL was largely inhibited in *pgr5* (approximately 0.16), which showed a low capacity for the xanthophyll cycle due to the lower  $\Delta$ pH formation. In contrast, this inhibition of de-epoxidation was partially recovered in the triple mutant with a statistical difference (**Figure 6b**), which was consistent with the partial recovery of the NPQ value. Thus, the recovery of NPQ induction in *sapx tapx pgr5* was provided by the activation of qE.

## Involvement of NDH-dependent cyclic electron flow in NPQ recovery

The qE is a  $\Delta pH$ -dependent photoprotective mechanism. The partial recovery of NPQ induction in *sapx tapx pgr5* might suggest that the additional lack of chloroplast APXs partially alleviated the failure of  $\Delta pH$  formation in pgr5. CEF around PSI mediates electron flow from ferredoxin to plastoquinone (Yamori and Shikanai, 2016). This electron flow generates trans-thylakoid  $\Delta pH$  via the quinone cycle in the Cyt  $b_6 f$  complex without net NADPH production, thereby contributing to subsequent ATP production and induction of photoprotective mechanisms (Yamori and Shikanai, 2016). Recently, the NADH dehydrogenase-like complex (NDH)-dependent pathway for CEF, which has a minor contribution compared to the

PGR5-dependent pathway (Munekage *et al.*, 2004; Shikanai, 2016), was found to be activated by  $H_2O_2$  (Strand *et al.*, 2015), which led us to hypothesize that the acidification of thylakoid lumen via the minor NDH-dependent CEF might be involved in the recovery of NPQ induction in *sapx tapx pgr5*. Accordingly, we decided to introduce an additional mutation, *chlororespiratory reduction 2 (crr2)*, which inhibits the expression of an NDH subunit (NdhB) and causes dysfunction of the NDH pathway (Hashimoto *et al.*, 2003), into the *sapx tapx pgr5*. We first generated a *sapx tapx crr2* triple mutant by crossing *sapx tapx* with *crr2*. Unlike the *pgr5* mutant, the *crr2* mutant did not show an HL-sensitive phenotype, and this was also the case for the *sapx tapx crr2* triple mutant (**Figure S7**).

The *sapx tapx crr2* was then crossed with *sapx tapx pgr5* to generate the quadruple mutant (**Figure S8**). We also generated a *pgr5 crr2* double mutant by crossing single mutants. As reported previously (Munekage *et al.*, 2004), *pgr5 crr2* showed severe growth retardation under LL growth conditions, and *sapx tapx pgr5 crr2* had a similar phenotype (**Figure 7a**). Based on their phenotype and PSII photoinhibition, *pgr5 crr2* was more sensitive to HL compared to wild type, and the lack of chloroplast APXs markedly enhanced this susceptibility (**Figure 7a, b**). Both *pgr5 crr2* and *sapx tapx pgr5 crr2* exhibited a low NPQ induction upon actinic light exposure compared to the wild-type plants, but there was no difference in the NPQ value between the double and quadruple mutants (**Figure 7c**). Thus, the lack of APXs did not restore NPQ in the *pgr5 crr2* background, which supported our hypothesis.

# Sensitivity of *sapx tapx npq4* triple mutant to HL stress

Since the pgr5 mutant fails to increase  $\Delta pH$  across the thylakoid membrane, both

 $\Delta$ pH-dependent regulatory mechanisms, the xanthophyll cycle and photosynthetic control, are simultaneously compromised in this mutant (Munekage *et al.*, 2002; Suorsa *et al.*, 2012; Tikkanen *et al.*, 2015; Yamamoto and Shikanai, 2019). We crossed *sapx tapx* with *npq4*, in which only the induction of  $\Delta$ pH-dependent NPQ (qE) is impaired (Li *et al.*, 2002), to elucidate the mechanism critical for compensating chloroplast APXs. The *sapx tapx npq4* triple mutant was genotyped from the F<sub>2</sub> population (**Figure S8**). Chlorophyll fluorescence measurements revealed that NPQ induction was severely inhibited in both *npq4* and *sapx tapx npq4*, and there was no difference between these genotypes (**Figure 8c**).

The growth of npq4 and sapx tapx npq4 mutants under LL conditions was indistinguishable from that of wild type and sapx tapx (Figure 8a). Compared to wild type and sapx tapx, the decrease in F<sub>v</sub>/F<sub>m</sub> by HL stress was pronounced in the npq4 single mutant (Figure 8b), although the npq4 mutant did not exhibit a distinct damage phenotype (Figure 8a). These data are consistent with previous reports (Havaux and Niyogi, 1999; Li *et al.*, 2002). Notably, there was no difference in both the F<sub>v</sub>/F<sub>m</sub> value and visible phenotype between npq4 and sapx tapxnpq4 mutants (Figure 8a, b), indicating that the xanthophyll cycle alone was not a critical mechanism for compensating chloroplast APXs.

# DISCUSSION

Previously, the potential rate of the Mehler reaction was estimated to be very high; for example, 30% of the total linear electron transport in C<sub>3</sub> plants (Asada, 2000). However, the rate has been reevaluated to range from 0 to 1% in angiosperms (Driever and Baker, 2011; Shirao *et al.*, 2013), which is thought to be achieved by  $\Delta$ pH-dependent mechanisms that fine-tune the

redox state of PSII and PSI (Tikhonov, 2014; Miyake, 2020). The low probability of the Mehler reaction is consistent with the negligible phenotype of *sapx tapx*. However, we emphasize that although the Mehler reaction rate is markedly low, this does not mean that the chloroplast-derived  $H_2O_2$  has little impact on plant physiology under HL. The accumulation of  $H_2O_2$  in chloroplasts under HL has been detected in many experiments (Fryer *et al.*, 2002, 2003; Driever and Baker, 2011; Exposito-Rodriguez *et al.*, 2017). Furthermore, as mentioned previously, the triple mutant lacking 2CPA, 2CPB, and tAPX was recently found to exhibit an apparent HL-sensitive phenotype (Awad *et al.*, 2015), suggesting that cooperation of chloroplast peroxidases is required to maintain  $H_2O_2$  at low levels and to prevent oxidative damage. Thus, the redundant function of chloroplast peroxidases can explain the negligible phenotype of mutants lacking chloroplast APXs. In contrast, it has never been experimentally addressed whether the  $\Delta$ pH-dependent mechanisms might compensate for the lack of chloroplast APXs. We focused on this point using multiple mutants lacking both APXs and  $\Delta$ pH-dependent photo-protective mechanisms.

Compared to the wild-type and *sapx tapx* plants, the single *pgr5* mutant showed enhanced sensitivity to HL. This HL-sensitivity of *pgr5* was not affected by the additional lack of sAPX or tAPX alone but was drastically accelerated by the simultaneous knockout of both enzymes (**Figures 2 and S1**). These differences in the visible phenotype among genotypes were consistent with the degree of PSII photoinhibition (**Figures 3**). In contrast, we did not find a statistical difference in PSI photoinhibition between *pgr5* and *sapx tapx pgr5* (**Figure 3**). Since we measured the oxidizable  $P_m$  ratio only after 10 min of HL exposure, a more detailed analysis will be required for further discussion. However, our result may fit a recent notion reported by Takagi *et al.* (2016) that neither chloroplast APXs nor SODs can protect PSI from oxidative damage. This

is probably because PSI photoinhibition is caused by ROS produced within the thylakoid membrane, while antioxidant enzymes are located outside the membrane (Takagi *et al.*, 2016). The *pgr5* mutation causes severe overreduction of the PSI reaction center under HL stress (Munekage *et al.*, 2002; **Figure 3**), which probably leads to an enhanced rate in the Mehler reaction. Thus, it is plausible to consider that the extreme HL-sensitive phenotype of the triple mutant was provided by both enhanced  $H_2O_2$  production and limited capacity for its removal. This idea was supported by  $H_2O_2$  measurements (**Figure S3**).

Before and after HL treatment, glutathione redox state in *sapx tapx* and *pgr5* mutants were comparable to those in wild type, but this was significantly decreased in the sapx tapx pgr5 triple mutant after exposure to HL (Figure 4). This parameter is one of representative redox perturbations under severe oxidative stress conditions (i.e., *cat2* mutant; Han *et al.*, 2013; Kerchev et al., 2016). Interestingly, the accumulation of total glutathione was inhibited in pgr5 after 12 h of HL exposure, and this was more pronounced in *sapx tapx pgr5* mutants (Figure 4). This might be inconsistent with a general notion that oxidative stress enhances glutathione pool size, as *cat2* shows a drastic increase in total glutathione pool under photorespiratory conditions (Han et al., 2013). The reason for this discrepancy is currently unknow, but it might be possible that glutathione biosynthesis was inhibited in the sapx tapx pgr5 triple mutant under HL. Glutathione biosynthesis is highly regulated by the activity of  $\gamma$ -glutamylcysteine synthetase (encoded by GSH1) and cysteine availability (Noctor et al., 2011). Recently, De Smet et al. (2019) identified 132 chloroplast proteins of which cysteine residues are putatively sulfenylated by H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> treatment. These include enzymes involved in cysteine biosynthesis, such as sulfite reductase (SIR), ATP sulfurylase (APS1;3), and O-acetylserine (thiol) lyases (OASB and OASC). Furthermore, cellular

cysteine levels were found to be decreased by the  $H_2O_2$  treatment (De Smet et al., 2019). These findings suggest that  $H_2O_2$  may inhibit cysteine biosynthesis through sulfenylation of the biosynthetic enzymes. Since these enzymes are working in chloroplasts, enhanced accumulation of  $H_2O_2$  might disturb cysteine biosynthesis, causing a negative impact on glutathione pool in the *sapx tapx pgr5* triple mutant.

In addition to the decrease in glutathione redox state, ascorbate pool size was also decreased only in the sapx tapx pgr5 triple mutant under HL (Figure 4). As observed in cat2 (Han et al., 2013; Kerchev et al., 2016), the decrease in ascorbate pool is another representative oxidative damage symptom. However, this was somewhat unexpected because chloroplast APXs are enzymes that oxidize ascorbate, possibly facilitating ascorbate turnover. Furthermore, ascorbate levels were reported to be increased by the lack of cytosolic APX1 after a very short exposure to HL (Suzuki et al., 2015). In addition to APXs, ROS themselves, except for H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, can also oxidize ascorbate efficiently (Smirnoff and Arnaud, 2019). Thus, it might be possible that high accumulation of  $H_2O_2$  in the sapx tapx pgr5 triple mutant enhanced the probability of ascorbate oxidation reaction by other APX isoforms and the production of hydroxyl radical which oxidized ascorbate very rapidly. In contrast to ascorbate pool size, ascorbate redox state was not affected in the triple mutant. Recently, we generated a quadruple mutant having the pad2 (phytoalexin deficient 2) mutation that causes glutathione deficiency and T-DNA insertions in all three DHA reductase (DHAR) genes, called  $\Delta dhar pad2$  quadruple mutants (Terai et al., 2020). This mutant possesses a low capacity for ascorbate recycling from DHA because of negligible enzymatic DHAR activity and limitation in non-enzymatic DHA reduction by GSH. Although HL-induced ascorbate accumulation was almost completely inhibited in the  $\Delta dhat \ pad2$  mutant

due to ascorbate degradation, the ascorbate redox state was not clearly affected. This indicates that DHA is extremely unstable in *Arabidopsis* cells, and its degradation is favored when ascorbate oxidation exceeds its recycling capacity. This might be the case in the *sapx tapx pgr5* triple mutant. Oxidative stress-induced redox perturbations, which are increase in  $H_2O_2$  levels and decreases in the glutathione redox state and ascorbate pool size, only occurred in the *sapx tapx pgr5* triple mutant after 6 h of exposure to HL (**Figures 4 and S3**). The enhanced oxidative damage in the triple mutant was also evidenced by the drastic decrease in the  $F_v/F_m$  value (**Figure 3**) and the up-regulation of many ROS-responsive genes (**Figure 5 and Tables S1 and S3**). Based on these data, we conclude that the PGR5-dependent mechanisms and chloroplast APXs compensate for each other in photooxidative stress tolerance by limiting production of  $H_2O_2$  and by removing it, respectively.

Although the exact function of PGR5 remains under discussion, PGR5 and its partner protein, PGR5-like photosynthetic phenotype 1 (PGRL1), are considered to form a core component of the major CEF pathway (Munekage *et al.*, 2002; DalCorso *et al.*, 2008; Yamori and Shikanai, 2016). One of the other scenarios was that PGR5 might prevent proton leakage from the thylakoid membrane independently of its role in CEF (Rantala *et al.*, 2020), but this notion has been denied by a very recent study (Yamamoto and Shikanai, 2020). The PGR5 function itself is beyond the scope of our study, but an indisputable fact is that loss-of-function of these proteins results in the impairment of  $\Delta$ pH-dependent mechanisms, including the xanthophyll cycle and photosynthetic control (Munekage *et al.*, 2002; Tikkanen *et al.*, 2015; Yamamoto and Shikanai, 2019). Recent studies have indicated that the xanthophyll cycle is unlikely to have a substantial contribution to PSI photoprotection (Tikkanen *et al.*, 2015), even though it is important for

protection of PSII from photooxidative stress, which is apparently supported by the marked decrease in  $F_v/F_m$  in *npq* mutants under HL (Li *et al.*, 2002). In contrast, a critical role of photosynthetic control to avoid PSI overreduction has been reported (Suorsa *et al.*, 2012; Yamamoto and Shikanai, 2019). Since the electron transfer from plastoquinone to the Cyt  $b_{e}/f$  complex (at the  $Q_0$  site) is the rate-limiting step in the photosynthetic electron flow, the down-regulation of this step (photosynthetic control) allows the reaction center chlorophyll (P700) in PSI to be oxidized even under HL conditions where PSII is damaged (Tikhonov, 2014; Miyake, 2020). Considering that the Mehler reaction is only active when PSI is considerably reduced, it is plausible to consider that the extreme sensitivity of *sapx tapx pgr5* was provided by the impairment of photosynthetic control rather than the xanthophyll cycle. This notion was largely supported by our finding that the lack of chloroplast APXs did not enhance the HL-sensitivity of the *npq4* mutant (**Figure 8**).

The current work provides an intriguing finding that the lack of chloroplast APXs partially alleviates the failure of NPQ induction in *pgr5* (**Figure 6**). These results were consistent with the partial recovery of the de-epoxidation state of xanthophylls, which indicated that the xanthophyll cycle activity was partially recovered in *sapx tapx pgr5*. Since qE is the  $\Delta$ pH-dependent mechanism, this finding implies that  $\Delta$ pH formation in the triple mutant is higher than that in *pgr5*. Recently, Strand *et al.* (2015) found that foliar infiltration with H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> solution (more than 3 mM) activates CEF activity, and this activation is diminished in the *crr2* mutant, in which the NDH pathway is compromised. CEF activation was also observed in transgenic plants that overexpressed the chimeric glycolate oxidase fused to the chloroplast targeting signal, thereby allowing a markedly high H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> accumulation in chloroplasts through glycolate oxidation (Strand

*et al.*, 2015). These findings indicate that a high concentration of  $H_2O_2$  activates the NDH-dependent CEF. CEF is an alternative electron flow that contributes to  $\Delta pH$  formation through electron transfer from ferredoxin to plastoquinone. Accordingly, we hypothesized that the activation of the NDH pathway might be involved in the partial recovery of NPQ induction. Consistent with this, the partial recovery of NPQ induction observed in the *sapx tapx pgr5* was entirely diminished by the additional *crr2* mutation (**Figure 7**). This finding fits the scenario proposed by Strand *et al.* (2015) and suggests that the NDH-dependent pathway is activated in *sapx tapx pgr5*, which leads to the partial recovery of NPQ induction via the  $\Delta pH$  formation. Although further analyses, such as measurements of CEF activity and  $\Delta pH$  formation, are required for a more detailed discussion, activation of the NDH pathway by  $H_2O_2$  may mitigate ROS production under severe oxidative stress conditions (e.g., *sapx tapx pgr5*).

 $H_2O_2$  produced by photosynthesis acts as a retrograde signal from chloroplasts to the nucleus (Maruta *et al.*, 2012; Shigeoka and Maruta, 2014; Stael *et al.*, 2015; Maruta *et al.*, 2016). Additionally, other kinds of ROS, such as  ${}^{1}O_2$ , and chemicals derived from chloroplasts have signaling roles (Mullineaux *et al.*, 2000; Pintó-Marijuan and Munné-Bosch, 2014; Kim, 2020). Chloroplasts can interact with other organelles such as the nucleus, mitochondria and peroxisomes. Interestingly, it has been reported that  $H_2O_2$  is directly transferred from chloroplasts to the nucleus, by physical attachment or close proximity of both organelles, and regulates nuclear gene expression under HL (Exposito-Rodriguez *et al.*, 2017). Chloroplast APXs can act as regulators of retrograde signaling by controlling  $H_2O_2$ -responsive genes (Yabuta *et al.*, 2004; Exposito-Rodriguez *et al.*, 2017). Furthermore, it has been suggested that chloroplast APXs are

involved in the regulation of nitric oxide-induced cell death (Murgia et al., 2004; Tarantino et al., 2005). We previously generated a system in which tAPX expression was conditionally suppressed by estrogen treatment and identified 365 genes whose expression was up-regulated in response to tAPX suppression under normal light conditions (Maruta *et al.*, 2012). As there was little overlap between the identified genes and known oxidative stress-responsive genes, we proposed the specific role of the chloroplast-derived H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> signal (Maruta et al., 2012). In contrast, we found that many HL-, H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-, and/or oxidative stress-responsive genes were highly up-regulated in the sapx tapx pgr5 triple mutant under HL stress conditions (Figure 5 and Tables S1 and S2). Indeed, only 11 of the 365 genes previously identified were included in the up-regulated genes in the sapx tapx pgr5 mutant (Supplemental Table S4). This large difference can be explained by the degree of oxidative damage in tAPX-silenced plants and *sapx tapx pgr5*. The conditional tAPX suppression under normal light conditions did not considerably affect the photosynthesis and cellular redox state (Maruta et al., 2012), while the triple mutant exposed to HL showed severe oxidative damage symptoms (Figures 2-4 and S3). It is thus likely that chloroplast  $H_2O_2$  plays a specific role in gene regulation at low or moderate concentrations but triggers a general oxidative damage response under excessive accumulation. Our current transcriptome emphasizes that severe oxidative damage is caused by the simultaneous loss-of-function of chloroplast APXs and PGR5 under HL stress.

In conclusion, the function of chloroplast APXs as 'antioxidant enzymes' is largely compensated by  $\Delta p$ H-dependent mechanisms, especially photosynthetic control, which prevents the probability of the Mehler reaction in HL. When PSI is excessively reduced, for example, by the *pgr5* mutation plus HL irradiation, the H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> production rate is considerably enhanced, and

chloroplast APXs become more significant for the detoxification of  $H_2O_2$  (Figure 9). Thus, chloroplasts combine multiple systems to minimize or fine-tune  $H_2O_2$  levels by controlling its production and scavenging. This might fit a recent finding that extracellular NADPH oxidases, rather than photosynthesis, are critical sources of  $H_2O_2$  under HL stress (Xiong *et al.*, 2020). In general, PSI is very stable, and its photoinhibition rarely occurs under HL stress. However, recent progress in photooxidative stress studies has demonstrated that PSI is sensitive to artificial and natural fluctuating light conditions (Suorsa *et al.*, 2012; Kono *et al.*, 2014; Takagi *et al.*, 2016; Yamamoto *et al.*, 2016). It will be fascinating to study whether chloroplast APXs play a crucial role in protection against fluctuating light stress in future work.

### **EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES**

### Plant materials and growth conditions

*Arabidopsis thaliana* ecotype Col-0 and Col-*gl1* were used as wild-type plants. The T-DNA insertion lines, *sapx* (SALK\_083737), *tapx* (WiscDsLox457-460A17), and *npq4* were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center and were used for crossing. The *pgr5-1* and *crr2-1* mutants were gifted by Prof. Toshiharu Shikanai (Kyoto University). In the *pgr5-1*, single nucleotide substitution occurs in the second exon of *PGR5* gene, leading to an amino acid substitution from glycine 130 to serine (Munekage *et al.*, 2002; Yamamoto and Shikanai, 2019). In the *crr2-1*, the 167<sup>th</sup> tryptophan was substituted by the stop codon, truncating the CRR2 protein, which plays a role in the intergenic processing of chloroplast RNA and is essential for *ndhB* translation (Hashimoto *et al.*, 2003).

The wild-type and mutant seeds were sown in soil (Jiffy-7) and stratified in darkness for

2 to 3 days at 4°C. Unless otherwise stated, plants were grown in a growth chamber (NK system, KCLP-1000CCFL-6-8L-CO<sub>2</sub>) maintained at 22°C for 16 h of LL (40–60  $\mu$ mol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) and at 20°C for 8 h in the dark. At 18 days, the plants were exposed to HL stress (approximately 1,500  $\mu$ mol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) without a period of darkness. As described in Terai *et al.* (2020), we used a white LED lighting unit for the HL stress to reduce heating (the temperature around HL-exposed leaves was approximately 24–25°C). Plants were placed on a turntable and rotated during light exposure to homogenize light intensity from the light source.

#### Semi-quantitative RT-PCR and western blotting

From plant leaf materials, total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan). After DNase treatment, total RNA was used to synthesize the first-strand cDNA using reverse transcriptase (ReverTra Ace, Toyobo, Japan) with an oligo (dT) primer. These assays were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. The primers used are listed in **Table S5**.

Arabidopsis leaves (0.3 g) frozen in liquid nitrogen were ground and homogenized with 1 mL of potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.6) containing 1 mM EDTA and 1% (w/v) 3-[(3-Cholamidopropyl)dimethylammonio] propane sulfonate (CHAPS). After centrifugation (15,300 × g) for 20 min at 4°C, the supernatant was used for western blotting analysis. Protein concentrations were determined using the TaKaRa Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Takara). The protein solution (15  $\mu$ L) containing 1  $\mu$ g of protein was mixed with 15  $\mu$ L of SDS-PAGE loading buffer, consisting of 125 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 4% (w/v) SDS, 20% (w/w) glycerol, 0.01% (w/v) bromophenol blue, and 1% (w/w) 2-mercaptoethanol, and boiled for 5 min. The proteins were separated by electrophoresis in a 12.5% slab gel and then transferred onto a PVDF membrane. A

polyclonal rabbit antibody against APX (Agrisera) and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (VECTOR LABORATORIES) were used as the primary and secondary antibodies, respectively. Protein bands were detected using the ECL<sup>TM</sup> Prime Western Blotting Detection System (Cytiva).

### **Enzyme assays**

Arabidopsis leaves (0.5 g) frozen in liquid nitrogen were ground and homogenized with 1 mL of potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH7.6) containing 20% sorbitol (w/v), 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM ASC. After centrifugation (15,300 × g) for 20 min at 4°C, the supernatant was used for soluble APX activity measurement. The precipitate was washed twice with 1 mL of the same potassium phosphate buffer and solubilized with 500 µL of the buffer containing 1% CHAPS (w/v). After centrifugation (15,300 × g) for 20 min at 4°C, the second supernatant was used for insoluble APX activity measurement. The extract (50 µL) was added to 950 µL of the reaction mixture containing 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.6), 400 µM ASC, and 1 mM EDTA. The reaction was initiated by the addition of 10 µL H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (50 mM) and by monitoring the decrease in A<sub>290</sub> for 3 min at 25°C ( $\varepsilon$ 290 ascorbate = 2,800 M<sup>-1</sup> cm<sup>-1</sup>). A control experiment without the extract was performed to correct the chemical scavenging of H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, and control rates were subtracted from the rates obtained with the extract.

#### Measurements of H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, antioxidants and pigments

We measured  $H_2O_2$  using peroxidase-based assays (Chakraborty *et al.*, 2016; Noctor *et al.*, 2016). Measurements of ascorbate and glutathione were performed according to the methods

described by Shiroma *et al.* (2019) and Noctor *et al.* (2016), respectively, without any modification. Xanthophyll as well as chlorophyll content was determined according to the method reported by Thayer and Bjorkman, (1990). Briefly, xanthophylls were extracted using 82% (v/v) acetone and determined using an ultra-fast liquid chromatography (UFLC) system (Prominence UFLC, Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a C-18 column (LUNA C18(2), column 150 × 4.6 nm, Shimadzu). Xanthophyll concentration (mmol/mol chlorophyll A + B) and de-epoxidation state were calculated.

#### **Chlorophyll fluorescence measurements**

Chlorophyll fluorescence in Arabidopsis leaves was measured using a Closed FluorCam 800MF (Photon Systems Instruments). Plants before and after HL stress were incubated in the dark for 30 min and then used for chlorophyll fluorescence measurements. The chlorophyll fluorescence parameters,  $F_v/F_m$  and NPQ, were calculated using chlorophyll fluorescence parameters as follows (Baker, 2008):  $F_v/F_m = (F_m-F_o)/F_m$  and NPQ =  $(F_m - F_m')/F_m'$ . NPQ was measured under an intensity of actinic light (90 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>).

The PSI photoinhibition was evaluated as the total quantity of photo-oxidizable P700 (Pm) before and after HL using a Dual-PAM-100 fluorometer (Walz) (Klughammer and Schreiber, 1994). In this assay, 5-week-old plants grown under long-day conditions (100  $\mu$ mol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) were used since this assay requires relatively large leaves. After 30 min of dark incubation, the first P<sub>m</sub> and F<sub>v</sub>/F<sub>m</sub> values were determined. The second values were determined after 10 min of HL exposure (1,600  $\mu$ mol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) followed by the dark treatment for 30 min. The P<sub>m</sub> and F<sub>v</sub>/F<sub>m</sub> ratios relative to the initial values were calculated.

### Data analyses

Unless otherwise stated, the statistical analyses of data were based on Tukey-Kramer test. Calculations were performed on at least more than three independent biological replicates (see figure legends). In all experiments, except for the measurement of chlorophyll fluorescence, fully expanded leaves from more than three plants were pooled and used as one biological replicate.

### **RNA-seq analysis**

Total RNA was extracted as described above; then, it was purified using NucleoSpin RNA Plus (Takara) and fragmented and converted to double-stranded DNA using TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit v2 (Illumina). The quality and quantity of the resulting library were assessed using a Bioanalyzer and a KAPA library quantification kit (KAPA Biosystems), respectively, and were then sequenced using an Illumina MiSeq and the MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 300 cycle kit (Illumina). RNA-sequencing data from one experiment were statistically analyzed (n=3 for each group). Mapping reads, data processing, and statistical analyses were performed using the CLC Genomic Workbench (Ver.9.5.2). The gene expression data were deposited in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under accession number GSE156883. The GO enrichment analysis was performed using agriGO (version 2, Tian *et al.*, 2017).

Figure 9. Regulation of  $H_2O_2$  levels in chloroplasts by ascorbate peroxidases and proton gradient regulation 5

Chloroplast ascorbate peroxidases (APXs) are enzymes that scavenge  $H_2O_2$ . Proton gradient regulation 5 (PGR5) contributes to generate  $\Delta pH$  that activates the photosynthetic control and non-photochemical quenching (NPQ). The photosynthetic control inhibits electron transfer from photosystem II (PSI) to PSI, preventing the probability of  $H_2O_2$  production at PSI. In the wild-type plants (upper left), cooperation of chloroplast APXs and PGR5-dependent systems strongly restricts  $H_2O_2$  levels. In the absence of chloroplast APXs (*sapx tapx*, upper right),  $H_2O_2$  production is still suppressed by the PGR5-dependent systems, keeping  $H_2O_2$  levels low. Probably, other peroxidases (such as peroxiredoxins, not shown in this figure) also compensate for the lack of APXs. In the *pgr5* single mutant (lower left), the  $\Delta pH$ -dependent systems are compromised, putting severe burden on chloroplast APXs (as well as other peroxidases).  $H_2O_2$  levels are not completely but largely limited by the peroxidases. In the absence of both systems (*sapx tapx pgr5*, lower right),  $H_2O_2$  levels in chloroplasts are highly enhanced, leading to severe oxidative damage, redox perturbations, transcriptional reprograming and cell death.
