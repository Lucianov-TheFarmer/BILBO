#### ABSTRACT

Development and ripening of tomato fruit are precisely controlled by transcriptional regulation, which depends on the orchestrated accessibility of regulatory proteins to promoters and other *cis*-regulatory DNA elements. This accessibility and its effect on gene expression play a major role in defining the developmental process. To understand regulatory mechanism and functional elements modulating morphological and anatomical changes during fruit development, we generated genome-wide high-resolution maps of DNase I hypersensitive sites (DHSs) from the fruit tissues of the tomato cultivar 'Moneymaker' at 20 days post anthesis (DPA) as well as break stage. By exploring variation of DHSs across fruit development stages, we pinpointed the most likely hypersensitive sites related with development-specific genes. By detecting binding motifs on DHSs of these development-specific genes or genes in ascorbic acid biosynthetic pathway, we revealed the common regulatory elements contributing to the coordinate gene transcription of plant ripening and specialized metabolic pathways. This work will significantly contribute to a better understanding of the regulatory dynamics of genes through tomato fruit development and ripening.

#### INTRODUCTION

Tomato is an ideal model plant for investigating the mechanistic basis of fleshy fruit development and ripening because of its rapid life cycle and well-annotated genome sequence. After fertilization, tomato fruits undergo cell division and expansion during early fruit development, and then begin to ripen after mature green stage, which involves a range of physiological and morphological changes, including pigment accumulation for bright coloring, cell wall degradation for fruit softening and accumulation of compounds for nutrient and flavor (Karlova et al., 2014; Tohge et al., 2014). These morphological and anatomical changes are reflected by genetic mechanisms and transcriptional regulation during fruit development and maturation.

Transcription factors function as important nodes at the regulatory network during fruit development in controlling the formation and ripening of the fruit (Karlova et al., 2014). Recent advances have been made in understanding how transcription factors take part in ovary development, fruit metabolism and hormone response. For example, LOCULE NUMBER (LC) (Munos et al., 2011) and AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR (ARF) family members (Jones et al., 2002; Goetz et al., 2007; de Jong et al., 2009) act during early fruit development while RIPENING INHIBITOR (RIN) (Vrebalov et al., 2002), COLORLESS NON-RIPENING (CNR) (Manning et al., 2006) and TOMATO AGAMOUS-LIKE1 (TAGL1) (Itkin et al., 2009; Vrebalov et al., 2009) perform at fruit ripening.

Transcription of gene is usually activated or repressed by regulatory proteins binding to specific promoters or other DNA elements. Thus, which proteins as well as when and where these proteins bind to *cis*-regulatory DNA

elements is critical for understanding the regulation and function of a given gene. For example, *RIN*, which encodes a MADS box transcription factor, is expressed at the beginning of break stage and recognizes the CArG box element CYWWWWWRG, the typical binding motif of MADS box transcription factors, to regulate downstream genes (Ito et al., 2008; Zhong et al., 2013). During tomato ripening, RIN can directly bind to the promoters of ethylene biosynthesis genes, such as *ACS2* and *ACS4*, and genes involved in cell wall remodeling and carotenoid biosynthesis, such as *PG2a* and *PSY1* to control fruit maturation and pigment accumulation (Fujisawa et al., 2011; Martel et al., 2011; Fujisawa et al., 2012; Fujisawa et al., 2013). However, how such a large number of transcription factors control downstream genes during tomato fruit development is still unknown.

DNase I hypersensitive sites (DHSs) are genomic regions that exhibit hypersensitivity to DNase I cleavage when chromatin is partially digested by DNase I (Wu et al., 1979). Almost all active regulatory elements including enhancers, suppressors, insulators and the majority of transcription factor binding sites, are associated with open chromatin, which are marked by DHSs (Thurman et al., 2012; Sullivan et al., 2014; Zhu et al., 2015a). Thus, DNase I digestion followed by high-throughput sequencing (DNaseI-seq) has been used as an efficient tool for identification of regulatory DNA elements at the whole-genome level (John et al., 2011; He et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Inoue and Imai, 2014; Sullivan et al., 2014) . Due to the dynamic variation of DHSs among different tissues and cell types corresponding to distinct developmental stages and environmental cues, DNaseI-seq has also been applied to map regulatory regions for revealing specific regulatory elements, which are present in various cell types and developmental stages (Consortium, 2012; He et al., 2012; Mercer et al., 2013; Inoue and Imai, 2014; Sullivan et al., 2014; Wilken et al., 2015).

In this work, we used DNaseI-seq to generate stage specific genome-wide high-resolution DHS maps from fruit tissues of tomato cultivar 'Moneymaker'. Combining the genome-wide DHS analysis with gene expression data sets of 20 days post anthesis (DPA) and break stage fruits allowed us to characterize the display region and temporal specificity response of DHSs to fruit developmental cues. Moreover, we used the data obtained to specifically analyze genes involved in ascorbic acid biosynthesis, an important metabolic pathway in tomato fruit development, and found the evidence of coordinate regulation of these genes. Our findings demonstrate that the utilization of genome-wide DHS mapping could facilitate the systematical identification of *cis*-regulatory elements in tomato.

#### RESULTS

Genome-wide identification of DHSs in tomato during fruit development To investigate the regulatory DNA landscape during fruit development and ripening, we constructed two DNaseI-seq libraries (see Methods) for 20 DPA and break stage fruits, which represent the fruit enlargement stage and fruit ripening initiation, respectively. The DNaseI-seq of two biological replicates was performed by Illumina sequencing, and a total of 206.3 million reads were uniquely mapped to the tomato genome (SL2.50) (Supplemental Table 1). The reads count across the genome of these two biological replicates are highly correlated in both developmental stages (Supplemental Figure 1). The

sequence reads from two different biological replicates were combined for further analysis. The cross-correlation matrices show that our data sets were in good quality (Supplemental Figure 2). A total of 111,256 and 88,850 peaks were identified at 20 DPA and break stage using HOMER software (Heinz et al., 2010) with a false discovery rate (FDR) less than 0.0001 (see Methods), which covered 3.81% and 2.70% of the tomato genome, respectively (Supplemental Table 1).

To broadly assess DHS distribution in the tomato genome, we analyzed all DHSs along tomato chromosomes and found that DHSs were mainly located in the chromosome arm regions, which show similar pattern with genic region distribution (Figure 1A). We then investigated the relative location of DHSs (midpoint of DHS) to tomato genes. At both developmental stages, about 20% of DHSs were located in the promoter region defined as the 2,000 bp upstream of the transcription starting site (TSS), which covers 7.95% of tomato genome. Among these promoter-localized DHSs, 68.30% and 60.42% were within the 1,000 bp region upstream of the TSS at 20 DPA and break stage, respectively. About half of DHSs were identified in other gene regions, including the 5' untranslated region (UTR), exons, introns, the 3' UTR and the transcription termination site (TTS) region, which was defined as the 1,000 bp downstream of the TTS. The DHSs located in intergenic regions, counting for 26% of DHSs in 20 DPA fruits and 22% in break stage fruits (Figure 1B). DHSs in tomato fruit were relatively enriched around genes instead of intergenic regions (Supplemental Figure 3). Compared to previous findings in Arabidopsis and rice that more than 30% of DHSs are located in promoter regions 1,000 bp upstream of the TSS (Zhang et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Sullivan et al., 2014), DHS abundance in this region was clearly decreased in tomato.

The average length of DHS varied depending on the relative location to gene regions. DHSs located in the 5'UTR and 100 bp upstream of the TSS at the promoter region were longer than others. Introns contained the shortest DHSs among different regions (Figure 1C). This finding is consistent with the viewpoint that TSS-proximal regions are generally more susceptible to DNase I digestion than putative non-TSS regulatory elements (Boyle et al., 2008). Taken together, DHSs in tomato were also enriched at the promoter region of genes as in other species.

## Coordinate regulation of gene expression by DHSs and H3K4 trimethylation

To investigate the effect of DHSs on gene expression during tomato fruit development, we conducted RNA-seq of 20 DPA, 40 DPA, mature green and break stage fruits with three biological replicates for each sample. A total of 294.1 million uniquely mapped reads were obtained (Supplemental Table 1). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that three replicates of each sample were grouped together (Supplemental Figure 4). We found that most (78.1% and 80.1% at 20 DPA and break stage, respectively) of the genes contained a single DHS in their proximal promoter (from -1,000 to 100 bp relative to the TSS). Only 1.9% at 20 DPA and 1.5% at break stage have more than two DHSs in their proximal promoter (Supplemental Table 2). We compared the expression levels of genes having single DHS with genes containing multiple DHSs, and no significant difference was found (Figure

2A). Genes with only one DHS in their proximal promoter were sorted based on the length of DHSs, and then divided into three equal groups: top, middle and bottom (Figure 2B). In the top group, the genes have the longest DHSs, and about 30% of genes were highly expressed with RPKM values greater than 30, while about 12% of genes in this group had the RPKM value less than 1. From the top (long DHSs) to the bottom (short DHSs) group, the percentage of highly expressed genes declined, while the percentage of low expression genes increased dramatically (Figure 2C). Same results were found in the genes containing multiple DHSs in their proximal promoter (sorted based on the longest length or total length of multiple DHSs) (Figure 2C, Supplemental Figure 5). These findings suggested that genes with longer DHSs at their promoter region display comparative higher expression level.

Trimethylation at lysine 4 of histone H3 (H3K4me3) is regarded as an active histone mark associated with gene transcription (Schubeler et al., 2004; Bernstein et al., 2005; Zhang et al., 2009). To determine whether DHSs and H3K4me3 coordinately affect transcription, chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled with next-generation sequencing (ChIP-seq) was performed using specific antibodies against H3K4me3. Consistent with the DNaseI-seq analysis, 20 DPA and break stage fruits were collected for anti-H3K4me3 ChIP-seq and two biological replicates were performed. A total of 81.7 million reads were uniquely mapped to the tomato genome (Supplemental Table 1). The reads counts across the genome of these two biological replicates are highly correlated in these two developmental stages (Supplemental Figure 6). The cross-correlation matrices further showed that the ChIP-seq data were in good quality (Supplemental Figure 7), and

common peaks between these two biological replicates are used for further analysis.

A total of 9,261 and 13,555 peaks were obtained from 20 DPA and break stage fruits, respectively, using MACS2 with a q-value threshold less than 0.001 (Zhang et al., 2008). The H3K4me3 profile was consistent with previous reports in other plants with significant enrichment around the TSS region (Supplemental Figure 8) (Zhang et al., 2009; Du et al., 2013), and genes containing H3K4me3 peaks from -200 to 500 bp relative to the TSS were referred as H3K4me3-associated genes. To investigate the relationship among DHSs, H3K4me3 and gene expression levels, we firstly evaluated the concurrence of DHSs and H3K4me3 within individual genes. We defined genes with RPKM values greater than 0.1 as expressed genes and divided expressed genes into ten equal groups according to their expression level. In the group with the highest expression, approximately 22% of genes at 20 DPA contained both DHSs and H3K4me3 and another 53% contained either DHSs or the active histone mark. As expression reduction, fewer genes were found to contain both DHSs and H3K4me3, and more genes had neither of these two marks. The same trend was also observed in break stage fruits (Figure 2D). All these results suggest that histone active mark H3K4me3 and open chromatin state around the gene might cooperatively regulate the transcription levels of genes.

#### **Stage-specific DHSs during tomato fruit development**

Although numerous efforts have been made to uncover the mechanism regulating fruit ripening in tomato (Vrebalov et al., 2009; Fujisawa et al.,

2013; Fujisawa et al., 2014), little is known about the dynamic changes of transcriptional regulation during fruit development. To gain insight into the variation of regulatory region through tomato fruit development, we compared DHSs obtained from 20 DPA and break stage fruits and found obvious differences at some DHSs between these two stages (Figure 3A). A total of 54,832 DHSs specific to the 20 DPA were identified (i.e., absent at break stage), and the number of break stage-specific DHSs was 29,012 (Figure 3B). For example, IAA6 (Solyc03g120500) and IAA11 (Solyc03g120380), two members of the Aux/IAA gene family with important roles in auxin signaling, contained DHSs in their promoters specifically at 20 DPA, but not at break stage (Figure 3C). Conversely, *RIN* (*Solyc05g012020*) and NOR (Solyc10g006880), the key regulators of fruit ripening, only contained break stage-specific DHSs in their promoters (Figure 3D). These results suggested that hypersensitive sites related with transcriptional regulation of these genes vary at different fruit developmental stages. Consistent with changes of DHSs, we found that *IAA6* and *IAA11* were highly expressed at 20 DPA while expression levels of RIN and NOR were highest at break stage (Figure 3F). In addition to the known genes, numerous development- and ripening-specific DHSs were identified at the promoter regions of previously uncharacterized genes. For example, Solyc02g067380, a bHLH transcription factor, contained a 20 DPA-specific DHS in its promoter, while a lipoxygenase gene Solyc01g006540 had a break stage-specific DHS (Figure 3C and D). Varied expression levels of these genes between stages suggested that their transcripts might be regulated temporally by stage-specific DHSs during fruit developmental stages. In brief, these identified DHSs provide a large number of regulatory sites of genes during fruit development.

In addition to stage-specific DHSs, 53,836 common DHSs were found in both 20 DPA and break stage fruits (Figure 3B), but some of these DHSs showed different intensity between two distinct stages. For example, the promoter and gene body regions of *PSY1 (Solyc03g031860)*, a key enzyme involved in lycopene biosynthesis, were much more accessible to DNase I at break stage than at 20 DPA (Figure 3E). Consistent with the intensity of DHSs at this gene, *PSY1* showed highest expression at break stage though it was also expressed at other developmental stages (Figure 3F). These findings suggest that the alteration of chromatin status of *PSY1* increased its accessibility and led to the increase of its expression at break stage.

# Gene-distal DHSs marked by H3K4me3 are highly overlapped with long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) genes

Apart from gene-proximal DHSs, numerous DHSs were located in the intergenic region. DHSs located in the intergenic region (>1 kb downstream of TTS and >3 kb upstream of TSS) were defined as gene-distal DHSs and 21,938 and 15,153 gene-distal DHSs in 20 DPA and break stage fruits were identified, respectively (Figure 4A). We then investigated the overlap between these DHSs and H3K4me3 and found that 448 of 21,506 (about 2%) and 623 of 14,589 (about 4%) gene-distal DHSs were marked by H3K4me3 (> 10% overlapped in DHSs) in 20 DPA and break stage fruits, respectively, which were much less than gene-proximal DHSs marked by H3K4me3 (about 8% or 14%) (Figure 4A). Interestingly, read counts of H3K4me3 were

significantly enriched in gene-distal DHSs by calculating the average reads per base pair of H3K4me3 along  $\pm 5$  kb regions flanking from DHSs, indicating that gene-distal DHSs may also contain functional elements (Figure 4B).

Among the gene-distal DHSs that marked by H3K4me3, 152 and 321 of them were identified specifically in 20 DPA and break stage fruits, respectively, and 263 were common in both stages (Figure 4C). To evaluate whether these stage specific or common gene-distal DHSs affected gene transcripts (1-3 genes near the DHSs), we compared expression levels of genes near specific gene-distal DHSs between 20 DPA and break stage. No significant difference of expression levels of these genes was detected between these two stages, suggesting that these gene-distal DHSs could not regulate near coding gene transcription (Supplemental Table 3).

In *Arabidopsis*, about 19.8% of the gene-distal DHSs were overlapped with noncoding RNA genes (Zhu et al., 2015a). Thus we explored the relationship between DHS and noncoding RNA genes in tomato, using 3,566 long noncoding RNA genes (lncRNA genes) reported in tomato genome (except chromosome 0) (Zhu et al., 2015b). A total of 35 and 114 lncRNA genes have a minimum of 1 bp overlapping with the stage-specific gene-distal DHSs in 20 DPA and break stage fruit, respectively, and 83 were overlapped with the common gene-distal DHSs (Figure 4D). All these numbers are significantly higher than the number of lncRNA genes overlapped with permutated peaks on intergenic regions (empirical, *P* value < 0.001), indicating the co-occurrence of DHS and lncRNA genes are not random, and these gene-distal DHSs may be responsible for the regulation of these lncRNA genes. In these distal-DHS regions, some known and *de novo* motifs (defined by HOMER) were found in break stage-specific or common distal DHSs that marked by H3K4me3, but not in 20 DPA stage-specific gene-distal DHSs (Figure 4E, Supplemental Figure 9-12). For example, a CCAAAAADGG motif which is recognized by MADS-box transcription factors were detected in break stage stage-specific and common distal DHSs (Figure 4E).

# Distinct regulation of genes at different stages during tomato fruit development

To further assess the developmental regulation of genes with stage-specific DHSs at promoter region, we classified genes according to DHSs in their promoter region at each stage. A total of 5,601 and 2,510 genes were associated with stage-specific DHSs at 20 DPA and break stage, respectively, suggesting that a considerable number of genes in tomato are dynamically regulated with fruit development. Besides that, 9,510 genes were associated with common DHSs (Figure 5A). Interestingly, we observed that 516 genes contain non-overlapping DHSs at 20 DPA and break stage (Figure 5B). For example, *Solyc04g007000*, which encodes an ethylene-responsive transcription factor, contained two obvious distinct DHSs at 20 DPA and break stage (Figure 5C). These findings suggest that these genes may be differentially regulated at distinct fruit developmental stages. These overlapped genes at two different stages were found significantly (P < 0.01) enriched in cellular amino acid derivative metabolic process, lignin metabolic process, phenylpropanoid metabolic process (Supplemental Table 4).

DHSs can reveal binding sites of transcription factors at the genome. Previous study had identified 4,118 genes as RIN binding sites by ChIP and 292 of them were considered as potential RIN target genes combining with their transcriptome data (Zhong et al., 2013). We examined the RIN binding sites associated with DHSs in our DNaseI-seq data sets of different fruit developmental stages and found that RIN binding sites and potential RIN target genes are significantly (P<0.001) overlapped with the specific DHSs-containing genes in break stage fruit but not those in 20 DPA fruit or shared genes between 20 DPA and break stage fruits, which is consistent with major function of RIN in fruit ripening (Figure 5D-F). Thus, our DHS data sets provide candidate genes which are regulated during tomato development and ripening in a stage-specific manner.

# Identification of regulatory DNA elements at different stages of tomato fruit development

From the transcriptome data for four developmental stages of tomato fruit, we identified a total of 11,279 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by pairwise comparison among all stages with a threshold of *P* value less than 0.01 (Figure 6A). All of the DEGs were clustered into 6 groups with distinct expression patterns among the different stages (Figure 6B). Genes in cluster 1 and cluster 3 exhibited the highest expression at 20 DPA or break stage and had low expression levels at other stages. Cluster 2 genes were highly expressed at both 20 DPA and break stage but exhibited much lower expression levels at 40 DPA and mature green stage. Cluster 4 and cluster 5 genes showed a similar expression pattern with high expression levels at

mature green stage, while their expression levels at 40 DPA were significantly different. Cluster 6 genes showed a peak expression at 40 DPA and the expression levels were then gradually decreased during fruit development (Figure 6B). The genes in these 6 clusters were compared to the genes associated with specific DHSs at 20 DPA or break stage. Genes associated with 20 DPA-specific DHSs were significantly enriched in clusters 1 and 6, whose expression levels were much higher at 20 DPA than at break stage. Consistently, genes containing break stage-specific DHSs were significantly enriched in cluster 3, which had much higher expression at break stage than at 20 DPA (Supplemental Table 5). These results indicate that alteration of gene expression was correlated with the change of chromatin status during fruit development.

We analyzed the *cis*-regulatory elements (CREs) within DHSs located from -1,000 to 100 bp relative to the TSS of genes in clusters 1 and 3, which contain genes specifically expressing at the represented stages for DHS analysis. A number of transcription factor binding motifs were identified in the promoters of several known targets in each cluster. For example, the CACCTACC motif, a binding sequence of SIMYB12, was found in the 20 DPA specific-DHS located at the promoter of *ENO* (*Solyc03g114500*), which is a reported target of SIMYB12 in tomato (Zhang et al., 2015). Consistently, *ENO* was highly expressed at 20 DPA, but not at break stage (Figure 6C). Moreover, the motif CYWWWWWRG, which has been characterized as a recognition sequence of RIN, was identified in the promoter of *PG2A* (*Solyc10g080210*), which had higher expression at break stage (Figure 6C) and is reported as one target of RIN (Martel et al., 2011). These results

suggested that genes with temporal expression pattern during tomato fruit development might be regulated by stage-specific CREs.

We further identified other regulatory elements in the promoter regions of genes in cluster 1 and cluster 3 by HOMER. Several known and *de novo* motifs (defined by HOMER) significantly enriched in these two clusters were determined (Figure 6D, Supplemental Figure 13-16). The CCAAAAADGG motif was specifically enriched in the 5' region around the TSS in cluster 1 (Figure 6D), suggesting that a significant proportion of genes are regulated by MADS-box transcription factors, which may play important roles in tomato fruit expansion. A previously uncharacterized motif, RSYMATYHVN, was enriched in cluster 3 (Figure 6D), suggesting that uncharacterized transcription factors may function in regulating tomato fruit ripening.

# Coordinate regulation of ascorbic acid metabolic genes during tomato fruit development

During tomato fruit development and ripening, coordinate genetic regulation and biochemical events occur in numerous metabolic pathways and result in changes of fruit color, texture and flavor (Tohge et al., 2014). As an important natural nutrient, the ascorbic acid biosynthesis is well characterized in terms of the constituent enzyme-encoding genes (Zou et al., 2006). In tomato fruit, ascorbic acid can be synthesized through two independent pathways (Figure 7A). One involves the conversion of D-glucose to GDP-D-mannose, GDP-L-galactose, L-galactose, L-galactono-1,4-lactone and ascorbic acid (Wheeler et al., 1998) and the other uses myo-inositol as the precursor (Lorence et al., 2004). This complex network relies on the coordination of

key enzymes, which exhibit distinct temporal expression patterns during fruit development (Figure 7B). To investigate whether the ascorbic acid metabolic genes were co-regulated, we isolated DHSs from genes involved in ascorbic acid metabolism and further analyzed the enrichment of *cis*-regulatory DNA sequences within DHSs at promoter regions of these genes (Figure 7C). The MADS-box recognition motif was significantly enriched among these DHSs at both 20 DPA and break stage (Supplemental Figure 17). However, the motif CCAAT was only significantly enriched among these DHSs at break stage (Figure 7D). Seven in 24 genes contained the identical CCAAT core sequence within their promoters, and the motif was present more than once in the GGP1, APX3 and APX5 genes (Figure 7D). The CCAAT motif was previously identified as a binding sequence of NF-Y transcription factors in Arabidopsis (Calvenzani et al., 2012). A recent study reports that the transcript levels of NF-YC1, which encodes one of the NF-Y family transcription factors in tomato, was highly correlated with the expression of ascorbic acid metabolic genes (Ye et al., 2015). Our results suggest that several major genes involving in ascorbic acid biosynthesis might be regulated coordinately by the same transcription factor during tomato fruit development.

### DISCUSSION

DHS maps have been generated in many human cell types and several plant species, including *Arabidopsis* and rice (John et al., 2011; He et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Inoue and Imai, 2014; Sullivan et al., 2014), and provide an unprecedented genome-wide view of complex gene

regulation. Specifically, DHS information is particularly valuable for the identification of *cis*-regulatory DNA elements, which are bound by regulatory proteins such as transcription factors (Neph et al., 2012; Inoue and Imai, 2014; Sullivan et al., 2014). Similar to the euchromatin distribution patterns reported for rice and *Arabidopsis* chromosomes (Zhang et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Sullivan et al., 2014), tomato DHSs were also primarily located in enriched genic region. However, a major difference observed in tomato was that only 15% of DHSs were detected within the 1 kb region upstream of the TSS (the putative promoter region), which is much less than that in *Arabidopsis* and rice (Zhang et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Sullivan et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Sullivan et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Sullivan et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Sullivan et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Sullivan et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Sullivan et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Sullivan et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Sullivan et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012b; Zhang et al., 2012a; Sullivan et al., 2014). This difference may reflect the larger size of the tomato genome (900 Mb) compared to the smaller and more compact genomes of *Arabidopsis* (125 Mb) and rice (450 Mb).

DHS distribution is also connected to gene expression regulation (Weintraub and Groudine, 1976; Stalder et al., 1980; Wood and Felsenfeld, 1982; Jantzen et al., 1986; Boyle et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2012b; Du et al., 2013; He et al., 2014). We found that DHS length in promoters and the 5'UTR in tomato were about 150 bp longer than in other regions, which is consistent with previous observations (Stitzel et al., 2010; Wu et al., 2014). Our comparative analysis showed that DHS length was positive correlated with gene expression levels, as genes associated with longer DHSs had higher transcription levels. Moreover, the consistency of gene expression levels with the presence of H3K4me3 and DHSs in their promoters or TSS regions in this study further confirms the link between active chromatin status and open chromatin in coordination of gene transcript regulation (Du et

al., 2013).

DHSs vary among different tissues and cell types according to the developmental stage and in response to environmental cues (Consortium, 2012; He et al., 2012; Mercer et al., 2013; Inoue and Imai, 2014; Sullivan et al., 2014; Wilken et al., 2015). For example, DHSs previously identified in rice callus were 58% more than those in seedling (Zhang et al., 2012b). Also a few common DHSs are shared among different tissues (Song et al., 2011). In our work, there were dynamic changes in the DHS profile over the course of fruit development. Interestingly, we also found several genes harboring different *cis*-regulatory elements in their promoters at two distinct developmental stages. In aggregate, our results offer valuable information about DHSs in the tomato genome, which may provide better understanding of the dynamic changes in chromatin accessibility and gene expression during fruit development.

Across different plant species, metabolic pathway components have been found coordinately regulated by regulatory proteins, such as factors in the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway in *Arabidopsis* and tomato and the cucurbitacin C biosynthetic pathway in cucumber (Cui et al., 2014; Shang et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2015). The cucumber transcription factors Bi (Bitter leaf) and Bt (Bitter fruit) have been proposed to coordinately regulate genes involved in cucurbitacin C biosynthesis by binding the E-box element, CANNTG, in the promoters of their targets in leaf or fruit (Shang et al., 2014). DHSs were used as general markers for identifying the locations of CREs (Gross and Garrard, 1988; Henikoff et al., 2009; Boyle et al., 2011; John et al., 2011; He et al., 2012; Inoue and Imai, 2014; Vierstra et al., 2014; Wilken et al., 2015). In this study, we found that the promoters of some key genes encoding ascorbic acid biosynthetic enzymes in tomato harbor the element CCAAT, which is directly bound by NF-YB/YC in *Arabidopsis* (Calvenzani et al., 2012). Although the precise critical factor (NF-YB or NF-YC) is not fully determined and how NF-YB/YC regulates ascorbic acid biosynthesis is still unknown, our work has uncovered the existence of potential coordinate regulatory proteins participating in ascorbic acid biosynthesis in tomato. We also found that the promoter of *ENO*, a key phenylpropanoid biosynthetic gene, possesses the *cis*-regulatory element directly bound by MYB12 and *PG2A* by RIN. Thus, our approach of combining DNaseI-seq and gene expression data is practicable to identify CREs and will provide valuable and corroboratory information of regulatory protein binding sites.

#### **MATERIALS and METHODS**

#### **Plant materials**

Seeds of the tomato cultivar 'Moneymaker' were germinated in pots containing a 2:1:1 mixture of vermiculite, peat moss and perlite and grown at  $23 \pm 2^{\circ}$ C under a 16-h-light/8-h-dark cycle. After germination, seedlings were transferred into soil and grown under the same light-dark conditions in a greenhouse. The whole fruits were collected at the 20 DPA, 40 DPA, mature green and break fruit stages. The break stage was defined as the time when less than 10% of fruit skin was turning color. Then the fruit materials were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for the DNaseI-seq, ChIP-seq and RNA-seq experiments.

#### Library construction for DNaseI-seq and ChIP-seq

For the DNaseI-seq and ChIP-seq analysis, 1 g of 20 DPA fruit or 7 g of break stage fruit was ground to fine powder with a mortar and pestle in liquid nitrogen. The powder was suspended in nuclear isolation buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 0.4 M sucrose, 10 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 60 mM KCl, 1 mM Spermidine, 1 mM Spermine and 0.15% β-mercaptoethanol), and a previously published protocol was used for nuclei isolation (Ricardi et al., 2014). The prepared nuclei pellets were resuspended in 80 µl extract buffer (15 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 15 mM NaCl, 60 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM EGTA, 0.5 mM Spermidine and 0.3 mM Spermine). For each DNase I reaction, the nuclei suspension was pre-warmed at 37°C for 1 minute and then quickly mixed with 80  $\mu$ l pre-warmed 2× reaction buffer (400 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 60 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub> and 10 mM CaCl<sub>2</sub>, pH 7.9) to attain final DNase I concentrations of 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4 and 6 units. The reaction was carried out in a 37°C water bath for 10 minutes, and then terminated by adding 160 µl stop solution (50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS and 100 mM EDTA). Pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE; 20-60 switch time, 18 h, 6 V/cm; Bio-Rad) was used to check the digestion quality (Zhang and Jiang, 2015). We chose a concentration of 4 units DNase I to proceed with size fractionation (Supplemental Figure 18). Fragments less than 500 bp in length were obtained by sucrose gradient centrifugation (John et al., 2013). To purify the 100-150 bp fragments, the samples were size-fractionated using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman A63881). Digital DNase I libraries were constructed as previously described (Lu et al.,

2011) and sequenced using an Illumina single-end 50 bp strategy. ChIP was performed following a published protocol with minor modifications (Lu et al., 2011). Commercial antibodies against H3K4me3 (Millipore, 07-473) were used in the ChIP experiments. The ChIP DNA was ligated with Illumina single-end genome sequencing adaptors, size fractionated to obtain the 300 bp fragments, PCR amplified and sequenced with a single-end 50 bp strategy.

#### **RNA extraction and RNA-seq analysis**

Tomato fruits at 20 DPA, 40 DPA, mature green and break stages were used for RNA-seq analysis. Three biological replicates were prepared for each stage. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596-026), and contaminant DNA was removed using the TURBO DNA-free<sup>™</sup> Kit (Ambion, AM1907). Transcriptome libraries covering the different developmental stages were constructed using the dUTP method (Parkhomchuk et al., 2009), thereby allowing the transcript direction and strand-specific information to be reserved. The RNA-seq libraries were sequenced separately on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform with a single-end 50 bp strategy. STAR software (Dobin et al., 2013) was used to align the sequencing reads to the tomato genome (SL2.50), and FeatureCounts (Liao et al., 2014) was used to analyze the expression of annotated genes. The expression levels for gene models from ITAG2.4 were measured and normalized as reads per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (RPKM) (Mortazavi et al., 2008). The statistical package DEGseq was used for *P* value calculation with the MA-plot-based method (Wang et al., 2010). P < 0.01 with multiple testing was used as the threshold for the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Hierarchical clustering and heatmap generation was implemented using the heatmap.2 functions of the R statistical computing language v3.2.3.

#### ChIP-seq data analysis

The ChIP-seq reads were aligned to the tomato genome (SL2.50) using the BOWTIE program with the parameters "-v 0 -m 1" (Langmead et al., 2009). Only reads mapped to a unique position in the tomato genome were used for further analysis. ChIP-enriched peaks were identified using MACS2 (Zhang et al., 2008) with the parameters 'macs2 callpeak -f BAM -B -q 0.001'. Nearest genes associated with the enriched peaks were annotated using the annotatePeaks function. Genes associated with H3K4me3 were defined as genes with enriched peaks located in the region from -200 to 500 bp relative to the TSS.

#### DNaseI-seq data analysis

The DNaseI-seq reads were aligned to the tomato genome (SL2.50) using the BOWTIE program with the parameters "-v 0 -m 1" (Langmead et al., 2009). Only reads that mapped to a unique position in the tomato genome were used for further analysis. Read depth was normalized for all samples by subsampling each sample to the same read depth. DHSs were identified using the HOMER (Heinz et al., 2010) findPeaks function with the parameters 'findPeaks -style factor -region -C 0 -fdr 0.0001 -o auto -tbp 0'. The non-specific peaks were identified using the DNaseI digested naked DNA sequencing and removed from DHS. Annotation of the peaks was performed

with the HOMER annotatePeaks function using the gene annotation from International Tomato Annotation Group 2.4 (ITAG2.4). The promoter region was defined as -2000 to 0 bp relative to the transcription start site (TSS). The transcription termination site (TTS) region was defined as the 1,000 bp region downstream of the TTS. The HOMER mergePeaks function was then used to identify the specific DHSs and common DHSs at the two stages; the overlapped (a minimum of 1 bp) DHSs between 20 DPA and break stage fruit were merged into one DHS. The reads densities were calculated by reads per base pair. The visualizations of peaks were visualized by Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) (Robinson et al., 2011; Thorvaldsdottir et al., 2013). The best homologous genes of tomato in Arabidopsis were retrieved from Ensembl Plants (http://plants.ensembl.org). GO analysis was performed using the best homologous genes in Arabidopsis with DAVID (Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery, https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) (Huang da et al., 2009). For the identification of cis-regulatory elements (CREs), a 200 bp region centered around the DHSs was used for motif screening by the findMotifsGenome function in HOMER with the parameters 'findMotifsGenome.pl -size 200 -mask -dumpFasta'. Only the known motifs in HOMER with P <0.01 and de novo motif in HOMER with P <1  $\times$  10<sup>-12</sup> were considered to be enriched motifs.

For calculating DHSs enrichment in different parts of the genome (observed/shuffled) ratios: we used a moving window of 1 bp to extract the presumptive reads (sequences with 50 bp) from the tomato reference genome (S\_lycopersicum\_chromosomes.2.50.fa) to develop a fasta.data. And then we used BOWTIE with the same parameters as DNaseI-seq or ChIP-seq analyses

to map this fasta.data to tomato reference genome (SL2.50). The unmappable areas were then taken into account to calculating DHSs enrichment in different parts of the genome ratios. These unmappable areas were also taken into account to calculate the number of overlapped peaks between presumptive 20 DPA- or break stage-specific or their common gene-distal DHSs and lncRNA genes.

### **Permutation of DHSs**

For calculating DHSs enrichment in different parts of the genome ratios and the number of overlapped peaks between presumptive 20 DPA- or break stage-specific or their common gene-distal DHSs and lncRNA genes, we compared the ratio between observed DHSs with shuffled DHSs by randomly selected same number peaks on the genome with same length. The unmappable areas were excluded in shuffling DHS and the unmappable areas were determined as following: we used a moving window of 1 bp to extract the presumptive reads (sequences with 50 bp) from the tomato reference genome (SL2.50) and map to tomato reference genome using BOWTIE with the same parameters as DNaseI-seq or ChIP-seq analyses, and the regions with no reads aligned were regarded as unmappable regions.
