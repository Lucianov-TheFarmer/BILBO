## 45 **Summary**

46 Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) associations enhance the phosphorous and nitrogen nutrition of host plants, but little is known about their role in potassium (K+ 47 ) nutrition. *Medicago truncatula*  plants were co-cultured with the AM fungus *Rhizophagus irregularis* under high and low K+ 48 regimes for six weeks. We determined how K+ 49 deprivation affects plant development, mineral 50 acquisition, and how these negative effects are tempered by the AM colonization. The transcriptional response of AM roots under K+ 51 deficiency was analyzed by whole genome RNAseq. K+ deprivation decreased root biomass, external K+ 52 uptake, and modulated oxidative stress 53 gene expression in *M. truncatula* roots. AM colonization induced specific transcriptional responses to K<sup>+</sup> 54 deprivation that seem to temper these negative effects. A gene network analysis 55 revealed putative key regulators of these responses. This study confirmed that AM associations provide some tolerance to K+ 56 deprivation to host plants, revealed that AM symbiosis modulates 57 the expression of specific root genes to cope with this nutrient stress, and identified putative 58 regulators participating in these tolerance mechanisms.

## 59 **Introduction**

Potassium (K+ 60 ) is a macronutrient required by all organisms for growth and development. In plants, K+ 61 participates in many diverse processes including plasma membrane polarization, 62 growth, stomatal aperture regulation, and the acquisition of other nutrients (Wang and Wu, 2013). K+ 63 represents 2 to 10 % of the plant dry biomass, and its optimal cytoplasmic concentration for enzyme activities is around 100-200 mM (Leigh and Wyn Jones, 1984). Thus, a high cellular K+ 64 65 concentration is vital for plants to maintain a wide range of physiological processes (Adams and Shin, 2014; Benito et al., 2014). Depending on soil type, the concentration of available K+ 66 varies from 0.1 to 1 mM (Asher and Ozanne, 1967). Only two fractions of K+ 67 are immediately accessible to plants: K+ in water solution and K+ 68 in exchangeable form. These two fractions 69 represent about 2.2 % of the total amount of soil K (Zörb et al., 2014). This very low availability 70 combined with the constitutive demand of plants leads to the formation of depletion zones near 71 the root surface (Drew and Nye, 1969). Plants have developed efficient strategies to deal with limited K+ 72 availability, including the establishment of symbiotic associations with microbes.

73 Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi colonize the root cortex inter- and intracellularly 74 (Brundrett, 2004; Harrison, 2005). These AM fungi belong to the fungal subphylum of 75 Glomeromycotina and probably played a critical role in the emergence and evolution of land 76 plants (Delaux et al., 2015; Spatafora et al., 2016). This AM symbiosis is the oldest mycorrhizal 77 association and is found in about 65 % of extant land plants, including many major crop species 78 (Wang and Qiu, 2006). AM associations are characterized by the formation of arbuscules in the 79 cortical cells of host plants, which are the site of nutrient exchange between the plant and the 80 fungus (Smith and Read, 2008). The improvement of plant nutrient acquisition through AM 81 associations becomes particularly important when hydro-mineral resources are scarce. In return 82 for the nutrients provided by AM fungi, host plants provide up to 20-25% of their photosynthesis-83 derived carbohydrates to their symbionts (Fajardo López et al., 2008).

84 The improvement of plant nutrition through AM symbiosis and the molecular basis of 85 nutrient transfer are well characterized for phosphate, nitrogen and sulfate (Govindarajulu et al., 86 2005; Javot et al., 2007; Jin et al., 2012; Casieri et al., 2013; Courty et al., 2016; Garcia et al., 2016). However, the role of mycorrhizal symbioses in plant K+ 87 nutrition is still understudied and 88 misunderstood (Garcia and Zimmermann, 2014). A small number of studies reported AM fungal mediation of plant K+ nutrition. The assessment of K+ 89 distribution in mycorrhizal plants using particle-induced X-ray emission experiments revealed a higher K+ 90 concentration in root sections 91 of *Aster tripolium* colonized by *Rhizophagus irregularis* than in non-inoculated plants (Scheloske et al., 2004). The same trend of K+ 92 enrichment for plants mycorrhized by various AM fungi was 93 also observed in maize root steles (Kaldorf et al., 1999), *Pelargonium peltatum* shoots (Perner et 94 al., 2007), *Lactuca sativa* leaves (Baslam et al., 2013), and wheat stems (Oliveira et al., 2016). The expression of three K+ 95 transport systems likely involved in phloem loading/unloading (ZmAKT2), xylem release (ZmSKOR), and sodium/K+ 96 homeostasis (ZmSOS1) was differentially 97 regulated in AM maize roots (Estrada et al., 2013). These findings suggested that AM symbioses could play a significant role in plant K<sup>+</sup> 98 nutrition by the reorganization of molecular responses.

If K+ amendments are not used frequently, long-term K+ 99 deprivation can result in 100 irreversible damages to the crop including poor pest tolerance and significant yield losses. Soils from many parts of the world are increasingly K+ 101 deficient, reinforcing the need for understanding how plants can cope with K+ 102 deprivation (Moody and Bell, 2006; Römheld and 103 Kirkby, 2010; Zörb et al., 2014). However, most studies have only investigated the short-term molecular plant responses to K+ 104 deprivation, and not in the context of AM symbiosis. Transcriptional responses of plant roots to K<sup>+</sup> 105 deprivation was studied in various species

106 including *Arabidopsis thaliana* (Armengaud et al., 2004), rice (Ma et al., 2012; Shankar et al., 107 2013), wheat (Ruan et al., 2015), and sugarcane (Zeng et al., 2015). However, in all these studies, K+ 108 was completely absent from the medium, and the plants were analyzed after a relatively short 109 period of time (1-2 weeks). Following these drastic and short-term treatments, the up-regulation of high-affinity K+ 110 transporters was often reported. This response is a rapid and transient strategy 111 but probably not a sustainable one if the stress is prolonged. The transcriptional response of plant roots colonized by an AM fungus during K+ 112 deprivation is still unknown.

To address this question, we examined for the first time the effect of long-term K+ 113 114 deprivation on the development, nutrition, gene expression and AM formation using the model 115 legume *Medicago truncatula*. To evaluate the impact of AM symbiosis on plant adaptation to long-term K<sup>+</sup> 116 deprivation, we inoculated *M. truncatula* plants with the AM fungus *R. irregularis* and watered them with high or low K<sup>+</sup> 117 solutions. Plant biomass and the tissue content of various ions were determined after six weeks. The transcriptional profile of AM roots under K<sup>+</sup> 118 119 deprivation was investigated using RNA-Seq and revealed mycorrhizal-specific responses under K+ 120 deprivation. Finally, a network-based prioritization analysis revealed putative regulators controlling AM symbiosis and K+ 121 deprivation in *M. truncatula*.

## 154 **Arbuscular mycorrhizal association reverses most transcriptional responses to potassium** 155 **deprivation and reduces the production of ROS in** *Medicago truncatula* **roots**

RNA-Seq was performed on roots from AM and NM plants grown at low or high K+ 156 regime. 157 RNAs from three to four biological replicates composed of four plants each were sequenced, and 158 the reliability of our dataset was analyzed using principal component analyses and 159 multidimensional scaling (Fig. S1, S2). K-means clustering was also performed to identify 160 transcripts following the same expression profile along each condition: AM or NM plants at high (AM+K or NM+K) or low (AM-K or NM-K) K+ 161 levels. This analysis revealed groups of genes 162 with condition-specific responses. For example, two clusters of genes that were mainly up-163 regulated (clusters 1 and 21) and two that were mainly down-regulated (clusters 8 and 20) in the 164 AM-K condition (Fig. S3, Table S4).

The first step was to identify transcripts specifically regulated at a low K+ 165 level in NM 166 plants. Taking the NM+K condition as control, DEseq analysis allowed the identification of 353 and 317 up- and down-regulated genes, respectively, in *M. truncatula* roots under K+ 167 deprivation 168 (Fig. 2A, Table S1). GO enrichment analysis identified a very significant over-representation of 169 metal binding transcripts, particularly among up-regulated transcripts (Fig. 2B). Of these proteins 170 annotated as metal binding, one-third encoded peroxidases (6 and 2 were up- or down-regulated, 171 respectively) or cytochrome P450 enzymes (2 and 15 were up- or down-regulated, respectively). 172 These proteins are known to play an important role in oxidative stress responses in plants, and in particular under K+ 173 deprivation (Apel and Hirt, 2004; Hernandez et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2012). 174 Overall, 62 transcripts encoding proteins involved in oxidative stress were differentially regulated under K+ 175 deprivation (Table S2), and both up- and down-regulated transcript groups were 176 significantly enriched for oxidoreductases (Fig. 2B). Also, two-thirds of the differentially

177 expressed hydrolases were down-regulated (Fig. 2B, Table S3). Overall, our data revealed that many enzymes were differentially regulated in *M. truncatula* roots under K+ 178 deprivation, 179 suggesting that post-translational regulations play an important role in the long-term adaptation to K+ 180 deprivation.

To evaluate how AM symbiosis affects the transcriptional response of *M. truncatula* to K+ 181 182 deprivation, the expression of transcripts regulated in NM-K plants was investigated in AM-K plants. Investigating the expression of genes in AM plants at high and low K+ 183 levels revealed that the expression of two-thirds of them (387 transcripts) was altered in AM plants under K+ 184 185 deprivation (Fig. 3). As mentioned above, many of these transcripts code for proteins likely 186 involved in oxidative stress responses. Thus, the production of ROS in *M. truncatula* roots colonized or not by *R. irregularis* at high or low K+ 187 was determined using carboxy-H2DFFDA (Fig. 4). In NM plants, K+ 188 deprivation significantly enhanced the production of ROS in roots. 189 However, this effect was significantly reduced and visible only in a few cells when plants are 190 mycorrhized (Fig. 4A,B).

191 Altogether these observations revealed that AM symbiosis compensates the 192 transcriptional response of *M. truncatula* roots, and prevents the production of ROS in roots under K+ 193 -limiting conditions.

## 195 **Specific transcriptional responses are triggered by arbuscular mycorrhizal association** 196 **under potassium deprivation in** *Medicago truncatula* **roots**

197 Using NM+K plants as control, DEGs regulated in AM+K, AM-K, and NM-K plants were 198 identified to highlight specific and shared expression patterns in each condition (Fig. S4, Table 199 S5). Although, 230 transcripts were similarly and significantly regulated in both AM+K and AM-200 K plants, revealing molecular players that were involved in AM symbiosis independently of extracellular K+ 201 availability, including various transport systems, a total of 511 transcripts were 202 specifically regulated in plant roots under AM-K conditions (Fig. S5, Table S5). A Gene 203 Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed an overrepresentation of genes involved in redox 204 homeostasis, plant cell wall formation, and responses to oxidative stress (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, five transcripts encoding for transport systems were up-regulated in AM plants under K+ 205 deprivation, including an ortholog of the plasma membrane K+ /H+ 206 exchanger AtCHX20 from *A.*  207 *thaliana* (*Medtr7g099800.1*), a putative MFS transporter (*Medtr2g081930.1*), a mitochondrial 208 phosphate transporter (*Medtr6g033280.1*), and vacuolar malate (*Medtr4g133230.1*) or ion 209 (*Medtr4g094332.1*) transporters (Fig. 5B, S6). Among the 30 most DEGs in AM-K plants (Table 210 1), a clade A type 2C protein phosphatases (*Medtr5g009370.1*) was expressed and could be involved in the posttranslational regulation of K+ 211 transport systems. These results suggest that AM symbiosis activates specific mechanisms to tolerate long-term K+ 212 deprivation, including transport mechanisms to likely facilitate the acquisition of K+ 213 .

## 215 **Identification of putative regulators for the long-term adaptation of** *Medicago truncatula* **to** 216 **potassium deprivation in mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal roots**

We sought to identify regulatory networks associated with the AM and K+ 217 DEG sets previously 218 described (Fig. S4) by comparing them to our global *M. truncatula* regulatory network (Marx et 219 al., 2016). This network was inferred using the MERLIN algorithm (Roy et al., 2013). We 220 defined five condition-specific gene sets, each corresponding to a bar in Fig. S4 and Table S5. To 221 assess the connectivity patterns, we overlaid the gene sets on the regulatory network 222 (Supplementary Methods). Fig. 6 shows regulatory network connections involving the DEGs and 223 their prioritized network neighbors, summarized into three major categories: genes differentially expressed in AM roots regardless of K+ 224 level (Fig. 6A(i)), specifically differentially expressed in AM-K condition (Fig. 6B(i)), and differentially expressed under K+ 225 deprivation, regardless of 226 AM status (Fig. 6C(i)). Genes within each gene set were statistically enriched for direct 227 interactions compared to randomly chosen genes, controlling for the degree (Z-score >= 7.09; 228 Table S6). Within each condition, genes with the same direction of expression change (up or 229 down regulated) represented distinct network components (Fig. 6 panel (i)) and had significantly 230 shorter network distances than pairs of genes with opposite expression patterns (Mann-Whitney 231 rank sum test p-values<0.001; Fig. S7). This result indicates that genes that were induced 232 represent distinct components of a pathway compared to the genes that were repressed. Distinct 233 regulatory systems are likely involved in controlling the up- and down-regulated components of 234 each DEG set.

235 Two complementary network-based approaches were used to identify important regulatory genes associated with responses to AM and low K+ 236 conditions: diffusion kernel-based 237 prioritization and statistical enrichment of targets of regulators in DEG sets. While the diffusion 238 kernel approach can prioritize any gene in the network, the gene set enrichment analysis focuses 239 on regulatory proteins such as transcription factors and signaling proteins. The diffusion kernel-240 based approach identified several candidate genes from AM roots putatively involved in response to K+ 241 deprivation (Fig. 6B(ii), Table 2). For example, among the top 25 predicted genes for the 242 AM-K DEG set were nine kinases including a CBL-interacting kinase (Medtr2g105010.1) and an 243 ethylene response factor (Medtr4g078710.1) (Table 2). Full prioritization results for all gene sets 244 are available in supporting information (Table S7).

245 Statistical enrichment of regulatory targets in the DEG sets identified 56 putative 246 regulators (Table S7, Fig. S8, Supplementary Methods) that included both new and known 247 proteins such as MYB, BHLH, and AP2/ERF transcription factors, and chromatin-modifying 248 enzymes. For example, targets from a CBL-interacting kinase (Medtr2g105010.1; 9 targets), a 249 cysteine desulfurase (Medtr8g093560.1; 7 targets) and a GATA transcription factor (Medtr3g109760.1; 24 targets) were enriched for up-regulated genes in response to K+ 250 251 deprivation in both AM and NM conditions (-K, Up), suggesting their involvement in the tolerance to low K+ 252 stress. Similarly, targets from a dehydration-responsive element binding 253 protein (Medtr1g019110.1; 6 targets) a C2H2-type zinc finger protein (Medtr3g102980.1; 3 254 targets) or a LRR receptor-like kinase plant (Medtr8g014970.1; 10 targets) were specifically 255 enriched for up-regulated genes in AM-K condition. Finally, 29 and 17 targets from a cyclin-256 dependent kinase (Medtr8g092290.1) and a tyrosine kinase (Medtr7g116650.1), respectively, were enriched for up-regulated genes in AM roots regardless of extracellular K+ 257 level, suggesting 258 a major role of these proteins in the regulation of AM symbiosis.

259 Interestingly, the regulators identified by both methods were consistent with each other. 260 The 56 regulators identified by the enrichment analysis received diffusion kernel percentile ranks 261 ≥ 95 compared to all ranked transcription factors and signaling proteins in the regulatory 262 network. Each method was also able to identify some unique prioritized genes that the other 263 would miss or rank lower. Overall, prioritization and regulator enrichment approaches revealed 264 promising candidates for further functional analyses of the regulatory network controlling responses to changes in AM fungi and K+ 265 availability in *M. truncatula*.

## 267 **Discussion**

# 268 **Potassium deprivation affects growth, potassium acquisition and gene expression in** 269 *Medicago truncatula* **roots**

K+ 270 is an essential macronutrient for plants. Its limitation contributes to slowing down many vital processes. Plants elicit rapid responses to strong K+ 271 deprivation by inducing plasma membrane 272 hyperpolarization and the up-regulation of high-affinity transporters (Schachtman and Shin, 2007). Prolonged K+ 273 deficiency has been previously reported to limit root development 274 (Armengaud et al., 2004; Shin and Schachtman, 2004; Kellermeier et al., 2013), which was 275 observed in our experiments (Fig. 1). Transcriptional analysis of plants facing short-term and extreme K+ deprivation revealed an increase in expression of K<sup>+</sup> 276 transporters and channels from 277 the HAK, HKT and AKT families (Armengaud et al., 2004; Ma et al., 2012; Shankar et al., 2013; Ruan et al., 2015; Zeng et al., 2015). In our study, we analyzed the impact of long-term K<sup>+</sup> 278 deprivation in the model legume *M. truncatula*. In our case, no K+ 279 transport system was differentially regulated in response to long-term K+ 280 deprivation. Because the goal of our study was also to evaluate how a prolonged low K+ 281 treatment impacts AM symbiosis, and reciprocally how the AM status alters the plant responses to K+ 282 deprivation, the stress condition was 0.05 mM of external K+ 283 over six weeks. This treatment was obviously very different from previous studies in which short and extreme K+ 284 stresses were applied. Therefore, our results suggest that the up-285 regulation of HAK, HKT, and AKT transport systems observed in other plant species is probably 286 a rapid and transient response to an intense stress.

 It is well established that K+ 287 deficiency contributes to the accumulation of reactive 288 oxygen species (ROS) in plant roots, leading to a general oxidative stress (Shin and Schachtman, 2004; Kim et al., 2010; Hernandez et al., 2012). Long-term K+ 289 deprivation in *M. truncatula* 290 resulted in the differential expression of many transcripts related to oxidative stress including 291 peroxidases, cytochrome P450 enzymes, and reductases. Most of the peroxidases were upregulated under K+ 292 deficiency in *M. truncatula* roots. Interestingly, Kim *et al.* (2010) 293 hypothesized that the up-regulation of peroxidases could be the origin of ROS production 294 observed in *A. thaliana* roots. The accumulation of ROS production in *M. truncatula* roots detected under long-term K+ 295 deprivation supports this view and validates the *in silico* prediction 296 we made with our RNA-Seq approach. Overall, many enzymes were differentially regulated in 297 stressed plants, suggesting that posttranscriptional mechanisms may be mainly involved in the tolerance of K+ 298 deprivation (Schachtman and Shin, 2007).

#### 300 **Arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis modulates the plant responses to potassium deprivation**

The role of endo- and ectomycorrhizal associations on plant K+ 301 nutrition has been debated for decades. Although some studies described an improvement of K<sup>+</sup> 302 acquisition in plants 303 colonized by mycorrhizal fungi, others presented apparently contradictory results (reviewed in 304 Garcia & Zimmermann, 2014). The transport of nutrients from the external medium to the host 305 plant through mycorrhizal structures has been investigated using radioactive isotopes (He et al., 2009; Smith et al., 2011). Because K+ isotopes are not stable enough to track K+ 306 movement from 307 the soil to the mycorrhizal plants, some studies have used rubidium as an analog tracer 308 (Rygiewicz and Bledsoe, 1984; Hawkes and Casper, 2002). Further experiments will be needed in *M. truncatula*, using rubidium to evaluate the actual transport of K+ 309 from the soil to the 310 mycorrhizal plants. However, in a previous publication on ectomycorrhizae, we demonstrated an improvement of maritime pine K+ 311 nutrition when colonized by an ectomycorrhizal fungus only under K+ deprivation (Garcia et al., 2014). In the present study, the shoot K+ 312 content of *M. truncatula* was also significantly higher in AM plants compared to NM ones but only at low K<sup>+</sup> 313 314 levels and after six weeks of co-culture. This effect should be investigated in other plant species. We can assume that under K+ -sufficient conditions, host plants can acquire external K+ 315 by themselves. However, when K+ 316 ions in solution have been limiting for a relatively long period of time, AM fungi may help their hosts to acquire K+ 317 from the soil. Our analysis revealed that *M. truncatula* plants colonized by *R. irregularis* displayed an increase of biomass and K+ 318 ion content, and specific transcriptional responses to the low K+ 319 regime. These results confirmed that 320 AM symbiosis was able to provide specific adaptation mechanisms to the host plant to tolerate long-term K<sup>+</sup> 321 deprivation.

322 ROS have been detected in legume roots colonized by both AM fungi and nitrogen-fixing 323 bacteria, particularly in colonized cells (Salzer et al., 1999; Fester and Hause, 2005; Puppo et al., 324 2012). Moreover, some NADPH oxidase encoding genes were recently found up-regulated in 325 colonized cortical cells of *M. truncatula* roots, suggesting their role in arbuscule development 326 (Belmondo et al., 2016a; Belmondo et al., 2016b). Our results revealed that the two-thirds of *M. truncatula* root transcriptional responses to low K+ 327 were absent in mycorrhizal plants, including 328 many enzymes putatively involved in ROS production. The ROS accumulation observed in NM roots was correlated with the external K+ 329 availability but not with the AM symbiosis, suggesting specific ROS production in response to K+ 330 deprivation. This accumulation was significantly reduced in AM plants at low K<sup>+</sup> 331 , indicating that AM symbiosis helped *M. truncatula* to cope with long-term K<sup>+</sup> 332 deprivation.

333 Also, NM plants accumulated much more sodium in shoot than AM ones. Sodium can be 334 toxic to the cells at a high level. By preventing its accumulation, AM symbiosis may protect *M.*  335 *truncatula* from salt stress by buffering the uptake of sodium. Although the role of AM 336 associations on salt tolerance was suggested in other plants (*e.g.* basil, Zuccarini & Okurowska, 337 2008; olive, Porras-Soriano *et al.*, 2009; or corn, Estrada *et al.*, 2013), further experiments will be 338 needed to unravel the molecular mechanisms of this adaptation in *M. truncatula*.

339 The uptake of nutrients from inner symbiotic structures requires the specific expression 340 and regulation of plasma membrane transport systems in colonized cortical cells (Casieri et al., 341 2013; Garcia et al., 2016). Some plant transporters were described to transport phosphorus or 342 nitrogen from AM fungi to plant cells in arbuscules (see for example Harrison *et al.*, 2002; 343 Breuillin-Sessoms *et al.*, 2015). One study in the legume *Lotus japonicus* reported a 44-fold upregulation of a K+ 344 transporter in AM roots compared to NM ones (Guether et al., 2009). However, in transcriptome studies of mycorrhizal roots from *M. truncatula,* no K+ 345 transporter was ever found up-regulated in AM roots under standard K<sup>+</sup> 346 regime (Gomez *et al.*, 2009; Gaude 347 *et al.*, 2012; this study). In our study, we have found that some genes encoding putative transporters were up-regulated in mycorrhizal plants under K+ 348 deprivation, particularly a putative K+ /H+ 349 exchanger (CHX) (*Medtr7g099800.1*). A close homolog of this CHX protein in *A.*  350 *thaliana* was found expressed in guard cells (Padmanaban et al., 2007). Other members of the CHX exchanger family were regulated in starved K+ 351 roots, such as ATCHX17 (Cellier et al., 352 2004). As a consequence, further analyses will be required in *M. truncatula* to determine if and how this CHX can transport K+ during AM symbiosis under long-term K+ 353 deprivation.

## 355 **Network analysis identified putative regulators controlling the tolerance of mycorrhizal** 356 **roots to potassium deprivation**

357 The MERLIN algorithm (Roy et al., 2013) was used to infer an *M. truncatula* regulatory 358 network from publicly available gene expression data representing a wide array of conditions and 359 tissues. Such a network analysis allows identifying regulatory connections between differentially 360 expressed genes but also predicting which specific regulators (signaling proteins and transcription 361 factors) that may control the expression of genes of interest. In our study, this approach identified 362 differentially regulated genes and prioritized putative regulators that could be involved in controlling the tolerance of AM plants to K+ 363 deprivation. Among the top 30 up-regulated genes in 364 AM-K roots, a Clade A protein phosphatase type 2C (Medtr5g009370.1) was identified. This 365 gene encodes an ortholog of *AtPP2CA* in *A. thaliana* involved in the regulation of the weakrectifying K+ channel AKT2 (Chérel et al., 2002) as well as of the uptake and efflux Shaker K+ 366 367 channels AKT1 and GORK, respectively (Lee et al., 2007; Lan et al., 2011; Lefoulon et al., 368 2016). Both prioritization and target enrichment analyses suggested the importance of a CBL-369 interacting kinase (Medtr2g105010.1) in AM-K plant roots. CBL-interacting kinases are major regulators of nutrient transporters, abscisic acid responses and K+ 370 homeostasis (D'Angelo et al., 371 2006; Ho et al., 2009; Hashimoto et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2013; Ragel et al., 2015). This 372 observation suggests a role of Medtr2g105010.1 in the post-translational regulation of transport 373 systems in *M. truncatula* roots colonized by AM fungi to promote the symbiotic acquisition of K+ 374 . The targets of regulators involved in plant adaptation to oxidative stress were also predicted 375 in AM-K roots and included a cysteine desulfurase (Medtr8g093560.1) and a dehydration-376 responsive element binding protein (Medtr1g019110). Dehydration-responsive element binding proteins are involved in salt, and hydric stresses, which are consequences of K+ 377 deprivation in 378 plants, and the overexpression of one of them in *A. thaliana* (AtDREBP2C) improved its 379 tolerance to global oxidative stress (Hwang et al., 2012).

380 Altogether, our study revealed that AM symbiosis allows host plant to cope with longterm K+ 381 deprivation. This tolerance mechanism probably involves the regulation of a gene network to protect host plants against oxidative stress, but also mechanisms to facilitate K+ 382 383 uptake from the soil through symbiotic structures. Further studies will be needed to validate these predictions, and to fully decipher the role of AM associations in plant K+ 384 nutrition.

## 386 **Materials and Methods**

## **Plant growth conditions, fungal inoculation, and long-term K<sup>+</sup>** 387 **deficiency treatment**

388 *M. truncatula* Jemalong A17 seeds were acid-scarified and surface-sterilized, plated on 1% agar supplemented with 1 µg ml-1 389 gibberellic acid, vernalized at 4°C for four days, and allowed to 390 germinate overnight at room temperature. The germinated seedlings were placed for two weeks 391 on modified Fahräeus medium as described previously (Catoira et al., 2000). The plants were 392 transferred to pots filled with Turface® and inoculated with 400 spores of *R. irregularis*. Plants were watered regularly with standard (+K, 3.75 mM of K<sup>+</sup> 393 ) Long Ashton solution (3.75 mM 394 KNO3, 2 mM Ca(NO3)2,4H2O, 7.5 μM NaH2PO4, H2O, 1 mM MgSO4,7H2O, 0.05 mM NaCl, 5.0 μM MnSO4, 0.5 μM CuSO4, 1.0 μM ZnSO4, 16.5 μM H3BO3, 0.1 μM Na2MoO4) or low K+ 395 (-K, 0.05 mM of K+ 396 ) solution (0.05 mM KNO3, 3.85 mM Ca(NO3)2,4H2O, 7.5 μM NaH2PO4, H2O, 1 397 mM MgSO4,7H2O, 0.05 mM NaCl, 5.0 μM MnSO4, 0.5 μM CuSO4, 1.0 μM ZnSO4, 16.5 μM 398 H3BO3, 0.1 μM Na2MoO4). The plants were harvested after six weeks of treatment.

#### 399 **Mycorrhizal quantification, dry weight determination, and ion content in plants**

Shoots and roots of 6-week-old *M. truncatula* plants growing in K<sup>+</sup> 400 -sufficient or -deficient 401 conditions were harvested separately. Mycorrhizal colonization rates were assessed using the 402 gridline intersection method on roots previously stained with Sheaffer ink (McGonigle et al., 403 1990). The fresh weight was determined, and roots and shoots were dried at 60°C for one week 404 for dry weight determination. The dried samples were sent to the Soil & Forage Analysis Laboratory (University of Wisconsin-Madison, Marshfield, USA) to determine the K+ 405 , sodium, 406 phosphorus and calcium content in plant tissues by inductively coupled plasma optical emission 407 spectrometry (ICP-OES).

#### 408 **RNA isolation and sequencing**

409 Frozen root tissues were ground in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted from the resulting powder using PureLink® RNA Mini Kit (Thermofisher), and treated with TURBOTM 410 411 DNase (Thermofisher). RNA quality was assessed using the Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer 412 technology (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Six micrograms of total RNA were 413 used to construct polyA selection libraries using the Illumina TrueSeq RNA Sample Preparation

- 414 kit; 100-nt single-end reads were obtained on an Illumina Hiseq2000 platform using TruSeq v3
- 415 (University of Wisconsin Biotechnology Center DNA Sequencing Facility, USA).

#### 416 **RNA-sequencing analysis, annotations, cut-off, and analysis.**

417 RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) data were processed to obtain differentially expressed genes 418 (DEGs) using TopHat2-SE, Cufflinks2, Cuffmerge2 and Cuffdiff2 applications from the iPlant 419 Collaborative platform (http://www.iplantcollaborative.org). The DESeq Bioconductor package 420 version 3.3 for R (Anders and Huber, 2010) was used for the analysis of DEGs. Cuffdiff2 421 sequencing data were analyzed using the cummeRbund package to generate principal component 422 and multidimensional scaling analyses on biological and technical replicates. K-means clustering 423 was also performed using the cummeRbund package for R with K = 25. For further analyses, 424 DEGs were called by DEseq using a 1.5-fold change cut-off threshold and False Discovery Rate 425 (FDR) adjusted q-value < 0.05. DEGs were annotated with the Mt4.0v1 version of *M. truncatula*  426 Jemalong A17 genome (http://jcvi.org/medicago/index.php). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment 427 analysis was conducted with AgriGO web-based tool 428 (http://bioinfo.cau.edu.cn/agriGO/analysis.php) using the Mt4.0v1 version of *M. truncatula*  429 genome. The Venn diagram was obtained using the Bioinformatics & Evolutionary Genomics 430 web tool (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/). Finally, clustering and heatmaps 431 were generated on R with scripts based on heatmap.2 function as available in the "gplots" 432 Bioconductor package. The data discussed in this publication have been deposited in NCBI's 433 Gene Expression Omnibus (Edgar et al., 2002) and are accessible through GEO Series accession 434 number GSE94266 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE94266).

#### 435 **ROS Detection and Measurement**

To detect ROS, the root samples of AM and NM 6-week-old plants grown under K+ 436 -sufficient or 437 -deficient conditions were incubated with 20 μM carboxy-H2DFFDA (ThermoFisher) for 20 min in high or low K+ solution, respectively. The roots were washed twice with high or low K+ 438 439 solution before microscopy visualization. All fluorescence images were captured using a Leica 440 DMi8 microscope and a Leica DFC365 FX camera. ROS fluorescence was quantified and 441 converted into pseudocolor images using ImageJ software program. Background noise was 442 subtracted from the fluorescence intensity value for quantification.

#### 443 **Regulatory network analysis and visualization.**

444 The base network visualized in all panels of Fig. 7 was selected from a global inferred 445 *M. truncatula* regulatory network (Marx et al., 2016) by extracting any interaction between a 446 differentially expressed gene (DEG) and top *n* computationally prioritized genes for each K+ and 447 AM condition, where *n* is the size of the experimentally identified DEG set. For visual simplicity, 448 connected components involving only one edge were omitted. Network images were created 449 using Cytoscape (Shannon et al., 2003). See Supporting Methods for full details for the 450 regulatory network analyses, including prioritization and regulator gene set enrichment.
