# SUMMARY

During Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation of plant cells a part of the tumour-inducing plasmid, T-DNA, is integrated into the host genome. In addition, a number of virulence proteins are translocated into the host cell. The virulence protein VirE3 binds to the Arabidopsis thaliana pBrp protein, a plantspecific general transcription factor of the TFIIB family. To study a possible role for VirE3 in transcriptional regulation, we stably expressed virE3 in A. thaliana under control of a tamoxifen-inducible promoter. By RNA sequencing we showed that upon expression of virE3 the RNA levels of 607 genes were increased more than three-fold and those of 132 genes decreased more than three-fold. One of the strongly activated genes was that encoding VBF (At1G56250), an F-box protein that may affect the levels of the VirE2 and VIP1 proteins. Using Arabidopsis cell suspension protoplasts we showed that VirE3 stimulates the VBF promoter, especially when co-expressed with pBrp. Although pBrp is localized at the external surface of plastids, co-expression of VirE3 and pBrp in Arabidopsis cell suspension protoplasts resulted in the accumulation of pBrp in the nucleus. Our results suggest that VirE3 affects the transcriptional machinery of the host cell to favour the transformation process.

Keywords: Agrobacterium tumefaciens, Arabidopsis thaliana, RNAseq, VBF, F-box protein, VirE3.

# INTRODUCTION

The plant pathogen Agrobacterium tumefaciens can cause crown gall disease by transferring part of its tumour inducing (Ti) plasmid, called T-DNA, to plant cells, where it integrates into the genome (for review see Hooykaas and Beijersbergen, 1994; Gelvin, 2000; Citovsky et al., 2007; Pitzschke and Hirt, 2010). Due to the expression of oncogenes located on the T-DNA the transformed plant cells proliferate and form crown gall tumours. The genes responsible for T-DNA processing and transfer are present in the virulence (vir) region of the Ti plasmid. The virulence protein VirD2 generates nicks in the bottom strands of the 24-bp border sequences surrounding the T-DNA region. Release of this part of the bottom strand of the Ti plasmid generates the T-strand, which has the VirD2 protein attached at its 50 -end (Vogel and Das, 1992). The VirD2–T-strand complex (T-complex) is transported into the plant cells via a type IV secretion system (T4SS). In the recipient cells the T-complex migrates to the nucleus, which it can enter by virtue of the nuclear localization signal (NLS) of the VirD2 protein.

Some of the virulence proteins (VirE2, VirE3, VirD5 and VirF) are effector proteins that are directly translocated via the T4SS into recipient cells independent of T-DNA (Vergunst et al., 2000; Schrammeijer et al., 2003). The VirE2 protein is a single-strand DNA-binding protein that may coat the T-strand in the cytoplasm of the host cell and may thus convert the T-complex into a long thin thread, which has been proposed to facilitate nuclear entry and prevent degradation by host nucleases (Citovsky and Zambryski, 1993). The VirE2 protein can interact with a bZip transcription factor, called VIP1, in the host (Tzfira et al., 2001). Within minutes of Agrobacterium infection the VIP1 protein is activated by phosphorylation and translocated into the nucleus, where it activates defence genes. The VirF protein contains an F-box motif through which it interacts with plant homologues of the yeast Skp1 protein (ASK1 and ASK2) to form a Skp–Cullin–F-box protein (SCF) complex (Schrammeijer et al., 2001). It has been reported that SCFvirF targets the plant transcription factor VIP1 and its associated Agrobacterium VirE2 protein for degradation by the 26S proteasome (Tzfira et al., 2004). This degradation may be required for uncoating of the Tcomplex in the nucleus, enabling integration of T-DNA into one of the host cell's chromosomes (Tzfira et al., 2004). Degradation of VIP1 may also negatively affect the defence response of the host cells. Some plant species like Arabidopsis thaliana do not require VirF for transformation. It has been shown that in such species the infection with Agrobacterium leads to induction of the expression of a plant F-box protein, VBF, that can functionally replace VirF (Zaltsman et al., 2010).

The VirE3 protein is another translocated effector (Schrammeijer et al., 2003) and is highly conserved in different Ti and Ri plasmids, suggesting that VirE3 plays an important role during AMT. However, upon mutation of virE3 no, or hardly any, attenuation of virulence is seen. Its role in virulence becomes apparent when the virF gene is also inactivated (Garcıa-Rodrıguez et al., 2006). VirE3 has two potential NLS sequences, and it has been shown that VirE3 binds to importin-a and is localized in the nucleus of plant host cells (Lacroix et al., 2005; Garcıa-Rodrıguez et al., 2006). It has also been reported that VirE3 may mimic the function of VIP1 by promoting the entry of VirE2 into the host nucleus (Lacroix et al., 2005). We have shown previously that VirE3 interacts with Csn5, a component of the host signalosome complex, and with pBrp, a plant-specific general transcription factor of the TFIIB family. Coupling of VirE3 to a DNA-binding domain leads to local transcriptional activation, suggesting that VirE3 may act as a transcriptional activator in host cells (Garcıa-Rodrıguez et al., 2006). However, until now direct evidence for a role of VirE3 in transcriptional regulation has been lacking. Here we investigated the effect of expression of virE3 on the genome-wide transcription profile of A. thaliana. One of the genes that is strongly up-regulated upon expression of virE3 turned out to be the gene encoding VBF. In Arabidopsis cell suspension protoplast assays we have confirmed that VirE3, assisted by pBrp, activates the promoter of the VBF gene. Our results thus explain why the tumorigenicity of Agrobacterium is only weakly affected when either virF or virE3 is inactivated, but a much stronger deficiency is seen when both are inactivated at the same time.

# RESULTS

#### Construction of transgenic plants expressing virE3

In order to investigate whether and how the Agrobacterium virulence protein VirE3 influences the transcriptional machinery of the host cell, T-DNA constructs were made containing the coding sequence of virE3 under control of a tamoxifen-inducible promoter. These constructs were integrated into the A. thaliana genome by Agrobacterium-mediated floral dip transformation. In addition, control lines transformed by the empty vector, but lacking virE3 sequences, were generated. Three independent lines of the plants with virE3 and six of the control lines were selected for further analysis. To this end, seedlings from each line were grown on solid medium for 10 days, transferred to liquid medium, grown for an additional 4 days and subsequently tamoxifen was added to half of the seedlings. Induction of virE3 did not result in an obvious effect on the morphology in any of the plant lines (Figure S1). Four hours after the addition of tamoxifen, RNA was isolated from the plantlets and analysed for the expression of virE3 by semi-quantitative RT-PCR. As shown in Figure 1, in all three lines containing virE3 coding sequences virE3 RNA was only detected after the addition of tamoxifen. In the control lines, virE3 RNA could not be detected whereas the A. thaliana rotamase cyclophilin (ROC1; At4g38740) RNA could easily be found.

## Validation of RNA-seq data by quantitative RT-PCR analysis

To verify the differential gene expression detected by RNAseq, four genes found to be highly induced upon virE3 expression (At5G54720, encoding an ankyrin repeat family protein; At5G13320, encoding an enzyme capable of conjugating amino acids to 4-substituted benzoates; At4G19925, encoding a Toll-interleukin-resistance (TIR) domain family protein; and At1G19250, encoding flavin-dependent monooxygenase 1) and four control genes (encoding Roc1, actin-2, beta-6 tubulin and EF1), were selected for analysis by quantitative (q)RT-PCR. The effect of tamoxifen-induced virE3 expression on the expression of these genes was determined not only in the plant line (line 1) that was used for RNA-seq analysis, but also in two independent transgenic lines (lines 3 and 4) containing virE3 under control of a tamoxifen-inducible promoter. As shown in Table 2, qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that these four selected genes were strongly upregulated upon virE3 expression. This upregulation was found not only in line 1 but also in the other two independent plant lines, indicating that the effect of virE3 on the expression of these four genes is not an accidental effect restricted to one plant line.

#### Expression of virE3 results in increased VBF expression

Inspection of the list of genes upregulated upon virE3 expression revealed the presence of At1G56250 encoding VBF (also named ATPP2-B14, PHLOEM PROTEIN 2-B14 or PP2-B14), an F-box protein reported to be capable of replacing VirF during AMT (Zaltsman et al., 2010). This gene was strongly induced (492-fold, from 0.06 reads per million per kilobase in the non-treated virE3 line to 30.5 reads per million per kilobase in the tamoxifen-induced virE3 line) (Table S3). The tamoxifen-dependent induction was 17-fold in the control line. A related gene, At1G56240, encoding phloem protein 2-B13 (also named AtPP2-B13, PP2-B13) was also strongly upregulated (1678-fold) upon induction of virE3 expression. We verified whether or not the effect of VirE3 on the expression of VBF was specific for the selected plant line by performing RNA gel blot analysis of the effect of VirE3 on VBF expression in all the three independent lines with the construct giving tamoxifeninduced virE3 expression. As shown in Figure S3, the virE3 and VBF genes were strongly induced in plants of all three lines after incubation with tamoxifen for 4 h. In contrast, no virE3 gene expression and VBF induction were detected in the control plants after tamoxifen treatment. This corroborates that virE3 activates the expression of the VBF gene.

In order to further analyse the effect of VirE3 on the expression of VBF we fused the VBF promoter (857 or 1183 bp of sequences upstream from the At1G56250 start codon) to the GUS reporter and constructed plasmid pRT101 expressing virE3 under control of the 35S promoter. Co-transformation of Arabidopsis cell suspension protoplasts with a VBF promoter–GUS reporter construct and the effector plasmid pRT101-virE3 showed that VirE3 stimulates the VBF promoter approximately three-fold (Figure 3a). To study the effect of VirE3 on the expression of At1G56240, we similarly fused its promoter (1202 and 1607 bp of sequences upstream from the At1G56240 start codon) to the GUS reporter. Co-transformation with At1G56240 promoter–GUS and pRT101-virE3 of Arabidopsis protoplasts revealed an approximately 30% stimulation of the At1G56240 promoter (Figure 3a).

#### Effect of the general transcriptional activator pBrp on VirE3-dependent gene expression

Previously we found in a yeast two-hybrid screen that VirE3 interacts with the Arabidopsis general transcriptional activator pBrp (Garcıa-Rodrıguez et al., 2006). Therefore, we investigated the effect of pBrp on the VirE3-dependent activation of the At1G56250 (VBF) and At1G56240 promoters in a protoplast transactivation assay. As shown in Figure 3(b), expression of pBrp by itself hardly affected the activity of either promoter, whereas VirE3 activated these promoters approximately two-fold. However, co-expression of both VirE3 and pBrp together led to synergistic stimulation of both promoters, suggesting that VirE3 and pBrp act together in the activation of transcription.

In wild type protoplasts pBrp is naturally present, and whether the effect of VirE3 on the expression of the two genes fully depends on the presence of pBrp could therefore not be determined. In order to examine this, we made use of a pbrp homozygous mutant line (Figure S4). Plants

#### Effect of VirE3 on the subcellular localization of pBrp

Previously, it was found that pBrp localized to the outer surface of plastids, but upon certain forms of stress it is localized in the nucleus to stimulate transcription (Lagrange et al., 2003). We tested whether the expression of virE3 influenced the subcellular localization of pBrp. To this end a construct expressing a fusion of pBrp to YFP was transformed into Arabidopsis cell suspension protoplasts. As can be seen in Figure 4, in agreement with previous data the pBrp protein localized to the plastids. Transformation of a construct expressing a fusion of VirE3 to RFP revealed RFP fluorescence in the nucleus, as expected. To establish whether VirE3 might induce the translocation of pBrp from the plastid to the nucleus, cotransformations of the constructs encoding YFP-pBrp and RFP-VirE3 were performed. Interestingly, in the presence of VirE3 we found that pBrp now localized not only at the plastids but also in the nucleus. This suggests that VirE3 induces the translocation of a part of the pBrp molecules into the nucleus (Figure 4).

# DISCUSSION

During AMT the virulence proteins VirD2, VirD5, VirE2, VirE3 and VirF are translocated from the bacterium into the host cell (Vergunst et al., 2000). In the host cell the single-stranded DNA-binding VirE2 is thought to coat the T-strand, protecting it on its journey into the nucleus. The VirE2 protein can interact with VIP1 (Tzfira et al., 2001), a transcription factor that is phosphorylated during infection and then migrates into the nucleus to mediate the expression of defence genes (Djamei et al., 2007). Binding to the T-complex may facilitate the nuclear translocation of the complex assisted by the NLS signals present in VIP1 and thus promote transformation (Tzfira et al., 2001; Djamei et al., 2007). However, it was recently reported that VIP1 does not influence the localization of VirE2 in the host cell and no negative effects on transformation were seen after inactivation of the vip1 gene in the host (Shi et al., 2014). Within the nucleus the proteins need to be removed from the T-strand to allow integration into the host chromosome. The translocated F-box protein VirF may play a role here as it can mediate the targeted proteolysis of both VIP1 and VirE2 bound to VIP1 (Tzfira et al., 2004). Destruction of VIP1 would at the same time limit the defence response. It has been observed that when VirF is absent (as in nopaline strains) a host F-box protein called VBF may take over its function (Zaltsman et al., 2010). The role of VirE3 is less clear. It has nuclear localization sequences and is located in the nucleus of host cells. Also it has

Our earlier findings (Garcıa-Rodrıguez et al., 2006) that VirE3 has transcriptional activation activity and interacts with the general transcription factor pBrp prompted us to investigate whether expression of virE3 in plant cells would change the transcriptome. To this end, we stably expressed virE3 in A. thaliana and determined the effect on the genome-wide transcription profile using RNA-seq. We integrated an expression cassette with a tamoxifeninducible promoter into the A. thaliana genome and the transcriptional profile was determined before and after tamoxifen induction. These studies showed that expression of virE3 had a considerable effect on the genomewide transcription profile. Using the chosen criteria, 607 genes were found to be specifically upregulated more than three-fold upon virE3 expression, and 132 genes were found to be specifically downregulated more than threefold upon virE3 expression (Table S3). The upregulation of several of these genes was corroborated by RT-PCR.

We discovered that the VBF gene was amongst the genes most highly induced by VirE3. Induction of expression of VBF during transformation suggests that in the absence of VirF the VirE3 protein ensures that sufficient VBF is produced for targeted proteolysis of VIP1 and VirE2. This is in line with the much stronger attenuation of virulence of the virF virE3 double mutant compared with the single mutants. Another gene that we found to be highly induced by VirE3 that attenuates the defence response was NIMIN1, which encodes a protein that binds to NPR1 and thus reduces PR gene expression (Weigel et al., 2005). In a protoplast activation assay we showed that the NIMIN1 promoter is activated upon expression of virE3 (Figure S5).

Previously, we showed that VirE3 interacts with pBrp, a plant-specific general transcription factor of the TFIIB family (Garcıa-Rodrıguez et al., 2006). Therefore, we studied the effect not only of VirE3 but also of pBrp on the activation of the A1G56250 (VBF) and At1G56240 promoters in protoplasts. As shown in Figure 3, expression of pBrp by itself hardly affected the activity of either promoter, while VirE3 activated these promoters approximately two-fold. However, co-expression of both VirE3 and pBrp together further stimulated the promoters, suggesting that VirE3 and pBrp act synergistically in the activation of transcription. In contrast to its interaction with pBrp, we were unable to show an interaction of VirE3 with the classical TFIIB protein (Figure S6). The pBrp protein localizes to the outside of protoplasts, but may transfer to the nucleus when the cells are stressed. Our results show that pBrp is present in the nucleus when VirE3 is present in the cells, which then allows their collaboration in enhancing specific host genes during AMT.

The effect of Agrobacterium infection on the A. thaliana transcriptome has been investigated (Ditt et al., 2006). At 48 h after inoculation with A. tumefaciens, upregulation was found for 115 genes, of which 13 were more than three-fold upregulated. Downregulation was found for 188 genes, of which only one was downregulated more than three-fold. The genes upregulated after Agrobacterium infection include genes that we found upregulated after induction of virE3 expression and included the VBF gene. The upregulation seen after infection by Agrobacterium was much weaker than after induction of virE3 expression. This may be because Ditt et al. isolated RNA 48 h after inoculation and we isolated RNA 4 h after virE3 induction. Furthermore, overexpression of virE3 may lead to much higher levels of VirE3 that are present in all of the plant's cells in contrast to natural infection where limited amounts of VirE3 are directly translocated only in some of the plant's cells. Other changes in gene expression seen after Agrobacterium infection that are not visible after the induction of virE3 expression may be due to other translocated effector proteins such as VirE2, VirF or VirD5, due to a response to the presence of T-strands or be a host response to pathogen-associated molecular patterns of Agrobacterium.

#### EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

#### Binary constructs and plant transformation

The Agrobacterium virE3 coding sequence was amplified by PCR on plasmid pSDM3659 (A. den Dulk, Institute of Biology, Leiden University, unpublished data) using the oligonucleotides SpeI-VirE3-Fw (GGACTAGTATGGTGAGCACTAC) and EcoRI-VirE3-Rev (CGGAATTCTTAGAAACCTCTGGA). The resulting PCR fragment was inserted into the pJET1.2 PCR Cloning vector (Thermo Scientific,<http://www.thermoscientific.com/>) yielding pJET1.2[VirE3 (SpeI, EcoRI)] (pRUL1238). Next, a SpeI–EcoRI fragment with virE3 was cloned into pUG34VN (Sakalis et al., 2014) to construct pUG34VN[VirE3](pRUL1262). To introduce NotI sites at the ends, another PCR was carried out using the oligonucleotides 5<sup>0</sup> - AAAGCGGCCGCAAATGGTGAGCACTACGAAGAAA-3<sup>0</sup> and 5<sup>0</sup> - AAAGCGGCCGCTTAGAAACCTCTGGAGGTGGA-3<sup>0</sup> on pUG34VN [VirE3]. The DNA fragment was inserted into pJET1.2, yielding pJET1.2[virE3(NotI)]. A DNA fragment containing virE3 was obtained by digestion of pJET1.2[virE3(NotI)] with NotI and was subsequently cloned into the binary vector pGPINTAM-Not (Friml et al., 2004), digested with NotI, producing pGPINTAM-Not-VirE3 (pSDM3480). All PCR fragments were verified by sequencing. Correct ligation was checked by restriction analysis and by sequencing the junctions between the plasmids and DNA fragments.

The binary vectors pGPINTAM-Not-VirE3 and pGPINTAM-Not (as a negative control) were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain AGL1 by triparental mating (Ditta et al., 1980). Arabidopsis plants (Columbia, Col-0) were transformed using the floral dip method (Clough and Bent, 1998). Dipped plants were grown at 21°C in a growth chamber (16-h light/8-h dark, 2500 lux) for approximately 2 months until seeds could be harvested. Seeds were surface-sterilized by incubation for 1 min in 70% ethanol and for 20 min in 1% hypochlorite, followed by six rinses with sterile water. Then, the seeds were resuspended in 0.1% agarose and stored at 4°C for 4 days. To select for transgenic plants, seeds (1 g) were plated on 10 plates of ½ MS medium (Murashige and Skoog, 1962) containing 100 mg L-<sup>1</sup> timentin, 50 mg L-<sup>1</sup> kanamycine and 100 mg L-1 nystatin and incubated at 21°C in a growth chamber for 14 days. The seedlings were transferred to soil and grown until new seeds could be harvested. Then, seeds were taken from three virE3 plant lines and six control plant lines and stored to be used for further experiments. An aliquot of the seeds was germinated as described above, and after 10 days DNA was extracted from leaf samples as described (de Pater et al., 2009). T-DNA integration was checked by PCR using PhusionTM High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase. For transgenic virE3 plants, the primers 5<sup>0</sup> -GTACCCGGGGATCTGTC-GACCTCGATCGAGAT-3<sup>0</sup> and 5<sup>0</sup> -AAAGCGGCCGCTTAGAAACCTC TGGAGGTGGA-3<sup>0</sup> were used. For transgenic control plants, the primers 5<sup>0</sup> -GTACCCGGGGATCTGTCGACCTCGATCGAGAT-3<sup>0</sup> and 50 -AGTCGACTCATAGACCGCGCGTTGATCGAGGT-3<sup>0</sup> were used. To identify the insertion position of T-DNA in the genome of transgenic virE3 plant line 1, the primers 5<sup>0</sup> -CAGTATAGGCTTTTT GTGTCG-3<sup>0</sup> (P1) and 5<sup>0</sup> -NTCGASTWTSGWGTT-3<sup>0</sup> (P2) were used for thermal asymmetric interlaced (TAIL)-PCR as previously described (Liu et al., 1995).

#### Extraction of RNA and RT-PCR analysis

Transgenic seeds were germinated as described above, and after 10 days 15–20 seedlings were transferred to a 50-ml polypropylene tube containing 10 ml of liquid ½ MS medium (Murashige and Skoog, 1962) without antibiotics. Tubes were incubated at 21°C with shaking at 120 r.p.m. for an additional 4 days. Treatments with tamoxifen were performed by adding 10 ll of 10 l<sup>M</sup> tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich, T5648-1G, [http://www.sigmaaldrich.](http://www.sigmaaldrich.com/) [com/](http://www.sigmaaldrich.com/)) dissolved in DMSO. In control incubations 10 ll of DMSO was added. Four hours after the addition of tamoxifen or of DMSO the plantlets were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen.

For RNA isolation, the frozen plantlets were ground under liquid nitrogen in a TissueLyser II (Qiagen, [http://www.qiagen.com/\)](http://www.qiagen.com/). RNA was isolated with the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), using the optional on-column DNase treatment. The RNA samples were stored at -80°C. For RT-PCR analysis, 1 lg of RNA for each sample was first treated again with DNase I (Ambion, [http://](http://www.thermofisher.com/uk/en/home/brands/invitrogen/ambion.html) [www.thermofisher.com/uk/en/home/brands/invitrogen/am](http://www.thermofisher.com/uk/en/home/brands/invitrogen/ambion.html)[bion.html](http://www.thermofisher.com/uk/en/home/brands/invitrogen/ambion.html)), in the presence of 0.5 ll RNasin (Promega, [http://](http://www.promega.com/) [www.promega.com/\)](http://www.promega.com/) in a total volume of 10 ll. Subsequently, 0.5 lg of treated RNA from each sample was used for Oligo (dT) primed cDNA synthesis, using M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (Promega) according to the protocol recommended by the supplier. In a control reaction the reverse transcriptase was omitted. The reaction mix was diluted 10-fold in water and 2.5 ll was used for PCR analysis in a total volume of 25 ll using PhusionTM High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase. For analysis of virE3 expression, the primers 5<sup>0</sup> - AAAACTAGTATGGTGAGCACTACGAAGAA-3<sup>0</sup> and 5<sup>0</sup> -AAA-GAATTCGAAACCTCTGGAGGTGGAACG-3<sup>0</sup> were used; for analysis of ROC1 expression the primers 5<sup>0</sup> -GAACGGAACAGGCGGT-GAGTC-3<sup>0</sup> and 5<sup>0</sup> -CCACAGGCTTCGTCGGCTTTC-3<sup>0</sup> were used.

#### Illumina mRNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

Illumina mRNA-seq libraries were generated and sequenced by BaseClear BV (<http://www.baseclear.com/>). Messenger RNA was used as input for the Illumina TruSeq RNA sample preparation kit v.2 with adaptations. The mRNA fraction was purified from total RNA by polyA capture, fragmented and converted to doublestranded cDNA using random hexamers. DNA adapters including sample-specific barcodes were ligated to both ends of the DNA fragments and subjected to PCR amplification. The resulting libraries were checked on a Bioanalyzer (Agilent, [http://www.agi](http://www.agilent.com/)[lent.com/\)](http://www.agilent.com/) and quantified. The libraries were multiplexed, clustered and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 (TruSeq v.3 chemistry) with a single-read 50-cycle sequencing protocol and indexing. The sequencing run was analysed with the Illumina CASAVA pipeline (v.1.8.2), with demultiplexing based on samplespecific barcodes. Sequence reads of low quality (only 'passing filter' reads were selected) and reads containing adaptor sequences or PhiX control sequences (with an in-house filtering protocol) were removed.

#### Data processing

Raw sequencing data were processed using CLC Genomic Workbench v.4.6. Processed reads were aligned to the A. thaliana annotated coding sequences (TAIR v.10; [http://www.arabidopsis.org\)](http://www.arabidopsis.org). The read counts were subsequently summarized per annotated gene. For all genes annotated as protein coding, absolute counts were normalized to RPKM. This entails dividing read counts per gene by a sample-specific scaling factor based on sequencing depth (in millions of aligned reads) and sample composition, and by the mean length of annotated transcripts (in kilobases). Several scaling factors were determined and evaluated using the R package edgeR (v.3.2.4) (Robinson et al., 2010). Simple division by read counts gave the best normalization results. Expression data were further processed using Microsoft Excel.

To analyse the effect of tamoxifen on gene expression in the control lines we omitted genes with a very low expression, i.e. genes for which the sum of the number of matching reads in the tamoxifen-treated control line (TC) and the number of matching reads in the non-treated control line (NC) was less than 10. To find genes that are upregulated by virE3 expression we used the following criteria:

- the sum of the number of matching reads in the tamoxifen-treated control line (TC) and the number of matching reads in the non-treated control line (NC) is at least 10;
- the sum of the number of matching reads in the tamoxifen-treated virE3 line (TE) and the number of matching reads in the non-treated virE3 line (NE) is at least 10;
- the number of reads per million per kilobase in the tamoxifen induced virE3 line is at least three-fold higher than in the tamoxifen-treated control line.

#### Quantitative RT-PCR analysis and Northern blot

Quantitative RT-PCR was performed using a DNA Engine Thermal Cycler (MJ Research) equipped with a Chromo4 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad,<http://www.bio-rad.com/>) with SYBR Green I (SYBR Green I Nucleic Acid Gel Stain, 10 0009 concentrate in DMSO, S-7563, Life Technologies, [http://www.thermo](http://www.thermofisher.com) [fisher.com.](http://www.thermofisher.com)) as the fluorescent detection dye. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 0.5 lg of DNase I-treated RNA, as described above. A dilution series (1:6 to 1:1296) of the cDNA was made and 5 ll of the diluted cDNA was used for qPCR analysis in a total volume of 25 ll using 0.1 ll PhusionTM High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase plus 1.25 ll 209 SYBR Green I stock solution. Specific primers were designed for reference and target genes using the PRIMER 3 online software ([http://biotools.umassmed.edu/bioapps/pri](http://biotools.umassmed.edu/bioapps/primer3_www.cgi)[mer3\\_www.cgi](http://biotools.umassmed.edu/bioapps/primer3_www.cgi)) to generate specific fragments of around 150 bp. The primers used are listed in Table 3. For all qPCR reactions the conditions were: 98°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 98°C for 10 sec, 60°C for 20 sec and 72°C for 20 sec. The fold-change in transcript levels following induction by tamoxifen was calculated using the following equation: (primer efficiency)DCt, where DCt is the number of PCR amplification cycles needed to reach the threshold in the non-induced sample minus the number of PCR amplification cycles to reach the threshold in induced samples. The efficiency was determined using a serial dilution of the cDNA from one of the induced samples.

For Northern blot analysis, 6-lg RNA samples were loaded for electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose/1% formaldehyde gels, and blotted to nylon membranes (Roche, [http://www.roche.com/\)](http://www.roche.com/). The procedures of (pre-)hybridization, washing and subsequent exposure were performed as previously described (Memelink et al., 1994). DNA fragments used as probes were obtained by PCR, and 32Plabelled by random priming (DecaLabel DNA Labeling Kit, Thermo Scientific). The following primer sets were used: 5<sup>0</sup> -CGGAATTCAT GGTGAGCACTACGAAGAAAAG-3<sup>0</sup> and 5<sup>0</sup> -GCTCTAGATTAGAAACC TCTGGAGGTGGAAC-3<sup>0</sup> for virE3, 5<sup>0</sup> -ATTCTCGGAGTCAGCTGAAC-3<sup>0</sup> and 5<sup>0</sup> -TTATGTTTTAGGCCTCACTTC-3<sup>0</sup> for VBF (At1g56250), 50 -ATGGAACCCAAACCTTTCTTTC-3<sup>0</sup> and 5<sup>0</sup> -GTTAGTCTTCAAGGT TACAGAG-3<sup>0</sup> for At1g04110, 5<sup>0</sup> -TGCCATGGAGATGAAGTGTCCGT ACTGTTCATC-3<sup>0</sup> and 5<sup>0</sup> -TTCCCGGGTCATGACACTGGCAGTTCCA AAC-3<sup>0</sup> for pBrp (At4g36650), 5<sup>0</sup> -CGGGAAGGATCGTGATGGA-3<sup>0</sup> and 5<sup>0</sup> -CCAACCTTCTCGATGGCCT-3<sup>0</sup> for ROC1 (At4g38740).

## Plasmid constructs and Arabidopsis protoplast transient expression assays

Plasmids pSDM4501, pSDM4502, pSDM4503, pSDM4504 and pSDM4511 containing 857 bp of promoter sequences of At1G56250 (VBF), containing 1183 bp of promoter sequences of At1G56250, containing the 1202 bp of promoter sequences of At1G56240 or containing the 1607 bp of promoter sequences of At1G56240, and containing the 1506 bp of promoter sequences of At1G02450 (NIMIN1), respectively, were obtained by inserting the promoter sequences into plasmid GUSXX (Pasquali et al., 1994). The seedlings were grown without selection and genotyped with LBb1 (5<sup>0</sup> -GCGTGGACCGCTTGCTGCAACT-3<sup>0</sup> ; P4) and TDNApBRpF1 (5<sup>0</sup> -TCCTAAAATCGATGCCACATC-3<sup>0</sup> ; P3) or TDNApBRpR1 (5<sup>0</sup> -T GCTGAAGCCAAAAGATGAAC-3<sup>0</sup> ; P5) to identify homozygous individuals. Arabidopsis mesophyll protoplasts were isolated from 4 week-old pbrp homozygous seedlings grown on sterile MA medium (He et al., 2007). As controls, co-transformations with the empty pRT101 GUS expression vector were done. Protoplasts were incubated at 25°C for at least 16 h prior to harvesting by centrifugation and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The GUS activity assays were performed as described (van der Fits and Memelink, 1997). We normalized GUS activities from triplicate transformations against total protein content to correct for differences in protein extraction efficiencies.

#### Plasmid constructs and confocal microscopy

Galvan Ampudia) to generate pART7YFP-virE3 and pART7YFP-pBrp, respectively. #### Yeast two-hybrid assays

Full-length VirE3 (pSDM4514) was cloned into pACT2 (pSDM4512), while full length pBrp (pSDM4515) and TFIIB (pSDM4516) were cloned into pAS2.1 (pSDM4513) (James et al., 1996). Primer sequences are presented in Table 3. All PCR fragments were verified by sequencing. Interaction assays were performed by cotransformation of bait and prey plasmids into yeast strain PJ69-4A as previously described (Gietz et al., 1992), and plated on minimal synthetic defined (SD)-glucose medium, supplemented with methionine, uracil and histidine and lacking leucine and tryptophan (SD/–LW). Subsequently, cells were incubated for 16 h in liquid SD/–LW and spotted onto selective solid SD medium lacking leucine, tryptophan and histidine (SD/–LWH) supplemented with increasing concentrations of 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT, Sigma) ranging from 0 to 30 mM. Yeast cells were allowed to grow for 5 days at 30°C.
