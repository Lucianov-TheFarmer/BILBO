#### **Abstract**

The ability of climbing plants to grow upward along others to reach canopy for photosynthesis is hypothesized as a key innovation in flowering plants. Cucurbitaceae, a family containing ~1000 species and many important crops, are mostly climbers and have characteristic tendrils and pepo fruits. Here, we present 127 newly sequenced transcriptomes and genomes along with other datasets for a total of 136 cucurbits representing all tribes to establish a robust Cucurbitaceae phylogeny containing eight highly resolved major clades. We analyzed whole genome duplication (WGD), diversification dynamics and ancestral morphologies, and found that after early genome duplication event(s), a burst of diversification and morphological innovations in flower, fruit and root characters occurred under the climate optimum in the Early Eocene. Species radiation under Mid-Eocene Climatic Optimum also coincided with several morphological changes shared by 80% of cucurbits. We found that the cucurbit-specific tendril identity gene *TEN* originated from a paleo-polyploidization at the origin of the family. Our results support the hypothesis that cucurbit diversifications were probably driven by increased genetic diversity following polyploidizations and novel state of traits under paleo-climate upheavals. This study provides a phylogenetic framework and new insights into morphological and genomic changes facilitating the adaptive evolution of Cucurbitaceae.

*Key words:* Cucurbitaceae, transcriptome, phylogeny, divergence time, genome duplication, diversification, morphology, adaptive evolution.

## **Introduction**

Morphological innovations have great impacts during evolution and provide a force of stability in the face of changing environments; for example, the novel structures that allow plants to climb upright along trees have originated independently and contributed to the success of many plant groups (Isnard and Feild, 2015; Wang et al., 2012). During the angiosperm history, the climbing habit has evolved many times in diverse families; furthermore, the innovation of climbing has been associated with plant groups that are more diverse than their closely related lineages (Gentry, 1991; Gianoli, 2004; Isnard and Feild, 2015). Confronted with the severe spatial competition and disadvantages of shadow from neighboring plants, climbers have evolved multiple strategies to support themselves upright toward the forest canopy to increase their access to the sunlight and photosynthetic biomass (Schnitzer and Bongers, 2002). One dominant strategy is the development of clasping tendrils, with ~17 morphological variants that are classified into two major types as either modified leaves or shoots (Sousa-Baena et al., 2018b). Climbers with leaflet-derived tendrils are found in the families Asteraceae, Fabaceae, Bignoniaceae and Papaveraceae, whereas those having shoot-derived tendrils include members of Cucurbitaceae, Sapindaceae and Apocynaceae (Sousa-Baena et al., 2018a; Sousa-Baena et al., 2014; Sousa-Baena et al., 2018b).

Among angiosperm families with members producing tendrils, Cucurbitaceae are the second largest family of the order Cucurbitales in rosids, which are one of the two largest subdivisions of eudicots. Cucurbitaceae were established by Linnaeus (1792) on the basis of floral characters, while the tendril is a synapomorphic character and is considered homologous to shoot (Schaefer and Renner, 2011b; Wang et al., 2015). Cucurbitaceae members (cucurbits, also called gourds) are mostly herbaceous annual vines (some with perennial tubers or roots) or perennial lianas, and include many important vegetables and fruits, such as cucumber (*Cucumis sativus*), melon (*Cucumis melo*), wax gourd (*Benincasa hispida*), watermelon (*Citrullus lanatus*), pumpkin or squash (*Cucurbita pepo, C. moschata*, and *C. agyrosperma*), and bitter gourd (*Momordica charantia*). Additional cucurbits contribute to human livelihood in other manners; for instance, the fruit of monk fruit (*Siraitia grosvenorii*) and the whole plants of *Gynostemma pentaphyllum* and *Actinostemma tenerum* are used as medicines, while the attractive fruits of *Ecballium elaterium* and *Lagenaria siceraria* are popular ornamentals. Members of cucurbits have also served as model systems for understanding molecular regulation of tendril development and bitter compound

biosynthesis (Chen et al., 2005; Shang et al., 2014; Shibuya et al., 2004; Wang et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2016), as well as paleo-polyploidizations (Sun et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018).

The cucurbit tendrils can have simple (branchless) or branched forms, which either coil below the branch point or not, as a trait for cucurbit taxonomy (Schaefer and Renner, 2011b). Another distinctive character of this family is the pepo type of fruit, described as a fleshy fruit with a relatively hard shell, typified by several commercial species like cucumber, watermelon and pumpkin. At the same time, other fruit types are also found among cucurbits, including berry, capsule and samara. Cucurbitaceae includes nearly 1000 species that are primarily native to tropical and subtropical regions, but a small number of species are also found in temperate regions (Lu et al., 1986; Schaefer and Renner, 2011b). Traditional systematics have used tendril features and fruit types, as well as seed coat and pollen structure, to classify Cucurbitaceae into two subfamilies, 11 tribes and 130 genera, with 50 of the genera having a single species each (Jeffrey, 1980; Jeffrey, 2005). Early molecular phylogeny of Cucurbitaceae was reconstructed using five chloroplast markers, weakly supporting the two subfamilies Cucurbitoideae and Nhandiroboideae, and recovering eight of the 11 tribes as defined by Jeffery (Kocyan et al., 2007). Recent Cucurbitaceae phylogenetic analyses using 14 molecular markers from nuclear, plastid and mitochondrial genomes (Renner and Schaefer, 2016; Schaefer and Renner, 2011a; Schaefer and Renner, 2011b) resulted in a new classification with 15 tribes and 95~97 genera. However, the backbone relationships of tribes are still unresolved possibly due to limited phylogenetic signals of the molecular markers with a large proportion (over 70%) of missing data (Schaefer and Renner, 2011a). A comprehensive and well-resolved Cucurbitaceae phylogeny is needed as a strong foundation in understanding the evolutionary history and comparative studies of the family.

Compared with plastid markers, nuclear genes provide genetic information from both parents; furthermore, with the advent of new sequencing technologies, phylogenies using large numbers of nuclear markers from transcriptomes and genomes have resolved some long-standing ambiguous relationships (Chen et al., 2019; Fernandez et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2016a; Li et al., 2018; Qi et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2017a; Xiang et al., 2017; Zeng et al., 2017; Zeng et al., 2014). Moreover, 12 cucurbit genomes have been published belonging to eight genera and five tribes, providing important resources for comparative analyses (Barrera-Redondo et al., 2019; Garcia-Mas and Puigdomènech, 2012; Guo et al., 2013; Huang

et al., 2009; Itkin et al., 2016; Li et al., 2019; Montero-Pau et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2017;

Urasaki et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2017b; Xie et al., 2019; Zhang et al. 2020).

Here, we used multiple sets of nuclear genes from 127 newly sequenced transcriptomes and genomes, in combination with previous datasets, to reconstruct the Cucurbitaceae phylogeny with highly supported and well-resolved relationships among all 15 tribes and numerous genera. The phylogenetic model here contains eight well-defined major clades, each with one or more tribes. Furthermore, our phylotranscriptomic analysis identified both old and recent WGDs, with support from syntenic regions in several sequenced genomes. Additional statistical analyses support four increases in species diversification rates. Reconstructions of ancestral states of 15 morphological characters reveal the trends of character transitions related to tendril, flower and fruit, as well as some cases of parallel evolution. Moreover, morphological changes and shifts in diversification rates occurred coincidentally during the geological times with challenging condition, such as the Cretaceous-Paleogene (K-Pg) boundary and the hot climate optimum in Eocene. Our findings suggest a positive role of WGDs as a driving force for increased biodiversity, and further suggest that the synergistic effect of WGD, morphological changes and extreme environments probably played an important role in the evolutionary success of Cucurbitaceae. We propose a set of crucial morphological evolutionary changes of reproductive organs in Cucurbitaceae, including floral simplification and transitions in seed dispersal mechanisms.

# **RESULTS**

### **Generation of 127 New Cucurbit Transcriptomic and Genomic Datasets**

To obtain nuclear gene sequences for phylogenetic reconstruction and WGD detection, we sequenced 122 new transcriptomes and five genomes, and retrieved nine transcriptomes from public resources for a total of 136 cucurbit datasets (Table S1). The sampling represents all 15 Cucurbitaceae tribes, 11 of which have over 50% of the genera included, with a total of 52 genera that cover nearly 90% of the species richness of this family. We also included datasets from 45 species in five orders (Cucurbitales, Rosales, Fabales, Fagales and Malpighiales) as outgroups.

## **A Well-Resolved Cucurbitaceae Phylogeny**

To reconstruct the Cucurbitaceae phylogeny, we use six sets of nuclear genes (referred here as orthogroups, or OGs) with 1503, 1181, 942, 737, 460 and 258 OGs, respectively, selected by using a multi-step procedure with consideration for species coverage, length of aligned matrices and other factors (see Methods and Figure S1 for details). Phylogenetic analysis of Cucurbitaceae was conducted by using a coalescent method with single nuclear gene trees from each of the aforementioned six sets of OGs (Figures S2-S7). The resulting phylogenies are overwhelmingly congruent with strongly supported monophyly and relationships as summarized in Figure 1. Specifically, the Cucurbitaceae family is monophyletic with maximal support (BS = 100%), with high supports (BS ≥ 99%) for monophyly of each of 13 tribes represented by two or more species here (with Indofevilleeae being monotypic and Siraitieae sampled for one species), consistent with previous phylogenetic studies (Figure S8) (Kocyan et al., 2007; Renner and Schaefer, 2016; Schaefer et al., 2009; Schaefer and Renner, 2011a; Zhang et al., 2006).

In addition, the highly supported phylogeny provides resolution for relationships at the tribe level for the first time with eight successive major clades (Clade I to Clade VIII). The earliest diverging lineage (Clade I) consists of tribes Actinostemmateae and Gomphogyneae as sisters with BS ≥ 90% in three trees and BS between 75% and 90% in three other trees (Figures 1 and S2-S7). The next to diverge is Clade II containing tribes Zanonieae and Triceratieae with maximum supports in all trees. The remaining 11 tribes group together with maximal supports in all trees, but the phylogenetic position of the tribe Indofevilleeae is uncertain with two alternative topologies (Figure S9). In one of these, Indofevilleeae and Thladiantheae are successive sisters to a large clade of the other nine tribes, in four trees (from analyses using 1503, 1181, 942 and 737 OGs, respectively) with weak supports (BS < 70%) (Figures S2-S5, S9). Alternatively, Indofevilleeae and Thladiantheae cluster as sisters in two trees (460 and 258 OGs, respectively) with strong supports (BS ≥ 95%; Figures S6, S7 and S9), forming Clade III in our phylogenetic model (Figure 1). In previous studies, the monotypic Indofevilleeae was either reported as a branch next to the clade of the other ten tribes (Kocyan et al., 2007) or sister to Actinostemmateae (Schaefer and Renner, 2011a), although both topologies were reconstructed by using a few markers and received low supports (Figure S8). The next three successive clades (Clades IV to VI) in our model are tribes Siraitieae, Momordiceae and Joliffieae, respectively (all BS = 100%). The other six tribes constitute Clade VII and Clade VIII. In Clade VII, Shizopeponeae and Bryonieae are

sister tribes with maximum supports, and then Sicyoeae is next to the two tribes with strong supports (BS = 94% to 100%) in all the six trees. In Clade VIII, Benincaseae and Coniandreae group together (BS = 100%, 100%, 100%, 100%, 79%, 69%), and Cucurbiteae is sister to them with maximum supports in all trees. However, in previous results using plastid genes (Kocyan et al., 2007; Schaefer and Renner, 2011a), Cucurbiteae was found to group with Benincaseae, different from our model. Nevertheless, a tree using three nuclear genes (Schaefer and Renner, 2011a) revealed the same relationship as ours that Benincaseae grouped with Coniandreae, albeit with low support (BS < 60%) (Figure S8). The two topologies are possibly a result of the varied inheritance of the plastid and nucleus.

The relationships in our phylogenetic model are further supported by similarities of some morphological characters. For example, the sisterhood of the tribes Indofevilleeae and Thladiantheae is supported by similar characters, including broadly ovate leaf blade with short and acuminate apex, dioecious plants, four of five free stamens forming two pairs, indehiscent fruits, and ovate and compressed seeds with shallow margin (Chatterjee, 1947). The sister tribes Benincaseae and Coniandreae also share features of reticulate 3-colporae pollens, and similar chromosome base number of n = 12~14, whereas Cucurbiteae is characterized by echinate 3-porate to periporate pollens and n = 20.

The relationships among genera are also largely resolved with high supports regarding the 52 genera included here, with monophyly for 28 of the 29 genera with two or more taxa sampled (Figure 1). Among the 15 Cucurbitaceae tribes, six have three or more genera sampled here, providing an opportunity to examine their relationships. In the tribe Gomphogyneae, we sampled representatives for four out of the six genera and found that *Neoalsomitra* is first to diverge from the other three and *Gynostemma* is sister to the pair of *Hemsleya* and *Gomphogyne*. In Zanonieae, *Gerrardanthus* and *Siolmatra* are successive sisters to a clade with *Xerosicyos* and *Zygosicyos*. Next in Thladiantheae, *Thaldiantha* is represented by five species here and occupies the position of sister to *Baijiania*. The species-richest tribe Sicyoeae is represented by ten genera here, with *Luffa* occupying the earliest divergent branch and the other nine genera forming two clades: in one clade, *Gymnopetalum* is nested within the large genus *Trichosanthes,* similar to that reported by Boer et al. (2012), whereas in the other, the Asian *Hodgsonia* is the first to separate from other genera from the New World. Furthermore, these New World genera form two small clades: one with the pair of *Marah* and *Echinocystis*, and the other containing three genera [(*Sicyos*, *Echinopepon*), *Cycylanthera*)]. In the tribe Cucurbiteae, *Cayaponia* and *Sicana* are

successive sisters to *Cucurbita,* which is monophyletic for the eight taxa here with strong supports (Figure 1). In the tribe Coniandreae, among the nine genera sampled here, *Trochomeriopsis* and *Seyrigia* form a branch separated from the remaining seven genera that each diverge from the others successively. In the tribe Benincaseae, *Melothria* diverges firstly and is followed by the pair of *Coccinia* and *Diplocyclos*. Next to separate is the clade including bottle gourds (*Lagenaria*), *Peponium* and watermelons (*Citrullus*). *Solena* and *Cephalopentandra* together form the sister lineage to a clade with five genera, including the pair of *Benincasa* and *Trochomeria*, and a clade with *Zehneria* and *Cucumis*, with *Mukia* nested in *Cucumis*, consistent with the proposal that *Mukia* should be merged into *Cucumis* (Schaefer, 2007).

#### **Molecular Clock Estimate of the Cucurbitaceae Origin in the Late Cretaceous**

To obtain the evolutionary timescale of cucurbit lineages, we used the tree from the 258-gene concatenations with 160 species (136 of cucurbit taxa and 24 outgroups) as the input to estimate divergence times using a Bayesian method. We chose eight fossils (Table S2) as calibrations in our analysis. As illustrated in our chronogram (Figures 2 and S10), the crown group of Cucurbitaceae is dated to ~80 Ma (million years ago) with 95% credibility interval (CI) from 69.9 to 90. Shortly after that were the emergence of the crown group of Clade I (~76.7 Ma, 95% CI: 66-87) and the divergence of Clade II from the remainder of cucurbits (~77.7 Ma, 95% CI: 67.8-87.7). It took over ten million years for the descendants to diverge into more lineages by the almost simultaneous appearances of the most recent common ancestors (MRCA) of Clade II (~65.7 Ma, 95% CI: 51.8-78.4) and that of Clade III and Clade IV-Clade VIII (~64.4 Ma, 95% CI: 53.4-75.1). Separation of the two species-rich clades (VII and VIII) is dated to the Middle Eocene (~42 Ma, 95% CI: 34.9-51.1), with nearly simultaneous origins of the crown groups of Clade VII (~39.8 Ma, 95% CI: 32.8-48.6) and Clade VIII (~39 Ma, 95% CI: 32.3-48.2).

#### **Genome Duplications for Major Clades and the Cucurbitaceae Tribes**

To improve our understanding of the evolutionary history of cucurbit genomes, we searched for gene duplications as evidence for potential whole genome duplication (WGD) events using a phylogenomic approach to identify homologs and compare gene trees of the identified gene families with our phylogenetic model (Figure 1) as performed previously for other groups of angiosperms (Huang et al., 2016b; Jiao et al., 2012; Jiao et al., 2014; Ren et al., 2018a; Yang et al., 2018b; Yang et al., 2015). After examining 20023 gene trees (see Methods), we found strong evidence for four WGD events (referred as to CucWGD1 to CucWGD4) with relatively large numbers and proportions of gene trees exhibiting the duplication (with > 500 and > 5% percent of gene duplications as a relatively cautious cutoff in comparison with recent literature) (Figures 3 and S11). We identified an early event of large-scale duplications at the origin of the family (CucWGD1: 1053 GDs, 19.59%). This is consistent with previous results (Wang et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2019), which revealed a peak of Ks values for paralogs that are shared by the tribes Benincaseae (*Cucumis*, *Citrullus*, *Lagenaria*, *Benincasa*) and Cucurbiteae (*Cucurbita*), yet are younger than the gamma event shared by core eudicots, as supported by collinear gene pairs with an estimated age at ~90 Mya. We also found three relatively recent WGDs within three tribes, respectively. The tribe Cucurbiteae in Clade VIII probably had one at its origin (CucWGD2: 590 GDs, 5.84%), consistent with the previous findings of a Ks peak (~0.2-0.5) in the *Cucurbita* genomes (Sun et al., 2017; Xie et al., 2019). In addition, at least five genera of the tribe Sicyoeae in Clade VII also exhibit evidence for one event (CucWGD3: 896 GDs, 9.03%), whereas members of *Hemsleya* of tribe Gomphogyneae (Clade I) likely experienced another large-scale duplication (CucWGD4: 748 GDs, 7.32%).

To obtain additional support for some of the WGD events, we investigated the genomic location of gene duplicates identified in our CucWGDs in eight publicly available genomes (see Methods) that are proposed to have experienced CucWGD1 and/or CucWGD2, but there is no genome available to conduct this investigation for the CucWGD3 in Sicyoeae and CucWGD4 in Gomphogyneae. Our analyses suggest that genomic evidence in the form of collinear gene order (synteny) between genomic regions was detectable for CucWGD1 in the genomes of tribes Benincaseae (Figure 4A) and Momordiceae (Figure S12A). In addition, gene duplicates supporting CucWGD2 shared by members of the tribe Cucurbiteae were also found in syntenic regions in the *Cucurbita* genomes (Figure 4B). The occurrence of CucWGD2 in Cucurbiteae might have concealed the collinearity of gene duplicates from CucWGD1; indeed, further examination of the genomes of the Cucurbiteae members uncovered syntenic regions that are distinct from those corresponding to CucWGD2 and contain gene duplicates for CucWGD1 (Figure S12B). Additional support for CucWGD2 is

- also revealed by the analysis of genomic location of *TEN* (see below). Overall, the ancestor of Cucurbitaceae originated probably as a polyploid, and Cucurbiteae, some Sicyoeae and *Hemsleya* of Gomphogyneae had each separately experienced a second round of large-scale duplications. We further examined the gene ontology (GO) of retained gene duplicates from each of the four CucWGDs, and found that the duplicates from the proposed WGDs are enriched for several GO categories (Figures 5 and S13), relative to genome-wide frequency, such as cell division and movement (including endosome and cytoskeleton) and carbohydrate/secondary metabolic processes. The enrichment of the same GO categories in retained duplicates from two or more WGDs suggests that potential functional innovations associated to cell division and metabolism might have benefited cucurbits at multiple times during evolution.

#### **Major Radiations Around the Climate Optimum in the Early and Middle Eocene**

To investigate the species diversification dynamics in Cucurbitaceae, we used MEDUSA (Alfaro et al., 2009) and BAMM (Rabosky et al., 2014) to estimate the diversification rates and their shifts by using our time-calibrated phylogeny (Figures 2 and S10). We identified two instances of rate acceleration using BAMM and two using MEDUSA, totaling four rate shifts as shown in Figure 3. Two of such shifts in diversification rates were along the Cucurbitaceae phylogenetic backbone, both having occurred during climate upheavals. Specifically, the use of BAMM identified a rate up-shift for the MRCA of Clades V to VIII (shift 1) during the hot period of the Early Eocene [Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM) and Early Eocene Climate Optimum (EECO) (55-53 Ma)], while the MEDUSA analysis uncovered another rate acceleration for MRCA of Clades VII and VIII (shift 2) during the Mid-Eocene Climate Optimum (MECO) (42 Ma). Other events of rate acceleration are found for clades of *Psiguria*-*Wilbrandia* (shift 3) and *Thladiantha* (shift 4) during Miocene.

## **Reconstruction of Ancestral States of Cucurbitaceae Morphological Characters**

Based on our resolved Cucurbitaceae phylogeny with branch lengths, we traced the ancestral states of 15 morphological characters (Tables S4 and S5). Among them, eight characters of habit, stem, leaf, fruit size, seed appendage, sexual system, floral symmetry and stigma number have no detected transitions along the backbone of the cucurbit phylogeny (Figures S14-S21). Both herbaceous and woody stems had equal possibilities to be the ancestral character in the early history of cucurbits. The predominance of herbaceous stem was traced back to the ancestors of both crown Clade I and that of Clade VII-Clade VIII (Figure S15). Morphological state of three traits (stamen number, pollen size and style number; Figures S22-S24) had transformed once along the phylogenetic backbone at the MRCA of Clade V-Clade VIII. The cucurbit ancestral stamen number was five, then the number was reduced to and maintained at three, after the split of Clade IV, starting at the MRCA of Clades V-VIII and to a large part of the descending nodes (Figure S22). Pollen grains of Cucurbitaceae ancestor had medium sizes (25-50 μm) but became large (50-100 μm) since the origin of the MRCA of Clade V-Clade VIII (Figure S23). Although it is equivocal whether the MRCA of Cucurbitaceae had one or three styles, ancestors of Clades II to IV likely had three styles, but those of Clades V to VIII had reduced to one, with an apparent reversion to three in *Wilbrandia* (Figure S24).

The remaining four of the 15 examined characters (tendril, fruit type, root and petal color; Figures 6 and S25 to S27) are highly diverse in modern cucurbits and their evolutionary patterns are complex. The fibrous root system was probably the ancestral state at the crown group of Cucurbitaceae and has been maintained in Clade I, whereas tuberous root (pachypodia) originated in the MRCA of Clades II to VIII (Figure S25) and was the predominant root type spanning from the Late Cretaceous to the Early Eocene (77.7–48.6 Ma). The tendril, the characteristic and taxonomic trait of Cucurbitaceae (Schaefer and Renner, 2011b), can be classified as simple tendril or branched tendrils with or without coiling below the branch point. A branched tendril with a coiling basal part was the original state for this family, whereas those without coiling appeared in the MRCA of Clades V-VIII (Figure 6). The simple, branchless tendril was derived from branched tendril in Clade VIII and became a prominent character in the tribe Coniandreae and a subclade (*Cephalopentandra*-*Cucumis*) of Benincaseae. There were also incidents of parallel evolution of simple tendril in some other tribes. Petal colors vary greatly among Cucurbitaceae, with white and yellow being common and red, pink and others also found in some taxa (Schaefer and Renner, 2011b). Greenish-yellow was likely the ancestral appearance of cucurbit flowers and has been maintained in Clade I (Figure S26). A transition from greenish-yellow petals to yellow ones occurred after Clade I diverged, whereas white petals appeared in the MRCA of Clades V-VIII; multiple other colors, including red, orange, purple and pink, appear to have

originated in several genera (Figure S26).

One of the most recognized morphological characteristics of cucurbits is the pepo fruit, which is considered as a special type of berry that has hard or leathery pericarp. Cucumber, melon, watermelon and pumpkin have indehiscent pepos, whereas bitter gourd and loofah have dehiscent ones, which split open at maturity to release seeds. Other members produce the dry fruits capsule and samara (the latter only found in *Cyclantheropsis*); we thus coded cucurbit fruits into five types: capsule, berry, dehiscent pepo, indehiscent pepo and samara, and reconstructed the history of fruit type evolution (Figures 6 and S27). Our result supports capsule as the ancestral fruit type for Cucurbitaceae and for Clades I and II. The fruit type was transformed into fleshy indehiscent pepo at the origin of crown group of Clade III–Clade VIII. The fruit then became dehiscent pepos after the divergence of Clade IV and was reverted back to indehiscent pepo in the progenitor of Clade VIII and that of the majority of tribe Sicyoeae. A less parsimonious alternative is that the backbone for Clades III to VIII had indehiscent pepos, but dehiscent pepo evolved in parallel in each of Clades V-VII. Moreover, the juicy berry evolved independently at least five times from either dehiscent or indehiscent pepo, and is largely found in tribes Coniandreae and Bryonieae.

#### **Origination of Cucurbitaceae**

Tendrils in Cucurbitaceae are thought to have evolved from modified stems/shoots (Gerrath et al., 2008), and the formation of tendrils in cucumber requires the tendril identity gene, *TEN*, which encodes a putative transcription factor belonging to the CYC/TB1 clade of the TCP family (Wang et al., 2015). Members of the TCP family have been reported to function in the development of axillary meristems into flowers or lateral shoots and in regulation of floral symmetry (Luo et al., 1996; Martin-Trillo and Cubas, 2010; Wang et al., 2015). *TEN* gene is crucial for regulating tendril development of cucurbits with an additional motif (CNNFYFP) (Wang et al., 2015) in homologs from species of tribes Benincaseae, Cucurbiteae, Sicyoeae and Momordiceae. We identified 22 *TEN* homologs in cucurbits by using *TEN* sequences from *Cucumis sativus*, *Cucumis melo* and *Citrullus lanatus* genomes (Enright et al., 2002; Wang et al., 2015) as queries to search transcriptomic and genomic datasets and by amplifying cDNAs from basal Cucurbitaceae genera using primers matching conserved regions in *TEN* homologs. The reconstructed phylogeny of *TEN* homologs (Figure 7A) revealed two duplication events at the crown group of Cucurbitaceae and that of tribe Cucurbiteae, respectively, coincident with CucWGD1 and CucWGD2 detected here (Figure 3). We identified *TEN* genes in species across all eight Cucurbitaceae clades and all of the encoded proteins have the characteristic CNNFYFP motif, whereas descendants of the other copy of *TCP* genes from the same duplication do not have this motif. By examining eight publicly available genomes, we found *TEN* homologs are located in syntenic blocks in five genomes (Figure 7B). The synteny results in *Lagenaria siceraria*, *Citrullus lanatus* and *Cucumis sativus* for gene duplication resulting in the ancestral *TEN* gene are consistent for the duplication being part of CucWGD1 (red circle). The syntenic regions for later gene duplications in the two *Cucurbita* genomes are likely part of CucWGD2 (green circles). The *C. melo* chromosomal region containing *TEN* underwent extensive rearrangement; in addition, the region in *M. charantia* genome has retained partial collinearity yet *TEN* and two other genes were translocated to another genomic position. These results and gene family phylogeny together strongly support that *TEN* is a duplicate resulted from CucWGD1. Following the early duplication, further duplications were not detected for most species, suggesting that no additional benefits were offered by more copies. The duplication at the origin of Cucurbiteae, as supported by the retention of two gene copies in *Cucurbita* species (Figure 7A), corresponding to CucWGD2, raises an interesting possibility of additional functional divergence of *TEN* duplicates in this tribe.

# **DISUSSION**

We report a highly resolved Cucurbitaceae phylogeny that supports eight well-defined clades and relationships among all tribes. Our investigation of divergence times, large-scale gene duplications, diversification dynamics and character transformations uncover striking coincidences for the three kinds of events under challenging conditions in the geological history. Furthermore, a clear picture of an overall adaptive strategy during cucurbit evolution emerges from the synthesis of character changes in the flower, fruit, stem and root. The history of Cucurbitaceae revealed here serves as an important example for a synergistic effect of intrinsic and extrinsic factors in plant evolution.

We found that several events of diversification accelerations and transformations of morphological characters occurred along the Cucurbitaceae phylogenetic backbone around the ages of geological upheavals. The origin of crown cucurbits in the Late Cretaceous was

associated with the early CucWGD1. Shortly afterwards were the transformations of root (from fibrous to tuberous root) and fruit types (from dry capsule to fleshy pepo). CucWGD1 possibly contributed to the morphological transitions by providing abundant genetic materials as duplicated genes, as noted generally for WGDs in angiosperms (Soltis and Soltis, 2016). In addition, at the K-Pg boundary, one of greatest mass extinction (>75% species) in Earth's history, probably provided open niches for early cucurbits; at the same time, the polyploid cucurbits might have had high adaptive potential, as proposed previously for ancient polyploids (Vanneste et al., 2014a; Vanneste et al., 2014b). For example, the innovation of pepo likely enhanced seed dispersal by attracting mammals whose diversity also greatly increased after the K-Pg extinction of dinosaurs and others (Halliday et al., 2017; Halliday and Goswami, 2016; Longrich et al., 2016).

Besides the cucurbit divergence near the time of K-Pg mass extinction, there were also radiations and intensive character innovations during the hot periods in Paleocene/Eocene, along with a burst of transformations in flower, fruit and tendril characters at the MRCA of Clades V-VIII. These changes might have contributed to adaptation to the rapid and extreme global warming event in early Paleogene (PETM and EECO) characterized by the highest global temperatures in the Cenozoic era. Another coincidental detection of environmental changes and species radiation in Cucurbitaceae was near the MRCA of both Clades VII and VIII (Figure 3) in Mid-Eocene, when a surge of global temperature (MECO) was reported (Doria et al., 2011; Pross et al., 2012; Zachos et al., 2008), suggesting a relationship among morphological changes, environmental influence and species diversification. Moreover, changes in key traits could have potentially contributed to the radiation under MECO, including the evolution from fibrous roots to tuberous roots for storage (Figure S25) and the transformation from dehiscent pepo back to indehiscent pepo for enhanced seed dispersal (Figure 6B). About 80% of modern cucurbits diverged after the changes during MECO, indicating the probable impact of such changes. Our results provide strong support for interactions among phenotypic transitions and changing environmental conditions can enhance adaptation and lead to diversity bursts during evolution.

One major cucurbit adaptive strategy is to climb using tendrils, enabling the plants to reach the canopy to increase photosynthesis, pollination, and seed dispersal with minimal energy. The emergence of the cucurbit-specific tendril identity gene *TEN* due to gene duplication event shared by the family and likely as part of the CucWGD1 event (Figure 7)

possibly contributed to the morphological innovation at the origin of cucurbits (Figure 6A). Furthermore, the ancestral cucurbit tendrils were branched with a coiling basal part (Figure 6A), followed by a trend of simplification that included an initial reduction of the coiling base in the MRCA of Clades V-VIII and a further change to simple, branchless tendrils in some derived clades in Clade VIII. Also, cucurbit stems tend to evolve from woody to herbaceous (Figure S15), with the latter form growing rapidly and providing relatively easy access to the plant of the upper space and sunlight for photosynthesis. The simplified tendrils permit plants to re-distribute their resources to produce more tendrils along the fast-growing stems, and thus increase the clinging area.

Our results support capsule as the ancestral fruit type in Cucurbitaceae and in the early-diverging Clades I and II, indicating that dehiscence of the dry capsule was the early strategy for seed dispersal. This was subsequently, near the K-Pg boundary, replaced by the fleshy pepo fruit, which attracts animals to consume, providing a means for long distance seed dispersal. In addition, the unusual form of pepo with dehiscent pericarp evolved near PETM, providing an alternative way of seed dispersal in the absence of animals, whereas the juicy and indehiscent pepo became predominant again near MECO. The fruit type changes that followed the CucWGD1 event suggest that the increased genetic materials from the large-scale duplications might have facilitated the subsequent morphological changes, which in turn contributed to the adaptation of cucurbits during periods of geological and climate upheavals and radiations in the Early and Middle Eocene. In addition, although pepo was previously thought to be a specialized type of berry, the Cucurbitaceae berry is a derived fruit type independently evolved from either indehiscent or dehiscent pepo. Thus, unlike the evolution from dry capsule to fleshy berry widely observed in other angiosperm lineages, the Cucurbitaceae pepo seems to be an intermediate fruit form between capsule and berry.

Additional morphological transformations along the phylogenetic backbone involve floral characteristics. Modern cucurbits exhibit a tremendous diversity of flower color often correlated with other floral traits, contributing to the recognition of pollination syndromes (Fenster et al., 2004). The showy petals (yellow or orange) are more attractive to birds and pollen-foraging bees, whereas white flowers are mainly pollinated by moths and bats. Our analysis revealed an overall trend for the transformation of petal colors in Cucurbitaceae from yellow to white (Figure S26). The transition to white petals reduces the visibility of plants to

bees (Sletvold et al., 2016), implying the change of or the decrease of the demand for pollinators. The reduction of stamen number (Figure S22) and the enlargement of pollen size (Figure S23) seem to simplify the structure of flowers, allowing the plants to reallocate the energy and resources to other purpose, such as to increase ovule production (Sicard and Lenhard, 2011). The sexual strategy of monoecy in half of extant cucurbits is derived from the ancestral dioecy (Figure S19), consistent with the little heterosis known in this family (Gusmini and Wehner, 2008; Schaefer and Renner, 2011b). The evolution of monoecy allowed self-pollination while still facilitating cross pollination (Sicard and Lenhard, 2011). Taken together, cucurbits tend to simplify their reproductive organs and to reduce their requirements in pollinators, perhaps to increase the attraction of their fruits to animals as a trade-off. This trend of simplification is similar to the evolution pattern of tendrils.

In short, this study has established a well-resolved Cucurbitaceae phylogeny, providing a valuable foundation for many evolutionary and comparative studies. Further analyses here on divergence time, diversification rates, and morphological histories have illuminated striking coincidences of these events with geological upheavals. Such coincidental changes following the CucWGD1 event further support the proposal that the interactions among WGD, novel morphological features, and the dramatic environmental changes during major geological periods have contributed to major radiations and evolutionary success of Cucurbitaceae.

# **Methods**

#### **Taxon Sampling and Sequencing**

The species sampled here included Cucurbitaceae members and others in Cucurbitales, Rosales, Fabales, Fagales and Malpighiales (Table S1). Sequence Read Archive (SRA) data were retrieved from GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/; last accessed, 2016.10). For transcriptome or genome sequencing, total RNA or genomic DNA were extracted from young tissues or silica gel-dried leaf materials using ZR plant RNA MiniPrep kit or modified CTAB method (Zhang et al., 2012), respectively. Transcriptomes and genomes were both sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq2000 platform, generating paired-end reads of over 100 bp, which were *de novo* assembled into contigs using Trinity (Grabherr et al., 2011) with parameters described in Huang et al. (2016a) for transcriptomes and SOAPdenovo (Luo et al., 2012) with default settings for genomes. Redundant contigs from each sample were reduced using CD-HIT 4.6 (Fu et al., 2012) with the parameter -c 0.98. Raw reads of transcriptomes and genomes generated in this study are available at NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) Sequence Read Archive database as Bioproject PRJNA624798, and the assemblies can be downloaded at Figshare (https://figshare.com/s/111835077bd85f68d659, https://figshare.com/s/9c8cc7464ae06b40e0c2).

#### **Ortholog Identification and Gene Selection**

Orthologous genes (OGs) were identified with OrthoMCL v1.4 (Li et al., 2003) through an all-against-all BLASTP searches with an E value cutoff of 10−20 from 16 cucurbit species representing most tribes (*Baijiania taiwaniana*, *Bolbostemma biglandulosum*, *Cucumis sativus*, *Cucurbita pepo*, *Ecballium elaterium*, *Gerrardanthus parviflora*, *Gynostemma pubescens*, *Hemsleya chinensis*, *Ibervillea sonorae*, *Indofevillea khasiana*, *Luffa aegyptiaca*, *Trichosanthes dunniana*, *Momordica boivinii*, *Schizopepon monoicus*, *Siraitia grosvenorii*, *Telfairia pedata*). The resulting 1503 OGs from these species were used as seeds to retrieve homologous sequences from the other species using HaMStR (Ebersberger et al., 2009) with E values of e-20 . Nucleotide sequences were aligned and trimmed by MUSCLE v3.8.31 (Edgar, 2004) and trimAl v1.2 (Capella-Gutiérrez et al., 2009) with default settings, respectively.

To increase the robustness of phylogenetic analyses, we aimed to maximize species coverage and phylogenetic signal and reduce missing data at single-gene level. Thus starting from the dataset of 1503 OGs, we selected 1181 OGs with at least one sequence from each tribe; also, 942 OGs were identified that had at least 90% species coverage. Among these 942 OGs, 737 OGs were further selected with lengths of aligned matrix of at least 700 bp. Previous and our preliminary analyses revealed that Cucurbitaceae likely experienced one or more rounds of WGDs, resulting in multiple paralogs, or even hidden paralogs due to asymmetric loss. To reduce the effect of (hidden) paralogs, we examined single-gene trees of the 737 OGs and found tribe Thladiantheae was not monophyletic in 277 gene trees. Because tribe Thladiantheae was consistently monophyletic in previous studies (Kocyan et al., 2007; Schaefer and Renner, 2011a) and our analyses using the 1503, 1181, and 942 OG datasets, these 277 OGs might contain (hidden) paralogs. Therefore, we obtained another a gene set with 460 OGs after excluding the 277 OGs. Because the datasets of *Gerrardanthus lobatus* and *Siolmatra pentaphylla* of the early-divergent tribe Zanonieae have small numbers of contigs and N50 value (Table S1) and only have 139 and 101 sequences in the 460 OG set, we

selected 201 OGs containing sequences from both of these two species from the 460 OGs in

order to include more phylogenetic information for relationships of the early tribes. We also included 57 OGs discarded during filtering by the monophyly of tribe Thladianthaeae (from 737 to 460) that had sequences of the above two species and two *Fevillea* species with limited recovery of gene sequences in part due to the shotgun sequencing of their genomic DNAs. The resulting 258 OGs were also used in subsequent analysis.

#### **Phylogenetic Reconstruction**

We reconstructed the phylogeny of Cucurbitaceae using genes from 136 datasets in Cucurbitaceae and two outgroups (*Begonia leprosa* and *Tetrameles nudiflora*). In the coalescent approach, phylogenetic trees of individual OG were reconstructed using Maximum Likelihood (ML) method by RAxML 7.0.4 (Stamatakis, 2006) with 100 replicates. For each of the six OG sets (1503, 1181, 942, 737, 460 and 258 OGs), the coalescent tree was calculated by Astral 4.4.4 (Mirarab et al., 2014) with the summarized ML tree and the 100 replicates of individual OG. We also concatenated the 258 OGs into a supermatrix and reconstructed the phylogenetic tree using RAxML 7.0.4 (Stamatakis, 2006) with 1000 replicates under the GTR + CAT model.

#### **Divergence Time Estimation**

We used a Bayesian method to estimate divergence times of Cucurbitaceae by MCMCTREE in the PAML package (Yang, 2007), using as the input tree that from ML reconstruction with the concatenated 258 OGs while assigning the topology as shown in Figure 1. We selected eight fossils and a secondary calibration for dating analyses (Table S2). The branch lengths were estimated in program BASEML in PAML package under the GTR + G model (model = 7). The overall substitution rate (rgene gamma) and rate-drift parameter (sigma2 gamma) were set as G (1, 4.1) and G (1, 1.2) respectively, according to an estimation using strict molecular clock (clock = 1 in BASEML) with root age constrained between 115.8 Ma [based on the MRCA of Cucurbitales and Malpighiales in Magallón et al. (2015)] and 125 Ma (the age of tricolpate pollen grains fossil) (Hughes and Hughes, 1994). The independent rate model was used to specify the rate priors for internal nodes. # **Homology Inference, Gene Duplication Mapping, Syntenic Analysis and GO Enrichment**

To investigate genome duplications, we mainly followed a previous procedure (Yang et al., 2018a; Yang et al., 2015) to identify homologous groups, and used tree reconciliations to map gene duplications. In brief, all-against-all BLASTP was performed on datasets of 144 species (127 cucurbits and 17 outgroups; Table S1). After filtering by identity and coverage, the BLASTP results were subjected to MCL program (Enright et al., 2002) to identify homologous groups with an inflation value of 6.0. Each cluster containing at least 11 cucurbit taxa was aligned using MAFFT (Katoh and Standley, 2013) and trimmed by Phyutility v2.2 (Smith and Dunn, 2008). Preliminary gene trees were reconstructed using FastTree (Price et al., 2009) and filtered iteratively to remove long branches (Yang et al., 2018a; Yang et al., 2015). The final gene trees were mapped onto the species tree to locate duplication events using scripts from previous studies (Ren et al., 2018b), with an additional requirement for at least 30% species coverage in each of the duplicated subclades in gene trees using rePhylo package of R (https://github.com/Chien-Hsun/rePhylo/). Genomic collinearity of eight available cucurbit genomes were analyzed using the MCScan toolkit (V1.1) (Wang et al., 2012) [https://github.com/tanghaibao/jcvi/wiki/MCscan-(Python-version)] and visualized as circle plots by Circos (Krzywinski et al., 2009). The eight genomes were from two *Cucumis*, one *Citrullus*, one *Lagenaria*, three *Cucurbita*, and one *Momordica* species. Four other cucurbit genomes (*Cucurbita argyrosperma*, *Luffa cylindrica*, *Benincasa hispida*, and *Siraitia grosvenorii*) became available very recently and were not available when we were performed the phylogenomic analyses. Functional annotation of gene duplicates and the enrichment tests were performed using information from homologs in the *Arabidopsis thaliana* dataset by AnnotationHub (Morgan, 2019) and clusterProfiler packages (Yu et al., 2012) of R v3.6.1. Slim version of plant GO was downloaded from the TAIR website (https://www.arabidopsis.org/download\_files/GO\_and\_PO\_Annotations/Gene\_Ontology\_Ann otations/TAIR\_GO\_slim\_categories.txt).

#### **Diversification Rate Analysis**

Diversification rates and their shifts were analyzed using the R package MEDUSA (Alfaro et

al., 2009) under the birth-death model with AICc criterion, and using the Bayesian analysis of

macro-evolutionary mixtures (BAMM) (Rabosky et al., 2014). The species richness data were compiled from Scheafer and Renner (2011b). To account for non-random incomplete taxon sampling, we specified the sampling fraction for each genus in the family (Table S6). For BAMM, we performed two independent runs for 10,000,000 generations with sampling every 1000 generations. The first 25% samples were discarded as burn-in. The effective sample size of likelihood and the number of shifts were all > 200 by using the R package CODA (Plummer et al., 2006). Results from BAMM was analyzed by the R package BAMMtools (Rabosky et al., 2014).

#### **Ancestral Character State Reconstructions**

We encoded 15 characters covering nearly all morphological organs, including roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruits, seeds and habit, from the literature and by examining our specimens, as summarized in Tables S4 and S5. All traits were reconstructed with the genus tree by collapsing tips of our chronogram from age estimation into genera. The analyses were carried out by Mesquite (Maddison and Maddison, 2018) under the parsimony model.

#### **Molecular Evolution of the Tendril Gene** *TEN*

A total of 127 cucurbit ingroups and six outgroups (Table S1) were selected to identify the *TEN* gene essential for tendril (Wang et al., 2015). Here, we identified *TCP* genes in the 133 datasets by HMMER v3.1 (Mistry et al., 2013) using the TCP entry (PF03634.13) of Pfam-A from Pfam v32.0 (Bateman et al., 2018). Protein sequences were retrieved using HMMER v3.1 (Mistry et al., 2013). Additionally, we extracted RNA from seven basal cucurbit taxa using CTAB protocol (J. J. Doyle and Doyle, 1987), and used primers TEN\_F (AGCAAGATTTACACAGCTCA) and TEN\_R (GGAGAWGGCTTCCACATGAT) to amplify cDNA fragments of *TEN* homologs. All protein sequences were aligned by MAFFT (Katoh and Standley, 2013; Nakamura et al., 2018) and trimmed by trimAl (Capella-Gutiérrez et al., 2009). The ML tree of the *TCP* gene family was reconstructed using FastTree (Price et al., 2009) under the WAG model. Then, we extracted CYC/TB1 subclade including *TEN*, realigned and trimmed sequences, and reconstructed the phylogeny by IQ-TREE (Nguyen et al., 2015) under the WAG + G model.
