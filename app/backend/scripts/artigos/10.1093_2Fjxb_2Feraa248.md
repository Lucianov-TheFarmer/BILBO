# **Highlight**

Over-expression of a maize transcription factor mEmBP-1 increases photosynthesis, biomass and yield in rice by up-regulation of genes coding proteins involved in photosynthetic light reactions and carbon metabolism.

Accepted Manuscript

Identifying new options to improve photosynthetic capacity is a major approach to improve crop yield potential. Here we report that over-expression of one transcription factor, mEmBP-1 gene, led to simultaneously increased expression of many genes in photosynthesis, including genes encoding chlorophyll *a*, *b*-binding proteins (Lhca and Lhcb), photosystem II (PsbR3 and PsbW) and photosystem I reaction center subunits (PsaK and PsaN), chloroplast ATP synthase subunit and electron transport reaction components (Fd1 and PC), and also major genes in the Calvin Benson Bassham cycle, including Rubisco, GAPDH, FBA, TK and PRK. These increased photosynthesis gene expressions resulted in increased leaf chlorophyll pigment, photosynthetic rate, biomass growth and grain yield both in the greenhouse and also in the field. Using EMSA experiments, we showed that mEmBP-1a protein can directly bind to the promoter region of photosynthesis genes, suggesting that the direct binding of mEmBP-1a to the G-box domain of photosynthetic genes upregulates expression of these genes. In summary, this study shows that mEmBP-1a is a major regulator of photosynthesis, which can be used to increase rice photosynthesis and yield in the field.

#### **Key Words:**

Above ground biomass, grain yield, G-Box motif, Photosynthetic capacity, RNA-seq, transcription factor

#### Introduction

Improvement of photosynthetic efficiency is a major option to increase crop light use efficiency (Zhu *et al.*, 2010; Long *et al.*, 2015). Combination of systems modeling and genetic manipulations led to identification of a number of feasible options to improve crop photosynthesis, including increased speed of recovery from photoprotection (Zhu *et al.*, 2004; Kromdijk *et al.*, 2016), increased expression of SBPase (Zhu *et al.*, 2007; Simkin *et al.*, 2015) and engineering photorespiratory bypasses (Kebeish *et al.*, 2007; Xin *et al.*, 2015; South *et al.*, 2019; Shen *et al.*, 2019). This improved photosynthetic efficiency indeed resulted in increased biomass production in the field (Simkin *et al.*, 2015; Kromdijk *et al.*, 2016; South *et al.*, 2019; Shen *et al.*, 2019). In the case of Shen *et al.* (2019), increased photosynthesis directly resulted in the improved rice yield in the paddy field conditions. These successes provide convincing evidence that crop yield can be improved by enhancing photosynthesis.

So far, most of the research on enhancing photosynthesis focuses on improving a particular step or property of photosynthesis. Another approach to manipulate photosynthesis is to identify regulators of photosynthesis, which can simultaneously up or down-regulate the expression of multiple genes involved in photosynthesis. The expression of photosynthesis genes is regulated by different transcription factors (TFs) by binding either singly or in combination to particular binding motifs in the promoter regions of photosynthesis genes (Saibo *et al.*, 2009; Hibberd and Covshoff, 2010). Earlier, Ambavaram *et al.* (2014) identified a transcription factor which can coordinate expression of photosynthesis genes and result in increased rice yield under stress conditions, suggesting that identification of TFs which can simultaneously regulate expression of many photosynthesis genes is a feasible approach to increase yield potential.

One way to identify new TFs that can simultaneously regulate many photosynthetic genes is to develop a robust gene regulator network (GRN) for photosynthesis. With such a goal, we have previously developed a GRN for photosynthesis by simultaneously considering the binding affinity of TFs to its binding targets and the expression correlation between TF and its target genes (Yu *et al.*, 2014). This analysis enabled us to identify a set of TFs whose target genes are enriched in photosynthesis (Yu *et al.*, 2014). These identified TFs were systematically tested using transgenic experiments. This study reports the result of transgenic testing of one of these identified TFs, i.e. mEmBP-1 (Fig. 1A). We show that ectopic expression of mEmBP-1 led to increased expression of many photosynthesis genes in rice; furthermore, the increased expression of photosynthesis genes resulted in increased photosynthetic CO<sub>2</sub> uptake rate both in the greenhouse and also in the paddy field, suggesting that this TF is a potential regulator of photosynthesis. We further showed that mEmBP-1 can directly bind to promoters of multiple photosynthesis genes and activated their expression (Fig. 1A). This study therefore demonstrates that manipulation of EmBP-1 can be an effective approach to increase crop photosynthesis and hence yield potential in rice.

#### **Materials and Methods**

#### Identification of Transcription factor genes

The mEmBP-1 was from the GRN developed in Yu *et al.* (2014). The expression of mEmBP-1 shows a highly significant correlation (p < 0.001) with photosynthesis gene expression (Yu *et al.*, 2014).

#### Construction of the Phylogenetic tree of the gene EmBP-1

We constructed the phylogenetic tree of EmBP-1 in representative species among Viridisplantae, which span from basal species belonging to Chlorophyte (Micromonas pusilla and Chlamydomonas reihardtii), to Embryophyte (Marchantia polymorpha), to Tracheophyte (Selaginella moellendorfii), and to Angiosperm (Amborella trichopoda), including 10 eudicot species and 8 monocot species. For the eudicot, we included 6 species belong to Brassicaceae; and for the monocot, we included 7 species belong to the grass family. In total, 29 representative species were used to develop the phylogeny of EmBP-1 orthologs. The protein sequences of representative species were downloaded from Phytozome (https://phytozome.igi.doe.gov/). We applied OrthoFinder (V 0.7.1) (Emms and Kelly, 2015) with default parameters to predict genome wide ortholog groups among the 29 species and genes from ortholog group containing Z. mays EmBP-1 were thus regarded as EmBP-1 orthologs. Protein sequences of EmBP-1 orthologs were then aligned using MUSCLE (Edgar, 2004). Phylogenetic tree was constructed applying Raxml (Stamatakis, 2006) based on protein sequence using PROTGAMMAILG model, which assumes the site variations follow gamma distribution. Bootstrap scores were calculated by running 100,000 independent gene trees. GRMZM2G019106 from Z. mays was used as outgroup as it shows the highest similarity with Z. mays EmBP-1 among Z. mays genome, with a protein sequence similarity of 33.06 %.

#### Subcellular localization of mEmbP-1a protein

The whole coding sequence of mEmBP-1a (GRMZM2G095078) without stop codon was amplified by PCR with gene specific primers 1-F and 1-R (Supplementary Table S2) and inserted into BamHI digested pA7 vector via homologous recombination method (C112, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd), to generate pA7-mEmbP-1a-YFP fusion construct under the control of CaMV 35s promoter and tNOS terminator (Fig. 1C). The construct was confirmed by sequencing and used for transient transformation of rice protoplasts via PEG mediated transfection. A control construct pA7, having nuclear localization signal (NLS) linked with RFP was from (see Zhai et al., 2019, Fig. 1C). The procedure for protoplast isolation and PEG transfection followed Zhang et al. (2011). In brief, sterilized rice seeds with seed coat removed were grown on ½ strength MS medium in the growth chamber conditions [12 h light (~160  $\mu$  mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) and 12 h dark photoperiod at 26°C] for 7-14 days. Approximately, 30 seedlings at 0.4-0.8 inches tall were used to obtain leaf sheaths and stem parts; these parts were cut into strips of ~ 0.5 mm long with sharp razors followed by transferring to 0.6 M mannitol under dark conditions. Subsequently, strips were transferred to enzyme solution for enzymatic digestion (0.75% Macerozyme R-10, 1.5% Cellulase RS, 10 mM MES and 0.6 M mannitol at pH 5.7, 10 mM CaCl, and 0.1% BSA) in dark conditions for 4-5 hours with gentle shaking (~50 rpm). After enzymatic digestion, an equal volume of W5 solutions (125 mM CaCl<sub>2</sub>, 154 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl and 2 mM MES at pH 5.7) was added followed by short timed (~10 sec) vigorous shaking, filtration (with 45 µm nylon meshes) and 3-4 times washing with W5 solution. Furthermore, pellets were collected by centrifugation (1500 rpm), quantified using a hemocytometer and re-dissolved (at  $2 \times$ 10<sup>6</sup> cells ml<sup>-1</sup> concentration) in MMG solution (15 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub> 0.4 M mannitol and 4 mM MES at pH 5.7) followed by PEG mediated transfection (Zhang et al., 2011; Page et al., 2019).

Transfected protoplasts were observed under confocal laser scanning microscopy (Leica TCS 5 SP5 AOBS). RFP and YFP were excited at 488 nm and 514 nm wavelengths and detected at 588 nm and 527 nm wavelengths respectively. Scanned images were captured using Zeiss LSM software and processed using Adobe Illustrator software (Adobe Systems, Mountain view, CA).

#### Vector construction and generation of transgenic rice lines

For the construction of mEmBP-1a OE vector, the coding region of mEmBP-1a (GRMZM2G095078) was amplified by PCR with gene specific primers 2-F and 2-R (Supplementary Table S2) and the cDNA which reverse transcribed from total RNA extracted from 10 d-old maize seedlings (accession B73). Amplified mEmbP-1a segment was cloned into the OE vector (the vector containing maize ubiquitin-1 promoter and NOS terminator which derived from pCAMBIA1302, (Cambia, Australia) and fused

with the FLAG-tag in the C-terminus. Besides, same OE vector was used as a control empty plasmid containing ubi-1 promoter fused with flag tag only. Both vectors were multiplied using DH5 $\alpha$  *E. coli* strain and integrated into the embryogenic callus of rice mature seeds (*Oryza sativa* ssp. *japonica* cv. Nipponbare) through the agrobacterium-mediated method (Hiei *et al.*, 1994).

Successfully regenerated transgenic shootlets over the hygromycin selection medium were transferred to the environmentally controlled growth room conditions maintained at 28 °C  $\pm$  1 day (14 h light) and 25 °C  $\pm$  1 night (10 h dark) and 65 % relative humidity (RH). The screening of mEmBP-1a transgenic lines (named as EmBPOE rice) and control vector transgenic lines (named as control lines) were done at the T0 generation by PCR determination of hygromycin gene (using primers HYG-F and HYG-R; Supplementary Table S2).

# **Plant growth conditions**

The T1 and T2 generation seeds collected from the positively screened T0 generation plants of mEmBP-1a transformants (EmBPOE rice lines) and control empty vector plants were grown in environmentally controlled growth room conditions (28 °C day and 25 °C night, 1200 μmol photons  $m^{-2}$  s<sup>-1</sup> PPFD, 16 h : 08 h Light : dark photoperiod and ~75 % relative humidity). Presence of mEmBP1-a gene (using primers 3-F and 3-R) was tested by PCR in more than 10 hygromycin resistant lines (Supplementary Table S2). Out of 10 positive lines, four homozygous lines (named as EmBPOE1, EmBPOE2, EmBPOE3, and EmBPOE4) of T3 generation were used for final experimental analysis. Hence, T3 generation seeds of EmBPOE transgenics together with WT (Oryza sativa ssp. japonica cv. Nipponbare) and the seeds from control empty vector plants were transplanted into paddy field conditions at two experimental locations, Shanghai (121.4530°E, 31.0428°N), China, in May to September 2017 (SH), and Hainan (110.0375°E, 18.5060°N), China, in December 2017 to April 2018 (HN). We planted seedlings in 20 rows and each row contained 10 seedlings with a row distance of 20 cm and distance between seedlings within the same as 20 cm. From the seedling transplantation stage to booting stages, the average temperature range was around 25 ~ 31 °C and day length was >14 h in SH (long day conditions). While for HN average temperature range was about 23 ~ 36 °C and day length was <12 h (short day condition). Standard local agronomic practices were followed to maintain the well-watered rice paddy fields as in Qu et al. (2017). EmBPOE lines, control empty vector plants and WT seeds of T3 generations were also grown in 6-L pots filled with soil (Pindstrup Substrate no. 4, Pindstrup Horticulture Ltd, Shanghai, China) in controlled growth room (28 °C day and 25 °C night, 1200 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> PPFD,16 h Light: 08 h dark photoperiod and ~75 % relative humidity).

## Protein extraction and Western Blot analysis

Accumulation of mEmbP-1a protein in EmBPOE rice lines compared with WT were analyzed by immunoblotting of leaves taken at ~ 30 DAP as described previously (Zhai *et al.*, 2019). Briefly, leaf tissues (20 mg) were grounded into powder in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in three volume of extraction buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 50 mM NaCl and 2% polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (PVPP)]. Homogenized extracts were centrifuged for 20 min at 15 000 *g* and 4 °C, obtained supernatants were subjected to Western blotting analysis. The protein concentrations were analyzed by utilizing a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). Further, 100 ug protein per well was resolved with SDS-PAGE in 12 % gels and electrophoretically transported to Hybond-P membrane (GE Healthcare UK Ltd.). The blotting membrane was treated with an affinity-purified anti-FLAG primary antibody (Sigma Corp, USA), subsequently incubated with secondary anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody coupled with horseradish peroxidase (Sigma Corp, USA). Signals of immunoreaction were perceived using an Immuno-Blot Assay Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) following manufacturer's instruction.

### **Chlorophyll content measurements**

Chlorophyll content in the WT and EmBPOE lines was determined using the youngest fully expended leaves taken at around ~ 40 d plants following Hamdani *et al.* (2019).

#### Leaf level photosynthetic physiological measurements

Leaf gas-exchange measurements were performed using a LI6400 Portable Photosynthesis System (LiCor, Lincoln, NE, USA) using the flag leaves of EmBPOE and WT plants. Measurements were performed between 10:00 AM to 4:30 PM at ~ 50 DAP. Gas-exchange parameters i.e., net photosynthetic CO<sub>2</sub> uptake rate ( $A_{sat}$  or A), stomatal conductance ( $g_s$ ), intrinsic water use efficiency (*i*WUE = A/gs) and internal CO<sub>2</sub> concentration ( $C_i$ ) were measured under a reference CO<sub>2</sub> concentration of 425 µmol mol<sup>-1</sup>, a PPFD of 1800 µmol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> and a flow rate of 500 µmol s<sup>-1</sup>. During the measurements, leaf temperature was maintained at 30 °C and relative humidity at 62-75 %. All these measurements were finished within 2 days and for each line ten replicates were used. Also, in growth chamber grown plants  $A_{sat}$  and  $A_{low}$  were measured under PPFD of 1200 and 400 µmol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> respectively. The leaf chamber block temperature was set to be around 25 °C to match the growth room environment.

Net photosynthesis rates under different internal leaf  $CO_2$  concentrations (*A-Ci* curve) and different photosynthetic photon flux densities (*A-Q* curve) were measured following Chang *et al.* (2017). In

Brief, for the *A*-*C<sub>i</sub>* curve, measurements were initiated from a reference CO<sub>2</sub> concentration of 425  $\mu$ mol mol<sup>-1</sup>, followed by gradual lowering *C<sub>i</sub>* from 425 to 350, 250, 150, 100 and 50  $\mu$ mol mol<sup>-1</sup> and then increased stepwise from 425 to 500, 700, 900, 1100, 1300, 1500 and 1800  $\mu$ mol mol<sup>-1</sup> CO<sub>2</sub>, under a constant PPFD of 1800  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>. For *A*-*Q* curve, measurements were conducted following a series of decreasing PPFD from 2000 to 1800, 1600, 1400, 1200, 1000, 800, 600, 400, 200, 100, 50, 25 and 0  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> at a constant CO<sub>2</sub> concentration of 425  $\mu$ mol mol<sup>-1</sup>. For each *A*-*C<sub>i</sub> and A*-*Q* curve measurement, 6 replicates were taken. Analysis of response curve of the photosynthetic CO<sub>2</sub> uptake rate versus *C<sub>i</sub>* was done using the classical steady state biochemical model of C<sub>3</sub> photosynthesis (Farquhar *et al.*, 1980).

#### Measurement of chlorophyll fluorescence parameters

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters related to PSII and PSI were measured using leaves of ~ 50-60 DAP plants with a pulse-amplitude modulated fluorimeter (DUAL-PAM101, Walz) following Schreiber and Klughammer (2008). All measurements were performed at a temperature of 25 °C. The photosynthetic electron transfer rates around PSII and PSI were measured and calculated following Schreiber and Klughammer (2008) and Yamori *et al.* (2016), as ETRI =  $0.5 \times 0.84 \times$  YI and ETRII=  $0.5 \times 0.84 \times$  YII, where 0.5 is the fraction of absorbed light receiving to PSII or PSI and 0.84 is the leaf absorptance.

The photochemical quenching (qL), quantum yield of photochemical quenching (YII and YI), redox state of  $Q_a$  (1-qP), were measured following Kramer *et al.* (2004) and Klughammer and Schreiber (2008).

# RNA-seq data collection and analysis

The second to the last leaf was collected at ~ 10:00 a.m. at the early heading stage from field grown plants. Leaves were immediately frozen into liquid nitrogen and stored in -80°C fridge until RNA extraction. To isolate RNA, leaf samples were grounded with liquid nitrogen and RNA was then extracted using the PureLink RNA Mini Kit following manufacturer's protocol (Life Technologies Corporation). The quality of extracted RNA was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent) and the RNA samples with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) higher than 6.8 were used for RNA library construction. RNA libraries were prepared for sequencing based on standard Illumina protocols and sequenced using Ilumina X Ten platform in paired-end 150 mode.

The quality of RNA-seq data (fastq files) was assessed by the FastQC software (<u>http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/</u>). Then adaptor and reads with low

quality from RNA-seq reads were trimmed using trim galore software raw (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim\_galore/) (Adaptor of read1: AGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCAC; Adaptor of read2: AGATCGGAAGAGCGTCGTGTAGGGAAAGAGTGT). Then RNA-seq data were analyzed with the STAR software (version 2.5.3a) (Dobin et al., 2013) with the rice reference genome IRGSP-1.0 as well as GTF file (downloaded from Ensembl Plants http://plants.ensembl.org/). After generating genome index, the clean RNA-seq reads were aligned by STAR (Dobin et al., 2013) with "--quantMode GeneCounts" option to count number reads per gene.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined by the R package "DESeq2" (Love *et al.*, 2014) with the read counts reported by STAR (Dobin *et al.*, 2013). Genes with the adjusted *p*-value below 0.01 and absolute value of log2 fold change higher than 1 were considered as DEGs.

The RNA-seq data were deposited in NCBI's Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and are accessible through GEO Series accession number GSE143259 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE143259).

# RNA isolation and gene expression analysis via qRT-PCR

Total RNA from leaves of the plants at the early heading stage was extracted using Purelink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) following the manufacturer's instruction. The mRNA was digested with DNase I to remove DNA contamination. The cDNA synthesis was conducted using 1µg of RNA, Oligo (dT) was used as primer to synthesize the first strand cDNA with SuperScript<sup>®</sup> III as reverse transcription enzyme (SuperScript<sup>\*</sup> III First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix for gRT-PCR. Carlsbad, CA). The gRT-PCR assays were performed using Power SYBR<sup>®</sup> Green PCR master mix (ThermoFisher) kit on a LightCycle480 System (Roche Applied Science, Indianapolis, USA). Primers for different genes (Os07g0544800, Os07g0558400, Os08g0200300, Os07g0141400, Os12g0420400, Os07g0577600, Os05g0560000 and Os01g0869800) were designed as 18-25 bp oligomers with Tm 55-60 to amplify a product of 100-200 bp long (Supplementary Table S2). PCR reactions were executed in the white plates of 96-well containing 20  $\mu$ l volume including 1  $\mu$ l of cDNA template (100 ng), 1  $\mu$ M of each primer, and 10 µl of 2X SYBR Green PCR Master Mix. Further reactions were subjected to a heating step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 36 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing and elongation at 60 °C for 60 s. Gene expression quantification was done by  $2^{-\Delta\Delta C(t)}$  using ACTIN1 as a housekeeping gene for normalization. Each qRT-PCR reaction was performed three times, calculated error bars represent the standard deviations from three replicates.

#### **EMSA** assay

The procedure for the EMSA assay followed Zhai et al., (2019). Briefly, the cDNA of mEmBP-1a gene (primers 4-F and 4-R; Supplementary Table S2) was cloned into the expression vector pET51b (+) between the restriction enzyme sites BamHI and SacI via homologous recombination method (C112, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd). The cDNA of mEmBP-1a was linked with Strep II tag at 5' end and His x10 at 3" end (StrepII::mEmBP-1a::10 x His) in pet51b (+) plasmid. Protein was expressed in competent E. coli cells (strain BL21) and induced by using IPTG (50 mM). To synthesize G-box containing DNA probes, G-box motifs were searched within 3000 bp upstream of ATG in the promoter regions of the genes Os11g0171300 (FBA1), Os12g0291100 (Rbcs3), Os08g0104600 (Fd1), Os07g0558400 (Lhcb4/CP29), Os12g0189400 (PsaN) and Os08g0200300 (PsbR3). To synthesize the fluorescent probes, the DNA amplifying primers were linked with CY5 fluorescent labelling at the 5'end and amplified motifs (60-100 bp) were gel purified as probes. Purified mEmBP1-a protein (200 ng) was incubated with fluorescent probes for 30 min at 25 °C in the buffer containing EMSA buffer [100 mM Tris (pH 7.9), 25 % glycerol, 0.2 ug ul<sup>-1</sup> BSA] and binding buffer (5x EMSA buffer, 1 M MgCl<sub>2</sub> and 0.5 M DTT, 1mg/ml salmon sperm DNA). For the competition test, non-labelled probes and mutated probes were added into the binding reaction. Non-labelled probes were amplified with the non-cy5 labelled primers, while the mutated probes were synthesized by Sangong Private Limited (Shanghai, China). Primer sequences and the motif sequence details used are listed in Supplementary Table (S2). Each reaction was loaded on a 6.5 % native polyacrylamide gel with 0.5 X TBE buffer and was run at 100v for 1 hour. The gel was then exposed to X-ray film to take pictures.

#### Evaluation of Agronomic traits

Agronomic traits including plant height (cm), tiller number, plant biomass (g), grain yield (GY) and grain yield components (Number of panicles, panicle length, number of spikelets, number of filled grains and 1000 grain weight (g) per plant bases (Ambavaram *et al.* 2014) were measured with 15 plants per line at ~ 45 DAP and ~ 95 DAP (days after planting) at both SH (Shanghai, China) and HN (Sanya, China) sites. Plants heights were measured for the main tiller. Samples for above ground shoot biomass analysis per plant were prepared by drying at 110° C for 1h followed by drying at 70° C for at least 3 days using a baking oven till samples were completely dried (Qu *et al.*, 2016).

At the maturity state, all panicles per plant were harvested and total number of panicles, spikelets and number of filled grains per plant were counted. Panicle length per plant was measured for the main panicle. The filled grains per plant were counted manually and dried at 37° C for 72 h and weighed. For 1000-grain weight measurements filled grains were picked randomly. For each plot plants were taken from the inside of the plots to eliminate boundary effect.

#### Results

#### Identification of TF gene, mEmbP-1 in the regulation of photosynthesis

Our previous study by Yu *et al.* (2014) constructed the regulatory network of photosynthesis, in which we found that EmBP-1 can potentially regulate 43 photosynthesis genes (Yu *et al.*, 2014), suggesting that EmBP-1 is a potential regulator of photosynthesis genes. See Fig. 1A showing schematic presentation of the whole study. The mEmBP-1 was shown previously that it can also bind to and regulate the G-Box motifs containing genes (Carlini *et al.*, 1999). We analyzed the photosynthesis genes in rice genome and found that ~ 45 % genes showed the presence of G-Box motifs (Supplementary Table S1). These results suggest that mEmbP-1 can potentially regulate photosynthesis. Earlier, the wheat bZIP protein EmBP1 has been found to bind to G-Box motifs palindrome (GCCACGTGGC) with a high affinity (Niu and Guiltinan 1994). Similar to this, several TF proteins bind to the G-Box motifs in the promoters of some photosynthesis genes i.e., GBF1 can bind to the G-box motifs in the promoters of several rbcs isoforms (Giuliano *et al.*, 1988), Opaque-2 controls the expression of cytosolic PPDK by binding to its G-box motifs (Maddaloni *et al.*, 1996).

# Phylogenetic analysis of EmBP-1 gene

We identified orthologs of EmBP-1 in 29 species from Viridisplantae and constructed the phylogenetic tree of EmBP-1 orthologs (see Fig. 1B). Interestingly, the orthologous group of maize EmBP-1 only contains monocot species. The gene with the highest sequence similarity with maize EmBP-1 in Arabidopsis is AT4G36730 (GBF1), showing an identity of 35 % at protein level, which is categorized as a different orthologous group. There are two types of EmBP-1 in monocot species, e.g., type I EmBP-1 has one copy whereas type II EmBP-1 has two copies, and the two copies is a result of a recent gene duplication based on phylogeny (Fig. 1B). In maize, the two copies of type II EmBP-1, namely GRMZM2G095078 (EmBP-1a) and GRMZM2G171370 (EmBP-1b), had a protein similarity of 89.89 %, whereas the two types of EmBP-1 had about 62 % protein sequence similarity

(Supplementary Fig. S1). The type II maize EmBP-1a shows high degrees of sequence similarity with its counterpart in *Sorghum bicolor* (89.29 %), *Setaria viridis* (85.26 %), *Oryza sativa* (79.67 %) and Wheat (68 %; see Carlini *et al.*, 1999), suggesting high degree of conservation of this gene in monocots.

#### The mEmBP-1 protein is localized in nucleus

The subcellular location of mEmBP-1 protein was assessed using a protoplast transient gene expression system. After examining the YFP signal in rice protoplasts, we found that mEmBP-1 is targeted to the nucleus (Fig. 1C).

#### Transformation and expression of mEmBP-1a gene in rice

To evaluate the effect of mEmBP-1 gene on photosynthetic performance, we expressed mEmBP-1a isoform in rice. We amplified and linked full-length cDNA of mEmBP-1a gene with a maize Ubi-1 promoter, generating a construct Ubi-1:: mEmBP-1a linked with FLAG tag (Fig. 2A). We also generated another construct containing only Ubi-1 promoter as a control empty vector. These constructs were transformed into the rice cultivar Nipponbare (as mentioned in the MM section). More than ten hygromycin resistant T0 generation lines were identified by PCR. Seeds from the transgenic rice at the T3 generation were collected and four homozygous lines named as EmBPOE1, EmBPOE2, EmBPOE3 and EmBPOE4 at the T3 generation were used for further analysis. The protein of the correct molecular weight (~ 40 KDa) is readily detectable in EmBPOE transgenics and not in WT lines (Fig. 2B). These transgenic lines over-expressing mEmBP-1a were named as EmBPOE rice. EmBPOE rice lines, WT and plants expressing control empty vector were grown at the experimental field base in SH and HN under well-watered paddy field conditions, as well as in controlled growth room conditions in Shanghai.

#### Over-expression of mEmBP-1a improves growth and photosynthesis performance of rice

Compared to WT, EmBPOE rice lines showed increased biomass, plant heights (Fig. 5), took 06-08 days longer to bolt and flower. Fig. 2C shows ~ 40 d (DAP) plants grown at SH (Long day conditions) and Supplementary Fig. S2 shows ~ 48 d plants at HN (Short day conditions). We further measured the photosynthetic parameters in these EmBPOE lines. We measured the responses of photosynthetic CO<sub>2</sub> uptake rate (A) to intercellular CO<sub>2</sub> concentrations (A-C<sub>i</sub> curve) and the response of A to PPFD (A-Q curve) for three EmBPOE lines, while for all other photosynthetic parameters, we have measured them for all four EmBPOE lines. The light saturated photosynthesis (A<sub>sat</sub>) in EmBPOE lines were up to 31 % higher ( $p \le 0.001$ ) compared to WT (Fig. 3A). There was a significant ( $p \le 0.01$ )

increase in stomatal conductance ( $g_s$ ) in EmBPOE rice lines compared to WT (Fig. 3B), while there was a significant ( $p \le 0.001$ ) decrease in the CO<sub>2</sub> concentration in the intercellular air space ( $C_i$ ) (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, the intrinsic water use (*i*WUE) efficiency was also found to be significantly ( $p \le 0.01$ ) increased (Fig. 3D). EmBPOE rice lines showed increased photosynthetic CO<sub>2</sub> uptake rates (A) under different light (Fig. 3E) and CO<sub>2</sub> levels (Fig. 3F). At a photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 2000 µmol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>, EmBPOE lines showed an increase of (A) by up to 30 % ( $p \le 0.001$ ) compared to WT (Fig. 3E). Experiments at HN showed up to ~ 28% increase in photosynthetic rates in EmBPOE lines than the WT (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Also, in controlled growth room conditions, EmBPOE rice plants showed up to 24 % increase in photosynthesis at 1200 µmol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> ( $A_{sat}$ ) and a 15 % increase at 400 µmol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> ( $A_{low}$ ) in EmBPOE lines over WT plants (Supplementary Fig. S5A, B). Transgenic rice plants expressing ubi-1 promoter only (control empty vector) were also tested in SH, HN and growth room conditions, no difference were found between WT and control empty vector transgenic rice line (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. S4, S5).

Across different CO<sub>2</sub> levels, the EmBPOE rice lines showed increased CO<sub>2</sub> uptake rates (Fig. 3F). The estimated RuBP and CO<sub>2</sub> saturated rate of Rubisco carboxylation ( $V_{cmax}$ ) and light saturated rate of electron transfer ( $J_{max}$ ) in EmBPOE lines were significantly higher than those of the WT. We found that there was about 25 % increase ( $p \le 0.01$ ) in  $V_{cmax}$  and 20 % increase ( $p \le 0.001$ ) in  $J_{max}$  in EmBPOE transgenic lines compared to WT (Fig. 3G, H).

## Photosynthetic pigment and quantum yield of PSII and PSI was increased in EmBPOE lines

For plants grown in SH (Shanghai, China) and HN (Sanya, China) under well-watered paddy field conditions, chlorophyll contents (Chl) were analysed in ~ 40 d (DAP) plants by using youngest fully expended leaves. The concentrations of chlorophyll contents (Chl) in EmBPOE plants were higher by 15 % and 13 % respectively than WT (Supplementary Fig. S3 and S4A). Furthermore, EmBPOE lines showed significantly increased ( $p \le 0.01$  and  $p \le 0.001$ ) electron transport rates around PSII and PSI respectively (Fig. 4A, B), together with significantly increased ( $p \le 0.001$ ) quantum yields of photochemical energy conversion of PSII (YII) and PSI (YI) (Fig. 4 C, D). The fraction of open PSII centers available for photochemistry (qL) was also significantly ( $p \le 0.01$ ) higher in EmBPOE lines, while the level of  $Q_a$  reduction (1-qP) was found to be significantly decreased which represents enhanced  $Q_a$  oxidation (Supplementary Fig. S6A, B). Therefore, over-expression of mEmBP-1 TF led to overall improved light absorption, increased oxidized status and increased electron transfer rates in PSII and PSI per unit leaf area.

#### Growth, biomass and yield is increased in the EmBPOE rice plants

Agronomic traits including plant height (cm), tiller number, above ground biomass and grain yield were measured in all four EmBPOE rice lines compared with WT at both the tiller stage (~ 45 day after planting, i.e. 45 DAP) and maturation stage (~ 95 DAP). We found an approximately 14 % increase in plant height, 16 % increase in tiller number and ~18 % increase in shoot dry biomass in EmBPOE transgenic lines at ~ 45 DAP compared to WT (Fig. 5A, B, C). At the maturation stage at SH, i.e. at ~ 95 DAP, we found 21.6 % increase in plant height, 19.5 % increase in tiller number and 22.6 % increase in the above ground shoot biomass in EmBPOE transgenic lines compared to WT (Fig. 5D, E, F). At HN, similar increases in plant height, tiller number and above ground shoot biomass were found in EmBPOE transgenic plants compared to WT (Supplementary Fig. S7A, B, C). At SH, EmBPOE transgenic lines showed up to ~29 % increase ( $p \le 0.001$ ) in the grain yield compared to the WT. The EmBPOE transgenic rice lines also showed increased numbers of panicles and spikelets per plant than WT (Table 1). Fig. 6 shows ~ 95 d (DAP) mature stage EmBPOE rice plants grown at SH. At HN, the EmBPOE transgenic rice lines also showed a significant ( $p \le 0.001$ ) ~ 26 % increase in grain yield over the WT (Supplementary Fig. S7D).

## The mEmBP1-a protein in the rice can regulate the expression of photosynthesis genes

To elucidate the molecular mechanism of how mEmBP-1a gene caused these observed changes in photosynthesis, biomass and grain yield, we first compared the transcriptomics data from the transgenic EmBPOE rice lines and WT (Fig. 7A, B, Supplementary Fig. S8A, Supplementary Table S4). Since the EmBPOE1, EmBPOE2, EmBPOE3 and EmBPOE4 lines show similar changes in photosynthesis, biomass and grain yield, as compared to WT, we chose EmBPOE4 as a representative line to conduct the transcriptomics analysis. Our results show that there were 7038 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between WT and EmBPOE line, including 53 upregulated and 4 downregulated photosynthesis related genes (Supplementary Table S4). Gene ontology enrichment analysis shows that the upregulated genes were enriched in photosynthesis, cellular nitrogen compound metabolic process, cellular aromatic compound metabolic process, response to chemical stimulus, and oxidation reduction pathways, with photosynthesis being the pathway showing the highest enrichment factor (Fig. 7A).

The DEGs related to photosynthesis include genes coding chlorophyll a/b-binding (Cab E, Lhca4, Lhcb1a, b, CP29, CP24 etc.), photosystem II (PsbR3, PsbW, PsbY etc), photosystem I reaction center subunits (PSI-F, PsaK, PsaN, PsaO etc), cytochrome *b6f* complex, chloroplastic ATP synthase subunit (delta chain, subunit b and gamma) and other electron transport components (Fd1, FdC2, LFNR1 and

PC) (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Table S4). Furthermore, many genes in the Calvin Benson cycle i.e. Fructose Bisphosphate Aldolase, Transketolase, Phosphoribulokinase, Glycerol aldehyde Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenease and Rubisco, showed up-regulation in EmBPOE transgenic lines compared to WT (Fig. 7B).

# The mEmBP-1a protein can directly activate the G-Box containing photosynthesis gene's promoters

About 53 genes related to photosynthesis were upregulated in EmBPOE lines compared to WT; among them, 15 genes have complete matching G-box motifs (GCCACGTGN; Supplementary Table S4-3) in their promoters and 9 genes have core motif of the G-Box (GCCACGTGGC) in their promoters. So, we performed an *in vitro* DNA electrophoretic mobility-shift assay (EMSA) to check mEmBP-1a protein's direct binding to the G-Box motifs in these photosynthesis genes. DNA fragments containing the G-Box motifs in the promoter region of six representative photosynthesis genes, including PsbR3 (PSII), RbcS3 (CBB cycle), FBA1 (CBB), Fd1 (ETR), PsaN (PSI) and CP29 (LHCII), were used as CY5 fluorescence labelled probes. Furthermore, the recombinant StrepII-mEmBP-1a protein was shown to bind with all the tested photosynthesis gene's promoter fragments (Fig. 8A-F).

## Discussion

This study demonstrates that over-expression of mEmBP-1 can result in significantly improved photosynthetic capacity per unit leaf area, which can be further translated into improved biomass production and rice yield in the field. The procedure used to identify mEmBP1 can be used as a generic approach to identify new options to improve photosynthetic capacity in crops. In this section, we discuss about the approach utilized in this study to identify new options to improve photosynthetic capacity in crops and also briefly comment on the potential of mEmBP-1 to improve photosynthesis.

The EmBP-1 was originally predicted as a major regulator of photosynthesis from our earlier bioinformatics analysis (Yu *et al.*, 2014). The bioinformatics analysis simultaneously considered two factors, i.e. the binding affinity between TF and its binding targets and also the relationship between the expression patterns of the TF and the candidate binding targets (Yu *et al.*, 2014). Considering that in this bioinformatics analysis, the position weight matrix of mEmBP-1 used to predict the TF binding to its targets was from maize, while the sequences of promoters used in the analysis are from Arabidopsis, which diverge from maize by at least >150 million years ago (Brendel *et al.*, 2002), it came out as a surprise that over-expression of mEmBP-1 indeed resulted in increased

photosynthetic capacity in rice (Fig. 3A, E). On one hand, this suggests that the function of mEmBP-1 is conserved among monocot species, as shown by the phylogenetic tree of EmBP-1 (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S1); on the other hand, this also shows that this bioinformatics pipeline combined with transgenic testing indeed can be used as an effective approach to identify new TFs controlling photosynthetic capacity in crops. In fact, with the improved number of ChIP-Seq data and position weight matrix now available for many crop species, such as rice, maize (Liseron-Monfils *et al.*, 2013; Feng *et al.*, 2018), it should be more effective to apply this approach to identify new TFs regulating photosynthetic capacity.

In this study, we show that over-expression of mEmBP-1a, a single TF factor, resulted in 30 % increase in leaf photosynthetic rate on a leaf area basis, which resulted in 22.6 % increase in biomass and 29 % increase in grain yield formation (Fig. 3, 5; Table 1). In the over-expression lines, a large number of genes in both the light reactions and also the Calvin Benson cycle were simultaneously up-regulated (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Table S4), which resulted in increased RuBP saturated rate of RuBISCO carboxylation, i.e. V<sub>cmax</sub>, and increased rate of electron transport rate, J<sub>max</sub> (Fig. 3G, H). These changes in photosynthesis was accompanied with an increased leaf chlorophyll content (Supplementary Fig. S3, S4A), which resulted in increased leaf light absorption, and increased quantum yields of PSII and PSI (Fig. 4C, D); all these together resulted in increased electron transfer rates at both PSII and PSI (Fig. 4A, B). All these suggest that the mEmBP-1 TF indeed is a major regulator of photosynthesis, resulting in an increase in overall photosynthetic properties in EmBPOE rice lines (Fig. 3, 4). So far, proteins and enzymes involved in photosynthesis has been studied for decades, essential components of photosynthesis are well characterized; many potential TFs regulating photosynthesis related genes are well characterized (Wang et al., 2017; Ambavaram et al., 2014, 2018; Zhou and Li, 2016). This study shows that the EmBP-1 is new important regulator of photosynthesis related genes.

It is interesting to note that the increased photosynthetic rate (*A*) in the EmBPOE lines is not due to an increased stomatal conductance ( $g_s$ ), since in these lines, though the gs was increased however the intercellular CO<sub>2</sub> levels ( $C_i$ ) was decreased (Fig. 3B, C). This is also shown in the *A*-*C*i curve where at the same *C*i the *A* in EmBPOE lines were typically higher (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, the initial slopes of the responses of electron transfer rates to PPFD are similar between EmBPOE lines and WT (Supplementary Table S3), which suggests that the EmBPOE influence the light reactions mainly through increased quantity of light reaction components, as shown by the increased expression of genes related to the light reactions (Fig. 7 B), rather than an increased efficiency of photosystems to absorb light and use it to drive electron transfer. We also calculated the intrinsic water use efficiency (*i*WUE), which is the ratio of *A/gs* (Farquhar and Sharkey 1982), and found that the EmBPOE lines show higher *i*WUE compared to WT (Fig. 3D). Field experiments are needed to confirm whether this increased *i*WUE can be translated into increased drought resistance in the field.

How does mEmBP-1 regulate photosynthesis gene expression? mEmBP-1 and its orthologs can bind to G-Box motif in the Em gene (Carlini *et al.*, 1999; Razik and Quatrano, 1997). Many studies reported the G-Box motif binding efficiencies of mEmBP-1a and wheat EmBP genes as well as analyze the affinity of binding to the variation in G-Box motif structures (Guiltinan and Miller, 1994; Niu and Guiltinan, 1994; Niu *et al.*, 1996; Razik and Quatrano, 1997; Schultz *et al.*, 1998; Kim and Guiltinan, 1999). Here we show that mEmBP-1 binds to the promoter regions of many photosynthesis related genes (Fig. 8). The over-expression of mEmBP-1a promotes the expression of these photosynthesis related genes (Supplementary Fig. 7B). It seems like that the EmBP-1 is a major regulator that promotes the growth-related process, such as photosynthesis, while repress the senescence and death related process (Fig. 7A).

Similar to this study, where we show that over-expression of one transcription factor leading to concurrently increased expression of many photosynthesis genes, ultimately leading to an increased photosynthetic capacity and increased biomass production and grain yields (Fig. 3, 4,5; Table 1), earlier, HYR and Myb106 were also shown to result in increased expression of many photosynthesis genes, translating into increased photosynthesis, biomass and yield production (Ambavaram *et al.*, 2014; Zhou and Li, 2016). These successes demonstrate that identifying and utilization of major or master regulators of photosynthesis genes is another effective approach to increase crop photosynthesis for greater productivity, in addition to the current efforts of identifying and engineering the limiting step of photosynthesis (Long *et al.*, 2015; Simkin *et al.*, 2015; Kromdijk *et al.*, 2016) or synthetic pathways to increase photosynthetic efficiency (South *et al.*, 2019; Shen *et al.*, 2019).
