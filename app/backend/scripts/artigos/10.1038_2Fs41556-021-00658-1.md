# The chromatin remodeler DDM1 prevents transposon mobility through deposition of histone variant H2A.W

Akihisa Osakabe<sup>1,5,8</sup>, Bhagyshree Jamge<sup>1,8</sup>, Elin Axelsson<sup>1</sup>, Sean A. Montgomery<sup>1</sup>, Svetlana Akimcheva<sup>1</sup>, Annika Luisa Kuehn<sup>1,6</sup>, Rahul Pisupati<sup>1</sup>, Zdravko J. Lorković<sup>1</sup>, Ramesh Yelagandula<sup>1,7</sup>, Tetsuji Kakutani<sup>2,3,4</sup> and Frédéric Berger<sup>1</sup>

Mobile transposable elements (TEs) not only participate in genome evolution but also threaten genome integrity. In healthy cells, TEs that encode all of the components that are necessary for their mobility are specifically silenced, yet the precise mechanism remains unknown. Here, we characterize the mechanism used by a conserved class of chromatin remodelers that prevent TE mobility. In the *Arabidopsis* chromatin remodeler DECREASE IN DNA METHYLATION 1 (DDM1), we identify two conserved binding domains for the histone variant H2A.W, which marks plant heterochromatin. DDM1 is necessary and sufficient for the deposition of H2A.W onto potentially mobile TEs, yet does not act on TE fragments or host protein-coding genes. DDM1-mediated H2A.W deposition changes the properties of chromatin, resulting in the silencing of TEs and, therefore, prevents their mobility. This distinct mechanism provides insights into the interplay between TEs and their host in the contexts of evolution and disease, and potentiates innovative strategies for targeted gene silencing.

ransposable elements (TEs) are mobile DNA sequences that self-propagate and comprise a substantial proportion of eukaryotic genomes<sup>1-3</sup>. TE mobilization, which is also known as transposition, carries regulatory elements that rewire gene regulatory networks<sup>4,5</sup>. As a result, transposition provides rich substrates for evolution<sup>6-9</sup>. At the scale of an individual organism, TE expression threatens genome stability due to their potentially disruptive genomic insertions, deletions and rearrangements<sup>10</sup>. Thus, specific mechanisms must limit transposition in healthy somatic cells, and deviation from this state leads to diseases<sup>11</sup>. In invertebrates, fungi and plants, non-coding RNAs target H3K9 methylation to form repressive heterochromatin at TEs<sup>1,2</sup>. In Drosophila, Piwi-interacting RNA (piRNAs) are small RNAs that are produced by long precursors expressed in the germline<sup>12-14</sup>. piRNAs drive the deposition of the heterochromatin mark H3K9me3 that recruits heterochromatin protein 1 (HP1), which compacts chromatin leading to transcriptional silencing<sup>15-21</sup>. The role of piRNAs is conserved across metazoans but is not general to all eukaryotes. Although an HP1 orthologue exists in plants, it has no impact on transposons<sup>15</sup>. Plant short interfering RNAs (siRNAs) produced by the RNA-dependent DNA methylation (RdDM) pathway guide the deposition of DNA methylation on transposons but, on its own, this pathway is ineffective at maintaining the silencing of transposon-coding regions and does not prevent transposition<sup>17-19</sup>. Furthermore, the loss of DNA methylation in mutants deprived of the maintenance DNA METHYLTRANSFERASE 1 (MET1) or the plant-specific methyltransferases CHROMOMETHYLASES 2 and 3 (CMT2 and CMT3) does not cause transposition<sup>22,23</sup>. Only the joint loss of the activity of several DNA methyltransferases causes a strong derepression of TEs and transposition<sup>22-24</sup>, but whether this effect is direct or indirect is unknown.

In flowering plants, single mutations in the SWI/SNF2 chromatin remodelling factor DECREASE IN DNA METHYLATION 1 (DDM1) are characterized by massive transposition events leading to genome instability<sup>22,25-31</sup>. Loss of DDM1 also causes a global reduction in DNA methylation and H3K9 methylation<sup>32,33</sup>, which was assumed to be responsible for the loss of TE silencing<sup>28,33,34</sup>. In plants, DNA methylation is deposited both in the CG context by the DNA methyltransferase MET1, and the non-CG context by the methyltransferases CMT2 and CMT3 and de novo methyltransferases from the RdDM pathway<sup>17</sup>. Most of the TEs that are upregulated in *ddm1* mutants are methylated in the non-CG context by CMT2, but not by the RdDM pathway<sup>35,36</sup>. Other pathways that are associated with the deposition of the heterochromatic modifications H3K9me1/2 and H3K27me1 do not impact silencing of TEs with an amplitude that is comparable to DDM1 (ref. <sup>24</sup>). These observations suggest that the mechanism of DDM1 does not rely directly on the feedback loops involving H3K9 methylation, non-coding RNAs and DNA methylation. This idea is also supported by the fact that the loss of the DDM1 orthologue in the fungus Neurospora crassa does not affect DNA methylation<sup>37</sup>. The mammalian DDM1 orthologues LSH and HELLS do silence transposons<sup>38</sup>, suggesting that DDM1 orthologues share a common mechanism. Similar to the loss of DDM1 in plants, the loss of LSH causes DNA demethylation<sup>39,40</sup>, but recent evidence shows that this effect is indirect<sup>41</sup>. In this Article, we focus on the relationship between DDM1 and the histone variant H2A.W that evolved in vascular plants<sup>43,44</sup>. Specific residues in the L1 loop and a long C-terminal tail with a KSPK motif distinguish H2A.W from the other H2A variants and confer specific properties to the nucleosome<sup>43,45</sup>. H2A.W is confined to heterochromatin, but evidence of the mechanisms that are responsible for its deposition is lacking. Here we show that DDM1 binds to and deposits H2A.W into heterochromatin and, through the deposition of H2A.W, DDM1 participates in the silencing of TE protein-coding regions that are responsible for mobility.

### Results

**Conserved domains of DDM1 bind to the histone variant H2A.W.** DDM1 is proposed to regulate the dynamics of linker histone H1, which is enriched in constitutive heterochromatin<sup>36,42</sup>, and we considered that DDM1 could control its deposition. Other chromatin remodelers that are closely related to DDM1, such as SWR1 and INO80, control H2A variant dynamics<sup>46,47</sup>, leading us to consider a possible link between DDM1 and another major component of heterochromatin, the histone variant H2A.W44. To test whether DDM1 binds either to H1 or H2A.W, we expressed and purified DDM1 as a recombinant protein (Extended Data Fig. 1a). To validate DDM1 activity in vitro, we showed that purified DDM1 has DNA-dependent ATPase activity (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c), as reported previously48. This activity was abolished by substitution of one amino acid, which was predicted to be essential for catalytic activity on the basis of structural homology with the DEAD-box RNA helicase Vasa<sup>49</sup> (Extended Data Fig. 1c-e). Thus, we identified the conserved catalytic site of DDM1 and obtained active recombinant DDM1, which could be used to investigate whether DDM1 has histone-binding activity.

To identify the sites at which DDM1 mediates the deposition of H2A.W, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled with DNA sequencing (ChIP–seq) and compared the profiles of H2A.W with H2A.X and H1 in *ddm1* mutants and WT plants. The loss of DDM1 caused the expression of about 40% of all TE genes, (1,506 out of 3,903; Fig. 2d), a result that is comparable to previous reports<sup>19</sup>. We focused our study on TEs with protein-coding potential as defined in TAIR10. Hereafter, we refer to them collectively as TEs and distinguish them from TE fragments, which are remnants of coding and non-coding regions of transposons that are unable to gain mobility on their own.

The loss of H2A.W in *ddm1* mutants was more accentuated on TEs than on TE fragments (Fig. 2c,e, effect size (Cohen's d) was -0.278 for TE fragments over protein-coding genes versus -1.288 for TEs over protein-coding genes; Extended Data Fig. 3b,c). This observation suggested that DDM1 mediates H2A.W deposition preferentially on the coding regions of transposons. We assessed how widespread this mechanism is and found that 83% of all TEs were enriched with H2A.W in the WT (3,257 out of 3,903 TEs; cluster group 1 in Fig. 3a,b) and, more specifically, in pericentromeric regions (Extended Data Fig. 3d). A small group of TEs that were not covered with H2A.W in the WT (646 out of 3,903 TEs; cluster group 2 in Fig. 3a,b) were mainly distributed outside of pericentric heterochromatin (Extended Data Fig. 3d) and were not upregulated in *ddm1* mutants (Fig. 3b,c). All TEs from group 1 lost H2A.W in *ddm1* mutants (Fig. 3a), leading to the conclusion that DDM1 is necessary for the deposition of H2A.W on the majority of TEs.

### **Discussion**

In conclusion, the *Arabidopsis* chromatin remodeler DDM1 is necessary and sufficient to inactivate potentially mobile TEs. We identified two broadly conserved domains of DDM1 that bind to H2A.W. These domains are required for the deposition of H2A.W and to initiate and maintain transcriptional silencing of genes that enable transposon mobility. DDM1-mediated remodelling of nucleosomes prevents the acquisition of chromatin marks that are normally found on protein-coding genes. Thus, DDM1 promotes distinct chromatin properties opposed to transcriptional activity at coding regions of active transposons.

Although DDM1 is required for the maintenance of DNA methylation and H3K9 methylation, the mechanism involved remains unclear. The linker histone H1 ensures sufficient chromatin accessibility for the action of the DNA methyltransferase[s42,](#page-9-18) but we report that DDM1 does not bind to H1 and has no major impact on H1 deposition. It is therefore improbable that H1 is directly involved in the loss of DNA methylation in *ddm1*. An alternative mechanism might involve the chromatin environment enriched in H3K27me3 that takes the place of H2A.W/H3K9me2 in *ddm1*, as suggested by the antagonism between H3K27me3 and DNA methylation[54](#page-9-31),[57](#page-9-34).

In mammals, the DDM1 orthologues LSH and HELLS[58](#page-9-35) also maintain silencing of a large fraction of TEs in somatic cell[s38.](#page-9-14) The conservation of the H2A.W-binding regions between the DHL remodelers DDM1 and LSH/HELLS (Fig. [1e\)](#page-1-0) and a recent report that links deposition of macroH2A to LSH[59](#page-9-36) suggests that the mechanism of H2A variant deposition described in this study also pertains to the deposition of heterochromatic macroH2A variants[60](#page-9-37) by DDM1 orthologues in mammals. However, in mice, the loss of macroH2A deposition in *lsh* mutants also affects gene repressio[n59,](#page-9-36) suggesting that DDM1 orthologues have roles that are more diverse than suppressing TE transposition. Uncovering the molecular mechanism of this special class of chromatin remodelers will yield a clearer understanding of how chromatin dynamics and genome evolution are governed. Mobility of transposons and endogenous retroviruses threatens genome integrity and is associated with diseases[11,](#page-8-7) and we anticipate that the mechanism uncovered in this study will provide the foundation to study health issues associated with mobile elements and find innovative strategies to silence genes.

### **Methods**

**Plant material.** Te WT *Arabidopsis thaliana* plants used were accession Col-0. Homozygous frst-generation *ddm1-2* mutant plants were obtained from the self-pollinated progeny of the heterozygote, and F1 *DDM1/ddm1* plants were obtained from crosses between WT and frst-generation homozygous *ddm1-2* mutants. We also used *met1-3* frst-generation plants from *met1-3*/+ and *suvh4*/*5*/*6* triple mutants (SALK\_041474, Gabi\_263C05, SAIL\_1244\_F04).

Seeds were sown on soil and then stratified at 4 °C in the dark for 3d. Plants were grown in the growth chamber under long-day conditions (21 °C; 16h light–8h dark). Genotyping for *ddm1-2* was performed using the cleaved amplified polymorphic sequence method with the primers listed in Supplementary Table 2. This was followed by RsaI (Thermo Fisher Scientific) restriction enzyme digestion.

**DNA methylation analysis.** Genomic DNA was purified using the DNeasy plant mini kit (Qiagen) from 4–5-week-old leaves according to the manufacturer's instructions. After purification of genomic DNA, genomic DNA (200ng) was sheared by sonication using a Covaris S2 Focused Ultrasonicator for 45 s at 4 °C (duty cycle, 10%; intensity, 5.0; cycles per burst, 200) in 55 µl Covaris microTUBEs (AFA Fiber Snap-Cap). After sonication, end repair/dA-tailing, adaptor ligation, oxidation of 5mC and 5hmC, deamination and library preparation were performed using the NEBNext Enzymatic Methyl-seq kit (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Libraries were sequenced using Illumina Novaseq SP to generate paired-end 250bp reads. The samples were prepared from two independent biological replicates. The sequenced EM-seq treated reads were mapped to the TAIR 10 assembly ([https://www.arabidopsis.org/index.jsp\)](https://www.arabidopsis.org/index.jsp) along with chloroplast genome using methylseq, a Nextflow pipeline ([https://github.com/](https://github.com/nf-core/methylseq) [nf-core/methylseq](https://github.com/nf-core/methylseq)). Conversion rates were calculated on the basis of methylation levels present on reads mapped to the chloroplast genome using methylpy[62,](#page-12-0)[63](#page-12-1). We calculated weighted methylation levels across protein-coding genes, TE fragments and TE genes, hereafter referred to as TEs annotated from TAIR 10 using custom python scripts.

**Purification of recombinant** *Arabidopsis* **DDM1.** The DNA fragment encoding the *Arabidopsis DDM1* coding sequence was cloned into a pET-15b vector (Novagen). Freshly transformed *Escherichia coli* strain BL21 (DE3) containing minor tRNA expression vector (Codon (+) RIL, Stratagene) was grown on LB plates containing ampicillin (50µgml<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ) at 37 °C for 16h. After incubation, a single colony was inoculated into LB medium containing ampicillin (50µgml<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ) and cultured at 25 °C. When cell density reached an absorbance at 600nm (*A*600) of 0.4, 0.5mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside was added to induce DDM1 expression and cultured cells were incubated at 25 °C for an additional 12h. Cells expressing DDM1 were collected and lysed with sonication in buffer A (50mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 10% glycerol, 1mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 0.05% IGEPAL CA-630 (Sigma-Aldrich) and 2mM 2-mercaptoethanol) containing 500mM NaCl. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation and the lysate was mixed with Ni-NTA agarose resin (Qiagen) at 4 °C for 1h. After binding, the resin was washed with buffer B (50mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 500mM NaCl, 10% glycerol and 2mM 2-mercaptoethanol) containing 5mM imidazole. The His6-tagged DDM1 protein was eluted by addition of buffer B containing 300mM imidazole. The His6 tag was cleaved from the DDM1 fragment by the addition of thrombin protease (15 Umg<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> of protein; GE Healthcare) and then immediately dialysed against buffer C (20mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 10% glycerol and 2mM 2-mercaptoethanol) containing 150mM NaCl. After removing the His6 tag, DDM1 was processed using the RESOURCE Q anion exchange column (GE Healthcare) and proteins were collected from the flow-through fractions. The DDM1 protein solution was diluted with buffer C to a final concentration of 50mM NaCl and then processed using the RESOURCE S cation exchange column (GE Healthcare) and purified by elution with a linear gradient of 50–300mM NaCl in buffer C. The peak fractions containing DDM1 were collected, and DDM1 was further purified by Superdex 200 (GE Healthcare) size-exclusion column chromatography with buffer C containing 50mM NaCl. Purified DDM1 was collected and concentrated using the Amicon Ultra 30K filter (Millipore) with buffer exchange into buffer C containing 150mM NaCl. The vector encoding the DDM1E334N mutant was generated by site-directed mutagenesis using the Q5 Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (New England Biolabs) and the DDM1E334N protein was purified as described above.

DNA fragments encoding DDM1 truncations, DDM1(1–440) or DDM1 (441–764), were cloned into pET-15b. His6–DDM1(1–440) and His6–DDM1 (441–764) were purified as described above, with the exclusion of the His6 tag cleavage step. However, His6–DDM1(441–764) was collected in the flow-through fractions from the RESOURCE S cation exchange column.

DNA fragments encoding the DDM1 truncations, DDM1(1–24), DDM1 (1–55), DDM1(1–74), DDM1(1–99), DDM1(1–123), DDM1(1–152), DDM1 (1–172), DDM1(1–196), DDM1(25–123), DDM1(56–123), DDM1(75–123), DDM1(100–123), DDM1(639–764), DDM1(674–764), DDM1(700–764) and DDM1(731–764) were inserted into the EcoRI and XhoI sites of pGEX-4T-1 (GE Healthcare). Expression of DDM1 truncations was performed as described above. Cells expressing DDM1 truncations were collected and lysed by sonication in buffer D (20mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 1M NaCl, 0.2mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100 and 1mM dithiothreitol). Cell debris was removed by centrifugation and the lysate was mixed with glutathione Sepharose 4B resin (GE Healthcare) at 4 °C for 1h. After binding, resin was washed with buffer D, rinsed with 20mM Tris-HCl pH8.0 and then GST-tagged DDM1 truncations were eluted by buffer E (20mM Tris-HCl pH8.0 and 20mM glutathione).
