### 32 **ABSTRACT**

33 RNA splicing and spliceosome assembly in eukaryotes occur mainly during transcription. 34 However, co-transcriptional splicing has not yet been explored in plants. Here, we built nascent 35 transcriptomes of chromatin RNAs in *Arabidopsis thaliana* and showed that, nearly all introns 36 undergo co-transcriptional splicing, and this occurs with higher efficiency for introns in protein-37 coding genes than for noncoding RNAs. Total intron number and intron position are two 38 predominant features that correlate with co-transcriptional splicing efficiency, and introns with 39 alternative 5′ or 3′ splice sites are less efficiently spliced. Furthermore, we found that *trans*-40 acting protein mutations lead to more introns with increased splicing defects in nascent RNAs 41 than in mature RNAs, and that introns with increased splicing defects in mature RNAs are 42 inefficiently spliced at the co-transcriptional level. Our results not only uncovered widespread 43 co-transcriptional splicing in *Arabidopsis*, but also identified features that may affect or be 44 affected by co-transcriptional splicing efficiency.

#### 46 **INTRODUCTION**

47 RNA splicing, the central processing step in eukaryotic RNA maturation that defines the identity 48 and function of RNAs, plays a pivotal role in regulating development, cell differentiation, and 49 responses to the environment. The recognition of *cis-*acting elements in pre-mRNA sequences by 50 *trans*-acting splicing factors is crucial to spliceosome assembly, and this concept formed the 51 foundation for the study of splicing regulation and the deciphering of the splicing code 52 (Jaganathan et al., 2019; Will and Luhrmann, 2011; Xiong et al., 2014). However, the splicing 53 code based on RNA sequence features accounts only partially for the observed patterns of 54 alternative splicing (Reddy, 2007), indicating that additional splicing regulation factors exist.

55 Indeed, a growing body of studies have reported functions of chromatin structures and 56 histone modifications in alternative splicing, including the coupling of transcription with splicing 57 (Bauren and Wieslander, 1994; Bentley, 2014; Beyer and Osheim, 1988), the exonic enrichment 58 of several epigenetic modifications (Li et al., 2015; Luco et al., 2011; Schwartz et al., 2009; 59 Spies et al., 2009), and regulation of H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 in alternative splicing (Guo et al., 60 2014; Luco et al., 2011; Luco et al., 2010). The epigenetic regulation on splicing in principle 61 requires splicing to be occurred on chromatin-associated transcripts. Profiling of biochemically 62 fractionated chromatin-associated nascent transcriptome in model organisms such as yeast, 63 insects, and mammalian cells has led to a general agreement that splicing occurs predominantly 64 at the co-transcriptional level (Carrillo Oesterreich et al., 2010; Khodor et al., 2012; Khodor et al., 65 2011; Tilgner et al., 2012). Depleted intron reads observed from GRO-seq (Global nuclear run-66 on sequencing) indicate the existence of co-transcriptional splicing in *Arabidopsis* (Hetzel et al., 67 2016), nevertheless, no global studies on co-transcriptional splicing have been reported in plants. 68 Although RNA splicing is a conserved pre-mRNA processing event in eukaryotes, there are 69 many plant-specific features of this process, including a low proportion of genes undergoing 70 alternative splicing, a high level of intron retention and low level of exon skipping, a small 71 number of introns per gene, long exons and short introns, splice site recognition occurring 72 predominantly by intron definition, and the presence of a large number of SR proteins (a family 73 of conserved splicing factors) (Reddy, 2007).

74 Spliceosome assembly involves numerous regulatory *trans*-acting protein factors. The 75 MOS4-associated complex (MAC) in *Arabidopsis thaliana* (containing MAC7, MAC3A, 76 MAC3B, PRL1, PRL2, and other factors), also known as the Prp19 complex (Prp19C) in yeast

77 or NineTeen Complex (NTC) in humans, is a conserved complex associated with the 78 spliceosome and is involved in both steps of intron removal (Hogg et al., 2010; Jia et al., 2017). 79 Global splicing defects are also observed for *pp4r3a-1* and *pp4r3a-2*, two mutants in the gene 80 encoding the regulatory subunit of one form of the PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 4 (PP4) complex, 81 which is a conserved serine/threonine-specific phosphoprotein phosphatase (Wang et al., 2019). 82 SNW/Ski-interacting protein (SKIP), a splicing factor and component of the spliceosome, links 83 alternative splicing with both the circadian clock and salt tolerance (Feng et al., 2015; Wang et 84 al., 2012). PRMT5 is a conserved type II protein arginine methyltransferase that methylates core 85 spliceosome proteins to regulate pre-mRNA splicing (Bezzi et al., 2013; Deng et al., 2010; 86 Hernando et al., 2015). The RNA recognition motif (RRM)-containing protein RRM25, which 87 copurifies with intact spliceosomes, is a putative global splicing factor (Carlson et al., 2017; 88 Zhan et al., 2015). Polypyrimidine tract binding protein homologs (PTBs) are RNA-binding 89 proteins that regulate splicing through the nonsense-mediated-decay RNA surveillance pathway 90 (Ruhl et al., 2012). Although global regulation of RNA splicing by these proteins has been 91 revealed through mRNA-seq, which examines the splicing outcome at the mature mRNA level, 92 knowledge of the connection between the splicing outcome at the mature RNA level and co-93 transcriptional splicing remains limited.

94 In this study, we performed directional RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) with chromatin 95 RNAs to profile the nascent transcriptomes and uncovered global co-transcriptional splicing in 96 *Arabidopsis*. We also discovered inefficient co-transcriptional splicing at introns close to 97 transcription end sites or introns in genes with fewer introns or alternative introns. Utilizing 98 public mature transcriptomes and nascent transcriptomes from this study, we discovered that co-99 transcriptional splicing efficiency negatively correlates with the degree of splicing defects caused 100 by *trans*-acting protein mutations in mature RNAs, but not nascent RNAs.

### 102 **RESULTS**

#### 103 **Profiling of nascent transcriptomes in** *Arabidopsis*

104 To capture nascent transcripts, we first isolated the chromatin fraction from *Arabidopsis*. Nuclei 105 were separated from cytoplasm using a well-established protocol (Wang et al., 2011). The 106 nuclear pellets were treated with urea and nonionic detergents to remove loosely associated 107 RNAs and proteins without disrupting ternary Pol II complexes (Pandya-Jones and Black, 2009).

108 The chromatin fraction was precipitated, with the supernatant being the nucleoplasmic fraction. 109 We evaluated the purity of the fractions by western blotting using antibodies against GAPDH, 110 SERRATE, and histone H4 (Figure 1A). Histone H4, a chromatin protein, was present only in 111 the chromatin fraction, and GAPDH, a cytoplasmic protein (Zaffagnini et al., 2013), was found 112 only in the cytoplasmic fraction, whereas the nuclear speckle protein SERRATE (Reddy et al., 113 2012) was highly abundant in the nucleoplasm and was found in trace amounts in the chromatin 114 and cytoplasmic fractions. These data confirmed that the fractionation process was effective. We 115 further evaluated the distribution of unspliced RNAs in these cell fractions. We carried out RT-116 qPCR to amplify both unspliced and spliced RNAs using primer pairs spanning either exon-exon 117 or exon-intron at four introns and quantified levels of unspliced RNAs normalized by levels of 118 spliced RNAs (Supplemental Table 1). We observed a higher level of unspliced RNAs in 119 chromatin when compared to levels in total cells, cytosol and nucleoplasm (Figure 1B). The 120 highest level of unspliced RNAs in chromatin indicates the existence of co-transcriptional 121 splicing in *Arabidopsis*.

122 Next, we extracted RNAs from both the chromatin fraction and unfractionated samples 123 and subjected them to rRNA depletion followed by RNA-seq library construction that preserves 124 the strandedness of RNAs. Three separate experiments gave highly reproducible results 125 (Supplemental Figure 1A). It is expected that, as compared to the total RNAs, nascent RNAs 126 might contain higher levels of unspliced introns and might display 5′ to 3′ decreasing read 127 intensity for long transcripts (Carrillo Oesterreich et al., 2010). We calculated the percentage of 128 intron reads for every non-overlapping gene and found that the percentage was significantly 129 higher in chromatin RNAs than in total RNAs (Figure 1C−D). In addition, we observed a pattern 130 of 5′ to 3′ decreasing read density for long transcripts, but not for all transcripts (Figure 1E−F).

131 Noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) act as pivotal regulators of genome structure and gene 132 expression through their interaction with chromatin-modifying enzymes and nucleosome-133 remodeling factors (Saxena and Carninci, 2011), so it is expected that ncRNAs should be 134 depleted from cytosol RNAs and enriched in chromatin RNAs. Araport 11 annotation defines 135 several types of ncRNAs, including long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), antisense lncRNAs, 136 pseudogene transcripts, and RNAs from transposable elements (TEs). We examined the 137 abundance of these categories of RNAs separately and found that their enrichment in the 138 chromatin fraction was significantly higher than that in the total RNAs, consistent with their 139 regulatory roles on chromatin (Figure 1G−H).

#### 141 **Global co-transcriptional splicing in** *Arabidopsis*

142 The level of retained introns is an indicator of intron splicing efficiency, so we calculated the 143 level of retained introns as the percentage of introns (PI; the higher the PI value, the lower the 144 intron splicing efficiency) using a web-based pipeline called SQUID. SQUID provides two ways 145 of calculating PI: PI\_Junction based on intron-inclusion and intron-skipping reads, and 146 PI\_Density based on reads at intronic regions and reads at exonic regions 147 (https://github.com/Xinglab/SQUID) (Figure 2A and Methods). Since calculation of intron-148 inclusion and intron-skipping reads is skewed for alternative introns and PI\_Junction performed 149 better than PI\_Density for constitutive introns (Figure S2A−B and Methods), we calculated PI as 150 PI\_Junction for constitutive introns and as PI\_Density for alternative introns.

151 Since 85% of introns from Araport 11 are constitutive introns, we calculated PI\_Junction 152 from chromatin RNA-seq and total RNA-seq. A total of 19,737 constitutive introns were 153 interrogated (Supplemental Table 2). As expected, the levels of retained introns are much higher 154 in chromatin RNAs than in total RNAs (Figure 2B). The facts that the median PI in total RNAs 155 is 0.02 and that PI is lower than 0.2 for most introns in total RNAs are indicative of relatively 156 complete intron removal in total RNAs. By contrast, the facts that the median PI in chromatin 157 RNAs is 0.18, and that PI is lower than 0.8 for most introns in chromatin RNAs, indicate that 158 there is genome-wide co-transcriptional splicing in *Arabidopsis*, which matches the patterns 159 previously reported in yeast (Carrillo Oesterreich et al., 2010), fly (Khodor et al., 2011), mouse 160 (Bhatt et al., 2012), and human cells (Tilgner et al., 2012). The positive correlation between PI in 161 chromatin and in total RNAs suggests that the efficiency of co-transcriptional splicing may 162 positively impact the final splicing outcome (Figure 2C). Inefficient and delayed intron splicing 163 of lncRNAs has been reported in human cells (Tilgner et al., 2012). In our study, higher PI both 164 in chromatin and in total RNAs was also observed for introns from ncRNAs, indicative of similar 165 inefficient splicing of ncRNAs in *Arabidopsis* (Figure 2D).

## 167 **Inefficient splicing of alternative introns with alternative 5′ or 3′ splice sites**

168 The splicing of constitutive introns is more co-transcriptional than that of alternative introns 169 (Brugiolo et al., 2013). To examine the effects of alternative splicing in *Arabidopsis*, we used 170 PI\_Density to calculate the splicing efficiency of alternative and constitutive introns. To 171 eliminate the bias caused by overlapping exons, we excluded alternative introns that overlapped 172 with exons from the analysis (Figure 3A and Supplemental Table 3). In *Arabidopsis*, there are 173 three major types of alternative introns: introns with alternative 5′ splicing sites (A5SS), introns 174 with alternative 3′ splicing sites (A3SS), and introns with exon skipping (ES) (Figure 3A−B). 175 The proportion of introns with ES is much lower than those of introns with A5SS or A3SS 176 (Keren et al., 2010). Consistent with previous reports (Brugiolo et al., 2013), the retained intron 177 level was higher for alternative introns than for constitutive introns in both chromatin RNAs and 178 total RNAs (Figure 3C). We then calculated PI for each type of alternative introns and observed 179 higher PI for introns with A5SS and A3SS compared to those for constitutive introns in both 180 chromatin and total RNAs, while for introns with ES, we observed significantly higher PI only in 181 total RNAs but not in chromatin RNAs (Figure 3C). To eliminate the potential effects caused by 182 different sets of genes, we analyzed constitutive introns and alternative introns residing in the 183 same set of genes and confirmed that the pattern was similar to that seen when all introns were 184 analyzed (Supplemental Figure 3). These results indicate that introns with alternative 5′ or 3′ 185 splice sites are less efficiently spliced than constitutive introns.

#### 187 **Predominant roles of total intron number and intron position in co-transcriptional splicing**

188 Features of introns, flanking exons, and intron-harboring genes may affect splicing, so we 189 calculated Pearson correlation coefficients between PI and these features. Among all of the 190 features tested, total intron number and gene length of intron-harboring genes showed the highest 191 correlations with PI in chromatin RNAs (Figure 4A and Supplemental Figure 4A−C). Since total 192 intron number correlates tightly with gene length (Supplemental Figure 4D), we attempted to 193 differentiate the influence of gene length and total intron number on co-transcriptional splicing. 194 We divided all tested introns into 10 deciles according to increasing PI in chromatin RNAs 195 (grouping 1), increasing total intron number (grouping 2), and increasing gene length (grouping 196 3) (Supplemental Figure 5). Correlations between total intron number and gene length were 197 calculated for grouping 1, correlations between gene length and PI in chromatin RNAs for 198 grouping 2, and correlations between total intron number and PI in chromatin RNAs for

199 grouping 3. The correlations between total intron number and gene length were not affected by 200 PI in chromatin RNAs (grouping 1), and correlations between total intron number and PI in 201 chromatin RNAs were not affected by gene length (grouping 3), while correlations between gene 202 length and PI in chromatin RNAs diminished when total intron number was in a narrow range 203 (grouping 2) (Figure 4B). This suggested that total intron number, but not gene length, plays a 204 major role in determining co-transcriptional splicing efficiency.

205 Co-transcriptional splicing efficiency is reported to decrease from 5′ to 3′ along genes: 206 i.e., introns close to transcription start sites (TSSs) are more efficiently spliced than introns close 207 to transcription termination sites (TTSs) (Bentley, 2014; Tilgner et al., 2012). We also found that 208 PI in chromatin RNAs negatively correlates with both intron number from TTSs (INT) and 209 distance to TTSs (Figure 4A). Since INT also correlates with total intron number, we further 210 differentiated the roles of total intron number and INT. First, we eliminated the effects of total 211 intron number to examine the role of INT. When we examined the PI in chromatin RNAs of 212 introns in genes with two or three introns, we found that PI in chromatin RNAs increases from 213 the first introns to the last introns (Supplemental Figure 4E−F). We then divided introns in genes 214 with more than three introns into four quartiles, Q1−Q4, with decreasing INT, and we found that 215 PI in chromatin RNAs increases from Q1 to Q4 (Figure 4C). The decreasing co-transcriptional 216 splicing efficiency along genes validated the role of INT, which is consistent with the previously 217 proposed "first come, first served model" of splicing (Bentley, 2014). Next, we eliminated the 218 effects of INT to examine the role of total intron number. We selected two groups of introns by 219 restricting INT (in G1) or total intron number (in G2) to a certain range. Since INT is also 220 limited by total intron number, G2 is a subset of G1 with lower total intron number. If total 221 intron number plays a role in co-transcriptional splicing efficiency, PI in chromatin RNAs should 222 be higher in G2 than in G1. As expected, PI in chromatin RNAs was higher in G2 than G1 223 (Figure 4D), which supports a role of total intron number. Since both total intron number and 224 INT correlate with PI in chromatin RNAs, we further compared their effects by calculating the 225 Pearson correlation coefficients for groups of introns with different minimal total intron numbers. 226 The difference between the Pearson correlation coefficients of INT and total intron number with 227 PI in chromatin RNAs increases with increasing total intron number (Figure 4E). The results 228 suggest that total intron number plays a dominant role for introns in genes with a small total

229 intron number, whereas intron position relative to transcription termination sites plays a 230 dominant role for introns in genes with a large total intron number.

231 In addition, we observed positive correlations between PI in chromatin RNAs and the 232 lengths of introns and flanking exons. Since length of introns and flanking exons is negatively 233 correlated with total intron number (Supplemental Figure 4G), we carried out a test similar to the 234 one done for gene length to differentiate their roles, by dividing all introns on the basis of PI in 235 chromatin RNAs (grouping1), total intron number (grouping 2), and length of introns or flanking 236 exons (grouping 3). Similar to what we found for gene length, we did not observe correlations 237 between PI in chromatin RNAs and length of introns and flanking exons in grouping 2 238 (Supplemental Figure 6), suggesting that it is total intron number, and not the length of introns 239 and flanking exons, that plays a major role in determining co-transcriptional splicing efficiency. 240 In addition, since ncRNAs tend to have fewer introns (Supplemental Figure 7A), we tested 241 whether higher PI in ncRNAs is due to low total intron number. We compared PI of ncRNAs and 242 genes with equal ranges of total intron number, and we still observed higher PI in both total and 243 chromatin RNAs for introns in ncRNAs (Supplemental Figure 7B−C), which suggests that other 244 factors lead to inefficiency of intron splicing of ncRNAs.

245 Moreover, we observed a weak negative correlation between splice junction strength and 246 PI in total and chromatin RNAs, which is consistent with the fact that splice junction strength 247 based on maximum entropy is a commonly used basis for predicting 5′ and 3′ splice sites (Yeo 248 and Burge, 2004) (Supplemental Figure 4A). The weak positive correlations between PI in total 249 and chromatin RNAs and intron GC content, and weak negative correlations between PI in total 250 and chromatin RNAs and exon GC content, may be explained by the influence of GC content on 251 pre-mRNA secondary structure (Supplemental Figure 4A) (Zhang et al., 2011). We also 252 observed weak negative correlations both between gene expression level and PI in chromatin 253 RNAs and between gene expression level and PI in total RNAs (Supplemental Figure 4A).

## 255 **PI in chromatin RNAs correlates with the degree of splicing defects caused by mutations in** 256 *trans***-acting proteins**

257 Spliceosome assembly at pre-mRNAs depends on the recognition of *cis*-elements in pre-mRNAs 258 by *trans*-acting proteins. Here, we sought to explore the connection between co-transcriptional 259 splicing and the action of these *trans*-acting proteins. As a first step, we identified introns that are

260 regulated by *trans*-acting proteins by analyzing splicing defects in the corresponding mutants 261 from public mRNA-seq data. We selected four mutants for which public mRNA-seq datasets 262 were available (Supplemental Table 4): *mac3a mac3b* (MAC3A and MAC3B are two 263 homologous MAC proteins with E3 ligase activity in vitro) (Jia et al., 2017), *prl1 prl2* (PRL1 264 and PRL2 are two homologous nuclear WD-40 proteins in MAC) (Jia et al., 2017), *pp4r3a*  265 (PP4R3A is a conserved serine/threonine-specific phosphoprotein phosphatase) (Wang et al., 266 2019), and *skip* (SKIP is a splicing factor and component of the spliceosome) (Wang et al., 2012). 267 Global splicing defects were found in these mutants, and differentially spliced introns (DSIs) 268 were identified using SQUID (Supplemental Figure 8). Since these four *trans*-acting proteins 269 with transcriptome data are splicing activators, we used only DSIs with increased PI in this study. 270 To quantify the degree of splicing defects at these DSIs, we also calculated delta\_PI as PI in 271 mutants minus PI in wild type, and used delta\_PI as an indicator of splicing defects: the higher 272 the delta\_PI, the higher the splicing defects.

273 Having identified the DSIs regulated by *trans*-acting proteins, the next step was to 274 examine the PI of these DSIs in the nascent transcriptomes we had generated in this study. We 275 found that these DSIs had a significantly higher PI in chromatin RNAs compared to all introns 276 (Figure 5A), suggesting that the introns regulated by these *trans*-acting proteins tend to have 277 lower efficiency of co-transcriptional splicing. Consistently with this, we found that these DSIs 278 have both lower total intron number and lower INT compared to all introns (Figure 5B−C). 279 Having shown that the DSIs tend to be poorly spliced at the co-transcriptional level, we next 280 sought to determine whether the splicing defects in the mutants at the DSIs correlated with the 281 efficiency of co-transcriptional splicing. We performed Pearson correlation analysis between 282 delta\_PI with PI in chromatin RNAs, total intron number, and INT. We discovered that PI in 283 chromatin RNAs positively correlates with delta\_PI (Figure 5D), whereas such correlations were 284 not found for total intron number or INT. This suggests that PI in chromatin RNAs is the 285 predominant factor that correlates with the splicing regulation by *trans*-acting proteins. We also 286 extended our analysis to DSIs regulated by more *trans*-acting proteins and DSIs responsive to 287 environmental stress using other public datasets (Supplemental Table 4), and found that these 288 DSIs always have higher PI in chromatin RNAs, but not necessarily lower total intron number 289 and INTs (Supplemental Figure 9), which is consistent with the dominant role of PI in chromatin 290 RNAs in necessitating regulation by *trans*-acting factors or environmental conditions.

291 One potential problem in the analyses above is that the public RNA-seq datasets were 292 from different tissues or plants grown under different conditions as compared to the samples 293 used in our chromatin RNA-seq. To evaluate whether RNA-seq from different samples gives 294 different splicing efficiencies of introns, we determined the splicing efficiency of wild-type 295 transcriptomes from different datasets. We found that the level of splicing of constitutive introns 296 in wild-type transcriptomes was comparable despite differences in growth conditions or tissue 297 types or stages (Supplemental Figure 10 and Supplemental Table 4). Thus, we focused only on 298 constitutive introns in the identifications of DSIs in the analyses above.

#### 300 **MAC mutations lead to different splicing defects in nascent versus mature RNAs**

301 We further explored the splicing regulation of *trans*-acting proteins by constructing libraries with 302 two biological replicates of rRNA-depleted nascent chromatin RNAs and poly-A mature RNAs 303 from two MAC double mutants: *mac3a mac3b* and *prl1 prl2* (Supplemental Figure 1B). We 304 found that levels of unspliced introns in the mutants were higher in both nascent and mature 305 RNAs, and that the splicing defects were more severe in nascent RNAs than in mature RNAs 306 (Figure 6A, Supplemental Table 5). To further examine the different splicing defects in nascent 307 and mature RNAs, we identified DSIs regulated by MAC3A and MAC3B or PRL1 and PRL2 308 from both nascent and mature RNAs. When we quantified the splicing defects for these DSIs, we 309 found that the degree of defects was similar in nascent and mature RNAs for DSIs identified 310 from mature RNAs, but was significantly lower in mature RNAs than in nascent RNAs for DSIs 311 identified from nascent RNAs (Figure 6B−C). When we examined co-transcriptional splicing 312 efficiencies in the wild type, we observed higher PI in chromatin RNAs at DSIs identified from 313 mature RNAs but not at DSIs identified from nascent RNAs (Figure 6D). Our results indicate 314 that splicing defects of nascent RNAs in *mac3a mac3b* and *prl1 prl2* can be rescued in mature 315 RNAs at introns with regular PI in chromatin RNAs, but not at introns with higher PI in 316 chromatin RNAs.

### 318 **DISCUSSION**

319 In this study, we found that in *Arabidopsis thaliana*, splicing begins for nearly all introns during 320 transcription, and this occurs with higher efficiency for introns in protein-coding genes than in 321 noncoding RNAs. Many intron-surrounding features are reported to correlate with co-

322 transcriptional splicing efficiency, including intron position, intron length, gene length, and 323 alternative splicing (Khodor et al., 2012; Khodor et al., 2011; Tilgner et al., 2012). Here, we 324 conducted a thorough investigation of the impact of these features on co-transcriptional splicing 325 in *Arabidopsis*. Intron position is a common and striking feature observed in *Drosophila*  326 *melanogaster*, mice, and human cells as well as in *Arabidopsis*: co-transcriptional splicing 327 efficiency decreases with the decreasing distance to TTS. Intron length negatively correlates with 328 co-transcriptional splicing efficiency in *Drosophila* and human cells, and gene length negatively 329 correlates with co-transcriptional splicing efficiency in *Drosophila* and mice. In *Arabidopsis*, we 330 initially observed a negative correlation between gene length and PI in chromatin RNAs and a 331 positive correlation between PI in chromatin RNAs and length of introns and flanking exons, but 332 we found that the correlation disappeared once the effects of total intron number were eliminated. 333 Single-intron genes are inefficiently spliced in *Drosophila* and mice. In *Arabidopsis*, we found 334 that total intron number is a striking feature positively correlated with co-transcriptional splicing 335 efficiency: total intron number is more important for introns in genes with a small total intron 336 number, whereas intron position is more important for introns in genes with a large total intron 337 number. Given that the cap-binding complex functions in the recognition of first exons, that there 338 is reciprocal interaction between 3′-end processing and last-intron splicing, and that spliceosome 339 assembly involves stages of snRNPs assembly and release (Herzel et al., 2017), it is expected 340 that introns with a small total intron number will take a longer time to assemble on a spliceosome. 341 Since there are on average four introns per gene in *Arabidopsis* versus eight introns per gene in 342 humans (Reddy, 2007), the role of total intron number in *Arabidopsis* is more striking. 343 Inefficiently spliced ncRNAs and alternative introns are two conserved features in both plants 344 and mammals (Tilgner et al., 2012).

345 Features of pre-mRNA sequences lead to different efficiencies of co-transcriptional 346 splicing; however, most introns that are not spliced during transcription are spliced later, after the 347 completion of transcription, either while transcripts are still attached to chromatin or after they 348 are released to nuclear speckles (Bhatt et al., 2012; Girard et al., 2012). An important question 349 thus arises: does co-transcriptional splicing efficiency matter? To address this point, we 350 examined the role of PI in chromatin RNAs in the context of the regulation of splicing by *trans*-351 acting proteins. Transcriptome profiling of mature RNAs is an approach that is widely used to 352 identify the regulatory role of *trans*-acting proteins. We observed higher PI in chromatin RNAs

353 at DSIs identified from eight transcriptomes with *trans*-acting protein mutations as well as four 354 transcriptomes with abiotic stresses, suggesting that introns with poor co-transcriptional splicing 355 tend to be targets of splicing regulation. We further profiled the transcriptomes of nascent and 356 mature RNAs with MAC protein mutations. For introns with regular co-transcriptional splicing 357 efficiency, the splicing defects in nascent RNAs are rescued in mature RNAs, probably through 358 post-transcriptional splicing or post-transcriptional RNA removal through the RNA surveillance 359 pathway (Drechsel et al., 2013; Girard et al., 2012). Interestingly, for introns with low co-360 transcriptional splicing efficiency, the splicing defects remain in mature RNAs in the MAC 361 mutants. These results lead us to propose the following model: the RNA-binding properties of 362 RNAs recruit *trans*-acting proteins to regulate splicing co-transcriptionally; but only the RNAs 363 with low co-transcriptional splicing efficiency (i.e., with high PI in chromatin RNAs) require the 364 *trans*-acting proteins for the final outcome of splicing in mRNAs. Thus, the regulatory role of 365 *trans*-acting proteins cannot be detected in RNA-seq at a portion of the binding sites of those 366 proteins (Meyer et al., 2017). Our findings thus provide novel insights into the substrate 367 repertoire of *trans*-acting splicing regulators.

### 369 **METHODS**

#### 370 **Plant materials and growth condition**

371 All tissues used in the study were from unopened flower buds, and all *Arabidopsis thaliana* 372 strains used were in the Columbia (Col-0) ecotype. Plants were cultivated at 22°C with 16-h-373 light/8-h-dark cycles. The *mac3a mac3b* and *prl1 prl2* mutants in the Col-0 ecotype were 374 previously described (Ji et al., 2015; Monaghan et al., 2009).

375

### 376 **Chromatin-nucleoplasmic-cytoplasmic fractionation**

377 The cytosolic and nuclear fractions were isolated following a published protocol with β-378 mercaptoethanol replaced by DTT (Wang et al., 2011). The chromatin fraction was further 379 precipitated with urea and nonionic detergents as followed (Pandya-Jones and Black, 2009). The 380 nucleus pellets were suspended in 500 µl glycerol buffer (20 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.9, 75 mM NaCl, 381 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.85 mM DTT, 50% glycerol, 0.125 mM PMSF, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 382 160 unit/ml RNase inhibitor) and carefully overlaid on top of 500 µl urea buffer (10 mM HEPES, 383 pH 7.6, 1 mM DTT, 7.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM EDTA, 0.3 M NaCl, 1 M urea, 1% NP-40, 0.5 mM

384 PMSF, proteinase inhibitor cocktail, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 160 unit/ml RNase 385 inhibitor). The tube was gently vortexed two times for 2 seconds, incubated on ice for 5 min, and 386 then centrifuged for 5 min at 4°C at 13,000 rpm. ### 419 **Definition of constitutive introns and alternative introns**

420 In this study, constitutive introns were defined as introns that do not overlap with other splicing 421 events, and all other introns were defined as alternative introns (Supplemental Figure 2A). Note 422 that introns with retained introns were not defined as alternative introns in this study, since most 423 of the introns in chromatin RNAs contain retained intron reads and PI\_Junction calculation is not 424 confounded by retained intron reads.

425

#### 426 **Quantification of intron retention**

427 Araport-11-annotated introns with a minimum length of 50 nt were obtained through in-house 428 python program. The level of retained introns were calculated as the percentage of introns (PI) 429 using a web-based algorithm, SQUID (https://github.com/Xinglab/SQUID). Briefly, there are 430 two ways of calculating PI: PI\_Junction is calculated as intron-inclusion reads divided by the 431 sum of intron-inclusion reads and intron-skipping reads (Figure 2A), and PI\_Density is 432 calculated as observed intron reads (normalized intronic reads) divided by expected intron reads 433 (normalized exonic reads). For alternative introns, both intron-inclusion reads and intron-434 skipping reads can be skewed by reads from alternative splicing transcripts, while for alternative 435 introns that do not overlap with other exons, both the intronic and exonic are not skewed (Figure 436 S2A). Theoretically, both PI\_Junction and PI\_Density should perform well on constitutive 437 introns and and PI\_Density should perform better on alternative introns that do not overlap with 438 other exons. To evaluate their performance at constitutive introns, both PI\_Junction and 439 PI\_Density were calculated for RNA-seq data simulated using Flux Simulator with a known true 440 PI (Griebel et al., 2012). Only reads with enough reads were kept, that is the sum of intron-441 inclusion reads and intron-skipping reads was no less than 20 for PI\_Junction calculation and 442 introns in genes with a minimum of 10 Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped 443 reads (FPKM) for PI\_Density calculation. The results suggested that PI\_Junction performed 444 better than PI\_Density on constitutive introns (Figure S2B), so in this study we used PI\_Junction 445 for analysis of constitutive introns and PI\_Density for analysis of alternative introns.
