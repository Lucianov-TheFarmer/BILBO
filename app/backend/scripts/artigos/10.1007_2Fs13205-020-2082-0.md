# **Exploitation of Hi‑C sequencing for improvement of genome assembly and in‑vitro validation of diferentially expressing genes in** *Jatropha curcas* **L.**

**Saakshi Jalali1 · Nagesh Kancharla1 · Vijay Yepuri1 · Savarimuthu Arockiasamy[1](http://orcid.org/0000-0003-2801-4597)**

Received: 22 October 2019 / Accepted: 20 January 2020 © King Abdulaziz City for Science and Technology 2020

# **Abstract**

*Jatropha curcas* is one of the major sources of renewable energy due to potential use of its oil as a biofuel. The genome of this crop is constituted by the high content of repetitive elements. We employed the Hi-C proximity ligation technique to re-scafold our existing hybrid genome assembly of an elite genotype (RJC1) developed using Illumina and Pacbio technologies. We assembled 99.81% of non-truncated reads to achieve 266.80 Mbp of the genome with an N50 value of 1.58 Mb. Furthermore, we compared the efciency of Hi-C-augmented genome assembly with the hybrid genome assembly and observed a ~ 50% reduction in scafolds and a tenfold increase in the N50 value. The gene ontology analysis revealed the identifcation of terms for molecular function (45.52%), cellular component (33.47%), and biological function (20.99%). Comparative genomic analysis of 13-plant species showed the conservation of 414 lipid metabolizing genes identifed in the KEGG pathway analysis. Diferential gene expression (DGE) studies were conducted in the healthy and Jatropha mosaic virus-infected leaves via RNA-seq analysis and observed gene expression changes for 2185 genes. Out of these, we observed 546 genes having more than two-fold change of transcript level and among these 259 genes were down-regulated and 287 genes were up-regulated. To validate RNA-seq data, two DEGs were selected for gene expression analysis using qRT-PCR and the data was in correlation with in silico results. RNA-seq analysis further shows the identifcation of some of the candidate genes and may be useful to develop JMV resistant plants after functional validation. This Hi-C genome assembly provides a detailed accurate reference genome which could be utilized to improve Jatropha and other economically important Euphorbiaceae family members.

**Keywords** Jatropha · Hi-C · Sequencing · Genomics · Biofuel · Oilseeds · Illumina

Plant genomes generally are larger in size and therefore tend to have a larger portion of repetitive sequences. One of the major concerns in de novo assembly of a genome using short reads is mis-assembly due to large gaps and incorporation of repetitive sequences with incorrect orientation. This issue is partially addressed by flling gaps in the short read assemblies by utilizing long reads derived from PacBio singlemolecule real-time (SMRT) or Oxford Nanopore sequencing technologies. However, the flling of repetitive sequences in the correct orientation in the fnal genome assembly could be achieved only with the utilization of genome fnishing technologies like Hi-C/optical mapping.

The application of hybrid sequencing technologies which has shown to reduce errors and flls gaps in the draft assembly, has been reported by several groups. However, locus control regions (LCR) and enhancer elements activate

transcription of distally linked genes with distance ranging from kilobases (Kb) to megabases (Mb) in the genome (West and Fraser [2005](#page-8-0)). Such a complex 3-dimensional organizational network of the genome wherein the function of genes can be determined through mapping of the interactions at the chromatin level. These physical interactions can be studied through Chromosome Conformation Capture (3C) methodology, which is routinely employed to map the network of interactions between diferent gene loci in humans and plants (Dostie et al. [2006](#page-6-0)). Hi-C technique has been developed based on 3C methodology to study chromatin packaging patterns and unravel the interactions between genes placed at distal loci in the genome. Further, Hi-C scafolding technology provides accurate orientation and ultimately reduces contig/scafold numbers to chromosomal level (Li et al. [2018](#page-7-0)). This sequencing technology enables the wholegenome representation of short and long-range chromosome interactions happening in the cell (Dekker et al. [2002\)](#page-6-1). This information could be utilized in genome scafolding to correct the orientation of genome sequences in draft genome assemblies. The high throughput generation of genome sequences could eventually lead to chromosome-scale scaffolds (Ghurye et al. [2017\)](#page-6-2). The use of Hi-C methodologies in the formation of long scafolds and chromosome-scale genome assemblies have been reported in Quinoa (Jarvis et al. [2017\)](#page-7-1), Lattice (Reyes-Chin-Wo et al. [2017](#page-7-2)) Amaranth (Lightfoot et al. [2017\)](#page-7-3). To interpret the functions of genes and their regulatory elements thoroughly, it is pivotal to understand the interactions between genes and their respective promoter elements. This task becomes more complicated due to re-arrangement of the regulatory components of genes, which are not always closely placed adjacent to each other in proper orientation and their genomic position does not reveal the interactions between them. Recently, Hi-C technology has been demonstrated in food crops like cucumber (Li et al. [2019](#page-7-4)), apple (Zhang et al. [2019\)](#page-8-1), black raspberry (Jibran et al. [2018\)](#page-7-5), and non-food crops such as weedy rice (Sun et al. [2019](#page-8-2)), Arabidopsis (Xie et al. [2015\)](#page-8-3) to achieve chromosome-level genome assembly.

*Jatropha curcas* L. is gaining attention nowadays due to its use as a renewable resource for generating biofuels and for its various value-added products like bio-glue, bio-pesticides, bio-feed, etc. (Nithiyanantham et al. [2012](#page-7-6); Pramanik [2003\)](#page-7-7). The positive attributes of Jatropha have not been completely understood or fully utilized in conventional plant breeding programs mainly due to lack of genetic information and genome architecture in public databases. To understand its genetic structure and to accelerate molecular breeding for development of stable, high yielding, and disease resistant hybrids, it is essential to construct an accurate reference genome assembly.

To the best of our knowledge, a combination of latest next-generation sequencing (Sato et al. [2011](#page-7-8); Hirakawa et al.

[2012](#page-7-9); Wu et al. [2015](#page-8-4); Ha et al. [2019\)](#page-7-10) and genome fnishing technologies have not been utilized for Jatropha oil crop to depict accurate genome assembly. Here, we report the frst hybrid genome assembly of Jatropha developed by utilizing latest genome scafolding technology such as Hi-C and demonstrated a 50% reduction of contig/scafold numbers and a tenfold increase in the N50 value compared to the hybrid genome assembly of RJC1 (RJC1-WGS; Reliance *Jatropha curcas*—Hybrid whole genome sequence) (Kancharla et al. [2019](#page-7-11)) generated using short and long reads.

# **Methods**

#### **Plant material**

Approximately, 2 g of leaf material was collected from a 20 day-old in vitro grown, etiolated Jatropha seedling of RJC1 (Reliance *Jatropha curcas*). The inbred line RJC1 of *Jatropha curcas* was grown at the Agricultural research farm in Andhra Pradesh. These leaf samples were used for DNA and RNA isolation. The extracted high-quality DNA was used for the construction of Hi-C sequencing library and RNA was used for RNA-sequencing. AgriGenome Labs Pvt. Ltd. (AGL, Hyderabad, India) carried out the DNA and RNA extractions by following their in-house protocol.

#### **Hi‑C library preparation and sequencing**

A total of eight Hi-C libraries were prepared for sequencing using Illumina HiSeq X Ten (San Diego, CA, United States) platform by following the in-house protocol of AGL Labs. For each sequencing library preparation, chromatin is frst fxed with formaldehyde in the nucleus and then this chromatin is digested with *DpnII* restriction enzyme. The 5′ overhangs were flled with biotinylated nucleotides and the free blunt ends were re-ligated. Ligation is followed by the crosslinking of DNA fragments and purifcation of genomic DNA to remove the non-biotin ligated fragments. Further, the DNA is sheared by sonication and selected 150 bp mean fragment size for sequencing library preparation by using the Illumina compatible adapters.

#### **Read mapping and scafolding**

The raw sequencing reads were pre-processed using the HiCUP (Hi-C User Pipeline) pipeline (Wingett et al. [2015](#page-8-5)). The reads were fltered out for experimental artifacts such as circularized and re-ligated reads, and all duplicate read pairs were removed. The pipeline utilizes Bowtie2 for aligning the paired-end reads with the existing Jatropha reference genome [RJC1-WGS]. We considered only reads with a MAPQ (MAPping Quality)≥30 for alignment. Further, an open-source software, SALSA2 (Simple AssembLy ScAfolder) was employed to accurately assemble the scaffolds utilizing the assembly sequence overlap information to reduce the errors arising from inversions (Ghurye et al. [2017](#page-6-2)). The basic scafolding algorithm of SALSA2 begins with aligning of Hi-C reads with reference assembly. Here we utilized the hybrid genome assembly [RJC1-WGS] for construction of scafold graph using ambiguous edges of the assembly and Hi-C reads (Kancharla et al. [2019](#page-7-11)). The quality and quantitative assessment of the genome assembly was performed using QUAST (Quality Assessment Tool for Genome Assemblies, v5.0.2) and BUSCO (Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs, v3) tools (Gurevich et al. [2013](#page-7-12); Simão et al. [2015\)](#page-8-6) respectively. The QUAST analysis was performed using default parameters to compare the three assemblies namely, RJC1-WGS, Hi-C assembly (generated using SALSA assembler), and Hi-C assembly (generated using Hi-Rise assembler performed by Dovetail Genomics). These three genome assemblies were evaluated by QUAST analysis based on criteria of N50 value, the number of contigs, size of the assembly, and largest contig size.

#### **Gene prediction and annotation**

We predicted the coding sequences (CDS) using the highly accurate BRAKER pipeline [v2.1.0] (Hof et al. [2016](#page-7-13)). The assembled genome sequences were soft-masked for repeats to accurately predict the genes. The pipeline was run on the masked genome by using ab initio parameter and highquality reads of RNA-seq derived from 13 tissues of various developmental stages of Jatropha to enhance the prediction of genes (Table S1). Further, the predicted CDS were annotated by running the blast tool against the Uniprot nr database. The genome sequence was also utilized to identify repeat regions using the RepeatScout program (Price et al. [2005](#page-7-14)).

#### **Transcriptome sequencing and data generation**

RNA sequencing was performed for 13 tissues of various growth stages of Jatropha plant using Illumina Hiseq2500 platform to generate 2 × 100 paired end reads (PE) per sample (Table S1). The low-quality reads were filtered and trimmed to obtain clean reads. The resulting fles were aligned to the reference genome to generate BAM fles which were used by the BRAKER pipeline for accurate prediction of CDS. The aligned fles were also utilized to perform transcriptome assembly using TopHat2 (Kim et al. [2013](#page-7-15)). Jatropha Mosaic Virus (JMV) belongs to the geminivirus group, which causes mosaic disease in Jatropha with yield loss up to 100% in severe conditions (Borah and Dasgupta [2012\)](#page-6-3). For diferential gene expression studies, healthy, and geminivirus infected leaf samples were collected from 60-day-old Jatropha plants. Test samples diferentiated as geminivirus infected (GI) and healthy (H) based on validation with geminivirus specifc coat-protein primers. Leaf samples positive for JMV coat protein (CP) gene primers, designated as GI and negative as H and were subjected then for RNA sequencing. We analyzed the diferentially expressed genes between the GI and H leaves using the cufdif2 package using default parameters (Trapnell et al. [2013](#page-8-7)). The expression level of genes in both the tissues was normalized and calculated as the value of fragments per transcript kilobase per million fragments mapped (FPKM), which eliminates the infuence of diferent gene lengths and sequencing discrepancies.

#### **Validation of diferentially expressing genes**

To validate the accuracy and feasibility of using transcriptome data for functional genomics, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) study was conducted with gene-specifc primers. Total RNA was extracted from Jatropha using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and qRT-PCR was performed using QuantiTect SYBR Green Mastermix (QIA-GEN) following the manufacturer's protocol. We conducted qRT-PCR on a LightCycler II (Roche) as follows: 3 min at 95 °C for initial denaturation, followed by 50 cycles of 10 s at 95 °C for denaturation, 30 s at 58 °C for annealing, and 30 s at 72 °C for extension. All gene expression analyses were performed using three independent biological replicates. The relative expression levels for each gene were normalized to the expression level of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; Internal control) gene from Jatropha, which was calculated from cycle threshold values using the 2−ΔΔ*Ct* method (Livak and Schmittgen [2001](#page-7-16)). The sequences of gene-specifc primers used in the qPCR analysis are listed in Table S2.

# **Genomic comparisons, orthologous clustering of genes, and proteins**

The assembled genome was utilized to predict genes and proteins using the BRAKER pipeline. We performed comparative genomic analysis using the predicted genes with 13 closely related oil-producing plants, algae and model plant species, for which sequences were downloaded from public databases. We grouped these 13 plant species into three categories for this study; (a) oil-producing plants (*Brassica napus*, *Brassica rapa*, *Arachis hypogaea*, *Helianthus annuus*, and *Ricinus communis*), (b) oil-producing algae (*Botryococcus braunii*, *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii*, *Monoraphidium neglectum*, and *Nannochloropsis gaditana*) and (c) model plant species (*Oryza sativa*, *Zea mays*, *Medicago truncatula*, and *Arabidopsis thaliana*). We performed a blast search with 80% and higher similarity cut-of to identify

specifcally oil synthesizing genes. We identifed the oil synthesizing genes in Jatropha by performing the KEGG pathway analysis. Hence, we identifed the lipid metabolizing and related pathway information.

# **Results**

# **Read mapping and scafolding**

A total of 507,363,354 raw reads was generated using Illumina HiSeq X Ten (San Diego, CA, United States) platform. The sequencing was done at 190X coverage amounting to 76.1 Gbp data. Using HiCUP pipeline, the raw reads were fltered and we were left with 506,423,663 non-truncated reads with loss of only 0.18% of the raw reads. These fltered reads were assembled using the SALSA pipeline using the hybrid assembly of Jatropha as reference (Kancharla et al. [2019](#page-7-11)). QUAST analysis was performed to assess the Hi-C assembly using SALSA assembler. The analysis showed the N50 value as 1.6 Mbp, a total of 1231 contig numbers, 266.80 Mbp of total assembly length and the largest contig size of 24.78 Mbp. On comparing the three assemblies (Table S3), we observed that the N50 value, total contigs number, largest contig, and the total assembly length parameters were best achieved for assembly performed using SALSA assembler. BUSCO analysis revealed that 86.6% of 1440 eukaryotes gene sequences were found to be complete in our assembly (Table S4). We observed a reduction in the number of contigs from 2959 to 1231, which is almost 50% less than the hybrid wholegenome assembly carried out previously and further the N50 value was increased by 10 times (Fig. [1](#page-3-0) and [2](#page-3-1)). The scafolds generated from the current assembly are found to be longer and observed to have a very lower rate of global misassembles.

# **Gene prediction and annotation**

Using the BRAKER pipeline, we predicted 20,858 coding sequences (CDS) which were annotated by using the BLASTn program against the NCBI nr database. Our analysis revealed that a minimum of one hit for each of the 18,637 predicted coding sequences and 16,825 CDS showed more than 65% similarity with the NCBI protein database. We also carried out gene ontology analysis for the predicted CDS. Total gene ontology terms identifed for molecular function (45.52%), cellular component (33.47%), and biological function (20.99%) and top terms of GO categories are shown in Fig. [3](#page-4-0). Using the de novo approach, we masked the Jatropha genome for repeat elements and identifed 647,925 elements, including interspersed and simple repeats. Of the assembled genome (266.80 Mbp), we found 109.2 Mbp of the genome to be repetitive, which accounts for nearly ~ 41.2%.

#### **Pathway analysis**

Out of the 16,825 CDS sequences, we extracted genes with greater than 80% similarity and categorized them based on pathway information available in the KEGG database. We were able to categorize 2641 genes into 132 KEGG pathways. We were majorly interested in the lipid metabolizing pathways to focus on the genes responsible for oil production. Out of the total 132 KEGG categories, 25 were identifed as lipid pathways comprising 414 Jatropha genes. The majority of the identifed lipid metabolizing genes were categorized as pentose and glucuronate interconversions, followed by peroxisome glycerophospholipid metabolism (Fig. [4\)](#page-5-0).

#### **Transcriptome sequencing and data generation**

To analyze the diferent levels of gene expression between the GI and H leaves, we calculated the FPKM of the assembled transcripts. We found 2185 diferentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two tissues, with 259 genes down-regulating and 287 up-regulating in virus-infected tissues. We used stringent parameters of *p* value (≤0.05), False Discovery Rate (FDR;<0.001), and fold change (log2 FC of 2) to flter the diferentially expressed genes between the two tissues. Furthermore, two of the computationally predicted DEGs were in vitro validated using qRT-PCR by taking Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal control reference gene. This in vitro analysis revealed that the data were in correlation with the data in the predicted analysis. The relative expression of these selected genes is shown in Fig. [5](#page-5-1) (Table S2).

### **Comparative genomics**

Pathway analysis revealed that 414 Jatropha genes were found to be precisely involved in lipid metabolic pathways. We compared these gene sequences across the closely related oil-producing plants, algae, and model plant species based on the sequence similarity. We identifed 18 conserved genes across oil-producing plants which include *Ricinus communis*, *Brassica napus*, *Brassica rapa*, *Arachis hypogaea,* and *Helianthus annuus*. Analysis showed that maximum number of genes were found to be conserved with *Ricinus communis* (2114) followed by *Arachis hypogaea* (718) and *Brassica rapa* (113) (Fig. S1). We also compared the Jatropha sequence at the protein level with *Glycine max*, *Arabidopsis thaliana*, *Arachis hypogaea*, *Brassica rapa*, and *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* and observed 2918 orthologous protein clusters shared among these species (Fig. S2).

# **Discussion**

Jatropha is one of the important biofuel crops. To understand the crop at the genome level, we have assembled the Jatropha genome using a hybrid whole-genome sequencing strategy (RJC1-WGS). In this study, we attempted to further improve the genome assembly using a comprehensive technique to capture the conformation of the genome. The chromosome conformation capture-based methodology enables us to look at the 3D organization of a genome

Hi-C methodology has been recently utilized for ultrascafolding of de novo genome assemblies for human, mouse and Drosophila (Burton et al. [2013;](#page-6-4) Selvaraj et al. [2013](#page-7-17); Kaplan and Dekker [2013](#page-7-18)). This has enhanced the chromosome-scale de novo assemblies with an accuracy of 99% in terms of order and orientation of scafolds in the human genome. This methodology has also been applied to barley (5 Gb) and wild emmer (12 Gb) genomes (Avni et al. [2017](#page-6-5); Mascher et al. [2017](#page-7-19); Schreiber et al. [2018](#page-7-20)) which allowed the formation of chromosome-scale assemblies without a genetic map as reported in raspberry (VanBuren et al. [2018](#page-8-8)). However, the linkage maps were utilised in strawberries (Shulaev et al. [2011](#page-7-21)), peaches (International Peach Genome Imitative [2013](#page-7-22)), apples (Zhang et al. [2019\)](#page-8-1), cucumber (Li et al. [2019](#page-7-4)), and weedy rice (Sun et al. [2019\)](#page-8-2) for anchoring the Hi-C scafolds to achieve the chromosomal level assembly.

Using the current approach, though we are not able to bring down the assembly to chromosome level, we have been able to drastically reduce the number of contigs by 50% and enhance the N50 value of the assembly 10 times from our previous assembly (Kancharla et al. [2019](#page-7-11)). The total size of the genome covered using Hi-C sequencing technology in the current study is 266.80 Mbp. In this study, we were able to obtain longer contigs with the maximum size of 24.78 Mbp and observed an average sequence length is 0.22 Mbp. This has enhanced the quality and accuracy of the assembly compared to whole-genome sequence reported previously for RJC1. It is reported that Euphorbiaceae family members are known for containing a large number of repeat elements in their genome and we have observed 41.2% of the repetitive genome in Jatropha (Pootakham et al. [2017\)](#page-7-23).

In the present study, we were also able to increase the number of protein-coding genes. We also annotated an additional number of protein-coding genes and assigned them in various metabolic pathways using the KEGG database. Based on the comparative studies with our earlier RJC1- WGS assembly of Jatropha, Hi-C assembly is enhanced and improved in terms of the orientation of scafolds, positioning of genes in the genome, and accuracy of assembly, which in turn has strengthened our downstream analysis. We have studied the gene expression changes between the GI and H leaves of Jatropha and found the genes like chlorophyll A-B binding proteins (Göhre et al. [2012\)](#page-6-6), carbonic anhydrase (DiMario et al. [2017\)](#page-6-7), multidrug and toxic compound extrusion protein (Santos et al [2017\)](#page-7-24), MYB family proteins (Ambawat et al. [2013\)](#page-6-8), pectin esterases (Marzin et al. [2016\)](#page-7-25) and auxin response factor genes (Fu and Wang [2011](#page-6-9)). These genes were found to express primarily during viral infections and further trigger plant defense mechanisms to combat viral infections through modulating their gene expression in an up or down-regulating pattern. These genes are also involved in signal transduction of disease resistance pathways such as jasmonic acid (Lee et al. [2001\)](#page-7-26), salicylic acid (Rafaele et al. [2006\)](#page-7-27) and gibberellic acid synthesis pathway (Murray et al. [2003\)](#page-7-28). The in vitro validation study of DEGs proved the positive correlation with the in silico predicted data. This shows the accuracy of gene prediction using the Hi-C genome assembly and its application in various other genomic studies. Here, we further demonstrate the importance of improved Jatropha reference genome to genetically improve the crop in terms of per unit feld productivity.
