Multiple activators and repressors tune the response to the central plant hormone auxin. Israeli et al. show that in leaf development, loss of an activator and a repressor of auxin-response reduces leaf-shape stability. Therefore, the complexity of the auxin response enables the plant to balance flexibility and stability in organ development.

#### SUMMARY

Auxin-signal transduction is mediated by the antagonistic activity of transcriptional activators and repressors. Both activators and repressors belong to gene families, but the biological importance of this complexity is not clear. Here, we addressed this question using tomato leaf development as a model by generating and analyzing mutants in multiple auxin-response components. In developing compound tomato leaves, auxin promotes leaflet formation and blade growth, and in the intercalary regions between leaflets, auxin response is inhibited by the Aux/IAA protein ENTIRE (E). e mutants form simple leaves due to ectopic blade growth in the intercalary domain. Using this unique loss-of-function phenotype and genome editing of auxin-response factor (ARF) genes, encoding auxin-response activators, we identified the contribution of specific ARFs to the e phenotype. Mutations in the related ARFs SlMP, SlARF19A, and SlARF19B, but not SlARF7, reduced the leaf blade and suppressed the e phenotype in a dosagedependent manner that correlated with their relative expression, leading to a continuum of shapes. While single e and slmp mutants affected blade growth in an opposite manner, leaves of e slmp double mutants were similar to those of the wild type. However, the leaf shape of e slmp was more variable than that of the wild type, and it showed increased sensitivity to auxin. Our findings demonstrate that the existence of multiple auxin-response repressors and activators stabilizes the developmental output of auxin and that tuning their activity enables shape variability. The increased complexity of the auxin response therefore balances stability and flexibility in leaf patterning.

#### INTRODUCTION

Hormones play major roles in the patterning of tissues and organs during the development of multi-cellular organisms. The plant hormone auxin regulates many developmental processes throughout the plant life [\[1, 2\]](#page-11-0). Auxin signaling is mediated by three protein families: the TIR1/AFB receptors from the F-box family, auxin-response factor (ARF) transcription factors, and auxin indole acetic acid (Aux/IAA) proteins. In the absence of auxin, Aux/IAA proteins repress ARF activity. Elevated auxin leads to rapid receptor-mediated degradation of AUX/IAA, releasing ARFs to regulate auxin-responsive genes [\[2–4\]](#page-11-1). ARF transcription factors have been grouped into three classes, of which class A includes transcriptional activators [\[5–9\]](#page-12-0). While in basal land-plant lineages there is only one Aux/ IAA, one TIR1/AFB, and one member in each ARF class [\[7,](#page-12-1) [10\]](#page-12-1), these families have expanded to different extents in flowering plants [[11–20\]](#page-12-2). However, the functional implication of the existence of multiple auxin-response activators and repressors is still not clear.

Diverse interactions between ARF and Aux/IAA proteins, combined with tissue-specific expression, are thought to act in different developmental processes to achieve process-specific outcomes of auxin response [[2, 21, 22](#page-11-1)]. In *Arabidopsis*, several class-A ARF-Aux/IAA pairs have been implicated in specific developmental processes, such as embryogenesis and lateral root formation [\[23–26\]](#page-12-3). However, due to redundancy in the activity of Aux/IAA proteins, the ascription of Aux/IAAs to specific Aux/IAA-ARF modules and developmental processes has relied on auxin-insensitive gain-of-function mutations in Aux/IAA genes [\[27, 28\]](#page-12-4). While this approach yielded important insights, the specificity of the interaction between Aux/IAAs and class-A ARFs is limited, and these gain-of-function mutations may lead to artificial outcomes and to an incomplete picture of functional Aux/IAA-ARF interactions [\[2, 22, 26, 29–32](#page-11-1)]. Therefore, we are still far from understanding the authentic roles of Aux/IAA-ARF networks in specific developmental processes, and the use of loss-offunction alleles should be very useful in addressing this question.

Plant leaves display a wide and continuous range of shapes [\[33\]](#page-12-5). A compound leaf is composed of separate blade units, termed leaflets. Leaflets are formed through differential growth at the leaf margin, where regions of blade growth are interspersed with intercalary regions in which growth is inhibited. Auxin is involved in the specification of these distinct regions: it promotes leaflet formation and blade growth, and in the intercalary domain, auxin response is repressed [\[34–38](#page-12-6)]. In tomato, the Aux/IAA protein ENTIRE (E, SlIAA9) and class-C, miR160 regulated ARFs repress auxin response and blade growth in the intercalary region. Loss-of-function *e* mutants develop single blades with partially fused primary leaflets, due to ectopic blade growth [\[34, 35, 39, 40](#page-12-6)]. Leaves overexpressing *EdII*, a stabilized form of *E*, have a reduced number of narrow leaflets. It is still not known which class-A ARF promotes leaflet initiation and growth downstream of auxin and is inhibited in the intercalary domain by E. Silencing of SlARF6, SlARF8A, and SlARF24 in the background of the *e* mutant did not substantially affect the *e* leaf phenotype, suggesting that these are not the main ARFs that are activated in *e* mutant leaves [\[41\]](#page-12-7). In fruit development, E was shown to functionally interact with SlARF7 and SlMP [[42](#page-12-8)]. The clear loss-of-function phenotype of *e* can serve as a powerful tool in identifying the relevant ARF(s).

Here, we investigated the functional implications of the existence of multiple activators and repressors of auxin response in the context of leaf development. By generating CRISPR/Cas9-derived mutants in four tomato class-A ARFs, we show that three related ARFs promote leaflet formation downstream of auxin and E in a dose-dependent manner. Analysis of multiple genetic interactions with *e* identified the contribution of each SlARF to the regulation of compoundleaf development and identified them as specific functional partners of E. Notably, while leaves of double *e slmp* mutants are similar to wild-type leaves, they show increased shape variability, suggesting that the existence of multiple auxinresponse activators and/or repressors stabilizes leaf shape. Together, our results show how tweaking the activity of multiple auxin-response components can lead to a continuum of leaf shapes and that combined activity of several auxinresponse repressors and activators enables stable yet flexible leaf patterning.

#### RESULTS

#### SlMP Promotes Organ Initiation and Growth

We hypothesized that one or more class-A ARFs promote leaflet initiation and growth downstream of auxin and are normally inhibited in the intercalary domain by E [\(Figure 1](#page-3-0)A). An EMS mutant screen identified the *pinocchio* mutant, with aberrant flower meristems and impaired flower-organ initiation and growth. *pinocchio* flowers were small, with a reduced number of sepals and no petals. Stamens were fused to the base of the reduced gynoecia that had short styles ([Figures 1](#page-3-0)B, 1C, [S1A](#page-11-2)–S1D, and S1G). The aberrant flowers occasionally developed into reduced, elongated seedless fruits [\(Figures S1](#page-11-2)E and S1F). The effect of *pinocchio* on flower and flower-organ development was reminiscent of the *Arabidopsis arf5*/*mp* mutant [\[43, 44](#page-12-9)]. *pinocchio* was mapped to a chromosome region that contains the tomato *ARF5/MP* ortholog (Solyc04 g081240), and sequencing of this gene in *pinocchio* revealed a point mutation leading to an early stop codon in exon 8 ([Figure 1](#page-3-0)M). We therefore renamed the mutant *slmp-1*. An allelic mutation, *slmp-2*, was identified in another population [\[45\]](#page-13-0), and several additional alleles were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing and designated *CR-slmp* ([Figure 1M](#page-3-0)). *slmp-1*, *slmp-2*, and *CR-slmp* all had nearly identical phenotypes [\(Figures S1H](#page-11-2)–S1K).

*slmp* mutants had impaired leaf and leaflet formation. They produced leaves slower than the wild type ([Figure S1](#page-11-2)L), and their leaves had fewer leaflets and longer petioles and rachis. Specifically, *slmp* leaves usually lacked the basal pair of primary leaflets and had fewer intercalary leaflets [\(Figures 1](#page-3-0)D, 1F, 1L, [S1H](#page-11-2)–S1K (red arrowheads), [S1N](#page-11-2), S1O, [S2A](#page-11-2), and S2B). In some cases, *slmp/+* leaves showed an intermediate level of *SlMP* expression and an intermediate number of leaflets [\(Figures](#page-11-2) [S1M](#page-11-2)–S1P). Therefore, SlMP promotes the initiation and growth of leaves, leaflets, and flower organs in a dose-dependent manner.

#### SlMP Mediates the Ectopic Blade Initiation and Growth in e Mutants

E was recently shown to interact with all class-A SlARFs [[42](#page-12-8)], and we confirmed its interaction with SlMP by yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) and bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay (BiFC) [\(Figures S3](#page-11-2)A and S3B). To genetically test the hypothesis that the release of SlMP from E inhibition is responsible for the ectopic blade growth in the *e* mutant, we generated double mutants between *e* and *slmp*. Such genetic interactions between *aux/iaa* and *arf* loss-of-function mutants have not been examined so far due to high redundancy within these gene families. Strikingly, while the *e* ectopic blade phenotype is epistatic to many genetic backgrounds [\[34, 46–49](#page-12-6)], *slmp* and *e* showed mutual suppression. The intercalary fate was restored in *e slmp* leaves, which had distinct and separate primary leaflets and intercalary leaflets, similar to wild-type leaves [\(Figures](#page-3-0) [1](#page-3-0)D–1G, 1L, and [Figures S3](#page-11-2)C–S3F). However, the leaf phenotype of *e slmp* was more variable than that of the wild type. Interestingly, *e slmp/+* leaves showed an intermediate phenotype ([Figures 1H](#page-3-0), 1L, and [Figures S3C](#page-11-2)–S3G), further suggesting that SlMP acts in a dosage-dependent manner. Downregulation of several class-A ARFs using the RNAi approach was shown to reduce *SlMP* expression to 50%–80% [[42, 50](#page-12-8)]. We used this genotype to further explore the dose-dependent effect of *SlMP* on the *e* phenotype. In vegetative apices of RNAi class-A ARF plants, the expression of *SlMP*, but not of the closely related ARFs *SlARF19A* and *SlARF19B*, was reduced to 70% [\(Figure S3](#page-11-2)H). Similar to *slmp/+*, RNAi class-A ARF plants had fewer leaflets and showed a partial suppression of the *e* phenotype ([Figures S3G](#page-11-2) and S3I). To further assess the specific quantitative relationship between E and SlMP, we overexpressed both *SlMP* and the stabilized *E* form *EdII-GUS* in leaves using the *FIL* promoter [[51\]](#page-13-1). Overexpression of *SlMP* alone did not lead to an aberrant phenotype, but it partially suppressed the *EdII-GUS* overexpression phenotypes without reducing the *E* transcript level [\(Figures 1](#page-3-0)I–1K and [Figure S4](#page-11-2)). Therefore, marginal growth is regulated by quantitative and antagonistic interaction between E and SlMP.

#### E, SlMP, SlARF19A, and SlARF19B Stabilize Leaf Shape in Response to Auxin

Our results show that SlMP and E play central roles in promoting the growth and intercalary domains, respectively. However, in the absence of both E and SlMP, discrete leaflets are still formed. Interestingly, while leaves of *e slmp* and some *e slarf19ab* mutants were very similar to wild-type leaves, we noticed that they were more variable. For example, while wild-type leaves almost invariably produced 6 primary leaflets, the number of primary leaflets in *e slmp* and *e slarf19ab* ranged between 3–6 and 0–6, respectively [\(Figures 6A](#page-8-0)–6C and [Figure 7B](#page-9-0)). Increased phenotypic variability between genetically identical individuals within the same environment may result from altered regulatory networks [\[53](#page-13-3)]. Interestingly, despite the mutual phenotypic suppression, in *e slmp-1* and *e slarf19ab* leaves, the *DR5* signal was elevated and expanded into the intercalary domain ([Figures](#page-8-0) [6](#page-8-0)D–6F, red arrowheads). Therefore, in the wild type, localized *DR5* distribution correlates with a stable developmental output of auxin, while removing an auxin-response repressor and activator disturbs *DR5* distribution and the stability of the developmental phenotype.

To further investigate the contribution of the antagonistic activity of E and SlMP to the robustness of leaf shape and size, we quantified additional leaf-shape parameters and their variability in wild type and *e slmp*. Quantification of the number of primary and intercalary leaflets and the relative blade coverage of the rachis showed decreased leaf-shape stability of *e slmp* plants compared to the wild type ([Figures 7](#page-9-0)A–7C). This was further confirmed by a higher coefficient of variation (CV) of blade coverage and total blade area in *e slmp* leaves relative to the wild type ([Figures 7D](#page-9-0) and 7E).

To further characterize leaf-shape stability, we examined the response to auxin. Auxin application results in ectopic intercalary blade growth. Leaves of *e slmp-1* double mutants showed increased sensitivity to auxin application compared to the wild type [\(Figures 7G](#page-9-0)–7J, [S7](#page-11-2)A, and S7B). To genetically test the sensitivity of the *e slmp* double mutant to endogenous auxin perturbation, we examined its genetic interaction with a mutant in the auxin-transporter gene *slsopin1* (solyc10 g078370) [\[54\]](#page-13-4). *slsopin1* mutants have a relatively weak phenotypic effect, likely due to redundancy [[54, 55](#page-13-4)]. In *slsopin1* mutants, early leaves show ectopic blade growth, similar to *e*, but later leaves are nearly normal [\(Figures 7K](#page-9-0), 7L, and [S7](#page-11-2)C–S7E). #### DISCUSSION

Flexible tuning of growth underlies much of the natural variability of shape and function. Here, we show that auxin-mediated growth in tomato leaves is implemented by a specific, dosedependent interaction between the Aux/IAA E and three related class-A ARFs, which balance the growth and the intercalary fates. The extremes of no blade growth or continuous growth, resulting from ''too much'' E and ''too much ARF,'' respectively, lead to a simple leaf shape. Balanced, alternate fates are required for the formation of separate leaflets and a compound leaf shape. The combined activity of these antagonistic auxinresponse components stabilizes leaf patterning, and tuning their activities leads to a wide variability of leaf shapes.

#### Specificity and Complexity of Auxin Response

One of the ways by which auxin is thought to promote many different outcomes is by tissue- and process-specific combinations of Aux/IAA and ARFs [[19, 22, 24, 30, 56\]](#page-12-11). However, due to a high level of redundancy between Aux/IAA proteins in *Arabidopsis*, the attribution of Aux/IAAs to specific ARFs and developmental processes was inferred from gain-of-function mutants [[22, 25, 32, 57\]](#page-12-12). Several studies showed that such gain-offunction forms can affect most tested developmental outputs of auxin, leaving open the question of their authentic ARF partners and specific developmental roles [[22, 26, 27, 29, 58–61](#page-12-12)]. Here, the use of loss-of-function *e* and *slarf* mutants enabled us to identify an authentic module containing the Aux/IAA E and the class-A ARFs SlMP, SlARF19A, and SlARF19B, which balances blade growth to achieve stable yet flexible leaf patterning. The relative contribution of each ARF to leaflet initiation and growth is correlated with their expression levels ([Figures](#page-11-2) [S5A](#page-11-2) and S5B). Previously, differences in Aux/IAA and ARF gene activity were shown to depend mainly on their expression but also on the strength of their activity [\[22\]](#page-12-12).

Interestingly, the related ARF7 protein is not part of this module and is probably involved in other processes, possibly at a different timing. In *Arabidopsis*, unique ARF combinations were implicated in different developmental processes [\[13, 14, 22,](#page-12-13) [24, 62, 63](#page-12-13)]. For example, MP and ARF7 act redundantly in embryogenesis, leaf venation, and hypocotyl elongation. In lateral root development, ARF7 and ARF19 act during early stages, while a different module containing MP is involved in later stages [\[64\]](#page-13-5). In tomato, SlARF7 was recently shown to interact with E and SlMP in fruit development, supporting the possibility that different Aux/IAA-ARF combinations are teamed for specific developmental processes [[42](#page-12-8)]. Recently, silencing of *SlARF6* and *SlARF8* in *e* background did not significantly suppress the *e* phenotype [\[41\]](#page-12-7). This suggests that either these SlARFs do not contribute considerably to the ectopic blade growth in *e* or that reduced expression is not sufficient for suppression. Examination of loss-of-function mutants in these genes may help to distinguish between these possibilities. It will be interesting to test whether additional Aux/IAAs are also involved in this module, further contributing to leaf-shape stability. In *Arabidopsis*, IAA9 and IAA8 were shown to redundantly affect leaf serration [\[35](#page-12-14)]. While *E* expression in leaves is much higher than all other Aux/IAAs [[48, 52\]](#page-13-2), the involvement of additional Aux/IAAs is supported by the enhancement of the *e* phenotype after auxin application and in the *e slsopin1* double mutant and by the mutual suppression between *e* and *slmp*.

#### Multi-Layer Feedback in Auxin Response

The breadth and intensity of the effect of auxin on all aspects of plant development and physiology call for a tight regulation of its activity. Accordingly, auxin activity is subjected to multi-layer feedback regulation to maintain a balanced and sensitive magnitude of response [[1, 65\]](#page-11-0). Here, we observed a unique uncoupling of the molecular and developmental response to auxin: reduction in *slarf* activity led to reduced growth, which was surprisingly correlated with increased *DR5* expression. Recently, local inhibition of auxin response in the *Arabidopsis* suspensor led to massive misregulation of auxin biosynthesis and response genes [[66\]](#page-13-6). In contrast, auxin biosynthesis and response genes were not significantly affected in *slmp* mutants, suggesting the existence of another layer of regulation that acts at the level of the signaling pathway. Mathematical modeling of auxin response suggested that sequestration of ARF or Aux/IAA would increase auxin responsiveness, which is in agreement with the current results [[67](#page-13-7)]. The model also predicted that active repression by Aux/IAA complexes is important for the response; therefore, loss of the activity of a central ARF may activate auxin-responsive genes by releasing other ARFs. Therefore, the robustness, specificity, and accuracy of the auxin response are tuned via multi-level feedback, which utilizes the complexity of the auxin response.

#### Stability and Flexibility

As plants do not move, they respond to the continuous external and internal changes by flexible development. At the same time, they need to retain a reliable basic developmental program [\[53, 68, 69\]](#page-13-3). Here, we show that the complexity of the auxin response enables plants to maintain a stable developmental output while tuning form diversity: loss of both the E repressor and the SlMP activator increases leaf-shape variability and auxin sensitivity. Recently, a similar phenomenon was reported for the circadian clock, where mutating a group of activators and repressors retained robust rhythms under normal conditions but not under non-optimal temperatures [[70\]](#page-13-8). Therefore, the involvement of multiple activators and repressors contributes to patterning stability by providing several layers of regulation, and removal of one of these layers exposes the potential for variability. It will be interesting to investigate whether these auxinresponse components converge on common targets or whether they act via partially distinct targets.

<span id="page-11-2"></span>Recently, a group of MADS-box transcription factors was shown to affect inflorescence architecture in a dose-dependent manner, leading to a continuum of inflorescence branching phenotypes [[71](#page-13-9)]. Alleles affecting regulatory regions were shown to have a quantitative effect on fruit size, flower number, and plant architecture [[71, 72\]](#page-13-9). The form flexibility in many developmentally related traits in tomato and perhaps in other species may therefore be attributed to the utilization of dose-dependent effects of multiple, partially redundant transcription factors.

The enormous variability of extant leaf forms raises the question of the functional importance of leaf shape. As leaves are the main photosynthetic source, perhaps an ideal leaf form will be the one with the highest ratio between the resources it produces and those invested in making it. Such a hypothetic shape will differ for each combination of species, developmental history, and environmental circumstances. Indeed, each species has a highly characteristic basic leaf shape, yet it is substantially affected by different environments. For example, leaf shape is affected by light quality and in heterophyllic plants, two very different leaf shapes are produced in terrestrial versus submerged conditions [[73, 74](#page-13-10)]. A correlation between leaf shape and yield was also suggested. Thus, leaf shape is tightly regulated within a species, and at the same time, the plant maintains the ability to adjust its shape in response to the changing environment. The existence of several Aux/IAA-ARFs family members is shown here to be one of the mechanisms allowing this combination of stability and flexibility [\(Figure 7\)](#page-9-0).

In summary, this work presents a new Aux/IAA-ARF module that regulates organ development in tomato and provides a useful tool for future studies on auxin response and the role of different Aux/IAAs and ARFs in plant development.

#### Plant Material

Tomato seeds (*Solanum lycopersicum* cv M82 were germinated and grown for four weeks in a growth room or a growth chamber in a 16/8 light/dark regime at 25C0 under fluorescent light. Seedlings were then transferred to a greenhouse or to an open field with natural day length and 25C/20C day/night temperature. The *slmp-1* allele, which was used throughout this work, was identified in a tomato ethyl methane sulfonate (EMS) population generated in our lab in the *la-6* background and was separated from *la-6* by backcrossing to the M82 wild type. The affected gene was identified as Solyc04 g081240 and the mutation identified as a C/T (Q/Stop) substitution 813 bp downstream of the transcription start site (TSS)/ATG, within exon 8. The *slmp-2* allele was identified in an independent EMS population [[45](#page-13-0)] and was mapped to Solyc04 g081240 and identified as a C/T (Q/Stop) substitution 1935 bp downstream of the TSS/ATG, within exon 12. Allelism of *slmp-1* and *slmp-2* was established by crossing *slmp-1/+* and *slmp*/+, which failed to complement each other. The *CR-slmp, CR-slarf7*, *CR-slarf19a* and *CR-slarf19b* alleles were generated by CRISPR/Cas9 (see below). The *slsopin1* allele was found in an EMS population [[45](#page-13-0)] and was mapped to SolyC10 g078370 and identified as a A/T (Q/Stop) substitution 1554 bp downstream of the TSS/ATG, within exon 4. All described transgenic genotypes were generated by the OP-LhG4 transactivation system [[75, 76\]](#page-13-11). The *E* (Solyc04 g076850) and *SlMP* (Solyc04 g081240) promoters contain a 3000bp and 4000bp segment, respectively, upstream of the TSS/ATG site, amplified by PCR from genomic DNA of M82, and cloned into the pENTR-D-TOPO (Invitrogen) entry vector, followed by LR-recombination into the LhG4 binary vector. For the *E* promoter, we recovered several driver lines with different strengths: *pE #2* was the strongest, *pE #5* and *#7* were weaker and *pE #6* the weakest. For the *SlMP* promoter we recovered one line. *FIL>>EdII-GUS* and *DR5::VENUS* have been described previously [[34, 39, 77\]](#page-12-6). In addition, we utilized several responder lines of the OP:EdII (*OP:EdII #1*, *OP:EdII #2*, *OP:EdII-GUS #3, OP:EdII-GUS #8*) with the different *pE* driver lines. The *op:SlMP* reporter lines were generated in this work. The *SlMP* gene was amplified from tomato M82 cDNA and cloned into pGEM with KpnI and HindIII sites and was inserted into the pART27 binary vector using NotI. Genotyping for the *e* mutation *(e3335)* was performed as previously described [[34](#page-12-6)]. The primers used in this study are listed in the [Table S1.](#page-11-2)

#### <span id="page-16-2"></span>METHOD DETAILS

#### Tomato transformation and Tissue culture

M82 seeds were used to generate transgenic tomato plants according to [[78\]](#page-13-12). Seeds were sterilized in 70% ethanol for 2 min, followed by 3.3% NaOC1 and 0.1% Tween 20 for 10 min. Seeds were then rinsed three times with sterile double-distilled water, and then germinated on Nitsch medium for 7-10 days, until seedlings formed cotyledons but no or only few true leaves. Cotyledons were dissected in a Petri dish, and incubated on a 7 cm diameter filter Whatman paper on top of a feeder medium with 100 mM Aceto Syingone over 1-2 nights in the dark. The cotyledons were then sub-cultured with 0.35-0.4 O.D diluted agrobacterium containing the transformation construct (*GV3101*) for 10-12 min. Excess agrobacterium was removed and the cotyledons incubated on feeder medium for an additional 48 hours in the dark, after which they were then moved to J1 culture media for 1-2 weeks. Appearing callus

or shoots were transferred to J2 culture media for further shoot organogenesis. The culture media was replaced every two weeks until small plants formed. Plants were removed from the cotyledons and transferred into J3 culture media for further growth. After establishing a vital meristem, plants were transformed to a rooting medium, and following rooting plants were transplanted to soil for further analysis and crosses. All stages from J1 on contained kanamycin antibiotics (25-75 mg/l) for selection, and additional kanamycin was sprayed on the plants after potting. Nitsch medium: N0224 medium 4.4 gr/l, Sucrose 20 gr/1, plant agar 9.5 gr/l and pH 5.7. J1 - Jones medium with Ticarcillin 500 mg/1, Zeatin 1 mg/1, Biotin 2mg/l, Kanamycin 25 mg/1 and phytagel 3gr. J2 - Ticarcillin 250 mg/1, Kanamycin 50 mg/1, Zeatine 1 mg/1, Zeatin riboside 0.5 mg/l, Biotin 2mg/l and plant agar 8.5gr. J3 - Ticarcillin 250 mg/1, kanamycin 75 mg/1, zeatin 0.2 mg/1, Biotin 2mg/l and plant agar 8.5gr. J1, J2 and J3 were made with M0256 medium 4.4 gr/l and contained 30% sucrose, pH = 5.7. Rooting media - Nitsch medium 4.4 gr/l with Ticarcillin 150 mg/1, kanamycin 50 mg/1, IBA 2 mg/1 and plant agar 9 gr/l, sucrose 30 gr/l, pH = 5.7. Cotyledon cut and Agro dilute solutions - M0256 medium 4.4 gr/l, sucrose 30 gr/l, pH = 5.7.

#### Trans-activation system

We used the Promoter:LhG4 (p) and Operator (OP) system as described before [\[77](#page-13-13)]. Briefly, in this system, driver lines expressing the synthetic transcription factor LhG4 under the control of a specific promoter are crossed to responder lines containing a gene of interest under the control of the *E.coli* operator, which is recognized by the LhG4 transcription factor but not by any endogenous plant transcription factor. A cross between a driver and a responder line produces an F1 plant in which the selected gene is expressed under the control of the selected promoter (*p>>GENE*).

#### Generation of CRISPR/Cas9 Mutants

Constructs were designed to generate defined deletions within the coding sequence of each target gene, using two sgRNAs alongside the Cas9 endonuclease gene. sgRNAs were designed using the CRISPRp server (<http://cbi.hzau.edu.cn/crispr>). All constructs were assembled using the Golden Gate cloning system [[79](#page-13-14)]. Level 1 constructs carrying the sgRNAs were cloned under the control of the *Arabidopsis thaliana* U6 promoter (AtU6) and were assembled as described [[80\]](#page-13-15). Level 1 constructs pICH47732::NOSp::NPTII, pICH47742::35S::Cas9, pICH47751::AtU6p::sgRNA1, pICH47761::AtU6p::sgRNA2, and the linker pICH41780 (Addgene no. 48019) were assembled into the level 2 binary vector pAGM4723 (Addgene no. 48015) as described [\[79\]](#page-13-14). For genotyping of the transgenic lines, genomic DNA was extracted. The CRISPR/Cas9-positive lines were further genotyped for mutations using a forward primer to the left of the sgRNA1 target sequence and a reverse primer to the right of the sgRNA2 target sequence. For the *CR-slmp* we recovered plants with 4 bp deletions within exon 1 that caused an early stop codon after 69bp from the TSS/ATG. For the *CR-slarf7* we recovered several independent plants with 107 bp deletions between bp numbers 95-202 that caused an early stop codon after 246 bp. For the *CR-slarf19a* and *CR-slarf19b* we recovered plants with a 1-bp insertion (G insertion, creating a new BstnI restriction site) in the first guide of each gene (*slarf19a-1* and *slarf19b-1*), which caused an early stop codon after 534 bp and 519 bp, respectively. For the *CR-slarf19a* we recovered a second allele with 4bp deletion in the first guide (*slarf19a-2*), which caused an early stop codon after 597 bp. A third allele of *slarf19a* (*slarf19-3*) was recovered. This allele contained a 3 bp deletion (bp number 514-516 from the TSS) in the first guide and a 2 bp substitution AG (bp number 1000-1001), to TA, causing an early stop codon after 1002 bp. For the *CR-slarf19b* we recovered two additional alleles with 1 bp (C or A) insertion in the first guide that caused the same stop codon as in *slarf19b-1.*

### RNA extraction, RNaseq and qRT-PCR analysis

RNA was extracted using the Plant/Fungi Total RNA Purification Kit (Norgen Biotek, Thorold, ON, Canada) according to the manufacturer's instructions, including DNase treatment. Library construction was performed using Quant-Seq (Lexigon) according to manufacturer instructions and sequenced using Illumina NextSeq. Reads were aligned to the ITAG3 tomato genome using bowtie2. Counts were derived using htseq-count using ITAG3.2 gene models. Reads aligning to the gene sequence and up to 500bp downstream to it were counted. Normalization and significant calling was performed using Deseq2 in R. cDNA synthesis was performed using the Verso cDNA Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) or SuperScript II reverse transcriptase (18064014; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) using 1 mg of RNA. qRT-PCR analysis was carried out using a Corbett Rotor-Gene 6000 real-time PCR machine, with SYBR Premix for all other genes. Levels of mRNA were calculated relative to the *EXPRESSED* (*EXP*) or *TUBULIN* (*TUB*) genes as internal controls as follows: in each biological repeat, the expression levels of the assayed gene and *EXP/TUB* were separately calculated relative to a standard curve obtained by a dilution series of a reference sample. The gene expression level in each biological repeat was calculated by dividing the gene expression value by that of *EXP/TUB*. Average expression values were then calculated and presented as 'relative gene expression'. *EXP* has been shown to be expressed at a similar level throughout tomato development [[83\]](#page-14-0). Similar results were obtained when *TUBULIN* (*TUB*) was used as a reference [\[76\]](#page-13-17). Primers used for the qRT-PCR analysis are detailed in [Table S1.](#page-11-2) Each biological repeat included between 3-15 leaf primordia, depending on the developmental stage.

#### Bimolecular fluorescence complementation assays (BiFC)

The split-YFP BiFC system was used to evaluate protein-protein interactions in tobacco leaf cells by an Agrobacterium-mediated transient gene expression system as described previously [\[84, 85](#page-14-1)]. Coding sequences for the *SlMP*, *Sl*D*MP* and *E* were sub-cloned into the pENTR vector (Invitrogen) and recombined into the pCL112 (n-YFP) or pCL113 (c-YFP) Gateway vectors. Leaves from 3-week-old *N. benthamiana* plants were used for Agrobacterium infiltration. Two days after infiltration, fluorescence signals were observed in the injection area using a fluorescence binocular.

#### Yeast two hybrid

The Y169 yeast strain was transformed according to the CLONTECH small-scale LiAc manual ([https://www.clontech.com/](https://www.clontech.com/xxclt_ibcGetAttachment.jsp?cItemId=17602) [xxclt\\_ibcGetAttachment.jsp?cItemId=17602\)](https://www.clontech.com/xxclt_ibcGetAttachment.jsp?cItemId=17602). Pairs of genes were transformed together, one in the pDEST32 (pBD) and the other in the pDEST22 (pACT). Transgenic yeast lines containing both plasmids were selected on Sabouraud Dextrose (SD) agar plates without tryptophan (-W or Trp) and without leucine (-L). As a control, we co-introduced an empty pBD vector with the genes in pACT vector. The SD agar plates were examined for b-galactosidase activity for the *in vivo* blue color assay. Positive protein interaction was identified by blue colonies, while white colonies indicated no protein interaction.

#### In situ hybridization

Experiments were carried out as described before [[29, 76, 86\]](#page-12-19). RNA probes were synthesized from a PCR product, in which the T7 promoter sequence was added to the forward primer, and were labeled with digoxigenin (Roche, catalog #11–209-256-910), using an RNA *in vitro* reverse transcription kit (CellScript, catalog #C-AS2607). Tissue samples were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), followed by a gradual transfer to ethanol, transfer to Histoclear, and embedding in Paraplast Plus (McCormick Scientific). Fixed tissues were sectioned into eight-micrometer- thick sections and mounted on ProbeOnPlus (Fisher Biotech) slides. Prior to hybridization, slides went through the following successive treatments: Histoclear, ethanol series, water, Proteinase K (1 mg/mL), PBS, 4% paraformaldehyde, PBS, triethanolamine (0.1 M, with stirring), PBS, and increasing ethanol series up to 100% ethanol. Slides were hybridized over-night at 55.8C, and then went through the following washing steps: several times with 0.23 SSC at 55.8C, several times with NTE (0.5 M NaCl, 10mMTris-HCl, pH7.5, and 1mMEDTA) at 37.8C. Following the washing steps, slides were blocked with 1% fresh Boehringer block followed by a 1% BSA solution (1% BSA, 100 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.3% Triton X-100). For probe detection, slides were incubated with antidigoxigenin antibodies (Roche) for 2 h at room temperature, washed three times with 1% BSA solution and two times with 100mM Tris, pH 9.5, and 100mMNaCl, and incubated with an NBP/BCIP substrate solution (Roche) for 1 day. Finally, slides were washed with water, mounted and analyzed.

#### Phenotyping and Imaging

Images analyzing the early developmental stages of the whole leaf primordium were captured using an Olympus SZX7 stereo microscope [\(http://www.olympus.com/\)](http://www.olympus.com/) equipped with a Nikon DXM1200 camera and ACTA software, or a Nikon SMZ1270 stereo microscope equipped with a Nikon DS-Ri2 camera and NIS-ELEMENTS software. Samples were viewed using a JEOL 5410 LV microscope (Tokyo, Japan). *In situ* hybridization slides were photographed with a Nikon DS-Ri1-U3 camera as described before [\[29\]](#page-12-19).

#### <span id="page-18-0"></span>QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

#### Analysis of leaf-shape variability

For the quantification of leaflet number, we counted all initiation events on the main leaf axis, namely the main petiole/rachis as described in [\[48\]](#page-13-2). % blade growth was calculated as the combined length of rachis covered by blade divided by the length of the leaf and multiplied by 100. 30 5th leaves from each genotype were measured.

The blade area of the entire leaf was measured using the ImageJ software. 5th leaves were pictured, and using the Photoshop software, we generated a black silhouette of the leaf and measured its area. For the calculation of coefficient of variation (CV), average, standard deviation and CV (the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean in %) of blade area and % blade growth were calculated for groups of wild-type and *e slmp* plants grown together in the same season. Four groups, each including at least 5 leaves from different plants, were included in the calculation. Average CV was calculated using several CV values from different growing seasons.

#### Leaf quantification

Phenotyping and quantification of leaf form and shoot architecture was performed on field- or greenhouse-grown plants. Collected Representative mature intact, 5th leaves were photographed using a Nikon D5200 camera and the photographs used for quantification of leaf-shape phenotype. In order to compare genotypes that were compromised in leaf and leaflets formation, we quantified also the 2nd and 3rd leaves. Leaflet order was defined and leaflet number quantified as described before [\[76, 88, 89\]](#page-13-17). Briefly, primary leaflets are separated by a rachis, and some of them develop secondary and tertiary leaflets. Intercalary leaflets are lateral leaflets

that develop from the rachis later than the primary leaflets and between them. Each genotype was represented by at least 3 biological replicates, consisting of leaves from different plants. Mean values were statistically analyzed using the Student's t test. LAS AF and ImageJ software were used for analysis, quantification, and measurements of captured images. Student's t test (two-tailed) was used for comparison of means, which were deemed significantly different at p % 0.05. Images were manipulated uniformly using adobe Photoshop. Boxplots in figure S1 were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software, San Diego, California USA, [https://www.graphpad.com.](https://www.graphpad.com)
