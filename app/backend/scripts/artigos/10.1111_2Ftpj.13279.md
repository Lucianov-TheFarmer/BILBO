Running Title: The Black-eyed pea gene expression atlas

**Keywords:** *Vigna unguiculata*, Transcriptome, *de novo* Assembly, Cowpea, Gene Atlas, Seed, Drought.

### **SUMMARY**

Legume research and cultivar development are important for sustainable food production, especially of high-protein seed. Thanks to the development of deepsequencing technologies, crop species have been taken to the front line, even without completion of their genome sequences. Black-eyed pea (*Vigna unguiculata*) is a legume species widely grown in semi-arid regions, which has high potential to provide stable seed protein production in a broad range of environments, including drought conditions. The black-eyed pea reference genotype has been used to generate a gene expression atlas of the major plant tissues (i.e. leaf, root, stem, flower, pod and seed), with a developmental time-series for pods and seeds. From these various organs, 27 cDNA libraries were generated and sequenced, resulting in more than one billion reads. Following filtering, these reads were *de novo* assembled into 36,529 transcript sequences that were annotated and quantified across the different tissues. A set of 24,866 unique transcript sequences, called unigenes, was identified. All the information related to transcript identification, annotation and quantification were stored into a gene expression atlas webserver (http://vugea.noble.org), providing a user-friendly interface and necessary tools to analyse transcript expression in black-eyed pea organs and to compare data with other legume species. Using this gene expression atlas, we inferred details of molecular processes that are active during seed development, and identified key

putative regulators of seed maturation. Additionally, we found evidence for conservation of regulatory mechanisms involving miRNA in plant tissues subjected to drought and seeds undergoing desiccation.

## INTRODUCTION

In the past decade, an international effort has been initiated to develop legume research. Legumes count for approximately on third of the world seed production and represent a major source of plant protein (Graham and Vance, 2003). Two plants were selected as model systems, *Medicago truncatula* and *Lotus japonicus*, and are widely used for functional genomic studies (Cook, 1999; Udvardi *et al.*, 2005).

The recent advancement of deep-sequencing technologies allows filling the gap between model and crop species. Black-eved pea (*Vigna unguiculata*) is an annual legume species, commonly named cowpea, southern pea, niebe or lubia depending on the geographical location. It belongs to the *Phaseoleae* tribe of crop legumes, thus is closely related to other *Vigna* species such as *Vigna* radiata (mung bean), but also *Phaseolus* (e.g. *Phaseolus vulgaris*, common bean), *Glycine* (e.g. *Glycine max*, soybean) and *Cajanus (Cajanus cajan*, pigeon pea) species. It is a warm-season crop, adapted to many areas ranging from humid tropics to temperate zones. It has a high tolerance to heat and dry conditions that makes it an ideal crop for dry land environments. Due to its relative yield stability in the face of abiotic stresses (e.g. drought, heat and soil fertility) (Hall, 2004), black-eyed pea is a widely grown crop in Africa, Latin America, Southern Asia and United States of America. This crop is used as forage for animal feed and its nutritious seeds are important for human diet, as well as livestock feed. Its seeds are consumed immature with pod walls or at maturity. They are rich in protein (between 20 and 30%) and carbohydrate (around 50-60%) (Ajeigbe *et al.*, 2008). This high protein concentration is potentially increasable from 16 to 31% considering the existing natural variability in black-eyed pea accessions (Burstin *et al.*, 2011).

The drought resistance, the low requirement in soil fertility, the nitrogen fixing capacity and the nutritional quality of seeds are the main reasons for the increase interest of this crop. Black-eyed pea is a diploid species (2n=22) with a relatively small genome estimated around 600Mbp (Takeda and Matsuoka, 2008). So far, few genomic resources have been developed such as a consensus genetic map based on EST derived SNPs (Muchero *et al.*, 2009) and the sequencing of the gene-rich space (Timko *et al.*, 2008). To date, most efforts related to this crop have been made regarding the transformation capabilities. Several studies reported efficient and stable transformation protocols of Black-eyed pea plants (Popelka *et al.*, 2006; Chaudhury *et al.*, 2007; Ivo *et al.*, 2008). With the increase of agronomical interest of this plant and due to the capacity of plant transformation, identification of transcript sequences and expression profiles will help functional genomic studies and support future agronomic improvement.

Gene expression atlases have been developed in model plants using microarrays or RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) methods, but also in crop plants, when the reference genome was available (Benedito *et al.*, 2008; Severin *et al.*, 2010; Garg *et al.*, 2011; Fasoli et al., 2012; Verdier *et al.*, 2013; O'Rourke *et al.*, 2014). Recently, using *de novo* assembly algorithms, RNA-seq experiments have been successfully conducted on plants lacking reference genome sequenced such as switchgrass (Zhang *et al.*, 2013) and pea (Alves-Carvalho *et al.*, 2015).

In this study, we introduced the development of a resource, the *Vigna unguiculata* gene expression atlas obtained from the *de novo* assembly of RNA-seq data from different plant tissues. This manuscript introduces how we identified transcript sequence and Unigene dataset in black-eyed pea that were annotated, and quantified to understand the molecular mechanisms occurring within the Black-eyed pea tissues with a specific focus on pod and seed development. Finally, using data generated in this study, we investigated if drought-associated miRNAs could be implicated in the seed desiccation mechanisms.

#### **RESULTS AND DISCUSSION**

# Illumina sequencing and *de novo* assembly revealed expression of 36,529 contigs expressed in the different organs

In organisms without reference genome such as Black-eyed pea, Illumina sequencing and *de novo* assembly algorithm has been widely combined to efficiently analyse gene expression profiles. Black-eyed pea plants (reference genotype IT97K-499-35) were grown under controlled conditions (*i.e.* 22/28°C night/day temperatures and 14h light/10h dark photoperiod). Tissues were collected from the major plant organs: leaf, stem, root and flower from 5 week-old plants, empty seed pods at 6, 10 and 16 days after pollination and seeds at 8, 10, 14 and 18 days after pollination (DAP)(Table S1). All samples were collected in three biological replicates (except seed pod tissues in one replicate) and were used to generate cDNA libraries. From these 27 libraries, we obtained more than one billion high quality paired-end reads using Illumina HiSeq2500 analysers (Table 1). Pre-processing of reads including removal of adapter sequences and low quality reads was performed. Then, Trinity package was used to *de novo* assemble the reads following the three tools: Inchworm, Chrysalis and Butterfly as described in Grabherr *et al.* (2011). A total of 1,240,950,901 paired-end reads were mapped and used for transcriptome assembly, which resulted in 154,852 contigs. These contigs were filtered to remove redundant or highly similar contigs based on two criteria: first we removed the low abundance transcripts displaying abundance less than one FPKM (fragments per kilobase per transcript per million mapped reads), then, we grouped transcript sequences displaying sequence similarities higher than 95% using CD-HIT-EST algorithm. From this analysis, we identified 36,529 contigs, representing putative transcript sequences, with a N50 length of 2,175 nucleotides and average length of 1,616 bp (Table 1). The distribution length of the transcript collection ranged from 224 to 14,522 nucleotides, which reflects a coherent size for transcript length (Fig. S1). These contigs represent the different isoforms of identified transcripts expressed in the analysed tissues (Table S1).

# Quality assessment and functional annotation of the Unigene dataset

From the Trinity package, we obtained the contigs arranged into clusters representing unique gene sequences, called Unigenes. Using this information, we rearranged the identified transcripts into 24,866 Unigenes, which represent the full-length sequences of unique transcript (Table 1). According to the transcript (contigs) and Unigene numbers, we identified an average of 1.46 transcript isoforms per Unigene across the different black-eyed pea tissues. The Unigene statistics are displayed in Table 1 and are summarized by a N50 length of 2,187bp and an average length of 1,610bp.

To evaluate the quality and the effectiveness of the assembly and to analyse the representativeness of the Unigene set, we compared the transcript sequences identified from Black-eyed pea with known protein sequences from Arabidopsis and Soybean, and with an existing black-eyed pea EST database (available at the HarvEST webserver, www.harvest.ucr.edu). We revealed a high ortholog proportion between Black-eved pea Unigene sequences and Sovbean and Arabidopsis protein sequences, respectively 83.37% and 80.98% alignment coverage considering the nearly full-length of transcript sequences (Table S2), supporting the effectiveness and accuracy of the assembly process. Compared to the existing black-eyed pea EST database, we identified 77% of the transcript sequences identified in this study were present in the EST database (Table S3), displaying a high proportion of transcript sequence similarity identified through two different approaches. In summary, the predictive transcript sequences identified using *de novo* assembly, displayed high homology with known Black-eyed pea ESTs and higher ortholog proportion with closely related species like Soybean (G. max) than more distant species such as Arabidopsis, which assessed the quality of the *de novo* assembly resulting in the transcript sequence identification.

To further analyse the Unigene set, we annotated the 24,866 Unigene sequences using different strategies. First, using Trinotate from the Trinity software, 19,535 Unigenes (78%) were annotated by homology search into Swissprot, PFAM and

EMBL Uniprot databases. Then, sequences containing transcription factor (TF) domains were identified using the online software PlantTFcat (Dai et al., 2013). From this analysis, 2,485 TFs were predicted to have TF conserved domains and annotated according to their TF family. A family representation of these TFs is provided in the Table S4. According to our prediction, 10% of the Unigenes (2,485 TFs/24,866 Unigenes) were predicted to belong to a TF family and as described in other species, the most abundant TF family is the zinc finger family with the C2H2 type displaying 413 members. Others common families displayed more than 100 member such as the MYB, bHLH and AP2-EREBP families with respectively 153, 119 and 102 members (Verdier et al., 2008; see review Qu and Zhu, 2006). Finally, to provide an overview of the biological functions and metabolic pathways, we attributed GO annotations and Mapman bin codes to the Unigene sequences using respectively Trinotate (part of the Trinity bundle) and Mercator (http://mapman.gabipd.org/web/guest/app/mercator) tools. A Mapman bincode file is provided in Table S5 and was formatted to be directly usable as a mapping file in PageMan or Mapman software (Usadel *et al.*, 2006).

# Transcript abundance estimation and identification of constitutively and differentially expressed gene sets

The alignment-based quantification method RSEM was used to estimate transcript abundance of all 36,529 identified transcripts based on fragments per kilobase per transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) (Figure 1). A correlation matrix and a cluster dendrogram were performed in order to validate the reproducibility of the biological replicates within all the different tissues (Figure S2). This correlation analysis of global transcript abundance between plant tissues showed two main groups of organs: the seed (at 8, 10, 14 and 18 DAP) and the other plant tissues (i.e. leaf, stem, root, flower, pod) (Figure S2). This observation based on the global transcript abundance could be explained by the specialized functions and differentiation during seed development that might involve different set of transcripts with differential expression in other tissues.

Genes displaying a stable or constitutive expression in all different plant tissues represent useful information for transcript normalization in comparative gene expression analysis (i.e. housekeeping genes) such as quantitative RT-PCR or probehybridization approaches. In order to identify stably expressed genes in all the different analysed tissues, we calculated the coefficient of variation (CV) of each transcript covering all the six major plant tissues (i.e. leaf, root, stem, flower, seed and pod). The CV is a good representation of the dispersion of the gene expression. Values ranged from 3% to 245%. Using a threshold of CV<10%, we identified 116 reference genes that may be suitable to use as internal normalizers of gene expression. In order to provide a useful list for further study, we divided this catalogue into 3 sub-lists according to the transcript abundance: 69 transcripts with low abundance (FPKM<10), 30 transcripts with medium abundance (FPKM between 10 and 20) and 17 transcripts with high abundance (FPKM>20) (Table S6). According to our contrasted dataset containing all major tissues and the time-course of seed developmental stages, we did not identified transcripts following constitutive expressions in the major tissues and during the seed development. For this reason, we separately analysed transcripts constitutively expressed during the seed development according to the same criteria than the other tissues. We identified 74 transcripts with stable expression throughout the seed developmental stages, for seed-focused analyses (Table S6).

Genes displaying preferential or specific gene expression in a particular plant tissue are important in the understanding of the specialized processes occurring within these tissues. In our analysis, we identified 1,197 transcripts with specific tissue expression (i.e. only detected in one tissue). 25 were identified as leaf-specific, 5 as stem-specific, 259 as root-specific, 210 as flower-specific, 14 as pod-specific and 684 as seed-specific (Table S7). We also identified 507 transcripts with preferential tissue expression (i.e. more than 20 times more abundant in a particular tissue) (Table S7). Considering the 1,704 transcripts with preferential or specific expression, we calculated the z-score to visualize these Black-eyed pea transcripts across all different tissues. A hierarchical clustering analysis was performed using

these tissue-specific or preferentially expressed genes using z-score as an indicator of the tissue specificity (i.e. high value indicates a high specificity) (Figure 2).

This list of tissue-specific genes represents significant information for targeted gene expression, and also to have insight into the specialized process occurring in these tissues. In order to further validate the annotations and the expression profiles of our dataset, we looked into the specific processes occurring in different tissues by an over-representation analysis (ORA) of tissues specific genes in different organs (Figure S3). As expected, we observed over-representation of predictable specialized processes in different plant tissues such as functional classes related to 'cell wall' and 'apoplast transport' in pod wall, 'pollen tube' in flower and 'aleurone grain' and 'storage lipid' in seed. The detailed over-representation analyses using the GO terms of all the specific genes for the different plant tissues are presented in Figure S3.

# Implementation of the VuGEA, a webserver to store, display and analyse transcriptomics data

To provide open access to the data generated in this study, we generated a web accessible database, called the Vigna Unguiculata Gene Expression Atlas (VuGEA, website http://vugea.noble.org). This webserver conserves the same architecture and tools that designed for the Medicago truncatula and Lotus japonicus gene expression atlas (He et al., 2009). All the identified sequences and corresponding gene IDs, annotations and transcripts abundance information are stored in this webserver and are accessible by a user-friendly interface. Transcripts sequences and abundance across the tissues can be obtained by a "search" function using the blackeyed pea ID, ortholog ID (from Medicago, Arabidopsis, Lotus and Soybean), gene description keywords, GO annotation or TF family name. Transcripts can also be found based on gene sequence using a blast homology search tool from a nucleic or protein sequence as query. The current version of the webserver offers two analytical modules: a multi-transcript viewer to visualize expression profiles of multiple transcripts and a co-expression analysis tool to identify positively or negatively co-expressed transcripts in selected tissues (using Pearson or Spearman correlation methods). Finally, we linked the three legume gene expression atlases

(Medicago, Lotus and Black-eyed pea) to have access to orthologous gene information from any of these gene expression atlases, allowing users to directly access expression profiles of orthologous gene(s) in other legume species. This webserver also allows users to download all or selected batches of data generated in this study.

This current version of the VuGEA contains expression data from six major plant tissues and two time-courses of pod wall and seed development, which represent a total of 27 RNA-seq experiments. This webserver will be updated with the latest gene annotation and with newly generated expression data in the future.

### Seed and pod development

Seed and pod developments are crucial aspects to study because of the social and economical importance of black-eyed pea in the human and animal diets due to the high content of seed protein. In order to understand seed and pod developments, we harvested and weighted pod walls and seeds every two days from 6 DAP to 22 DAP (i.e seed maturity) (Figure 3). We first observed that the number of seeds per pod was variable ranging from 6 to 16 with an average of  $11.2 \pm 1.9$  seeds per pod (N>50 pods). For this reason, we normalized the pod wall weight according to its seed content. We observed that the pod wall weight increased until 12 DAP suggesting the accumulation of nutrients, mainly resulting from the remobilization of nutrients from the plant to the pods. Then, between 12 and 18 DAP, pod weight decreased gradually suggesting the nutrient remobilization from pod to seeds. Finally at 18DAP, pod walls were dried, which explain the constant weight until 22 DAP. From the seed part, we observed the three distinctive seed developmental phases according to the weight change. Until 8DAP, the constant seed weight indicated that the seeds were at the embryogenesis phase. Then, from 8DAP to 16DAP, the gradual increase of the seed weight indicated the early maturation or seed filling phase, which is marked by the accumulation of macromolecules into the seed. Finally, from 16DAP to maturity, the slight decrease of seed weight indicated the onset of late seed maturation, which is marked by an important loss of water and the entry into dormancy (Figure 3).

In this study, we profiled transcript expression at three pod stages representing onset of pod nutrient accumulation at 6DAP (P6), active nutrient accumulation at 10DAP (P10) and nutrient remobilization to seeds at 16DAP (P16). Regarding seed development, we performed transcript profiling at four stages representing the end of embryogenesis at 8DAP (S8), the onset of seed filling at 10DAP (S10), the end of seed filling at 14DAP (S14) and finally the desiccation phase at 18DAP (S18) (Figure 3).

#### Transcriptional regulation of Black-eyed pea seed development

To continue to evaluate the quality of the *de novo* assembly and the transcript abundance quantification, together with deciphering the black-eyed pea seed development, we analysed the molecular mechanisms occurring between 8 and 18 DAP. For this purpose, we extracted from the whole transcript dataset a subset of 34,164 transcripts expressed during the seed development (33,774 transcripts displaying a CV>30% throughout seed development). According to a figure of merit analysis (FOM), this subset was divided into 5 different clusters according to their expression profile patterns. Using a K-means clustering analysis, we identified cluster "S8" (15,602 transcripts) displaying a maximum of expression at 8 DAP, cluster "S8-S10" (7,505 transcripts) with a maximum expression at 8 and 10 DAP, cluster "S14" (2,324 transcripts) with a maximum expression at 14 DAP, cluster S18 (5,460 transcripts) with a maximum expression at 18 DAP and finally cluster "S8-S10-S18" (3,273 transcripts) expressed throughout the whole seed development except at 14DAP. We performed an over-representation analysis of the functional classes in each of the different clusters to provide an overview of the molecular mechanisms occurring in different clusters. Pageman software (Usadel *et al.*, 2006) and the mapping file generated in our study (Table S5) were used to analyse these clusters using a Fisher's exact test in order to identify molecular mechanisms occurring at different seed developmental phases (Figure 3). Summary of the different functional classes with the highest statistical significances associated with the different clusters is displayed in Figure 4 and detailed analysis of all overrepresentation classes is provided in Table S8.

Transcripts belonging to Cluster S8 with a peak of expression at 8 DAP mainly belong to nucleotide metabolism, DNA synthesis/chromatin structure, cell organization and cell division confirming that the seed is at the embryogenesis stage, which is marked by intense cell division. Other functional classes such as transport, protein targeting, protein.aa activation or secondary metabolites were also over-represented showing the preparation to seed filling. Over-representation analysis revealed that transcripts from cluster S8-S10 belong to photosynthesis and protein.synthesis.ribosomal protein showing that at this stage seeds were having an intense photosynthetic activity and translation as shown in previous reports such as Verdier *et al.* (2013) in Lotus and Repetto *et al.* (2008) in Medicago.

As observed from the seed weight kinetic (Figure 3), 14DAP is marked by an increase of seed weight related to the seed-filling phase. By analysing the cluster "S14" with transcripts displaying a maximum expression at 14DAP, we observed that most of the over-representative functional classes were related to synthesis and accumulation of storage molecules such as 'major CHO metabolism', 'secondary metabolism', 'lipid metabolism' and 'storage protein' classes. In details, at this stage, we observed over-representation of transcripts involved in degradation of starch and synthesis of sucrose, production of secondary metabolites (isoprenoids, phenylpropanoids and flavonoids), and synthesis of tri-acyl glycerols and storage proteins (Figure 4, table S8).

Regarding the cluster "S18", transcripts displaying a maximum expression at 18DAP were mainly belonging to typical desiccation processes such as accumulation of raffinose (from the 'minor CHO metabolism.raffinose family' class), late embryogenesis abundant proteins (LEA) and various stress-related classes (e.g. stress.abiotic, stress.abiotic.heat, heat-shock transcription factor family) (Figure 4, table S8). Finally, from the cluster "S8-S10-S18" with transcripts expressed throughout the whole seed development except at 14DAP, we identified over-representation of classes related to RNA (e.g. RNA processing, RNA binding) and

protein.synthesis.ribosomal protein suggesting intense RNA regulation activity at these stages.

#### miRNA target prediction in seed development

During the seed maturation, seeds are able to initiate mechanisms to tolerate extreme dehydration. In a previous report, Barrera-Figueroa *et al.* (2011) identified 44 drought-associated microRNAs in Black-eved pea. In this study, we analysed if these miRNA involved in drought stress response could serve as molecular signal in seed desiccation mechanisms. First, we predicted putative target genes of these 44 miRNA using psRNATarget algorithm (Dai and Zhao, 2011) and transcript sequence dataset generated in this study. 28 of the miRNAs displayed putative target genes based on our black-eyed pea sequence database with differential transcript abundance during the black-eyed pea seed development (Table S9). Interestingly, two of the miRNAs, Vun295724 336 200 and Vun90329 258 100, were predicted to target auxin response factor genes (i.e. Vun T02367, Vun T13063 and Vun T03046), which are putative orthologs of *AtARF10* and *AtARF16* (Table S9). In Arabidopsis, it has been showed that these genes are regulators of the late seed maturation and germination by controlling ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE 3 (ABI3) gene expression, through a combined action of abscisic acid and auxin (Liu *et al.*, 2013). This result showed that similar miRNAs could perform similar regulation in different tissues, leaf and seed, following a drought/desiccation stress. It also showed that seed desiccation and plant drought stress response in Black-eyed pea might share some common mechanisms during abiotic stress signalling/response.

#### CONCLUSION

In this study, we performed *de novo* assembly and quantification of the black-eyed pea transcriptome in the major plant tissues. We identified, annotated and quantified the expression of 36,529 transcripts across all the major plant organs. From these dataset, we identified 24,866 unique transcript sequences, called unigenes. All the results generated were stored into a gene expression atlas webserver, displaying a user-friendly interface and providing tools for transcriptome

analysis and comparison with other legume species. Using this gene expression atlas, we provided a baseline description of the molecular mechanisms occurring during seed development. Additionally, we revealed that some of the mechanisms identified as drought-associated in the plant could be similarly regulated during the seed desiccation phase and involved the same miRNAs.

The development of this genomic resource for black-eyed pea provides a solid baseline for further functional genomic studies and agronomic improvement of this crop, and for further legume comparative studies.

#### **EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES**

### Plant materials and sample collection

*Vigna unguiculata* (reference cultivar IT97K-499-35) seeds were sowed in 1 gallonpots containing synthetic soil MetroMix-830 and were grown in a growth chamber under controlled conditions at 22/28°C (night/day) temperatures and 14h light/10h dark photoperiod. Tissues were collected from three biological replicates for root, leaf, stem, flower and seed developmental time course and one biological replicate was used for the pod developmental time course (i.e. a total of 27 samples: three root, three leaf, three stem, three flower, three 8dap-seed, three 10dap-seed, three 14dap-seed, three 18dap-seed, one 6dap-pod, one 10dap-pod and one 16dappod samples). Biological replicates were constituted by at least five plants randomly located in the growth chamber. Leaf, stem, root and flower tissues were collected from 5 week-old plants; seeds and empty seeds pods were collected at different developmental stages, i.e. 6, 10 and 16 days after pollination (DAP) for pods and 8, 10, 14 and 18 DAP for seeds. After harvest, collected tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C for RNA extraction.

Fresh seeds and pods were collected every two days and weighted immediately after harvest. Weight curves were determined using replicates collected from several plants.

#### RNA extraction, cDNA library preparation and Illumina sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from one gram of ground tissues using the cetyltrimethyl ammonium bromide and lithium chloride (CTAB/LiCl) method as described in Chang *et al.* (1993). RNA was purified using the RNeasy MinElute cleanup kit (Qiagen) according to manufacturer's instructions. Purified RNA was quantified using a nanodrop ND-100 spectrophotometer (Nanodrop Technologies) and evaluated for integrity using a Bioanalyser 2100 and the RNA 6000 Nano kit (Agilent).

Poly-A purification of mRNA, mRNA fragmentation, synthesis of first and second strand cDNA, end repair, ligation of adapters and enrichment of the cDNA libraries were performed using the 'low sample (LS)' preparation of the TruSeq RNA kit according to manufacturer's instructions (Illumina). All the libraries were paired-end sequenced (PE100) using a HiSeq2500 analyser. To generate longer paired-end reads in order to facilitate the assembly, a 'tissue mix', containing the same amount of RNA from all different tissues, was sequenced using a MiSeq analyser (PE300).

#### Raw read filtering and de novo assembly of contigs

Quality control of short reads was performed on the raw paired-end reads to discard low quality regions using SolexaQA (Q < 17) (Cox *et al.*, 2010), and adapter sequences using cutadapt (v1.3) (Martin 2011). After filtering reads, paired reads with length above 25 bp were referred to as "clean reads". Cleaned reads were *de novo* assembled using the Trinity software package (version v.2.0.6) with parameter setting of "—min\_kmer\_cov2", a fixed k-mer size of 25 and other default parameters (Grabherr *et al.*, 2011). We used the Linux cluster server available at the bioinformatics platform of the Shanghai Centre for Plant Stress Biology using 1TB of maximum memory. The complete Trinity command line used for the assembly process was: "–seqType fq –max\_memory 1000G –left reads1.fq –right reads2.fq –min\_contig\_lenght 200 –CPU 50 –min\_kmer\_cov 2"

#### Transcript quantification

The alignment-based quantification method RSEM from the Trinity suite was used to estimate transcript abundance based on FPKM. Transcripts with abundance superior to one FPKM were considered for further analysis. To remove highly similar or redundant contigs, we merged contigs with higher sequence identity than 95% using CD-HIT-EST.

Reads were mapped to transcript sequences identified from *de novo* assembly for each analysed tissue and stage using BWA (Li and Durbin, 2009). Then, SAMtools flagstat was used to calculate total mapped reads, uniquely mapped reads and perfectly mapped reads (Li *et al.*, 2009).

#### **Annotation of Unigene sequences**

Full-length transcript sequences were compared against the *Arabidopsis thaliana* database (TAIR10) and *Glycine max* database (G\_max\_109) using BLASTX algorithm (e-value 1e-20, best hit method: max\_target\_seqs 1, outfmt 6). Then a Perl script was used to calculate the percentage of the *Arabidopsis thaliana* target and *Glycine max* target alignment. The EST sequences from the Cowpea EST database (http://harvest-web.org/hweb/pickassy.wc) were compared to our contig assembly using BLASTN algorithm (e-value 1e-5, perc\_identity 95, max\_target\_seqs 1, outfmt 6), followed by a Perl script to calculate the percentage of cowpea EST matching our assembled dataset.

Annotation of the *de novo* assembly contig set was achieved using Trinotate (http://trinotate.github.io/), a software included into the Trinity suite, which performed homology search to SwissProt, protein domain identification (PFAM) and EMBL Uniprot databases. Functional annotation of contigs was done by Trinotate using eggnog and GO pathway databases. Mapman functional annotations are provided in a Mapman mapping file generated using Mercator (http://mapman.gabipd.org/web/guest/mercator; Lohse *et al.*, 2013).

Identification of orthologous genes of Soybean, Lotus and Medicago was performed by sequence similarity searches using BLASTX algorithm (e-value 1e-5, bit score 50, max\_target\_seqs 1, outfmt 6) between our assembled contigs and the *Glycine max* (G\_max\_109), *Lotus japonicus* (LjDB v.6) and *Medicago truncatula* (IMGA Mt4.0) gene database. Open reading frames (ORF) prediction of the transcript sequences was performed using the TransDecoder software (http://transdecoder.github.io/) included into the Trinity suite.

#### **Differential gene expression analyses**

Global heatmap analysis of all the black-eyed pea transcripts and correlation analyses were performed using "pheatmap" package from R software. Distance analysis was calculated using pairwise Pearson correlation and average linkage clustering method.

Tissue specific or preferential genes were identified using two criteria: only expression values in one tissue or 20-fold more expressed in a specific tissue with respect to others. GO enrichment analyses of the tissue specific or preferential genes were performed using "topGO" package available on R software (Alexa and Rahnenfurher, 2010). For different tissues, we visualized tissue-specific genes using a heatmap representation of Z-scores. Z-score =  $(T_s-T_a)/SD$ ,  $T_a$  is the mean transcript level for a given gene across all samples,  $T_s$  is the mean transcript level in a specific samples, SD is the standard deviation across all samples.

Housekeeping genes were identified using coefficient of variation (CV), where CV (coefficient of variation) was calculated as SD/mean. Housekeeping genes were selected due to their constant expression across different tissues (i.e. CV<10%).

The seed differential expressed gene set used for seed developmental analysis was made up of transcripts displaying a CV>30% regarding their expression throughout seed development. Then, K-means algorithm was used to cluster seed differential expressed genes using Pearson correlation as a distance metric. Subsequently, PageMan software

(http://mapman.gabipd.org/web/guest/pageman) performed over-representation analysis of functional classes using Fisher's exact test and Bonferroni correction.

### Implementation of the VuGEA web server and RNA-seq data deposition

The VuGEA webserver is using the same software and hardware as the Medicago and Lotus GEA web servers (Benedito *et al.*, 2008; Verdier *et al.*, 2013). All the data were deposited in an in-house MySQL database. The 'biologist friendly' interactive web interface was designed using PHP and Javascript programing combined with additional GNU web development packages as described by He *et al.* (2009). All the features and analytical tools of LjGEA and MtGEA are available the VuGEA web

server. Moreover, links between these three web servers were generated to compare expression of orthologous genes in these three legume species.

Raw and normalized RNA-seq data were deposited and publicly available at the VuGEA web server.
