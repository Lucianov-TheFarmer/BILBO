# **Introduction**

Jute (*Corchorus capsularis* L., Malvaceae s. l.) is an annual herbaceous plant, which is commercially cultivated for the extraction of lignocellulosic bast fibres valued for diverse textile and industrial uses and for different grades of highquality pulp. In a jute stem, bast fibres develop in phloem as triangular wedges of sclerenchymatous fibre cell bundles (FCBs) interspersed with medullary ray cells and other soft tissues (Kundu et al. [2012\)](#page-17-0). As compared with

gelatinous-type bast fibres of flax, hemp and ramie, jute fibre is classified as a xylan-type fibre, similar to those in kenaf and various types of wood cells including vessels and wood parenchyma (Mikshina et al. [2013\)](#page-17-1). A typical jute fibre contains 61 % cellulose, 15.9 % hemicellulose and 13.5 % lignin (Sarkar et al. [2010\)](#page-18-0). However, as high as 15–22 % lignin has also been reported in jute fibres (Sengupta and Palit [2004](#page-18-1); Meshram and Palit [2013\)](#page-17-2). This high lignin content distinguishes jute fibre from other non-wood bast fibres of flax, hemp and ramie including kenaf (Sarkar et al. [2010\)](#page-18-0).

Lignin is a complex polyphenolic polymer formed through dehydrogenative polymerization of three hydroxycinnamyl alcohols (monolignols), viz., *p*-coumaryl alcohol, coniferyl alcohol and sinapyl alcohol resulting in *p*-hydroxyphenyl (H), guaiacyl (G) and syringyl (S) units, respectively (Vanholme et al. [2010\)](#page-18-2). Monolignols are synthesized as a branch of the phenylpropanoid pathway in the cytoplasm near the ER membrane and then transported to the cell wall to get polymerized into lignin (Wang et al. [2013](#page-18-3)). Jute fibre lignin is characterized by an H/G/S composition of 2:32:66 and a high S/G ratio of 2.1, which is comparable with those observed in hardwood lignins (Del Rio et al. [2009](#page-16-0)). The high S-lignin content renders jute fibres more amenable to pulp and paper making due to relative ease in the delignification of the S-lignin during pulping. However, high lignin content has a negative effect on jute fibre separation as well as quality. There exists a negative relationship between fibre fineness and lignin content of jute fibre (Meshram and Palit [2013](#page-17-2)). Besides, the α-carbonyl group of the lignin structure reacts with UVradiation resulting in photo-yellowing of jute products. Hence, jute fibres are not suitable for making finer fabrics and other value-added products such as are made from flax fibres. Thus, development of low-lignin fibres has been a long-standing demand in jute industries.

Lignification is one of the key processes for the differentiation of bast and wood fibres, with both lignin composition and the degree of condensation differing in various layers of the cell wall (Gorshkova et al. [2012\)](#page-17-3). It is an important determinant for bast fibre differentiation in jute (Sengupta and Palit [2004\)](#page-18-1), with a positive relationship between lignin content and secondary cell wall (SCW) thickness (Meshram and Palit [2013](#page-17-2)). Metabolic pathways leading to lignin biosynthesis are elucidated in gelatinoustype bast fibres of hemp (van den Broeck et al. [2008\)](#page-18-4), and specific lignification genes have been identified as targets for reverse genetics (Koziel [2010\)](#page-17-4). In flax, Fenart et al. [\(2010](#page-17-5)) characterized monolignol biosynthetic genes differentially expressed between inner and outer stem tissues. However, there is no information about monolignol genes and pathways that regulate lignin biosynthesis in xylantype bast fibres, except for expression analysis of few monolignol genes in kenaf (Chiaiese et al. [2011\)](#page-16-1). Jute is a self-fertilizing diploid (2*n* = 2*x* = 14) species, with the smallest genome (300 Mbp) amongst the bast fibre crops (Sarkar et al. [2011\)](#page-18-5). It has a moderate resource of secondary phloic fibre-deficient mutants, most unique of which is a development-specific *deficient lignified phloem fibre* (*dlpf*) mutant in which lignification is specifically suppressed in the SCWs of the bast fibres at an early stage of plant growth, while the other vascular tissues remain unaffected irrespective of developmental stages (Sengupta and Palit [2004\)](#page-18-1). A shoot tip-based stable genetic transformation system is now available for jute (Saha et al. [2014](#page-18-6)). These make jute an attractive model to study xylan-type bast fibre differentiation and lignification of SCWs.

RNA-seq based on next-generation sequencing has become a routine approach for rapid and inexpensive development of transcriptomic resources, with a high level of accuracy and sensitivity for both model and nonmodel species (Martin et al. [2013](#page-17-6)). As compared with cDNAand EST-based microarrays, RNA-seq achieves a much higher dynamic range of expression levels, and is capable of de novo assembly and annotation without prior genome sequence knowledge (Martin and Wang [2011](#page-17-7)). This technique has been employed for de novo assembly and characterization of tissue-specific transcriptomes in a number of nonmodel plant species (Wang et al. [2010](#page-18-7); Hao et al. [2011](#page-17-8); Wong et al. [2011;](#page-18-8) Ashrafi et al. [2012](#page-16-2); Li et al. [2012;](#page-17-9) Lulin et al. [2012](#page-17-10); Xu et al. [2012](#page-18-9); Kalra et al. [2013;](#page-17-11) Sablok et al. [2014](#page-18-10)) including the bast fibre crop ramie (Liu et al. [2013b](#page-17-12)). For narrow genetic base, strong sexual incompatibility and lack of compatible breeding resources, conventional pedigree breeding has virtually no scope for developing lowlignin jute varieties. Genetic engineering and mutagenesisbased reverse genetics (e.g., TILLING) are the two most promising options for developing low-lignin jute cultivars with improved fibre qualities. However, jute does not have a reference genome sequence yet and has the least genomic resources (only 909 sequences deposited at NCBI till date). A reference bast (stem outer layer comprising epidermis, cortex and phloem FCBs) transcriptome of jute would not only enable identification of genes involved in bast fibre formation and initiate further functional genomics studies, but also serve as a target for the manipulation of fibre quality traits including lignin content through genomicsassisted molecular approaches.

Here, we report the first comprehensive bast transcriptome(s) of jute using Illumina paired-end sequencing of non-normalized cDNAs of the *dlpf* mutant and its wild-type (WT) followed by de novo assembly and characterization. We used three different approaches to assemble the transcriptome sequences de novo and identified the best assembler that recovered more full-length transcripts (unigenes), thereby reconstructing high-resolution bast transcriptomes of jute. Comparative transcriptomics and metabolic pathway analysis allowed us to discover genes and their isoforms involved in lignin biosynthesis in jute fibres via upstream shikimate-aromatic amino acid (AAA) and downstream monolignol pathways. We also identified bast-related transcription factors (TFs) and major genes involved in C1 metabolism (steps linked to monolignol pathway) and SCW development. Finally, we studied differential expression of selected candidate genes in WT and mutant bast tissues at two different growth stages by quantitative reverse transcriptase PCRs (qRT-PCRs), with an objective to explain the mutant phenotype at the molecular level in terms of lignin and SCW biosyntheses.

## <span id="page-2-0"></span>**Materials and methods**

## Plant material

In the present study, *Corchorus capsularis* L. cv. JRC-212 and its X-ray-irradiated (50 Kr) true-breeding secondary phloic fibre-deficient mutant designated as *dlpf* (INGR No. 04107) were used. As compared with WT, this mutant grows slowly, displays the most undulated phenotype with shorter internodes and wavy petioles/lamina (Online Resource 1a), yields much less fibre after retting and is characterized by ~50 % less lignin (8.5 % as against 18.3 % in WT) but ~30 % more cellulose (66.5 % as against 50.9 % in WT) in its fibre (Sengupta and Palit [2004](#page-18-1)). Plants were raised from seeds in glazed tile pots during summer (April–August; mean day/night temperature: 32.7/24.2 °C; RH: 68.8–93.7 %), according to Sengupta and Palit ([2004\)](#page-18-1) following recommended cultural practices. Stems were harvested from both mutant and WT plants 30 days after germination (DAG) by cutting 10 mm from the base, carefully scrapped using a sharp single-edged razor blade to collect the outer layers including the bast tissues but excluding the inner core tissues (Online Resource 1a) and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Unlike flax (Roach and Deyholos [2007](#page-18-11); Gorshkova et al. [2012\)](#page-17-3) fibre elongation and SCW deposition are not temporally separated along the length of a jute stem (Online Resource 1a), and therefore, no attempts were made to sample and separately analyze the bast tissues segment-wise from top to bottom.

# RNA isolation

Bast tissues collected from at least 10 plants from each sample (S1: WT and S2: mutant) were pooled and used for total RNA isolation using Invitrogen's (Carlsbad, CA) TRIzol® (cat. #15596-026) and PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit (cat. #12183018A), according to manufacturer's instructions. Total RNA was treated with RNase-Free DNase (cat. #79254; Qiagen GmbH, Hilden) for 30 min at 37 °C, checked on 1 % denaturing agarose gel, quantified using NanoDrop 8000 UV–Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham) and tested for quality based on RNA Integrity Number (RIN >7.5) using Agilent's RNA 6000 Pico Kit (cat. #5067-1513) on a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA).

# cDNA library construction and Illumina paired-end sequencing

The cDNA library was prepared for each sample using Illumina's (San Diego, CA) TruSeq® RNA Sample Preparation Kit (cat. #15026495), according to manufacturer's instructions. In brief, poly(A) containing mRNA was purified from total RNA using oligo(dT)-attached magnetic beads, fragmented using divalent cations under elevated temperature and then used as templates for random hexamer-primed synthesis of first-strand cDNA using SuperScript II reverse transcriptase. Second-strand cDNA was synthesized using RNase H and DNA Polymerase I followed by end-repairing of the cDNA fragments, 3′-adenylation of the blunt fragments and ligation of multiple indexing adapters to the ends of the ds cDNA. The products were purified and selectively enriched with PCR to create the final cDNA library. The library for each sample was validated using Caliper's HT DNA High Sensitivity Reagent Kit (cat. #760568) on a LabChip® GX platform (Caliper Life Sciences, Inc., Hopkinton, MA), with a size range of 290–292 bp. For pairedend sequencing, clonal clusters were generated by bridgeamplification of immobilized cDNA templates that have been captured by complementary adapter oligonucleotides covalently bound to the surface of paired-end flow cells on cBot (Illumina) using Illumina's TruSeq® PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (cat. #PE-401-3001). Finally, Illumina's TruSeq® SBS Kit v3-HS (cat. #FC-401-3001) was used to sequence DNA of each cluster with sequencing by synthesis on a flow cell of HiSeq™ 2000 (Illumina) platform, and the sequencing data were transformed by base calling into raw reads in fastq format.

Transcriptome data filtering and de novo assembly

The raw transcriptome sequence reads that did not pass the Illumina failed-chastity (≤1) filter with a chastity threshold of 0.6 on the first 25 cycles were discarded. We also discarded reads with adapter contamination, >20 % of bases with a quality score of Q ≤ 20 (Trimmomatic v0.17), >5 % unknown nucleotides and those mapped to non-coding RNA (rRNA, tRNA and miRNA) sequences (Rfam database v11.0) using Bowtie (v0.12.9; Langmead [2010](#page-17-13)). High-quality clean reads were used to assemble the transcriptome of each sample de novo using three assemblers, viz., CLC Genomics Workbench (v6.0; CLC bio, Aarhus), SOAPdenovo-Trans (Xie et al. [2014](#page-18-12)) and Trinity (version r2013-08-14; Grabherr et al. [2011](#page-17-14)). Since CLC does not perform gap filling, we used SOAPdenovo's GAP-Closer for filing 'N's in scaffold sequences using pairedend information resulting in the generation of unigenes. SOAPdenovo-Trans-127mer was used at *k*-mer lengths of 39, 41, 43, 45 and 47, with -*F* parameter that performed gap filling after the generation of scaffolds and yielded unigenes by re-using paired-end reads to fill in the gaps between the scaffolds, as described by Lulin et al. [\(2012](#page-17-10)). We used SOAPdenovo-Trans to assemble unigenes, with an optimized *k*-mer length of 47. Trinity was used with the default *k*-mer length to obtain unigenes, following the method as described by Liu et al. [\(2013b\)](#page-17-12). Next, we assessed the quality of assembled unigenes by determining the presence and length of gaps in the sequences. The gap distribution (%) was presented as the number of 'N's divided by the sequence length of the assembled unigene (Wang et al. [2010\)](#page-18-7). Since a reference genome is not available for jute, we used a simulation-based tool ESTcalc (Wall et al. [2009](#page-18-13)) to estimate the depth and breadth of our transcriptome dataset. To determine average read depth and distribution of unique-mapped reads of the assembled unigenes, we realigned all usable reads to the unigenes using SOAPaligner (v2.20; Li et al. [2009\)](#page-17-15) allowing up to 2-base mismatches and implemented the workflow as shown in Online Resource 1b. Based on comparative quality assessment (see "[Results"](#page-4-0)), we finally selected the unigenes assembled by Trinity and determined their sequence directions (5′ → 3′) by aligning them to protein databases in the priority order of NCBI Nr > SwissProt > KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes; Kanehisa et al. [2008\)](#page-17-16) > COG (clusters of orthologous group; Tatusov et al. [2003](#page-18-14)) using BLASTx (*E* value <10−<sup>5</sup> ), with unigene sequences having significant hits in one database not used to align to the subsequent database(s). These BLASTx results were used to extract the CDS from the unigene sequences, which were then translated into peptide sequences. For unigene sequences that remained unaligned to either of the databases, the CDS were predicted by ESTScan (v3.0; Iseli et al. [1999](#page-17-17)) and then translated into peptide sequences.

Gene annotation, functional analysis and metabolic pathway mapping

Trinity-assembled unigenes were annotated by both sequence-based (Nr, SwissProt and KEGG) and domainbased (COG) alignments (*E* value <10−<sup>5</sup> ). We processed the Nr-annotated BLASTx results with the Blast2GO (Conesa et al. [2005](#page-16-3)) program to obtain the GO (gene ontology; Ashburner et al. [2000](#page-16-4)) annotations of the unigenes, with corresponding enzyme commission (EC) numbers. Next, we used the web-based tool WEGO (Ye et al. [2006](#page-19-0)) to obtain GO functional classifications. The unigene sequences were also aligned to the COG database to retrieve and classify COG functional categories of the transcriptome (*E* value <10−<sup>5</sup> ). For metabolic pathway analysis, Blast2GO-aligned unigene sequences with corresponding ECs were mapped to the KEGG database using BLASTx (*E* value <10−<sup>5</sup> ) followed by the identification of BRITE (Kanehisa et al. [2012\)](#page-17-18) functional hierarchies by submitting the sequences to the KAAS (KEGG Automatic Annotation Server; Moriya et al. [2007\)](#page-18-15) that further annotated sequences with KO (KEGG orthology; Mao et al. [2005](#page-17-19)) identifiers. Finally, we identified the genes involved in phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan (PATH: ko00400, i.e., shikimate-AAA) and monolignol (PATH: ko00940, i.e., phenylpropanoid) biosyntheses and reconstructed the entire pathway leading to lignin biosynthesis. Similarly, we also identified other major genes associated with C1 metabolism (steps linked to monolignol biosynthesis) and SCW development using the BLASTx (*E* value <10−<sup>5</sup> ) annotation of the KEGG and the other databases as mentioned above.

Discovery of genes and isoforms for lignin biosynthesis and SCW development

The peptide sequences of the unigenes corresponding to genes involved in lignin biosynthesis and SCW development were rechecked with ExPASy Translate Tool ([http://](http://www.expasy.org/tools.dna.html) [www.expasy.org/tools.dna.html](http://www.expasy.org/tools.dna.html)) followed by searching for open reading frames (ORFs) using the NCBI ORF Finder [\(http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/gorf/](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/gorf/)). Next, they were checked for the presence of conserved amino acid motifs (*E* value <10−10) using the web-based tool Motif Search [\(http://www.genome.jp/tools/motif/](http://www.genome.jp/tools/motif/)), and unrelated proteins were removed. We used the online tool EMBOSS Matcher (Rice et al. [2000;](#page-18-16) [http://www.ebi.](http://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/psa/emboss_matcher/) [ac.uk/Tools/psa/emboss\\_matcher/](http://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/psa/emboss_matcher/)) to identify homologous isoforms of genes shared in the mutant and its WT. For comparative sequence analyses of homologous isoforms of genes, their peptide sequences were aligned by MUSCLE (Edgar [2004](#page-16-5)) using the software Unipro UGENE (v1.15.0; Okonechnikov et al. [2012\)](#page-18-17) followed by sequence similarity search and analysis employing the web-based (ENDscript 2 Web server; [http://www.end](http://www.endscript.ibcp.fr)[script.ibcp.fr\)](http://www.endscript.ibcp.fr) sequence alignment renderer ESPript 3.0 (v.11/9/2014; Robert and Gouet [2014;](#page-18-18) [http://www.espript.](http://www.espript.ibcp.fr) [ibcp.fr](http://www.espript.ibcp.fr)), which was implemented in the EXP mode, with SimilarityType set at the default %Equivalent (percentage of equivalent amino acid residues based on physico-chemical properties), SimilarityGlobalScore at 0.7, SimilarityDiffScore at 0.5 and alignments output color scheme at Flashy. For phylogenetic analysis, the fulllength peptide sequences of all genes from different plant species were downloaded and aligned to corresponding peptide sequences of both WT and mutant using MUS-CLE. The neighbor-joining tree for each gene family was constructed using MEGA 6.0 (Tamura et al. [2013](#page-18-19)), with amino acid substitution type, Poisson model, uniform rates among sites, pairwise deletion for gaps/missing data and 1000 bootstraps.

## Transcription factors (TFs) identification

For identification of TFs and transcriptional regulatory families, we searched the translated peptide sequences of the bast transcriptomes of both WT and mutant against all the TF protein sequences from PlnTFDB database (Pérez-Rodríguez et al. [2010](#page-18-20)) using local NCBI Blast-2.2.23+ blastp algorithm (*E* value <10−10).

## Gene validation and expression analysis by qRT-PCRs

Sixteen candidate unigenes (Online Resource 2) identified for their potential roles in lignin biosynthesis and SCW development were selected for differential gene expression analyses using qRT-PCRs. Total RNA was extracted and purified from the bast tissues of WT and mutant plants at 30 and 60 DAG following the method as described above. The purified RNA was reverse-transcribed using RevertAid H Minus First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (cat. #K1613; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham), according to manufacturer's instructions. Gene-specific primers (Online Resource 3) were designed based on conserved region of each unigene common to both WT and mutant using PrimerQuest® (Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc., Coralville) and synthesized from Xcelris Labs Ltd. (Ahmedabad). Primer efficiencies were determined in triplicate reaction sets using 3 concentrations (1.0, 2.0 and 5.0 ng) of the WT template and calculated using the software REST-384© Version 2 (Pfaffl et al. [2002\)](#page-18-21). Before setting up the final reaction set, melting-curve analysis was performed to check for primer-dimer formation or non-specific amplification. The qRT-PCRs were performed using the Light-Cycler® 480 SYBR Green I Master (cat. #04707516001) protocol in 20-µl reaction volumes (in a 384-well plate) on a LightCycler® 480 (Roche Diagnostics Corporation, Indianapolis) real-time PCR platform, with WT and 18S gene (housekeeping) as experimental and endogenous controls, respectively. There were three biological replicates for each treatment, with three technical replicates per reaction. Relative gene expression in mutant bast tissues was estimated and normalized using the 'E-method' of LightCycler® 480 software version 1.5 based on the model as defined by Pfaffl ([2001\)](#page-18-22).

## Lignin and SCW histochemistry

Hand-cut cross sections were prepared from upper, middle and lower stem segments sampled at 30 DAG and stained with Mäule (KMnO4-HCl/NH3) reagent that gives varying intensities of rose-red coloration with S-lignin as described previously (Kundu et al. [2012](#page-17-0)). Sections were also stained with zinc chloro-iodide (Hertzberg reagent) that does not react with cell wall-bound molecules in the manner of the Mäule reagent but intercalates into the cell wall, thereby distinguishing tissues with variable numbers of SCWs (Patten et al. [2007](#page-18-23)). Hertzberg stain was prepared as described in [http://www.cameo.mfa.org/wiki/Hertzberg\\_stain](http://www.cameo.mfa.org/wiki/Hertzberg_stain) and 1–2 drops were applied to tissue sections for 1 min in dark at room temperature. Toludine Blue (1 %, w/v) was used for general histological staining. Microscopic observations were made under a Zeiss Axiskop 40 (Carl Zeiss, Jena) bright-field microscope fitted with a Canon PowerShot A80 camera system.

Cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD) assay

CAD was measured in mutant and WT bast tissues at 30 DAG, according to Mitchell et al. [\(1994](#page-17-20)). Spectrophotometric assays were performed at 25 °C for 30 min in 1.0 ml of 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.8), 100 μM coniferyl alcohol (Sigma, St. Louis), 100 μM NADP (Sigma) and 200 μg crude protein extract. The conAD activity was monitored by following the formation of coniferaldehyde (*<sup>ε</sup>* <sup>=</sup> 2.10 <sup>×</sup> <sup>10</sup><sup>4</sup> <sup>M</sup>−<sup>1</sup> cm−<sup>1</sup> ) at 400 nm.

## Functional annotations of bast-related unigenes

## Differential expression of bast-related candidate genes

We performed qRT-PCRs to study differential expression of selected candidate unigenes in mutant and WT bast tissues. Of the 12 genes involved in the shikimate-AAA and monolignol pathways, eight (CcDHD-SDH2, CcSK1, CcCS1, CcPAL1, Cc4CL2, CcCCR1, CcCCoAOMT2 and CcCAD7) were under-expressed and four (CcDAHPS2, CcC4H2, CcF5H1 and CcCOMT1) over-expressed in mutant bast tissues at 30 DAG (Fig. 7). Maximum decline in transcript levels occurred for CcCAD7 (~11-fold) followed by CcPAL1 (~5-fold). A ~4-fold decline in expression was observed for CcCS1, while CcDHD-SDH2 and CcSK1 were slightly under-expressed. All over-expressed genes were ~8- to ~11-fold more highly expressed in mutant than in WT bast tissues. At 60 DAG, CcSK1 and CcCCoAOMT2 were over-expressed (~2- to 3-fold) accompanied by a slight increase in the CcPAL1 transcript levels. However, all four genes over-expressed at 30 DAG were under-expressed at 60 DAG, with CcF5H1 and CcCOMT1 having the lowest transcript levels (~5- to 6-fold decline). Except for CcFLA15, CcCesA7 and CcFLA6 involved in SCW development were over-expressed (~3- to 6-fold) in mutant bast tissues at 30 DAG, but they were much underexpressed (~11- to 21-fold) at 60 DAG. CcFLA15, which was not differentially expressed at 30 DAG, was ~12-fold less expressed at 60 DAG. A ~6-fold increment in CcDFR1 expression occurred in mutant bast tissues at 30 DAG, but it was ~18-fold less expressed at 60 DAG.

The expression profile of CAD gene was complemented by determining CAD activities in bast tissues of both mutant and WT at 30 DAG. # Discussion

# Bast transcriptome(s) of jute

We produced the first reference bast transcriptome(s) of jute by Illumina paired-end sequencing and de novo assembly. For a species like jute without a reference genome, de novo assembly is one of the most critical steps that affects not only the number of genes sequenced and their percent coverage, but also downstream applications of transcriptome data (Feldmeyer et al. 2011). Here, we demonstrated that Trinity afforded the best method for de novo assembly of the bast transcriptome(s). The relative superiority of Trinity to other short-read assemblers was reported earlier (Duan et al. 2012; Xu et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2013a). We assembled 34,163 WT unigenes, with the longest average length (1442 bp) and N50 (1999 bp) and the highest proportion (55.2 %) of  $\geq 1$  kb unigenes attained till date in plants, to the best of our knowledge, from Illumina pairedend transcriptome sequencing using both HiSeq 2000 and GA-II platforms (Wang et al. 2010; Hao et al. 2011; Wong et al. 2011; Agarwal et al. 2012; Ashrafi et al. 2012; Duan et al. 2012; Li et al. 2012; Lulin et al. 2012; Xu et al. 2012; Kalra et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2013a, b; Zou et al. 2013). Since mapping accuracy increases with increasing contig size (Gibbons et al. 2009), ~77-79 % of our WT and mutant unigenes were functionally annotated by BLASTx. This is comparable to that obtained in ramie (~78 %; Liu et al. 2013b) and higher than that reported in other plant species (references as above). If we assume that jute contains as many genes as in ramie (34,192 annotated unigenes representing the whole transcriptome; Liu et al. 2013b), our bast-related WT unigenes would represent ~77 % of genes in jute.

Our bast transcriptomes had high sequence similarities with the sequences of V. vinifera (~30 %), R. communis (~29 %) and P. trichocarpa (~26 %). Intriguingly, there were high sequence similarities between ramie and these three species in the same priority order, however, with higher similarity with that of V. vinifera than that of the other two species (Liu et al. 2013b). In APG-III (The Angiosperm Phylogeny Group 2009), all these species are classified as core eudicots in the superorder Rosids that consists of subgroups Fabids, Malvids and Vitales. Jute belongs to Malvales in Malvids, ramie (Urticaceae) to Rosales in Fabids, R. communis (Euphorbiaceae) and P. trichocarpa (Salicaceae) to Malpighiales in Fabids and V. vinifera (Vitaceae) to Vitales. Based on sequence similarity results, Liu et al. (2013b) proposed to place Vitales closer to Rosales. Since there was not much difference in the ratio of transcriptome sequence similarity between jute and either of these species that belong to Malpighiales and Vitales, we support such a placement. We assigned a substantial proportion of annotated unigenes to COG and GO classifications, indicating that the bast transcriptome(s) represents a wide diversity of transcripts involved in cellular and metabolic processes, with predominant binding and catalytic activities. The mapping of the highest percentage of unigenes (~19-21 %) to KEGG metabolism pathways indicates bast fibre development in jute involves active metabolic processes mainly driven by carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism and transport, in agreement with results reported in flax and other bast fibre crops (Fenart et al. [2010](#page-17-5); see Gorshkova et al. [2012;](#page-17-3) Long et al. [2012](#page-17-23)). More than 200 bast-related unigenes were mapped to plant hormone signal transduction pathway, implying the role of a combination of hormonal signals (Lev-Yadun [2010\)](#page-17-27) in bast fibre formation in jute (Meshram and Palit [2013](#page-17-2)). It is thus obvious that the bast transcriptome sequences generated here will be a valuable genomics resource for jute research.

## Jute fibre lignification: genes and biosynthetic pathways

We discovered maximum isoforms of candidate shikimate-AAA and monolignol genes in jute bast tissues as compared with that reported in other bast fibre crops (De Pauw et al. [2007](#page-16-7); van den Broeck et al. [2008](#page-18-4); Fenart et al. [2010;](#page-17-5) Koziel [2010\)](#page-17-4) and eudicot species like *Acacia* spp. (Wong et al. [2011](#page-18-8)), *A. thaliana* (Raes et al. [2003](#page-18-24)) and *P. trichocarpa* (Shi et al. [2010\)](#page-18-28). Phylogenetic analyses confirmed the identities of all these genes, reiterating the usefulness of this approach for validating genes involved in monolignol biosynthetic pathway (Xu et al. [2009;](#page-18-29) Shi et al. [2010](#page-18-28)). The absence of gaps in our bast transcriptome assemblies might have enabled accurate protein prediction because missing data seriously constrain sequence comparison and analysis (Wong et al. [2011](#page-18-8)). For 6 out of 26 gene families, more isoforms were identified in the mutant than in the WT (Online Resource 9) despite that more unigenes were assembled for the latter than the former. If we exclude the possibility of sampling biases, this might be due to relatively greater sequencing depth attained for the mutant as compared with that for the WT (Wong et al. [2011](#page-18-8)). Nevertheless, few candidate genes (e.g., *PAI* and *PAT*) were not detected in the mutant, which may be due to sequence irregularities during the assembly process. Although nearly half of the mutant isoforms had strictly conserved WT residues, many were characterized by stretches of extra or missing residues with or without single to multiple aa substitutions. It is difficult to substantiate at this stage whether these changes have accrued from sequence irregularities during the assembly process or from nonconservative mutations, although the former scenario seems most likely. Nevertheless, the identification of few mutant isoforms having strict homologies with their corresponding WT homologs but differing in 1–2 aa residues points to potential mutations in coding regions (see below). However, these results must be interpreted with caution because identification of somatic mutations or genomic variants from RNA-seq data, particularly in the absence of whole-genome or -exome sequencing data, remains a challenge due to the transcriptome's intrinsic complexity, e.g., splicing (Piskol et al. [2013](#page-18-30)). A robust experimental approach like RNA-seq-based bulk segregant analysis (BSA) together with splice-aware mapping of reads could only unequivocally identify candidate mutations (Miller et al. [2013\)](#page-17-28).

Our results indicate that lignin biosynthesis occurs in jute fibres via well-conserved shikimate-AAA and monolignol pathways (Fig. [4](#page-7-1); Vanholme et al. [2010;](#page-18-2) Maeda and Dudareva [2012](#page-17-21); Hao and Mohnen [2014\)](#page-17-22). In plant, Phe is primarily synthesized from prephenate via the arogenate route (Tzin and Galili [2010;](#page-18-25) Maeda and Dudareva [2012](#page-17-21)). The discovery of only one isoform of PPA-AT that converts prephenate to arogenate is not unexpected because the catalytic efficiency of this enzyme is ~50- to 250-fold higher than that of the bifunctional ADT-PDT (see Maeda and Dudareva [2012](#page-17-21)). However, the discovery of a large number of PPY-AT isoforms that convert phenylpyruvate (PPY) to Phe indicates that the PPY route in Phe biosynthesis may also be active in jute fibres. This may perhaps ensure a higher metabolic flux to Phe biosynthesis to sustain an increased production of monolignols in lignin-rich jute fibres. PPY also serves as a precursor for a number of secondary metabolites (Tzin and Galili [2010\)](#page-18-25). Further, the discovery of a large number of HCT isoforms in bast tissues indicates that the phenylpropanoid metabolic flux from the *p*-coumaryl CoA branch point is mostly routed to the monolignol instead of the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway, resulting in a higher accumulation of monolignols (Hoffmann et al. [2004\)](#page-17-29). Although several classes of CCoAOMT were identified, only one class of COMT that converts 5-OH-conferaldehyde to sinapaldehyde was present in bast tissues, in agreement with that reported in plants (Raes et al. [2003\)](#page-18-24) including hemp (van den Broeck et al. [2008](#page-18-4)). C1 metabolism provides SAM as a methyl donor for methyl transfer reactions catalyzed by CCoAOMT and COMT during monolignol biosynthesis (van den Broeck et al. [2008](#page-18-4)). The expression of several isoforms of SAMS, which converts methionine to SAM, together with other related enzymes of C1 metabolism such as MS and SAHase in bast tissues reflects the importance of methylation of lignin precursors in jute fibre lignification.

CAD is the final enzyme in the monolignol biosynthetic pathway that transforms cinnamaldehydes to their corresponding alcohol derivatives (Vanholme et al. [2010](#page-18-2)). The highest number of CAD protein isoforms identified in both WT and mutant, as compared with that in *Arabidopsis* (Raes et al. [2003](#page-18-24)), indicates a key role of this enzyme in jute fibre lignification (see below). Of note, cDNAs encoding CAD proteins were not detected in bast tissues of hemp, prompting the authors to conclude that CAD is neither a regulatory nor a key enzyme for lignification of hemp fibres (van den Broeck et al. [2008\)](#page-18-4). SAD instead of CAD has long been proposed to be involved in S-monolignol biosynthesis in angiosperms (Li et al. [2001](#page-17-30)). Although two of our CAD isoforms were found to be related to putative SAD proteins (Online Resource 11), detailed functional analysis is required to ascertain the involvement of SAD in S-monolignol biosynthesis in jute fibres that are rich in S-lignins.

Molecular phenotype of the *dlpf* mutant. I: validating lignin genes in jute fibres

Using Mäule staining (KMnO4-HCl/NH3) that specifically reacts with syringylpropanoid moieties of S-lignin to produce a rose-red color (Chapple et al. [1992](#page-16-11)), we showed that lignin deficiency in the *dlpf* fibres (see "[Materials and](#page-2-0) [methods"](#page-2-0); Sengupta and Palit [2004](#page-18-1)) results from a sharp reduction of S-lignin deposition in SCWs of fibre cells, as a result of which thin-walled, less-lignified fibre cells are formed that fail to make secondary FCBs typical of jute (Online Resource 1a). Maximum reduction of S-lignin content occurs in the basal portion of the mutant, as evident from Hertzberg staining that stains jute fibre cell walls from bright yellow to orange-red with decreasing layers of SCW. As the basal part develops earlier than the middle and upper parts of the stem, this result implies that lignification of phloem-fibre SCW is specifically suppressed in the mutant at an early developmental stage (Sengupta and Palit [2004](#page-18-1)). The characteristic growth stage-specific undulated, reduced-size phenotype of this mutant (see ["Materi](#page-2-0)[als and methods"](#page-2-0); Online Resource 1a) was attributed to a lower PAL activity at an early growth stage (Sengupta and Palit [2004](#page-18-1)). Indeed, we detected a marked decline in the expression of *CcPAL1* in mutant bast tissues at an early growth stage, however, its expression was restored to the normal level at the later growth stage. This may be the reason for why this mutant progressively reverts to a normal phenotype from 60 DAG onward (Online Resource 1c). The mutant isoform of CcPAL1 differs from its WT homolog by 5 missing residues together with a single aa substitution (see Results; Online Resource 10). However, the restoration of *CcPAL1* expression in mutant bast tissues at the later growth stage may suggest that these changes are not responsible for CcPAL1 disruption. *CcPAL1* down-regulation was concomitant with up-regulation of *CcDAHPS2* but down-regulation of *CcDHD*-*SDH2* and *CcCS1*, the three most important genes of the upstream shikimate pathway. However, at the later growth stage, all three of them were down-regulated in mutant bast tissues. DHD-SDH is a bifunctional enzyme that catalyzes the dehydration of 3-dehydroquinate to 3-dehydroshkimate followed by its reversible reduction to shikimate (Maeda and Dudareva [2012](#page-17-21)), while CS converts EPSP to chorismate, a central branch point metabolite in the synthesis of AAA and secondary metabolites (Tzin and Galili [2010\)](#page-18-25). By increasing the effective concentration of the intermediate substrate, 3-dehydroshikimate, DHD-SDH controls the metabolic flux through the shikimate pathway (Tzin and Galili [2010](#page-18-25)). Therefore, *CcDHD*-*SDH2* down-regulation in mutant bast tissues is presumed to have depressed the metabolic flux through the shikimate pathway. CcmDHD-SDH2 differs from its WT homolog CcDHD-SDH2 only by a single aa substitution (see ["Results"](#page-4-0); Online Resource 10). It is not unlikely that this single aa substitution might have reduced its substrate-binding efficiency. However, this interpretation will remain mostly speculative till the result is complemented by in-depth enzyme activity and kinetic (*K*m) analyses. Taken together, our results thus clearly demonstrate that a block upstream in the shikimate pathway is associated with PAL disruption in the mutant, thereby reiterating that lignin biosynthesis is a coordinated metabolic network (Anterola and Lewis [2002\)](#page-16-6) and PAL is not the critical entry step in the monolignol pathway (van den Broeck et al. [2008](#page-18-4)).

Since PAL deficiency would repress the biosynthesis of cinnamate from Phe, the up-regulation of *CcC4H2* in mutant bast tissues at an early growth stage is difficult to explain, especially when *Cc4CL2*, the next gene in the monolignol pathway that encodes the enzyme catalyzing *p*-coumarate conversion, was co-down-regulated with *CcPAL1*. One plausible explanation may be differential colocalization of PAL isoforms and C4H (Achnine et al. [2004](#page-16-12)). Similarly, *CcF5H1* and *CcCOMT1* up-regulation and *CcCCoAOMT2*, *CcCCR1* and *CcCAD7* down-regulation in mutant bast tissues at an early growth stage might have resulted from a feedforward regulation by a diminished pool of an unknown compound derived from cinnamate (Lee et al. [2011\)](#page-17-31). CcCOMT1, the sole WT isoform in jute bast tissues (see above), is required for S-lignin production (Fig. [4](#page-7-1); Hao and Mohnen [2014\)](#page-17-22). The absence of any candidate mutations in the coding region of its mutant homolog, which is characterized by conserved WT residues (Online Resource 10), categorically proves that S-lignin-deficient *dlpf* is not a COMT mutant. However, *CcCAD7* remained strongly and consistently down-regulated in mutant bast tissues irrespective of growth stages, perhaps one of the most decisive changes that seems to account for chronic lignin deficiency in mutant phloemfibre cell wall. This was supported by a three-fold decline in CAD (conAD) activity in mutant bast tissues at 30 DAG. *CcCAD7* down-regulation at the later growth stage was accompanied by several folds reduction in the expression of *CcCCR1*, *CcF5H1* and *CcCOMT1* that might have resulted in a diminished pool of CAD substrates (cinnamaldehydes). The mutant isoform of CcCAD7 lacks 18 WT residues upstream (Online Resource 10). Since CAD is known to have multiple substrate (*p*-coumaraldehyde, coniferaldehyde and sinapaldehyde; Fig. [4](#page-7-1)) specificities (Hao and Mohnen [2014\)](#page-17-22), detailed functional analysis supported by enzyme kinetic (*K*m) studies would allow us to confirm whether these deletions are responsible for reduced substrate-binding efficiency and/or altered substrate specificity of CcCAD7 in mutant bast tissues (Saballos et al. [2009](#page-18-31)). *CAD* down-regulation has been shown to reduce the lignin content and/or its composition (Sibout et al. [2003](#page-18-32); Fu et al. [2011](#page-17-32); Fornalé et al. [2012](#page-17-33); Bouvier d'Yvoire et al. [2013](#page-16-13)), change the composition and structure of flax fibre cell wall (Preisner et al. [2014](#page-18-33)) and specifically repress the deposition of S-lignin units (Trabucco et al. [2013\)](#page-18-34). However, *CcCAD7* down-regulation did not affect plant growth and development in the mutant, except for increased susceptibility to *Macrophomina* stem rot (data not shown). In flax, *CAD* silencing did not affect plant growth and development, but resulted in decreased resistance to *Fusarium* wilt (Preisner et al. [2014](#page-18-33)). CAD down-regulation has also been shown not to severely compromise plant growth and development in *Brachypodium distachyon* (Bouvier d'Yvoire et al. [2013](#page-16-13)). These effects make CAD a promising target for reducing fibre lignin content of high-yielding jute varieties using forward or reverse genetics approaches.

# Molecular phenotype of the *dlpf* mutant. II: validating SCW genes in jute fibres

Chemical analysis showed that the average lignin content in mutant fibre (de-waxed) was 8.5 % as compared to 18.3 % in WT fibre (i.e., ~50 % lignin deficiency); however, α-cellulose content was significantly higher (~30 %) in mutant (66.5 %) fibre than in WT (50.9 %) fibre (Sengupta and Palit [2004](#page-18-1)). This is consistent with our observation of a significant up-regulation of *CcCesA7* in mutant bast tissues at an early growth stage. The promotion of cellulose deposition linked to suppression of lignin biosynthesis is thought to act as a compensatory mechanism that allows metabolic flexibility (Hu et al. [1999\)](#page-17-34). CAD downregulation has been shown to result in increased levels of cellulose and other cell wall polysaccharides (Fornalé et al. [2012](#page-17-33); Preisner et al. [2014](#page-18-33)), possibly to maintain the integrity of the cell wall (Preisner et al. [2014\)](#page-18-33). A sharp downregulation of *CcCesA7* at the later growth stage seems to indicate the cessation of cellulose biosynthesis in lesslignified mutant fibre cells that could not form secondary FCBs (see below). In this context, it is to be noted that the long stretch of WT residues (115 aa) missing in the coding region of the mutant isoform of CcCesA7 seems to have not affected its activity, substantiating further our foregoing interpretation that such types of cryptic changes are associated with sequence irregularities during the assembly process.

FLAs, a subclass of arabinogalactan proteins (Johnson et al. [2003\)](#page-17-24), are involved in coordinating either the deposition of G-layers of the gelatinous-type fibre or the developmental transition of phloem fibres from cell expansion to SCW deposition (Roach and Deyholos [2007\)](#page-18-11). Here, *CcFLA6*, the likely ortholog of *AtFLA11* and *GmFLA11* (Fig. [5\)](#page-10-0), was co-regulated with *CcCesA7* in mutant bast tissues irrespective of growth stages. Interestingly, *AtFLA11*, the closest paralog of poplar tension wood-specific FLA, was reported to be co-regulated with SCW-specific *CesA* genes in *Arabidopsis* (Persson et al. [2005\)](#page-18-35). The FLA transcript enriched during flax phloem fibre development was very closely related to poplar tension wood-specific FLA genes, suggesting its specific role in developing G-layers of the gelatinous-type SCW (Hobson et al. [2010](#page-17-35)). Taken together, it is thus obvious that *CcCesA7* identified in jute bast tissues is SCW-specific, and there is a conserved function for CcFLA6 in coordinating the deposition of S-layers in the xylan-type SCW (fibre) similar to that of G-layers in the gelatinous-type one. The observation that *CcFLA15*, the closest ortholog of *AtFLA17* and *AtFLA18* (Fig. [5](#page-10-0)), was not differentially expressed in mutant bast tissues at an early growth stage but co-down-regulated with *CcCesA7* and *CcFLA6* at the later growth stage points to its putative role in coordinating the developmental transition of phloem fibres from elongation to SCW thickening. This is not unlikely because histological evidence suggests that phloem fibre cells of the mutant complete elongation, but could not mature by thickening of their SCWs with lignin deposition on the cellulose matrix (Online Resource 1a; Sengupta and Palit [2004\)](#page-18-1). The up-regulation of CcDFR1, which converts dihydroquercetin to leucocyanidin in the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway, in mutant bast tissues at an early growth stage may be related to enhanced flavonoid production due to repression of lignin biosynthesis, and the typical reduced-size, undulated phenotype of the mutant may be not due to the alterations of lignin biosynthesis or PAL disruption but to flavonoid-mediated inhibition of auxin transport (Besseau et al. [2007](#page-16-14)). However, Gallego-Giraldo et al. ([2011\)](#page-17-36) have argued that salicylic acid (SA), not flavonoids, mediates the reduced growth of lignin deficient plants, possibly via interference with gibberellic acid signaling or responsiveness. Therefore, our interpretation will remain mostly speculative till the mutant bast tissues are characterized for development-specific accumulation of SA and the whole set of flavonoids.

# Transcriptional control of lignin and SCW biosyntheses in jute fibres

Genome-wide identification of a large number of putative bast-associated TFs belonging to different families underscores the complexities of transcriptional switches underlying lignin biosynthesis and SCW development in jute fibres. Members of zinc fingers (C3H- and C2H2-type), MADS-box, FAR1, WRKY, NAC, MYB-related and AUX/ IAA families may regulate SCW development (Long et al. [2012](#page-17-23)), with NAC domain TFs possibly acting as the master switches of the regulatory network that controls xylan-type SCW deposition and programmed cell death of fibres (see Gorshkova et al. [2012](#page-17-3)). As compared with flax bark tissues where C2H2-type zinc finger was the most abundant TF family (Long et al. [2012](#page-17-23)), C3H-type zinc finger was the most abundant one in jute bast tissues. It represents a large gene family, with potential roles in wood development and drought tolerance regulation in *P. trichocarpa* (Chai et al. [2012](#page-16-15)). MYBs and zinc fingers are also known to regulate lignin biosynthesis by recognizing AC elements in the promoters of many monolignol biosynthetic genes (Zhao and Dixon [2011\)](#page-19-2). Nine WT and four mutant unigenes were identified as LIM domain TFs that regulate the expression of *PAL*, *4CL* and *CAD* genes of the monolignol pathway (Zhao and Dixon [2011](#page-19-2)). Since repression of a LIM gene results in an inhibition of lignin biosynthesis in tobacco (Kawaoka et al. [2000](#page-17-37)), functional characterization of our LIM genes in mutant bast tissues would likely to give us insights into the transcriptional control of lignin biosynthesis in jute fibres. There exists an elaborate IAA transport and regulatory network that decides procambial/ cambial fate during vascular differentiation, although the so called 'fibre factors' still remain elusive (Gorshkova et al. [2012\)](#page-17-3). The expression of several members of ARF, a transcriptional activator/repressor that binds to TGTCTC auxin response elements (AuxREs) in promoters of auxinresponse genes, in bast tissues indicates that they are recruited during bast fibre formation in jute. Several lines of evidence suggest that auxin transport and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis are tightly linked and colocalized in vascular tissues (Besseau et al. [2007\)](#page-16-14). Differential expression profiling and functional analysis of ARF genes in mutant and WT bast tissues would give us insights into the role that they play in bringing about the activation/repression of auxin-response genes vis-à-vis auxin-responsive transcription during lignin and SCW biosyntheses in bast fibres.
