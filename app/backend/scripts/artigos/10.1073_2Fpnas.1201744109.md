# Distinct phytochrome actions in nonvascular plants revealed by targeted inactivation of phytobilin biosynthesis

Yu-Rong Chen, Yi-shin Su, and Shih-Long Tu<sup>1</sup>

Institute of Plant and Microbial Biology, Academia Sinica, Taipei 11529, Taiwan

Edited by J. Clark Lagarias, University of California, Davis, CA, and approved April 5, 2012 (received for review February 1, 2012)

The red/far-red light photoreceptor phytochrome mediates photomorphological responses in plants. For light sensing and signaling, phytochromes need to associate with open-chain tetrapyrrole molecules as the chromophore. Biosynthesis of tetrapyrrole chromophores requires members of ferredoxin-dependent bilin reductases (FDBRs). It was shown that LONG HYPOCOTYL 2 (HY2) is the only FDBR in flowering plants producing the phytochromobilin (P $\Phi$ B) for phytochromes. However, in the moss Physcomitrella patens, we found a second FDBR that catalyzes the formation of phycourobilin (PUB), a tetrapyrrole pigment usually found as the protein-bound form in cyanobacteria and red algae. Thus, we named the enzyme PUB synthase (PUBS). Severe photomorphogenic phenotypes, including the defect of phytochrome-mediated phototropism, were observed in Physcomitrella patens when both HY2 and PUBS were disrupted by gene targeting. This indicates HY2 and PUBS function redundantly in phytochrome-mediated responses of nonvascular plants. Our studies also show that functional PUBS orthologs are found in selected lycopod and chlorophyte genomes. Using mRNA sequencing for transcriptome profiling, we demonstrate that expression of the majority of red-light-responsive genes are misregulated in the pubs hy2 double mutant. These studies showed that moss phytochromes rapidly repress expression of genes involved in cell wall organization, transcription, hormone responses, and protein phosphorylation but activate genes involved in photosynthesis and stress signaling during deetiolation. We propose that, in nonvascular plants, HY2 and PUBS produce structurally different but functionally similar chromophore precursors for phytochromes. Holophytochromes regulate biological processes through light signaling to efficiently reprogram gene expression for vegetative growth in the light.

Photosynthetic organisms develop sophisticated photoreceptor systems to regulate their growth and development (1). Phytochromes are the major class of photoreceptors that mainly absorb red and far-red light, to display differential photosensory activities for regulating physiological responses. Light perception of phytochromes depends on the association with open-chain tetrapyrroles as the chromophore. A wide variety of open-chain tetrapyrroles are present in photosynthetic and nonphotosynthetic organisms. The majority of them are found in the phycobiliproteins of pelagic phytoplankton, e.g., phycoerythrin and phycocyanin, where they function as light-harvesting chromophores (2, 3). Some of them, like phytochromobilin (PΦB), phycocyanobilin (PCB), and biliverdin IX $\alpha$  (BV), can also act as the chromophores of phytochromes (4). Although phycoviolobilin (PVB) and phycourobilin (PUB) have been identified as the chromophore of cyanobacteriochromes (5, 6), their occurrence as free bilin pigments has not been reported. Biosynthesis of these diverse tetrapyrroles requires ferredoxin-dependent bilin reductases (FDBRs), which reduce BV at different double bond positions (Fig. S1) (7). The greatest diversity of the BV-metabolizing FDBR members is found in the cyanobacteria. Land plants are thought to possess only HY2. Mutations in the HY2 gene lead to the loss of all photoactive phytochromes in plants, which results in the severe disruption in photomorphogenesis (8, 9).

Although their molecular properties are similar among all plants, phytochromes appear to mainly function in the cytosol of nonvascular plants and chlorophytes, which contrast with their nuclear function in flowering plants. It is well established that phototropic responses in mosses and ferns are mediated by phytochromes as well as by the blue-sensing phototropins and dual redand blue-sensing neochromes (10-13). The protonemal filaments sense red-light direction by phytochromes and neochromes to adjust their growth. Data from gene-targeted phytochrome knockout mutants in Physcomitrella patens and Ceratodon purpureus further supports the role of these red-light sensors in phototropism (14, 15). Relocation of chloroplasts in nonvascular plants is also controlled by phytochromes, neochromes and phototropins (14, 16, 17). For these photoresponses, cytosolic photosensors are required to sense the light direction. Morphological controls usually require changes in gene expression; it is therefore likely that phytochromes in nonvascular plants also play a gene regulatory role in the cytosol.

To further understand the function of phytochromes in cryptogams, generating knockout mutants or inactivating phytochromes is necessary. Physcomitrella possesses seven genes encoding planttype phytochromes, which raises the difficulty of studying phytochrome functions by this approach. Because phytochromes and neochromes share the same bilin chromophore, it should be possible to inactivate the whole family by simply knocking out the gene for the chromophore biosynthesis enzyme, HY2. Surprisingly, we found a second FDBR gene in the Physcomitrella genome that we name PUBS. Our studies show that, unlike HY2, which converts BV to  $P\Phi B$ , PUBS mediates the four-electron conversion of BV to PUB. Although targeted knockout of the gene for either PUBS or HY2 alone yields no obvious phenotypic consequences, the double knockout mutant showed severe photomorphogenesis-deficient phenotypes. Red-light-induced phototropic responses were also defective in the double mutant, indicating that both PUBS and HY2 contribute to phytochrome functions. Using the mRNA sequencing technique for transcriptome profiling, we further identified phytochrome-regulated genes in moss protonema during deetiolation by comparing the profiles of red-light-responsive genes in wild type and double mutant. This approach provides a global view of phytochrome-mediated gene regulation in a nonvascular plant.

PUBS Genes Are Present in Chlorophyte and Lycophyte Genomes. A genome search was next performed to identify PUBS orthologs in other photosynthetic organisms. No PUBS-related sequence was found in known flowering plant genomes. The genome of the spike moss Selaginella moellendorffii, an ancient plant grouped into the lycophytes, does however possess a PUBS-like gene (SmPUBS) in addition to its HY2 homolog. PUBS-related sequences were also found in several chlorophyte species including Ostreococcus lucimarinus, Ostreococcus tauri, Ostreococcus sp. RCC809, and Micromonassp. RCC299 [\(Fig. S3\)](http://www.pnas.org/lookup/suppl/doi:10.1073/pnas.1201744109/-/DCSupplemental/pnas.201201744SI.pdf?targetid=nameddest=SF3). To establish the function of these gene products, the PUBS-like genes from Selaginella moellendorffii and Ostreococcus lucimarinus were cloned and expressed in E. coli to assay the enzymatic activity of the gene products. Both Selaginella and Ostreococcus PUBS proteins converted BV to PUB, based on HPLC-based bilin reductase assay and by spectroscopic enzyme assays [\(Fig. S4](http://www.pnas.org/lookup/suppl/doi:10.1073/pnas.1201744109/-/DCSupplemental/pnas.201201744SI.pdf?targetid=nameddest=SF4)). By comparison, as controls, Synechococcus PebA only formed 15,16-DHBV, whereas PebS yielded PEB under the same assay conditions. These results indicate that PUBS is present in chlorophytes, bryophytes, and lycophytes, implicating the loss of this gene during evolution of seed plants.

PUBS Is Localized in the Plastid. We further tested whether the PUBS gene is expressed in vivo. Gene expression of Physcomitrella PUBS (PpPUBS) and PpHY2 were measured and compared with an internal control ACTIN 2 by quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR) (22). Both PpPUBS and PpHY2 were expressed in all developmental stages of mosses [\(Fig. S5](http://www.pnas.org/lookup/suppl/doi:10.1073/pnas.1201744109/-/DCSupplemental/pnas.201201744SI.pdf?targetid=nameddest=SF5)A). Expression of both genes was significantly higher in the sporophyte, suggesting that both PpPUBS and PpHY2 may be important for spore germination as well as development of the primary protonema.

Because a tp-like sequence is located in the N terminus of PpPUBS, this protein appears to be targeted to the plastid. The subcellular localization of PpPUBS was therefore determined by transient expression in gametophore cells using particle bombardment and by stable transformation via gene targeting. Transient expression of full-length PpPUBS-YFP and PpHY2- YFP in gametophore cells showed that both proteins localize to chloroplasts ([Fig. S5](http://www.pnas.org/lookup/suppl/doi:10.1073/pnas.1201744109/-/DCSupplemental/pnas.201201744SI.pdf?targetid=nameddest=SF5)B). We also constructed a transgenic line specifically replacing the coding region of PpPUBS with the cDNA copy fused with sGFP, which was driven by the endogenous PpPUBS promoter [\(Fig. S6](http://www.pnas.org/lookup/suppl/doi:10.1073/pnas.1201744109/-/DCSupplemental/pnas.201201744SI.pdf?targetid=nameddest=SF6)D). Imaging data confirmed that the PpPUBS protein was stably expressed and localized in chloroplasts (Fig. S5B, [Bottom](http://www.pnas.org/lookup/suppl/doi:10.1073/pnas.1201744109/-/DCSupplemental/pnas.201201744SI.pdf?targetid=nameddest=SF5)). Based on these data, we conclude that both FDBRs function in the plastid.

HY2 and PUBS Are Redundant for Photomorphogenesis in Physcomitrella. The discovery of PUBS indicates that, unlike flowering plants, many cryptogams possess two FDBRs. In Physcomitrella, both PUBS and HY2 are in the plastid and metabolize BV to synthesize PUB and PΦB, respectively. As the function of PΦB in photomorphogenesis is well known, these observations raised the possibility that PUB is important for photomorphogenesis. Gene targeting was performed to disrupt PpPUBS and PpHY2 loci in Physcomitrella, and single- and double-knockout mutants were obtained [\(Fig. S6\)](http://www.pnas.org/lookup/suppl/doi:10.1073/pnas.1201744109/-/DCSupplemental/pnas.201201744SI.pdf?targetid=nameddest=SF6). Both are single-copy genes; hence, these studies generated null mutants for loss-of-function analyses.

It is well known that red light can trigger phototropic responses in mosses (24). The protonema grows toward unilateral red light (positive phototropism) at high fluence rates (5–10  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>), away from it (negative phototropism) at low fluence rates (below 1  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>), and spreads randomly at intermediate fluence rates (25). Phytochromes have been implicated as the main photoreceptor sensing light direction in control of protonemal cell growth in *Physcomitrella* (14). To test the hypothesis that both

FDBRs contribute to the red-light-phototropic response, we analyzed the red-light triggered phototropic response in single and double mutants. Under unilateral red light at different fluence rates, the orientation of primary protonemata growth was scored for filaments oriented toward (positive) and away from (negative) light (Fig. S7B). Both *pubs* and *hy2* single mutants behaved similarly, with slightly weaker phototropic responses at both high and low fluence rates compared with the wild type. However, the *pubs hy2* double mutant lost phototropism completely (Fig. 3). This result indicates that PUBS and HY2 are both required for phytochrome-mediated phototropic responses in *Physcomitrella*.

Red-Light-Regulated, FDBR-Dependent Genes in Physcomitrella Overlap with Those Regulated by Phytochromes in Flowering Plants. To further assess the biological functions of the red-light-regulated transcriptomes, the 1,860 genes were subjected to the functional enrichment analysis and classified according to Gene Ontology (GO) terms. Nearly 50% of these genes have no known functions. Among those functionally annotated, overrepresented GO terms in the red light up- and down-regulated genes were identified (Table 1). In the up-regulated genes, protein folding and stressrelated genes were the most represented biological processes (Table 1 and Dataset S1). A similar result was observed by Tepperman et al. for red-light-regulated genes from Arabidopsis (27). By contrast with flowering plants, few transcription factors (TFs) were rapidly induced by red light in Physcomitrella. This finding indicates that a transcription factor cascade does not dominate the early red-light-induced gene network in *Physcomitrella* or that such a cascade is more rapid in Physcomitrella. Our studies suggest that enhancement of protein dephosphorylation and repression of protein phosphorylation play the major role in red-light signaling (Table 1 and Datasets S1 and S2).

Despite the paucity of TFs among the red-light-induced and FDBR-dependent gene pool, the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH), MYB, AP2/B3, and bZIP families were overrepresented among the TFs rapidly repressed by red light (Dataset  $\overline{S2}$ ). Many of these are known to be involved in hormone-mediated signaling pathways in flowering plants, such as the AP2/B3 and MYB TFs for ethylene, abscisic acid, and auxin signaling. Most notable of the down-regulated genes, those involved in cell wall organization were the most enriched in the red-light-repressed and FDBR-dependent genes. These include xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase (XTH), expansin, pectinesterase, and cellulose synthase genes (Table 1 and Dataset S2). The products of these genes are known to participate in cell wall loosening and biosynthesis, which are required for the elongation and expansion of plant cells. Such processes were not overrepresented in previous data from higher plants (27, 28), suggesting that immediate repression of cell wall organization upon red-light exposure is unique for the moss.

It is noteworthy that known light-related genes, although they are not functionally enriched, are among those responding rapidly to red light and also FDBR-dependent in Physcomitrella (Dataset S3). These include two CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED 1 (CCA1) homologs PpCCA1a and PpCCA1b were induced by 1-h red light. Of the two moss Golden2-like (GLK) genes encoding transcription factors important for photosynthetic gene expression that were up-regulated by red light, only *PpGLK1* was dependent on the presence of functional FDBRs (29). GLKs are likely responsible for the pronounced induction of photosynthetic genes, as significant amount of them were up-regulated by red light (Dataset S1). We also found transcript level of three moss phytochrome genes (*PpPHY3*, *PpPHY5A*, and *PpPHY5C*) were rapidly downregulated by red light, with similar pattern as reported for phytochrome A in Arabidopsis (30). One of the phototropin genes PpPHOTB1 was also repressed. Together, these data reveal that FDBR-dependent light sensors mediate the response to red light in mosses in a manner analogous to flowering plant phytochromes.

## Discussion

Phytobilins can be found in photosynthetic organisms ranging from aquatic prokaryotes to multicellular land plants (2). In cyanobacteria, red algae and cryptomonads, FDBRs function not only to synthesize the phytobilin precursors of the light harvesting phycobiliprotein antennae, but the light sensing phytochromes and cyanobacteriochromes as well. Because land plants and chlorophytes lack phycobiliproteins, the function of FDBRs appears restricted to the synthesis of phytobilin precursors of the chromophores of their phytochrome light sensors. Our study shows that green algae and nonvascular plants possess a member of the FDBR family, PUBS, that catalyzes the conversion of BV to PUB. HY2 (nonvascular plants) or PcyA (chlorophytes). Whereas HY2 and PcyA synthesize P $\Phi$ B and PCB, respectively, which are both functional precursors of phytochrome chromophores (2), the function of PUBS is unclear. The present study shows that PUBS and HY2 are both involved in photomorphogenesis of *Physcomitrella* under red light. This finding suggests a functionally redundant role for PUB and P $\Phi$ B in photomorphogenesis of nonvascular plants.

The *pubs*  $hy^2$  double mutant allows us to ascertain the phenotype of a phytochrome null in a nonvascular plant. Transcriptome analysis showed that the majority of red-light-responsive genes were misregulated in the pubs hy2 double mutant, consistent with the lack of photoactive phytochromes in pubs hy2. By comparing transcript levels of red-light-responsive genes in wild type and pubs hy2, we identified putative target genes of moss phytochrome signaling. Functional classification of these genes suggests that the rapid response to red light by mosses entails repression of specific transcriptional factors, kinase signaling, hormone-mediated signaling and cell wall extension pathways. Like phytochrome-mediated signaling in flowering plants, red light induces expression of genes involved in stress responses and photosynthesis in Physcomitrella. This supports the hypothesis that phytochromes mediate fundamentally similar processes in flowering plants and *Physcomitrella*, i.e., optimize harvesting and use of the available light energy for photosynthesis. In cryptogams, phytochromes also play a critical role in phototropism, which is not observed in flowering plants. We speculate that the retention of two FDBRs in cryptogams reflects their role to support bilin-based photoreceptors that regulate gene expression and those regulate phototropism.

We also propose that PUB functions as an alternative chromophore agonist for phytochrome action. Because it lacks the Aring ethylidene needed for a thioether linkage (31), PUB can bind only reversibly to apo-phytochrome to induce signaling function. In this regard, BV feeding to the Arabidopsis hy2 mutant partially rescued the long hypocotyl phenotype (8), suggesting that reversible binding of BV to apo-phytochrome can partially restore holo-phytochrome activities. Lamparter and colleagues also reported that the noncovalent bilin adducts of phytochrome retain photochemical activity (32). These studies support our hypothesis that reversible binding of PUB to apo-phytochromes in mosses may occur and contribute to the activities of phytochromes. The binding of PUB could potentially stabilize the phytochrome structure, maintain the protein level of phytochrome, or even generate the partially activated Pfr conformation in the cytosol. All of these can ensure moss phytochromes function in the cytosol for photomorphological control, such as phototropism and chloroplast relocation. On the other hand, because we still observed the light-dependent phenotypes in hy2 mutant, which has only PUB available for moss phytochromes, PUB is therefore possibly

isomerized by unknown factors in the moss cell or by apophytochrome itself to form both the C15,16 double bond needed for photoisomerization and the A-ring ethylidene group needed for covalent linkage. Indeed, an isomerization activity that converts PEB to PCB already has been reported for the red algae *Cyanidium caldarium* (33). Recent studies also showed cyanobacteriochromes can autocatalytically isomerize PCB chromophore into PVB (6, 34). Such isomerization activities in mosses remain to be identified.

It is well established that phytochromes regulate gene expression in seed plants (1). In Arabidopsis, for example, the expression of many transcription factors is down-regulated by red light; however, this mostly occurs after longer light exposure (27, 28). Our data indicate that red-light sensing by Physcomitrella also leads to rapid transcriptional repression, particularly those of hormone-related transcriptional networks. Such repression might be beneficial to enhancing other metabolic activities, such as photosynthesis. Gene repression could be executed either by nucleus-localized phytochromes or signal transduction from the cytoplasm, or both. We cannot exclude the possibility that a portion of moss phytochromes are transported into the nucleus that leads to the decrease of transcriptional activities for those down-regulated genes we identified. On the other hand, it is also possible that cytosolic signaling of phytochromes is dominated in nonvascular plants. One example is that phototropic responses mediated by phytochromes in nonvascular plants require the directional signal transmitted to the nucleus. It is doubtful that the light-activated, soluble phytochrome itself moves to the nucleus for directional signal transmission. More likely, light-activated phytochromes interact with components in the cytosol or near the plasma membrane to trigger signal transduction for nuclear gene regulation. Some phytochrome interacting proteins, like NUCLEOSIDE DIPHOSPHATE KINASE 2 (NDPK2) homologs, do exist in mosses that potentially work together with phytochromes in the cytoplasm for signaling (35). Our transcriptome data also reveal that rapid repression of kinase and enhanced expression of phosphatases accompany deetiolation of Physcomitrella, supporting the hypothesis that protein phosphorylation may play a more important role in red-light sensing of nonvascular plants compared with seed plants.

Low red to far red-light treatment has been shown to positively regulate expression of XTHs in *Arabidopsis*, which leads to the petiole elongation during shade avoidance (36). In *Physcomitrella*, red light down-regulates expression of cell wall modifying enzymes that would decrease the dynamic change of cell wall architecture in an analogous manner to attenuate the elongation of protonemal cells during deetiolation. Several genes encoding auxin transporters and responsive factors were also rapidly down-regulated by red light (Dataset S2). This likely contributes to inhibition of protonemal elongation during light exposure. Stem elongation of

the *pubs hy2* double mutant under white light may therefore reflect the loss of repression of auxin-responsive genes. Similar results were reported for moss mutants of cryptochromes under blue light (37). In view of these results, we propose that FDBR-dependent red-light sensors, e.g., phytochromes, rapidly inhibit protonemal growth via suppressing cell wall modification and auxin signaling. In gametophores, FDBR-dependent red-light sensors repress auxin responses that likely act together with cryptochrome signaling to inhibit stem elongation.

In conclusion, our study supports the hypothesis that the preservation of PUBS from algae to nonvascular plants has been important for land colonization. The alternative PUB chromophore found in cryptograms might reflect the retention of an ancient redundant signaling system that was lost in the streptophyte lineage as the phytochrome lineage expanded to permit adaptation to the changing ecology of the land environment (38). During the evolution of flowering plants, signaling factors not found in the genome of *Physcomitrella patens* were developed as the phytochrome family speciated. We envisage that HY2 became the only FDBR for chromophore biosynthesis.

## **Materials and Methods**

**Plant Materials and Growth Conditions.** Protonemata and gametophores of *Physcomitrella patens* subsp. *patens* were grown on solid Knop's medium or cultured in liquid PPNH<sub>4</sub>. Spores were germinated on solid Knop's medium supplemented with 10 mM CaCl<sub>2</sub> and overlaid with cellophane. Plants were cultured at 25 °C under continuous white light (80–100  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>).

**mRNA Sequencing.** MID-mRNA sequencing was carried out as suggested by Illumina. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina Genome Analyzer IIx at Yourgene Bioscience, Taiwan. Sequence reads were mapped to the *Physcomitrella patens* genome annotation V1.6. Detailed mRNA sequencing, data analysis, other experimental procedures and related references are in *SI Materials and Methods*.
