#### **ABSTRACT:**

The absence of a reproductive sink causes physiological and morphological changes in soybean plants. These include increased accumulation of nitrogen and starch in the leaves and delayed leaf senescence. To identify transcriptional changes that occur in leaves of these sink-limited plants, we used RNAseq to compare gene expression levels in trifoliate leaves from depodded and *ms6* male sterile soybean plants and control plants. In both sink-limited tissues, we observed a deferral of the expression of senescence-associated genes and a continued expression of genes associated with leaf maturity. GO-terms associated with growth and development and storage proteins were over-represented in genes that were differentially expressed in sink-limited tissues. We also identified bHLH, ARF, and SBP transcription factors expressed in sink-limited tissues, while the senescing control leaves expressed WRKY and NAC transcription factors. We identified genes that were not expressed during normal leaf development but that were highly expressed in sink-limited plants, including the *SGR3b* "nonyellowing" gene. These differences highlighted several metabolic pathways that were involved in distinct modes of resource partitioning of leaves with the "stay green" phenotype.

This paper describes the transcriptional differences in soybean leaves where the normal pattern of senescence is delayed by either the removal of or absence of pods. While the phenomenon of senescence delay is common among plants, and has been physiologically characterized for the obvious potential to impact seed filling and yield, it has not yet been subject to a molecular analysis using modern transcriptional profiling technologies. Applying these techniques in controlled conditions we found evidence for expression of a new member of the non-yellowing gene family in soybean that is related to Mendel's green cotyledon gene from pea. We also observed upregulation of genes from the lipoxygenase gene family and the squamosa binding protein (SBP) transcription factor family. We think that these results provide some insight into the metabolic and physiological changes that occur in sink-limited leaves and open avenues for future research into the regulation of senescence in soybean.

# **INTRODUCTION:**

Plant development and crop yield is highly dependent on the source-sink relationship where new tissues are supplied with nutrients from photosynthetic source tissues. During normal leaf senescence in soybean, nutrients are transported out of the leaf (source) and into the developing seed (sink). This relationship may be disrupted either by mechanical pod or flower removal or genetically in a male sterile line, which does not produce fertile pods when cross-pollination is not possible. In soybean, the absence of the reproductive sink delays leaf senescence, changes the leaf composition, and artificially prolongs the life of the plant (Mondal et al., 1978; Wittenbach, 1982, 1983).

Sink removal has been studied in many species including soybean, maize, wheat, sunflower, and citrus trees (Patterson and Brun, 1980; Christensen et al., 1981; Crafts-Brandner et al., 1984a; Schaffer et al., 1986; Dreccer et al., 1997; Alkio et al., 2003). In maize, senescence responses to ear removal are genotype dependent (Christensen et al., 1981; Crafts-Brandner et al., 1984a; Crafts-Brandner et al., 1984b). However in most cases ear removal led to an increase in carbohydrate content in both leaves and stalks and decrease in leaf nitrogen (Christensen et al., 1981). In hard red spring wheat, sink removal delayed senescence up to 3 weeks after anthesis (Patterson and Brun, 1980). In citrus trees such as grapefruit, a process called girdling cuts off nutrient transport to the roots, increasing fruit size (Fishier et al., 1983). Girdling or de-fruiting also caused starch levels to slightly increase in citrus leaves, and induced senescence (Schaffer et al., 1986).

A series of sink removal studies have been conducted in soybean with particular interest in how sink limitation alters yield or seed protein accumulation (McAlister and Krober, 1958; Egli et al., 1976; Mondal et al., 1978; Wittenbach, 1983; Kaschuk et al., 2010; Turner et al. 2012). These studies measured physiological traits such as seed number, seed weight, carbohydrate concentration and movement, photosynthesis, and leaf and stem composition. For dry matter production, plants that were depodded continuously for 30 days had higher dry matter weight than the control plants, suggesting that vegetative structures can serve as sinks when no seed is produced (Heitholt and Egli, 1985). In soybeans where flowers, flower buds and pods were continually removed, nonstructural carbohydrates and 14C labeled assimilate increased significantly in leaves, petioles, and stems (Egli et al., 1976). Sink removal in soybean inhibits normal leaf senescence and promotes the formation of new floral buds and pods. Decreased photosynthesis rates were observed in depodded soybean plants, but

they maintained 20% of maximum photosynthesis after similarly-staged control plants had senesced (Crafts-Brandner and Egli, 1987).

McAlister and Krober (1958) compared seed measurements in soybean plants with variable numbers of pods (sink) or leaves (source) removed. They found that 10-20% of sink removal did not affect the yield or number of pods, due to the background levels of natural abortion of pods in control plants. They also found that the higher the percentage of sink removed from the plant, the higher the seed weight in the remaining seed. Together with seed size, seed protein content increased in depodded plants as more sinks were removed (McAlister and Krober, 1958).

Since mechanical removal of flowers or pods from a plant can lead to a wounding response and the expression of defense-related genes, male sterile soybeans present an opportunity to study changes that occur when no seed is produced, while limiting the effect of wounding (Turner et al., 2012). Like depodded soybean plants, male sterile soybeans experience a delay in leaf senescence, change in leaf composition, and extended promotion of floral bud initiation (Wilson et al., 1978; Huber et al., 1983; Burke et al., 1984; Israel et al., 1985). In *Arabidopsis* male sterile plants, individual leaf longevity is not altered, while female sterile mutations shortened leaf longevity (Noodén and Penney, 2001). Similar to de-flowered *Arabidopsis* plants, sterile *Arabidopsis* show increased and prolonged production of bolts and leaves, with a positive effect on overall longevity (Noodén and Penney, 2001). To date 11 different male-sterile, female-fertile mutants have been identified in soybean. The *ms6* malesterile, female-fertile plant was a spontaneous mutation found in an F4 line of A78- 245014 and is a recessive trait (Skorupska and Palmer, 1989). While the gene

responsible for the male sterile phenotype has not been identified, sterility in *ms6* is caused by a disruption in the telophase II stage of meiosis, and mutant plants lack the formation of a tetrad during pollen formation (Skorupska and Palmer, 1989). The *ms6* male sterile mutant can be maintained as a heterozygote in the T295H line.

Transcriptional changes associated with normal leaf senescence in soybean have been described previously. Notably, families of transcriptional regulators mark the phases of leaf development from leaf initiation through maturity and the onset of senescence (Brown and Hudson, 2015). The AUX/IAA and C2C2Zn (Dof) transcription factors were expressed in young and mature leaves. In senescing leaves the NAC, WRKY, MYB, C2H2 zinc finger, bZIP, and AP2/EREBP families were expressed at elevated levels.

To understand what molecular changes underlie sink removal in soybean and which pathways signal to initiate the normal process of leaf senescence when sinks are produced we identified changes in leaf gene expression following sink removal. Because some of the molecular changes associated with sink removal may be a result of a wounding response due to the removal of pods, we performed, in parallel, developmental RNAseq expression profiling on leaves from sterile soybean plants to control for these effects.

## **MATERIALS AND METHODS:**

## **Plant material, growth, and tissue collection**

The sampling schedule is illustrated in Figure 1. In the sink removal ("depodding") time series experiment, Williams-82 soybean seeds were planted in one part Promix (BRK): two parts sand/soil mixture in 3-gallon pots. Plants were grown in Conviron PGR14

chambers in 12 hours light/12 hours dark with a combination of fluorescent and incandescent lighting at an intensity of 350 μmol m-2 s -1, constant temperature of 25 C and 75% relative humidity (Hudson, 2010). All samples were collected 6 hours after lights were turned on to minimize diurnal effects. The third trifoliate leaf was collected for each sample (no plant was sampled more than one time). Stage AP (after pollination) began after the first flowers self-pollinated and no pods had formed (38 days after planting). After the initial sample, plants were divided into control and depodded groups. On depodded plants all new pods were removed before they reached 3mm in length, continuously. Stage 2 was collected one week after sample AP, when pods were forming on the control plants at the R4 reproductive stage (Fehr and Caviness, 1977) (or 45 days after planting in our conditions). Stage 3 was collected 10 days after stage 2 and seed cavities formed within the pod, or at the R5 reproductive stage (54 days after planting). Stage 4 was collected 10 days later when the seeds filled the cavities or the R6 reproductive stage (64 days after planting). Stage 5 was collected 12 days after stage 4 when the leaves were beginning to senesce on control plants, or at soybean reproductive stage R7 (76 days after planting). Since the soybean reproductive stages (Fehr and Caviness, 1977) relate primarily to phases of pod development, we observed them on control plants, and the depodded plants were sampled simultaneously but had no pods and did not undergo senescence.

The *ms6* male sterile genetic stock line T295H (PI 533601) (Palmer and Skorupska, 1990) was obtained from the USDA Soybean Germplasm Collection. Fertile plants were harvested individually and progeny tested to identify heterozygous individuals segregating for male sterile and fertile progeny. Seeds from these heterozygous individuals were planted as described above for leaf tissue collection. The first sample

was collected after the flowers self-pollinated or 28 days after planting. The third trifoliate leaves were collected every week for 5 weeks, for 6 stages altogether (64 days after planting). RNA quality of the third sample (42 days after planting) was poor, so it was excluded from further analysis. A week after flower pollination wild-type plants developed pods and flower formation stopped after one and a half weeks, while the sterile plants continued to grow and produce flowers.

# **RNA Extraction, library preparation, and sequencing**

Total RNA was extracted from leaves using procedures described previously for RNAseq (Brown and Hudson, 2015). Five micrograms of total RNA was used for paired-end library construction. Median insert size was 200 bp. Three biological replicate RNA samples for each stage were sequenced on the Illumina Hi-Seq 2500, and the number of reads ranged from 12,491,776 to 22,456,386 for all samples. For both experiments, read lengths of 100 bp were collected. For the depodding time series, RNA was sequenced from all five time points. For the sterile/fertile time series, RNA was sequenced from stage AP and stages two and six. The raw data has been deposited in GEO (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) with the accession number GSE85714.

# **Read alignment and normalization**

Reads were aligned to the *Glycine max* cultivar Williams 82 primary transcriptome (Wm82.a2.v1) (Schmutz et al., 2010) using the bowtie2/2.2.5 aligner. For the sink removal (depodding) experiment, the very sensitive option was selected in the end-toend mode. Zero mismatches were allowed in the multiseed alignment, where the seed substring length was set to 32. Read pairs that failed to align concordantly were excluded from further analysis. On average 88% of the reads mapped to known soybean transcript models. Of those reads, 30% aligned to only one transcript and 57% aligned to multiple transcripts.

Reads from the male sterile samples were aligned by bowtie alignment and run in the default end-to-end mode, one mismatch was allowed within a seed during multiseed alignment, where the seed substring length was set to 32. Read pairs that failed to align concordantly were excluded from further analysis. On average 81% of the reads mapped to known soybean transcript models. Of those reads, 28% aligned to only one transcript and 52% aligned to multiple transcripts. Reads that aligned to more than one transcript were filtered from the bowtie output and only reads that matched only one transcript were used for further analysis. Read counts were obtained from the bowtie output using a set of Perl scripts. Transcripts with less than 10 total read counts throughout the whole timecourse were removed (Brown and Hudson, 2015). Transcripts were normalized using the DESeq2 software package. We documented the expression of 1000 housekeeping genes identified previously (Severin et al. 2010) to confirm sample integrity and comparability (Supporting Information Table S1).

# **Differential expression**

To identify differentially expressed genes both the DESeq2 and EdgeR software packages were used to perform pairwise comparisons of all stages (Robinson et al., 2010; Anders and Huber, 2012; Love et al., 2014). The set of differentially expressed genes common to both of these two methods make up the set of genes used for further analysis. Genes exhibiting significant differential expression were further filtered by a 2.0 log<sup>2</sup> fold change.

#### **Data analysis**

GO-term analysis was performed on differentially expressed genes both shared and specific to each experiment using the SoyBase GO-enrichment tool

(http://www.soybase.org)(Grant et al., 2009) using GO annotation terms for genome version Wm82.a2.v1. Each set of differentially expressed genes was split by direction of regulation (up or down) and analyzed separately. Shared genes between the two sets of differentially expressed genes were analyzed separately from sterile/depodding specific genes. Transcription factors were classified using Wm82.a2.v1 gene annotations, Mapman (Thimm et al., 2004) and the Plant Transcription Factor database (http://planttfdb.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) (Jin et al., 2013). Overrepresented motif analysis was performed using the Elefinder tool at [http://stan.cropsci.uiuc.edu/cgi](http://stan.cropsci.uiuc.edu/cgi-bin/elefinder/elefinder_all.cgi)[bin/elefinder/elefinder\\_all.cgi](http://stan.cropsci.uiuc.edu/cgi-bin/elefinder/elefinder_all.cgi) (Hudson and Quail, 2003). Multiple protein sequence alignment was done using Clustal Omega (Sievers et al., 2011) and Jalview (Waterhouse et al., 2009) and the phylogenic tree was produced using Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA)(Kumar et al., 2016).

# **RT-qPCR**

qPCR using the SYBR Green I Master Mix (Roche, Indianapolis, IN, USA) was performed on the Lightcycler 480 on all RNA samples. Gene expression was normalized using three soybean housekeeping genes: Glyma.10G221500 (*EF1α*), Glyma.02g299000 (proteasome α subunit), and Glyma.10g204500 (ribosomal protein) (as described in Hudson, 2010). Housekeeping genes were selected using the program geNorm (Vandesompele et al., 2002). Gene specific primers for the stay green genes are *D1*-F, 5'CCACATCAGTGGTGGTCATT 3', *D1*-R, 5' TAAACCCAAACCAAGGCATC 3', *D2*-F, 5' TGCCACATTAGTGGAGGTCA 3', *D2*-R, 5' TTGCAATTCTGGGTAGCTGT 3', SGR3a-F, 5'

# CTTCATTTTCCGCAAAGAGC 3', *SGR3a*-R, 5' CTTTCAATGGACCCCAACAT 3', *SGR3b*-F, 5' TCTGCAAAGAGCTTCCAGTG 3', *SGR3b*-R, 5' CCTGTAATTCTGGGTAGTCCTTG 3'.

# **RESULTS:**

Sink-limited soybean plants were morphologically distinct from control plants that produce a sink (Figure 1). Both depodded and sterile plants produced more nodes than fertile controls, continued producing flowers and remained green in color. The average number of nodes on depodded and sterile plants was 8.5 and 10.5, respectively, while on the controls the average number of nodes was 6.2 and 5.4 respectively. In sterile plants a whorl of small empty pods formed at the apex after node development stopped. Leaves, axillary meristems, and floral buds were produced at cotyledon nodes and secondary branches formed at lower nodes. Plants in the sterile-fertile comparison were shorter and the fertile plants had fewer nodes than the depodded Williams 82 plants because this genotype flowered and matured earlier (MGII) than Williams 82 (MG III) in our conditions. While plant height and timing to flowering and senescence differs between soybeans grown in growth chambers and field-grown plants, for transcript profiling, the growth chamber is a more controlled and reproducible environment as these parameters can also vary greatly from year to year in the field.

# **Stages**

RNA from five stages was sequenced from depodded plants and controls. The initial sample collected after flowers were pollinated is designated as "AP", and four subsequent stages were sampled from both depodded ("DP2-DP5") and control plants ("Cntrl2-Cntrl5"). RNA from three stages was sequenced from sterile and wild-type plants. The first stage is the initial stage collected after flowers self-pollinated

designated as "AP". The other two stages were collected from both sterile plants ("S2 and S6") and wild-type plants ("WT2 and WT6"). All of the plants were planted at the same time and plants that germinated late were eliminated. The sink-limited soybeans posses synchronized timing with their respective controls for flowering, however senescence (and physiological maturity) are delayed in the sink-limited plants.

Principal component analysis (PCA) (Supporting Information Figure S2) revealed that in later stages continuously depodded soybean leaves showed gene expression patterns more similar to each other than to control leaves (DP3, DP4, DP5 vs. Cntrl3, Cntrl4, Cntrl5). For the sterile-fertile comparison, PCA suggested that all of the stages showed distinct patterns of gene expression, though post-fertilization stages in sterile plants (S2 and S6) were more similar to each other than to the wild type control samples (WT2 and WT6).

# **Differential Expression**

38,177 (68.1%) genes were expressed in at least one tissue in at least one stage of the depodding time series (Supporting Information Table S3). A total of 37,403 (66.7%) genes were found to be expressed in at least one stage of the sterile time series (Supporting Information Table S3).

Differentially expressed genes were identified between timepoints of each experiment and also relative to controls at the same stage. Of the 56,044 genes in the soybean Wm82.a2.v1 dataset, 16,663 (29.7%) were found to be differentially expressed to a statistically significant degree between at least two stages within the depodding

experiment while 14,040 (25%) genes were differentially expressed between at least two stages of the sterile experiment. (Supporting Information Table S4).

# **GO-Term Analysis**

To identify pathways common to sink-limited soybean, differentially expressed genes from both experiments were compared between the later stages of sink-limited plants and their respective controls (DP5 and Cntrl5, S6 and WT6). 4,138 genes were differentially expressed between the later stages of the depodded soybeans and their controls (2,294 decreasing/1,844 increasing), while 6,109 genes were differentially expressed between the sterile plants and the wild type controls (3,707 decreasing /2,402 increasing). 2,567 genes were common between the two sets (1,500 decreasing/1,067 increasing).

GO-term enrichment analysis identified biological processes shared between mechanically and genetically sink-limited leaves, as well as terms found in only one of the sink-limited groups (Figure 2). In leaves from sterile plants, overrepresented GOterms included starch biosynthetic process and maltose metabolic process. In fertile control plants, terms for transport of nutrients and metals were overrepresented at later stages where normal senescence and nutrient remobilization occurred. Specific to mechanically depodded plants, genes were enriched for GO terms such as cytokinin mediated signaling, calcium:cation antiporter activity, and regulation of cell size. Genes expressed in control plants in the depodding experiment were enriched for GO-terms involved with defense responses and cell death. Unexpectedly, the GO-term "response to wounding" was associated with the control plants in the depodded timecourse experiment. We expected to see this GO-term overrepresented in the plants from which pods were removed. 1,031 genes in the soybean genome are assigned to this GO term,

and 55 of these genes were up regulated in senescing control leaves (Supporting Table S5), suggesting that this GO category contains genes involved with normal senescence. In control leaves from both the pod removal and sterile time series experiments, genes share GO-terms associated with normal senescence including defense responses and hormone biosynthesis. This group includes genes predicted to be involved in signaling pathways for jasmonic acid and abscisic acid, as well as ethylene biosynthesis. In leaves from both groups of sink-limited plants, enriched GO-terms were associated with continued growth and development, including photosynthesis, cell wall organization and polysaccharide biosynthesis. Interestingly, the GO-term spermidine CoA Nactyltransferase was also found to be highly significant in leaves of both sink-limited soybean plants. This GO-term is associated with 9 soybean genes upregulated in the sink-limited plants annotated as spermidine hydroxycinnamoyl transferases. Altogether, these nine genes comprise 8 over-represented GO-term categories (Complete lists of genes that contribute to each enriched GO term can be found in Supporting Information Table S5).

# **Transcription factors and cis-regulatory motifs associated with sink limited plants**

To investigate regulatory networks involved with delayed senescence, we identified transcription factor families that were differentially expressed between the later stages of both sink-limited experiments. A list of 5,069 soybean transcription factors and their respective families from the Plant Transcription Factor Database (Jin et al., 2013) were combined with Mapman classifications (Thimm et al., 2004) for a composite list of 8,017 transcription factors. We identified 500 transcription factors in 43 different families differentially expressed between the later two stages in the depodding experiment and

634 transcription factors from 49 different families differentially expressed in the sterile-fertile experiment. A total of 250 transcription factor genes were differentially expressed in both experiments. The distribution of the largest 20 families of transcription factors across differentially expressed comparisons is shown in Figure 3. The auxin response factors (ARFs, 11 genes) and auxin/indoleacetic transcription factors (AUX/IAA, 11 genes) were combined into one category since they are related gene families and exhibit overlapping redundant functions (Liscum and Reed, 2002). WRKY, NAC, PHOR1 and C2H2 zinc-finger transcription factor families were expressed in control/fertile tissues which senesced normally, while the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH), homeobox (HB), ARF &AUX/IAA, squamosa binding protein (SBP), cys3His zinc-finger (C3H) and Response Regulator (ARR) transcription factor families were expressed in sink limited tissues. This result confirms that the NAC and WRKY transcription factors are involved with senescence and the transcription factor families represented in sink limited families correlate with growth and development.

We identified potential *cis*-acting elements enriched in the promoters of genes differentially expressed in sink-limited leaves. Promoter motifs were identified from genes in each group using Elefinder (Hudson and Quail, 2003) [\(http://stan.cropsci.uiuc.edu/cgi-bin/elefinder/elefinder\\_all.cgi\)](http://stan.cropsci.uiuc.edu/cgi-bin/elefinder/elefinder_all.cgi) and statistical overrepresentation was determined using a hypergeometric test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Motifs overrepresented in the promoters of genes expressed at higher levels in control and fertile tissues undergoing senescence are the W-box, DPBF1&2, and SORLREP3 (Table 1). In sink-limited tissues the ARF1, ATB2/AtbZIP, AtMYC2 BS in RD22, G-box/RAV1-B, LTRE and RAV1-A motifs were overrepresented. The G-box motif was only overrepresented in promoters of genes

overexpressed in leaves from sterile plants, and the LTRE (low temperature response element) motif was only enriched in promoters of genes expressed in depodded plants. Bellringer/replumless/pennywise BS1 in AG, lbox and T-box motifs were overrepresented in genes differentially regulated in both control and sink-limited tissues.

#### **Lipoxygenase genes are highly expressed in sterile and depodded plants**

The GO-term for lipoxygenase activity (GO:0016165) was overrepresented in leaves of sink-limited plants. Genes with this annotation share other enriched GO-terms which include: membrane disassembly (GO:0030397), lipid oxidation (GO:0034440), and growth (GO:0040007). In the soybean genome a total of 45 genes are annotated as *LIPOXYGENASE1*, -*2*, or -*3*. In both the sink removal and sterile experiments, 30 lipoxygenase genes were found to be expressed in one of the time courses described here. Twenty-two of these genes were differentially expressed in the sink removal experiment, while 21 were differentially expressed in the sterile-fertile comparison. 16 of those genes were differentially expressed between the later two stages of the sink removal and control plants (15 expressed at higher levels in the DP stages) and 15 between the later two stages of the sterile and fertile plants (all 15 were higher in the sterile leaves) (Supporting Information Table S6). Of these DEGs (differentially expressed genes), 12 were shared between both classes of sink-limited plants (Figure 4). Eight of the DEGs have normalized expression values above 8,000 counts in stage DP6, with Glyma.07G006900 having the highest expression value of 40,000 counts (Supporting Information Table S6). These eight highly expressed genes include lipoxygenase genes: Glyma.13G347700/*vlxA*, Glyma.15G026500/*vlxB,* Glyma.07G006900/*vlxC*, Glyma.07G007000/*vlxD*. Most of these lipoxygenase transcripts initially increase in both control and sink-limited tissues, but decrease in

expression during senescence in the controls but increase in expression in the sinklimited tissues. Genes with an expression pattern similar to the lipoxygenase genes include genes encoding starch branching enzymes (a continual increase in expression in both sink limited tissues) are listed in Supporting Information Table S7.

#### **Expression of genes from the Stay-Green Family**

One category of genes highly expressed in depodded plants were the "non-yellowing" genes. Four genes in the soybean genome are annotated as "non-yellowing" and are homologs of the *Arabidopsis STAY-GREEN* (*AtSGR1/AtNYE1*/At4g22920) gene. These genes are *D1* and *D2* (Glyma.01G214600 and Glyma.11G027400), and two tandemly duplicated genes found on chromosome 17: Glyma.17G132800 (*SGR3a*), and Glyma.17G132900 (*SGR3b*). *D1* and *D2* are two unlinked paralogous genes involved in chlorophyll degradation, whose double recessive mutant (*d1d1d2d2*) results in a delayed senescence or a stay green phenotype (Guiamet et al., 1991; Fang et al., 2014; Hörtensteiner, 2009). qRT-PCR was performed to confirm the expression pattern of these genes in the timecourse experiments (Figure 5A and B). Expression of *D1* and *D2* increased during senescence in control and fertile plants with normalized expression values as high as 5,000 in stage Cntrl5/Cntrl6 at the onset of leaf senescence. In the depodded plants, *D1* and *D2* expression levels increased during the time course, but attained lower ultimate levels, with a 8-fold difference from the control at stage 5 for *D1* and a 3-fold difference for *D2* (Figure 5C). *SGR3a* fell below our threshold of detection in control and sink-limited plants. Interestingly *SGR3b* was initially expressed at relatively low levels but in depodded plants exhibited a 30-fold increase in expression between stages 4 and 5, while in the control plants was barely detectable.

A multiple alignment of stay green protein sequences was performed between the soybean "non-yellowing" genes, *Arabidopsis thaliana STAY-GREEN* genes, and Mendel's green cotyledon gene from *Pisum sativum* (garden pea) (Supporting Information Figure S8). The *D1* and *D2* genes are 95% similar at the amino acid level while *SGR3a* and *SGR3b* are 80.8% similar. *SGR3b* is 68% identical with *D1* and 67.5% with *D2*, while *SGR3a* is 69% identical with both *D1* and *D2*. Supporting Information Figure S9 shows phylogenetic analysis of the *D1, D2, SGR3a & SGR3b* genes along with other gene members of the *Stay-Green* family in legumes, Fabidae, *Arabidopsis*, and monocots. Three pairs of soybean genes are evident, including Glyma.04G243800 and Glyma.06G119500, which are related to the *STAY-GREEN LIKE* gene from Arabidopsis (At1g44000). *D1* and *D2* comprise a second pair, and a third pair is *GmSGR3a* and

*GmSGR3b*.

# **DISCUSSION:**

To characterize the delayed senescence observed in soybean plants where seed formation is absent, we profiled gene expression in leaves of plants that did not produce sink (seed) tissues as a result of either genetic or mechanical manipulations. The set of genes co-regulated in these two experiments gives insight into the normal process of senescence and separates, to the extent possible, the effect of wounding from mechanical depodding, thus furthering our understanding on the reproductive triggers influencing senescence.

# **Normal program of senescence is replaced by other biochemical processes in leaves of sink-limited plants**

Control and fertile soybean plants followed the normal pattern of senescence including the up-regulation of genes involved in programmed cell death and plant defense as observed in previous experiments (Brown and Hudson 2015). Gene expression in sinklimited leaves was characterized by GO-terms normally associated with mature, presenescent leaves. These GO-terms include cytokinin signaling and starch biosynthesis. Cytokinins are involved in many plant developmental processes including cell division, apical dominance, seed germination, source/sink relationships, and have also been shown to delay senescence (Hutchison and Kieber, 2002). The over-representation of starch biosynthesis in the leaves of sterile plants correlates with previous sink removal studies that found increased starch levels in the leaves of plants where the sink was removed (Mondal et al., 1978; Schaffer et al., 1986; Crafts-Brandner and Egli, 1987). Similarly we found that expression of genes encoding starch branching enzymes increased continuously in leaves of sink-limited plants.

Genes encoding putative spermidine hydroxycinnamoyl transferases (represented by the GO-term spermidine CoA N-actyltransferase) were found to be highly expressed in leaves of sink-limited soybean plants. In *Arabidopsis thaliana* the spermidine hydroxycinnamoyl transferases are best known for roles in pollen development and pollen exine formation (Grienenberger et al., 2009; Dobritsa et al., 2011). This enzyme has been found in the apical stems of *Aphelandra tetragona* (Hedberg et al., 1996). In leaf tissues, polyamines such as spermidine have also been proposed to act as freeradical scavengers and thus may function to preserve the aging tissues (Drolet et al., 1986). Polyamine biosynthesis and ethylene biosynthesis both rely on the precursor sadenosyl methionine (Even-Chen et al., 1982). Several reports suggest a role for polyamines in the regulation (through unknown mechanisms) of ethylene biosynthesis in soybean leaves and detached leaf discs, and when ethylene biosynthesis is inhibited, spermidine has been shown to accumulate (Pennazio and Roggero, 1990; Turano et al.,

1997).

**Senescence associated gene networks are not activated in sink-limited tissues** Transcription factors expressed at higher levels in sink-limited leaves correlated with genes involved in leaf growth and development, and were previously found to be enriched in young leaves (Brown and Hudson, 2015). These genes include the ARF and AUX/IAA and SBP transcription factor families. The ARF and AUX/IAA are conserved families of transcription factors found in plants and that interact with one another to function in plant growth and development (Reed, 2001; Wang et al., 2005). The SBP transcription factors are known to be involved in various processes including leaf development, plant architecture, GA signaling, and phase transition (Chen et al., 2010). In maize the *Cg1* gene encodes for a microRNA in the miR156 class that causes cleavage of the mRNAs from several SBP target genes, and alters the transition from juvenile to adult phases (Rhoades et al., 2002; Chuck et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2015). The maize *Cg1* mutant exhibits initiation of additional axillary branches and leaves, and elevated sugar levels in leaves (Whaley and Leech, 1950; Abedon et al., 2006). Overexpression of the *Cg1* miRNA in switchgrass increases the overall starch content of the plant and inhibits flowering (Chuck et al., 2011). These results suggest that SBPs may play a conserved role, and that manipulation of SBP levels in soybean warrants investigation as a target to alter leaf carbohydrate levels and potentially delay plant senescence.

Consistent with prior results we found bHLH family transcription factors expressed in the early stages of leaf development, while the WRKYs and NACs are expressed in senescing tissues in control plants (Brown and Hudson 2015). The ARR transcription factors found to be more abundantly expressed in sink-limited tissues are thought to be involved in cytokinin signaling (Sakai et al., 2001; Yokoyama et al., 2007) and correlates with the GO-term analysis.

Enrichment of *cis*-regulatory motifs in the promoters of co-expressed genes was found to correspond to the known binding sites of transcription factor families co-regulated in this experiment. Motifs associated with the early stage of leaf development, photosynthesis, and growth and development, are preferentially found in genes expressed at higher levels in sink limited tissues. These motifs include ATB2/AtbZIP, AtMYC2, and Ibox, which are often found in the promoters of genes associated with photosynthesis (Borello et al., 1993; Rook et al., 1998), and *ARF1* which is involved in growth and development and targeted by the AUX/IAA transcription factors (Ulmasov et al., 1997; Hagen and Guilfoyle, 2002). The G-box/RAV1-B and LTRE motifs are contained in promoters of many ABA responsive genes (Shen and Ho, 1995; Huang et al., 2007). The LTRE motif is also found in promoters responsive to stresses such as drought in *Arabidopsis*, and low temperatures in wheat and barley (Ouellet et al., 1998; Huang et al., 2008). Since the LTRE was only overrepresented in leaves from depodded plants, the LTRE promoter motif may also participate in the stress response to mechanical depodding/wounding. Similar to previous findings, the DPBF1&2 and Wbox motifs are highly overrepresented in genes upregulated in older/senescing tissues (Brown and Hudson, 2015). These results provide further evidence that these motifs are involved in the regulation of senescence-associated genes, since they are not

statistically overrepresented in the promoters of highly expressed genes in sink limited tissues of the same age.

### **Role of lipoxygenase in sink-limited soybeans**

Lipoxygenases are involved in various plant processes including defense responses, vegetative growth, germination, and senescence. The *LOX7* and *LOX8* genes have been shown to be expressed at high levels in leaves following wounding (Saravitz and Siedow, 1996). Similar results were found with the *TomloxD* lipoxygenase gene in tomato (Heitz et al., 1997; Chen et al., 2004). Lipoxygenases are required for jasmonic acid biosynthesis in plants (Vick and Zimmerman, 1983; Baysal and Demirdöven, 2007). In soybean leaves and cotyledons, lipoxygenase activity decreases as cotyledons senesce (Peterman and Siedow, 1985).

Previous sink removal studies found upregulation of lipoxygenase genes *vlxA, vlxB, vlxC, vlxD*, and *vlxE* in sink-limited leaves after anthesis, while expression of these genes decreases after anthesis when sinks are present (Bunker et al., 1995; Stephenson et al., 1998; Turner et al., 2012). In the present experiment, lipoxygenase genes exhibited some of the largest magnitudes of upregulation in both depodded and sterile soybeans, which supports a role for these genes beyond the wounding response, possibly in vegetative development and storage (Tranbarger et al., 1991).

**Confirmation of expression of a new homolog of the** *D1/D2* **non-yellowing genes** The *SGR3b* gene, which was highly up-regulated at stage 5 in depodded plants, showed only limited expression in previous studies, and was thought to be a pseudogene (Nakano et al., 2014). The homology and tandem arrangement of *SGR3a* and *SGR3b* suggest that they are the products of a gene duplication after the soybean wholegenome duplication (Nakano et al., 2014). *D1* and *D2* expression increases during leaf senescence and these genes have a positive (and redundant) function in chlorophyll breakdown, but expression is also high in the leaves of sink-limited plants that remain green. The *SGR3b* gene is only highly expressed in the later stages of leaves of plants that have undergone sink removal, where chlorophyll breakdown is delayed. Two homologs in *Arabidopsis* are *AtSGR1* and *AtSGR2* (At4g22920 and At4g11910). The function for *AtSGR1* in chlorophyll breakdown is established, and similar to that of the *D1* and *D2* genes in soybean (Ren et al., 2007; Aubry et al., 2008; Hörtensteiner, 2009). *AtSGR2* was shown to negatively regulate *AtSGR1*-mediated chlorophyll degradation (Sakuraba et al., 2014). While the *GmSGR3b* gene does not appear to be expressed during the course of normal soybean leaf senescence, the role of this gene in leaves in which senescence is prevented warrants further investigation at the genetic and biochemical levels.

We investigated the expression of approximately 38,000 genes in soybean trifoliate leaves from mechanically depodded plants and the *ms6* male sterile mutant. We identified transcriptional changes that occur in plants that do not produce a sink. We found that transcription factors such as ARFs, AUX/IAA, bHLHs, and SBPs are associated with growth and development and were represented in leaves from sink-limited plants. We found a number of biochemical and metabolic differences between sink-limited and normally senescing plants, including carbon and nitrogen metabolism. Specific gene families such as the lipoxygenases were strongly upregulated in leaves from sinklimited plants. Mechanical sink limitation enhanced the expression of the *SGR3b* "nonyellowing" gene, which may have an unforeseen role in the regulation of leaf senescence.
