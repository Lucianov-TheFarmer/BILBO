26 **Running title:** Unintended effect of genetically engineered plants.

**Keywords:** *Oryza sativa*, genetic engineering, unintended effect, transcriptome, metabolome 

#### **SUMMARY**

 Advancements in -omics techniques provide powerful tools to assess potential effects in composition of a plant at the RNA, protein and metabolite levels. These technologies can thus be deployed to assess whether genetic engineering causes changes in plants that go beyond the changes introduced by conventionally plant breeding. Here, we compare the extent of transcriptome and metabolome modification occurring in leaves of four GE rice lines expressing *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) genes that developed by genetic engineering and seven rice lines developed by conventional cross-breeding. The results showed that both types of crop breeding methods can bring changes at transcriptomic and metabolic levels, but the differences were comparable between the two methods, and were less than those between conventional non-GE lines. Metabolome profiling analysis found several new metabolites in GE rice lines when compared to the closest non-GE parental lines, but these compounds were also found in several of the conventionally bred rice lines. Functional analyses suggest that the differentially expressed genes and metabolites caused by both genetic engineering and conventional cross-breeding do not involve detrimental metabolic pathways. The study successfully employed RNA-sequencing and HPLC-MS technology to assess the unintended changes in new rice varieties, and the results suggest that genetic engineering does not cause unintended effects that go beyond conventional cross-breeding in rice.

53 Genetic engineering (GE) is widely used to introduce desirable traits such as insect resistance. 54 disease resistance, herbicide tolerance, drought tolerance and improved nutrition in crops (ISAAA, 2018). Amongst, the insect-resistant genetically engineered (IRGE) crops producing insecticidal 55 56 proteins, crystal (Cry) and vegetative insecticidal proteins (Vip) derived from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) have been widely grown worldwide (ISAAA, 2018). In 2018, a total of 57 104 million hectares of Bt-transgenic IRGE crops including soybean (Glycine max), cotton 58 (Gossypium species), maize (Zea mays), and to small extent eggplant (Solanum melongena) and 59 60 sugarcane (Saccharum officinarum) were planted (ISAAA, 2018). The wide growing of Bt crops 61 has provided area-wide suppression of some major lepidopteran crop pests such as pink bollworm 62 (Pectinophora gossypiella) (Carrière et al., 2003), cotton bollworm (Helicoverpa armigera) (Wu 63 et al., 2008), European corn borer (Ostrinia nubilalis) (Hutchison et al., 2010; Dively et al., 2018), and corn earworm (*Helicoverpa zea*) (Dively *et al.*, 2018), leading to significant decrease of 64 broad-spectrum chemical insecticide application (Klümper & Oaim, 2014; NASEM, 2016; 65 66 Brookes & Barfoot, 2018; Li et al., 2020). 67 In contrast to the general acceptance of crops obtained by conventional breeding and associated food products, IRGE crops are subjected to rigorous evaluation. The advancements in omics-based systems biology including genomics, transcriptomics, 83 proteomics, and metabolomics profiling have greatly enhanced the possibilities to analyze 84 unintended changes in plants and these techniques have been shown to be powerful approaches for 85 identifying changes in GE plants as has been demonstrated for example for Arabidopsis, rice, 86 maize, soybean, barley, and pigeon pea (Kuiper et al., 2001; El Ouakfaoui and Miki, 2005; 87 Ricroch et al., 2011; Gong & Wang, 2013; Herman & Price, 2013; Wang et al., 2018b; Tan et al., 88 89 2019). In most study cases, however, comparative analyses were restricted to one GE line and its 90 closest non-GE counterpart. These results commonly revealed certain differences in the 91 transcriptomes, proteomes, and metabolomes of the tested plants (Ricroch et al., 2011; Gong & 92 Wang, 2013; Wang et al., 2018b). However, such results can hardly tell whether such differences 93 are specific to plant genetic engineering breeding other than conventional breeding and whether 94 they represent safety problems and their value for risk assessment is thus questionable (Raybould 95 & Macdonald, 2018). So far, little effort has been made to compare the potential unintended 96 effects brought by genetic engineering and by conventional plant breeding approaches (Batista et 97 al., 2008; Gong et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2018b). Recently, a tiered evaluation strategy for 98 analyzing unintended changes in crops using -omics technologies was proposed by experts from 99 the National Academics of Science, Engineering and Medicine, which highly recommend that 100 unintended changes in a new GE variety should be evaluated by comparison with a set of 101 conventionally bred cultivars, but not just with their parental isolines (NASEM, 2016). 102 Rice (Oryza sativa) is an important staple food for more than half of the population in China (Li 103 et al., 2020). Rice production is constrained by a complex of insect pests, among which lepidopterans such as the rice striped stem borer (Chilo suppressalis), yellow stem borer 104 105 (Scirpophaga incertulas), and rice leaf roller (Cnaphalocrocis medinalis) can cause a massive 106 vield losses (Li et al., 2020). To control these pests in an efficient and environmentally friendly, 107 dozens of IRGE rice lines expressing Bt proteins, have been developed in China, and multiple

lines exhibited high efficacy in target pest control (Chen *et al.*, 2006, 2011; Liu *et al.*, 2016; Li *et al.*, 2020). When assessing the risks of Bt rice, most studies were target-oriented and focused on
the intended effects, while little attention was paid to the unintended effects (Xue *et al.*, 2012; Li *et al.*, 2017; Fu *et al.*, 2019).

In the current study, we compared the biological variation at mRNA and metabolite levels among four GE rice lines and nine conventionally bred rice cultivars that have close genetic relation with the GE rice lines (**Figure 1**). We performed two of the omics-based systems biology approaches including transcriptomics using RNA-seq (RNA-sequencing) and metabolomics using high-performance liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS), and instigated these datasets. Based on these results, the potential unintended effects caused by two different plant breeding methods were analysed comparably.

#### 267 **DISCUSSION**

Transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics have been widely used in assessing effects of genetic engineering in crop breeding at the mRNA, protein, and metabolite levels (NASEM, 270 2016). However, in most of these studies analyses were restricted to comparing one GE line and

its non-GE counterpart, and the results typically revealed some degree variation. Since the exact 271 functions of the majority of genes, proteins and metabolites in a plant cell are not clear, such 272 273 results can hardly indicate the biological relevance of the detected changes, and can not reflect whether the observed differences were caused by the genetic engineering or fall into the potential 274 275 variation range caused by conventional breeding (Ricroch *et al.*, 2011; Gong & Wang, 2013; Herman & Price, 2013; NASEM, 2016; Raybould & Macdonald, 2018). We thus attempted to test 276 in rice whether genetic engineering breeding will result in novel or greater unintended effects in 277 crops relative to conventional cross-breeding using a total of four GE and nine conventional rice 278 279 lines. We believe that rice is a good model plant for those investigations since it is an important 280 food crop and has been widely used in the past for plant biology studies including omics analyses. 281 Previous studies have suggested that environmental stresses brought more biological variation 282 than transgenesis and genetic background in plants (El Ouakfaoui and Miki, 2005; Batista et al., 2008; Montero et al., 2011; Asiago et al., 2012; Batista et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018b; Tan et 283 al., 2019). In the present study, the seeds of all 13 rice lines were prepared in the same way and 284 were cultivated and managed under the same conditions in a greenhouse to minimize the effects of 285 varying environmental conditions. In addition, the middle part of the top second fully expanded 286 287 leaf of tillering plants from all rice lines were sampled on the same day to reduce potential effects 288 caused by plant developmental stages. Thus, the differences in rice leaf transcriptome and 289 metabolome profiles should largely reflect the genotype differences of the tested rice lines. 290 Furthermore, to increase the comparability, conventional and GE rice varieties used in this study 291 have the same parental line either MH63 or XS11 to ensure that they had a similar genetic background (Figure 1). 292

Both PCA and hierarchical cluster analyses of the raw datasets showed a distinct separation between samples with GJ and those with XI genetic background at both transcriptome and metabolome levels, irrespective of the breeding method used to develop the rice lines (GE or conventional cross-breeding). This result is consistent with previous studies that the GJ and XI subspecies have clear distinction in gene expression, protein characterization, metabolite accumulation, and even in root microbiota composition (Jung *et al.*, 2013; Hu *et al.*, 2014; Wang

et al., 2018a; Zhang et al., 2019). In plants, natural variation in the transcriptome and metabolome 299 is very common (Catchpole et al., 2005; Batista & Oliveira, 2010; Baniasadi et al., 2014; Wang et 300 301 al., 2018a, b). Studies with wheat and barley showed a clear discrimination between different conventionally bred varieties but no discrimination between GE and non-GE counterparts at 302 transcriptomic or metabolomic levels (Ioset et al., 2007; Kogel et al., 2010). Similar results were 303 also found in Embrapa 5.1 common bean with higher similarity between a GE variety and its 304 non-GE near-isogenic line than between two common bean varieties in both leaf and grain 305 proteomic profiles (Balsamo et al., 2015; Valentim-Neto et al., 2016). The current finding together 306 307 with previous results suggest that the intrinsic differences in genetic background bring much 308 greater variation on plant transcriptome and metabolome than by the introduction of foreign genes 309 by genetic manipulation or cross-breeding methods.

310 As expected, pairwise comparisons did reveal some differences between the GE and parental non-GE rice lines in respect to gene expression and metabolite accumulation as reported 311 previously for GE maize and sovbean (Cheng et al., 2008; Coll et al., 2008; Hao et al., 2017; 312 Wang *et al.*, 2018b). But the number of DEGs observed when comparing GE and non-GE rice 313 314 lines were comparable to those present when comparing conventional bred rice lines and their 315 parental lines (Figure 2 and Figure 3). An exception was the comparison between HH1 and 316 MH63 where a little higher variation in gene expression was detected than between conventional 317 bred rice lines and their parental lines. This increase in variation, however, was not supported by 318 DAMs, i.e., the number of DMAs between HH1 and MH63 was much lower than that in most of 319 the comparisons between conventional bred rice lines and their parental lines. Also, compared to the common parental line XS11, KMD2 had nearly 3-fold DEGs and 2-fold DAMs than KMD1, 320 although the two GE lines have a matched genotype, express the same gene and were created with 321 322 the same gene transformation process (Table S10). These results thus suggest that genetic changes 323 commonly occur during the plant breeding process whether done by genetic engineering or 324 conventional crossing, and that the extent of those changes seems not always relevant to the extent of metabolomic change in rice plant. We therefore suggest, that transcriptomics results should be 325 integrated with results from other omics approaches such as metabolomics and proteomics to more 326

comprehensively show the possible unintended effects caused by plant breeding process. 327 328 The Venn diagrams of genotype-specific DEGs and DAMs showed that both genetic 329 engineering and the conventional breeding processes can result in a large number of DEGs and DAMs in plants. Although there were large overlapping sets of DEGs and DAMs caused by both 330 plant breeding processes, we did find some DEGs and DAMs specifically caused by genetic 331 332 engineering that were outside of the range of variation in conventional breeding rice lines (Figure 2f and Figure 3f). It implies that the genetic engineering process may bring different stress on the 333 334 host genome relative to conventional cross-breeding, namely two plant breeding processes may 335 lead to the variations in gene and metabolite at different levels. To conclude this, however, a larger 336 set of conventionally bred rice varieties would have to be analyzed. Interestingly, we detected 337 more DAMs in plants caused by conventional breeding than the genetic breeding process. This 338 may imply that conventional cross-breeding required multiple repeated crosses between two or more breeding lines, thus causing more changes on both genotypic and phenotypic levels (Coll et 339 340 al., 2009).

Our GO enrichment analyses indicate that the DEGs in different comparisons were involved in 341 342 "DNA integration". It can be speculated that the differences in gene expression brought by either genetic engineering or conventional breeding, or due to natural variation are all associated with 343 344 changes in DNA sequence. There were many DEGs and DAMs detected not only between GE rice 345 lines and their non-GE counterparts, but also between conventionally bred rice lines and their 346 parental lines (Figure 2f). However, the DEGs detected between GE rice line and their non-GE 347 counterparts were significantly enriched only in the photosynthesis, but the DEGs between conventionally bred rice lines and their parental lines were significantly enriched within multiple 348 349 pathways. Likewise, the DAMs were only found to be enriched only in the flavonoid biosynthesis 350 pathway between GE rice line and their non-GE counterparts, but the DAMs between 351 conventionally bred rice lines and their parental lines were enriched in both the flavonoid 352 biosynthesis pathway and the flavone and flavonol biosynthesis pathway. These results may suggest that genetic engineering does not bring unique effects on plant pathways compared to 353 conventional cross-breeding. All four GE rice lines used in the current study expressed foreign Bt 354

*cry* genes conferring resistance to insects. The expressed Cry proteins are not native to plants and exert no known metabolic activity in rice plants (Wang *et al.*, 2018b; Fu *et al.*, 2019). While if the inserted genes in GE plants are involved in plant metabolic pathways, the results may be different (Wang *et al.*, 2018b; Wang *et al.*, 2019). There is a case for example in GE plants that are tolerant to the herbicide glyphosate since the tolerance is conferred by introducing a glyphosate-insensitive version of the target enzyme 5-enolpyruvoylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase (EPSPS) which is a key enzyme in the shikimate pathway.

362 The substantial equivalence concept is an important part in the safety assessment of GE crops 363 (Ricroch et al., 2011; Asiago et al., 2012). Our metabolomics analysis did detect some 364 compounds, including (+)-piperitol, 2-methoxybenzoic acid, epigallate catechin gallate (EGCG), 365 icariin, cyanidin, procyanidin B2, and pinocembrin in GE rice tissues that had not been detected in the non-GE parental plants. However, all of these compounds were also found in several of the 366 conventionally bred rice lines. Since the conventionally bred rice lines have been widely planted 367 and have a long history of safe use, these compounds will not bring detrimental effects on human 368 health and the environment. Our results further strengthen the fact that assessment of unintended 369 370 effects of GE plants can not simply rely on the comparison between GE plants and their parental 371 lines but should include a set of conventional bred cultivars that represent the range of genetic and 372 phenotypic diversity in the crop (NASEM, 2016). The integrated application of multi-omics approaches can more comprehensively reflect the changes in the plants and their biological 373 374 relevance.

375 In conclusion, we successfully employed RNA-seq and HPLC-MS technology to investigate the changes in gene expression and metabolites accumulation in 13 rice lines developed by 376 conventional cross-breeding or genetic engineering. Our results demonstrate that the emerging 377 378 -omics approaches can provide a valid way for identifying unintended effects of GE varieties. The 379 current findings suggest that both breeding methods can result in potential changes at 380 transcriptomic and metabolic levels, and it appears that genetic engineering does not cause unintended effects that go beyond conventional cross-breeding. Although we did detect DEGs and 381 DAMs specifically caused by genetic engineering that were outside of the range of variation in 382

conventional breeding rice lines, this could be due to the limited number of conventionally bred
rice lines involved in the study; it can be expected that the natural variation in DEGs and DAMs is
actually much larger. Therefore, a comprehensive range of variation at transcriptome, metabolome
and proteome levels in commercially conventional bred cultivars of a crop species would have to
be established before those analyses are deployed for identifying the unintended effects in GE
varieties.

#### 391 Plant materials

392 A total of 13 rice lines (*Oryza sativa*) including four lines developed by genetic engineering (GE) 393 expressing *Bt* genes and nine lines obtained by conventional cross-breeding were used in this study (Figure 1 and Table S10). The Bt rice lines include T1C-19, which expresses a synthesized 394 *crv1C* gene driven by the maize *ubiquitin* promoter; the Bt rice line HH1 expresses a fused 395 *cry1Ab/Ac* gene driven by the rice *actin1* promoter. Both T1C-19 and HH1 share the same 396 397 non-transformed near isoline MH63, which is an elite XI restorer line for cytoplasmic male 398 sterility in China (Wu et al., 2011). The other two Bt rice lines are KMD1 and KMD2, and their 399 corresponding non-transformed near isoline XS11. XS11 is a GJ rice line widely cultivated in 400 China. KMD1 and KMD2 are independent homozygous events containing the same synthetic 401 *crylAb* gene under the control of the maize *ubiquitin* promoter. All the Bt rice lines were 402 developed by Agrobacterium tumefaciens infection. Laboratory and field experiments indicated 403 that all four Bt rice lines are highly resistant to target lepidopteran insects (Liu et al., 2016). Among the nine non-Bt rice lines used in this study, four are XI lines (XY63, JY63, XXY63, and 404 405 SY63) obtained by crossing MH63 with different rice varieties, and three are GJ lines (CJ03, JH1, 406 and T202) obtained by crossing XS11 with other rice varieties (Figure 1). The seven cross-407 pollination rice lines are all approved by provincial or national crop variety registration committees in China. Among these lines, SY63 was widely planted with the highest total area of 408 409 62.88 million hectares from 1984 to 2009 (Wu et al., 2011).

#### 410 Plant growth condition and tissue sampling

Rice seeds were dehusked, surface-sterilized using 75% ethanol for 5 min, and washed with 411 412 sterilized water. The seeds were then soaked in 4% sodium hypochlorite solution for 30 min and washed again with sterilized water. Subsequently the surface-sterilized rice seeds were germinated 413 414 on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium in a climate chamber at the conditions of 28 415  $\pm$  1 °C, a 16 L : 8 D photoperiod and 75  $\pm$  5% relative humidity (RH). A week later, seedlings 416 were transplanted into individual plastic pots (8 cm  $\times$  10 cm. diameter  $\times$  height) containing a mixture of peat and vermiculite in a 3:1 ratio (Meihekou Factory, Meihekou, China). All potted 417 418 plants were placed in a cement pool that was maintained in a glasshouse at the Langfang 419 Experimental Station of the Institute of Plant Protection, Chinese Academy of Agricultural 420 Sciences (CAAS). The growth conditions were set as  $28 \pm 2$  °C,  $65 \pm 10\%$  RH and a 16-h light / 8-h dark photoperiod. Nitrogenous fertilizer Sakefu (N [20%], P<sub>2</sub>O<sub>5</sub> [20%], K<sub>2</sub>O [20%]) and other 421 422 microelements (Sino-Arab Chemical Fertilizer Co., Ltd, Qinhuangdao, China) was applied once a week. Five weeks later, during the rice plants tillering stage, leaf samples were collected for the 423 424 analyses. From each plant, a leaf section (approximately 2 cm) was sampled from the middle part of 2<sup>nd</sup> leaf blade from top. Samples from five plants were pooled together as one biological 425 replicate, and three replicates were collected for each rice line. The leaf samples were immediately 426 427 frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for further extraction and analyses.

#### **RNA extraction, library preparation and RNA-sequencing**

Total RNA was isolated using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and treated
with RNase-free DNase I (NEB, USA) to remove any genomic DNA. A total amount of 3 μg of
total RNA per sample was used for the preparation of RNA-seq library. Sequencing libraries were
prepared using a NEBNext® Ultra<sup>TM</sup> RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) and were
sequenced on the Illumina Hiseq 4000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the
manufacturer's instructions by Nonogene (Beijing, China).

For transcriptome data, raw Illumina data of fastq format were first processed using in-house
perl scripts. After removing reads containing adaptors, reads containing poly-N and low-quality

reads from raw data, we obtained the clean data. The Q20, Q30, GC (guanine-cytosine)-content 437 438 and sequence duplication level of the clean data were calculated. The clean reads were aligned to 439 the reference genome IRGSP-1.0 (https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp)using HISAT2 tools (version 2.09) (Kim et al., 2015). Feature Counts (v1.5.0-p3) (Liao et al., 2014) was used to count the reads 440 numbers mapped to each gene. Gene expression levels were estimated using fragments per 441 kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) based on the length of the gene and reads 442 count mapped to this gene. Differentially expressed genes analysis was performed using the 443 444 DESeq2 R package (Love *et al.*, 2014). The resulting *P* values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg's approach for the control of the false-discovery rate (FDR). Genes with fold 445 change  $\geq 2$  or  $\leq 0.5$  and an adjusted P value less than 0.05 were considered as DEGs. GO, and 446 447 KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed using the clusterProfiler R package with an 448 FDR adjusted *P* value < 0.05 (hypergeometric test) as a cutoff (Yu *et al.*, 2012).

#### 450 Metabolites extraction process

Freeze-dried leaf samples of the 13 rice lines were grinded using a mixer mill (MM 400, Retsch,
Haan, Germany) with a zirconia bead for 1.5 min at 30 Hz. For each sample, 100 mg of leaf power
was weighted and mixed with 1 ml of 70 % aqueous methanol for extraction overnight at 4 °C.
Following centrifugation at 10000 *g* for 10 min the extracts were absorbed (CNWBOND
Carbon-GCB SPE Cartridge, 250 mg, 3 ml; ANPEL, Shanghai, China) and filtrated with a
membrane (SCAA-104, 0.22 µm pore size; ANPEL, Shanghai, China), and subsequently stored in
a glass vial before analysis.

## 458 High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) and ESI-Q TRAP-MS/MS running 459 conditions

460 Metabolites identification and quantification were performed using a liquid

- 461 chromatography-electrospray ionization-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-ESI- MS/MS) system
- 462 (high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC): Shim-pack UFLC SHIMADZU CBM20A

system; MS, Applied Biosystems 4000 Q TRAP) as previously described (Chen et al., 2013). The 463 464 HPLC analytical conditions were set as follows: the chromatographic column was a Waters 465 ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). The solvent system included mobile phase A, 0.04% acetic acid in water, and mobile phase B, 0.04% acetic acid in 466 acetonitrile. The gradient programme was set at 95:5 V(A)/V(B) at 0 min, 5:95 V(A)/V(B) at 11 467 min, 5:95 V(A)/V(B) at 12 min, 95:5 V(A)/V(B) at 12.1 min, 95:5 V(A)/V(B) at 15 min. The 468 temperature of the column was 40 °C, the flow rate was 0.4 ml/min, and the injection volume was 469  $5 \,\mu$ L. The effluent was then connected to an ESI-triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (Q TRAP)-MS. 470 Linear ion trap (LIT) and triple quadrupole (OOO) scans were acquired on a O TRAP-MS. 471 472 API 4500 Q TRAP LC/MS/MS System, equipped with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray interface, 473 operating in a positive ion mode and controlled by the Analyst 1.6 software (AB Sciex, Darmstadt, 474 Germany). The ESI source operation was set with the following parameters: ion source, turbo spray; source temperature, 550 °C; ion spray voltage (IS), 5.5 kV; ion source gas I, gas II, and 475 curtain gas were set at 55, 60, and 25 psi, respectively; the collision gas (CAD) was high. 476 Instrument tuning and mass calibration were performed with 10 and 100 µmol/L polypropylene 477 478 glycol solutions in QQQ and LIT modes, respectively. The metabolites were identified according 479 to the secondary spectrum information. QQQ scans were acquired as multiple reaction monitoring 480 mode experiments with the collision gas (nitrogen) set to 5 psi.

#### 488 Data analysis

489

Raw data associated with gene expression and metabolites accumulation were median-normalized,

log-transformed, and auto-scaled using MetaboAnalyst 4.0 (Chong et al., 2018). Hierarchical 490 491 clustering heat map was created using MetaboAnalyst 4.0 with Euclidean distance measure and 492 Ward clustering algorithm. The normalized data were fed to SIMCA 14.1 software (Umetrics, 493 Umea, Sweden) for principal component analysis (PCA). OPLS-DA (orthogonal projections to 494 latent structures-discriminant analysis) was conducted for identifying DAMs between any two rice lines using SIMCA 14.1 software. The variable importance in the projection (VIP) values  $\geq 1.0$ 495 496 generated in OPLS-DA processing were first used as a criterion for DAMs selection. The fold 497 changes  $\geq 2$  or  $\leq 0.5$  were defined as DAMs. Fisher's exact test was performed to identify the significant KEGG pathways related to the DAMs with an adjusted P value less than 0.05. 498
