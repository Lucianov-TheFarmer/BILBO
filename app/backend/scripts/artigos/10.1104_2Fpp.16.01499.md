### 24 **Abstract**

25 Epigenetic modifications play critical roles in diverse biological processes. Histone lysine to 26 methionine (K-to-M) mutations act as gain-of-function mutations to inhibit a wide range of 27 histone methyltransferases and are thought to promote tumorigenesis. However, it is largely 28 unknown whether K-to-M mutations impact organismal development. Using *Arabidopsis* as a 29 model system, we discovered that a transgene exogenously expressing histone 3 lysine 36 to 30 methionine mutation (K36M) acts in a dominant negative manner to cause global reduction of 31 H3K36 methylation. Remarkably, this dominant repressive activity is dosage-dependent and 32 causes strong developmental perturbations including extreme branching and early flowering by 33 affecting the expression of genes involved in developmental and metabolic processes. Besides 34 the established pathological roles of lysine to methionine mutations in tumor cells, we 35 demonstrate a physiological outcome for K-to-M induced H3K36 hypomethylation. This study 36 provides evidence for a conserved dominant negative inhibitory role of histone K-to-M mutation 37 across the plant and animal kingdoms. We also highlight the unique ability of K36M mutations 38 to alter plant developmental processes leading to severe pleiotropic phenotypes. Finally, our data 39 suggests K-to-M mutations may provide a useful strategy for altering epigenetic landscapes in 40 organisms where histone methyltransferases are uncharacterized.

#### 47 **Background**

48 Genome compaction restricts, permits, and fine-tunes gene expression. Chromatin is composed 49 of DNA coiled around nucleosomes and exists in relaxed or condensed forms (Luger et al., 1997). 50 The lysine residues on histone H3 function not only as peptide building blocks, but also as a site 51 of diverse post-translational modifications (Strahl and Allis, 2000). Modifications also serve as a 52 mark of the gene expression state and act as a platform for the active control of transcription. For 53 example, methylated histone lysine residues are known to recruit histone modifying enzymes, 54 chromatin remodelers, and reader proteins to moderate chromatin state and transcription (He et 55 al., 2011; Xu et al., 2014).

56 Histone modification can be associated with either gene activation or repression, 57 depending on its position and state. H3 lysine 36 trimethylation (H3K36me3) is a highly 58 conserved histone mark and is primarily correlated with active gene expression in eukaryotes 59 (Roudier et al., 2011; Wen et al., 2014). In *Arabidopsis,* H3K36me3 is enriched at the gene-body 60 regions and plays an important role in regulating the expression of development-related genes 61 involved in brassinosteroid signaling pathways, carbon and light responses, floral organ 62 development, and flowering (Xu et al., 2008; Grini et al., 2009; He et al., 2011; Wang et al., 63 2014; Yang et al., 2014; Li et al., 2015). Deposition of H3K36 methylation is mediated by a 64 collection of conserved SET domain-containing group (SDG) histone methyltransferases. Loss-65 of-function mutations in *Arabidopsis* SDG8 (also named as EFS/ASHH2) result in a genome-66 wide reduction of both di- and tri- H3K36 methylation (H3K36me2/3) and defects in biological 67 processes including proliferative branching (Dong et al., 2008; Cazzonelli et al., 2009), early 68 flowering (Soppe et al., 1999; Zhao et al., 2005), reduced plant pathogen response (Berr et al., 69 2010), decreased carotenoid biosynthesis (Cazzonelli et al., 2009), and weakened innate

70 immunity (Palma et al., 2010). SDG26 has also been shown to be required for H3K36me3 71 accumulation at individual loci (Berr et al., 2015), but does not appear to have an effect on global 72 H3K36me3 levels (Liu et al., 2016). Notably, the reduced, but not completely, eliminated 73 H3K36me2/3 in *sdg8* mutants indicates the existence of yet-to-be-identified methyltransferase(s) 74 that functions redundantly with SDG8. The increased H3K36me1 in *sdg8* also suggests that 75 distinct histone methyltransferase(s) may be responsible for H3K36me1 in *Arabidopsis*.

76 Previous studies have demonstrated that missense mutations of H3 lysine 27 to 77 methionine (K27M) exerted a dominant negative effect on genome-wide endogenous H3K27me3 78 levels by inhibiting Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) activity (Chan et al., 2013; Lewis 79 et al., 2013). In humans, these gross changes in histone methylation levels were highly associated 80 with pediatric glioma and high-grade astrocytoma formation (Chan et al., 2013; Lewis et al., 81 2013; Bechet, 2014). Similarly, exogenous expression of K27M and K9M was shown to reduce 82 H3K27 and H3K9 methylation, respectively. The corresponding methylation losses lead to the 83 de-repression of several developmental genes for K27M and suppression of position-effect 84 variegation for K9M in *Drosophila* (Herz et al., 2014). Global reduction of endogenous H3K36 85 methylation by exogenously expressed K36M mutant protein was also observed in cultured cells 86 (Lewis et al., 2013). Such K36M mutations were associated with 95% of chondroblastomas 87 (Behjati et al., 2013). More recently, K36M mutation was shown to contribute to tumorigenesis 88 by altering the expression of differentiation or cancer-related genes (Fang et al., 2016; Lu et al., 89 2016).

90 While K-to-M mutations promoting tumorigenesis have been investigated (Chan et al., 91 2013; Lewis et al., 2013; Fang et al., 2016; Lu et al., 2016), it is unknown whether K-to-M 92 mutations influence organismal development. Using *Arabidopsis* as a model system, we report

93 the first developmental phenotypic effects for the K36M mutation. We report a wide range of 94 developmental defects for K36M mutant plants, including increased branching and early 95 flowering. We further show that the K36M mutation reduces global H3K36 methylation and 96 contributes to pleiotropic plant development, in part by altering expression of developmental-97 and metabolic-associated genes. Together, these data demonstrate that K36M acts as a driving 98 mutation to alter plant development.

100 **Results** 

#### 101 **K36M mutation induces pleiotropic developmental defects in** *Arabidopsis*

102 To investigate the biological consequences of K36M, we generated a transgene construct 103 containing H3.3 K36M fused with a c-terminal 3xFLAG epitope tag under the control of its 104 native promoter (Supplementary Fig. S1A). As a control, we produced a wild type 3xFLAG 105 tagged H3.3 transgenic plant (named WT), which had no phenotypic differences from the 106 Columbia ecotype. As shown in Fig. 1A, transgenic plants expressing heterozygous K36M 107 (named K36M) exhibited a pleiotropic, highly stunted developmental phenotype compared to 108 WT. Interestingly, homozygous K36M transgenic plants (K36M hom) exhibit a gene dosage 109 dependent phenotype with further stunting and infertility (Fig. 1A). Overall, we recovered a total 110 of three independent transgenic lines, all of which displayed similar phenotypic defects (data not 111 shown). Given that heterozygous K36M is sufficient to cause strong developmental defects and 112 that homozygous K36M is nearly infertile, we focused our studies on heterozygous K36M. We 113 noted an early flowering time (Fig. 1B) and a significant increase in rosette stem production in 114 K36M (Fig. 1C). We also found that K36M mutants exhibited shorter siliques and a strong 115 reduction in the number of fertilized ovules compared to WT (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Fig. S1B 116 and C). Interestingly, null mutants of the H3K36 histone methyltransferase SDG8 also 117 phenocopy K36M heterozygous plants with increased shoot and stem branching, early flowering, 118 and decreased fertility (Fig. 1) (Soppe et al., 1999; Zhao et al., 2005; Dong et al., 2008; 119 Cazzonelli et al., 2009; Grini et al., 2009). Although K36M plants showed phenotypic similarity 120 to *sdg8* null mutants, K36M (hom) exhibited stronger pleiotropic defects.

121

## 122 **K36M alters the expression of development- and metabolism-associated genes**

123 To determine if the phenotypic defects in K36M were correlated with transcriptional alterations, 124 we performed whole transcriptome analysis by mRNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in K36M and WT 125 at 21 days after germination (DAG) (Supplementary Fig. S2A). We found a significant decrease 126 in the expression of 920 genes and an increased expression of 295 genes in K36M compared to 127 WT (p<0.05) (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Table 2). To examine the 128 molecular pathways affected by the K36M mutation, we performed a gene ontology analysis on 129 all differentially expressed genes. Consistent with phenotypic defects in K36M, we found significant enrichments of genes important for signal transduction (*p* =7.2x10-13 130 ), hormone response (*p*=1.2x10-3), oxidative stress response (*p*=5.0x10-5 131 ), and flower development (*p*=1.9x10-2 132 ) (Fig. 2B). Specifically, K36M mutants showed altered expression of genes 133 important for shoot branching and flowering (Fig. 2C-E). We found that both the MAF1 and 134 MAF4 floral repressor genes were downregulated in K36M and the inflorescence promoting 135 genes (AG, AP1, FT and PI) had increased expression (Fig. 2C-E). Additionally, the expression 136 of CRTISO (cis-to-trans carotenoid isomerase), a repressor of shoot branching (Cazzonelli et al., 137 2009), was downregulated in K36M (Fig. 2D, E). SDG8, an H3K36me3 methytransferase known 138 to be involved in flowering, showed no significant expression changes between K36M and WT 139 (Fig. 2D and E). The transcriptional reductions of MAF1 and CRTISO were further confirmed to 140 occur as early as 10 DAG (Supplementary Fig. S2B), correlating with the loss of H3K36me3 141 (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Thus, the pleiotropic developmental phenotypes in K36M are likely 142 caused by mis-expression of key development and metabolic-associated genes.

143

#### 144 **K36M mutation exhibits a dominant negative effect on endogenous H3K36 methylation**

145 Given knowledge of K36M mutation inducing global reduction of H3K36 methylation in 146 mammals (Lewis et al., 2013; Lu et al., 2016), we hypothesized that K36M may exert similar 147 effects on the histone methylation landscape in plants. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed the 148 accumulation level of various histone methylation marks in K36M by western blot. We included 149 a null mutant of H3K36 methyltransferase *sdg8* as a control, given the strong correlation of *sdg8* 150 mutant phenotype with the K36M phenotype (Fig. 1A). We found that H3K36me2 and 151 H3K36me3 were substantially reduced in two independent K36M transgenic lines at 21 DAG 152 (Fig. 3A). We further confirmed that the K36M reduction of H3K36me3 is independent of 153 developmental stage (Supplemental Fig. S2C). The reduction of H3K36me3 in K36M appeared 154 slightly stronger than that of *sdg8*. H3K4me3 showed no noticeable change in either K36M or 155 *sdg8*. These observations were further confirmed by mass spectrometry analysis showing the 156 reduction of H3K36 in all forms of methylation (me1, me2, me3) (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Fig. 157 S3). This suggests that K36M mutant histones act as a dominant negative inhibitor of 158 endogenous H3K36 methylation. To further determine the potency of the K36M as an inhibitor, 159 we searched for the peptide signature of M36, based on its mass difference from K36, using mass 160 spectrometry. Consistent with the low amount of K36M in tumors and cultured cells 161 (Schwartzentruber et al., 2012; Behjati et al., 2013; Fang et al., 2016; Lu et al., 2016), we found 162 that K36M peptides were present at an abundance of 1.4% among total detected H3.3 peptides 163 (Supplementary Fig. S3A). The low abundance of K36M peptides suggests that they act as 164 potent inhibitors of endogenous H3K36 methylation.

165 To determine whether K36M reduced H3K36 methylation at all or selected genomic loci, 166 we profiled the genome-wide H3K36me3 distribution in K36M utilizing chromatin 167 immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq). We observed a global reduction of 168 H3K36me3 in the euchromatic arms of five *Arabidopsis* chromosomes after plotting library 169 normalized H3K36me3 density in 500 kb increments (Fig. 4A). To further determine where 170 H3K36me3 loss was occurring (i.e. genes or intergenic regions), we first separated all genes in 171 the genome into six groups by size and plotted library normalized H3K36me3 reads over them. 172 We found a greater loss of H3K36me3 at the gene bodies of large genes than those of small 173 genes (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. S4A). Consistent with our RT-qPCR (Fig. 2E), we found the 174 loss of H3K36me3 in genes (*MAF1, CRTISO, PIF5*) involved in developmental and metabolic 175 processes (Supplementary Fig. S4B). We also identified 6,727 H3K36me3 enriched genes 176 defined in the WT background (Supplementary Table 3) and found that 4,464 were 177 hypomethylated for H3K36me3 in K36M (Supplementary Table 4). This demonstrates that 178 K36M affects many, but not all, H3K36me3 enriched genes (Fig. 4C). To identify the specific 179 features of K36M target genes, we determined the size, expression, and H3K36me3 levels over 180 two gene groups: 4,464 significantly hypomethylated genes in K36M and 2,263 genes with 181 unaltered H3K36me3 levels in K36M (defined in Fig. 4C). We found that K36M 182 hypomethylated genes tended to be large with lower H3K36me3 levels and lower average 183 expression than the unaltered genes (Fig. 4D-F, Supplementary Fig. S4C). To determine if a gene 184 dosage dependent H3K36me3 loss could explain the stronger phenotype of homozygous K36M

185 (K36M hom), we performed ChIP-seq of H3K36me3 in K36M hom plants. We noted that K36M 186 and K36M hom hypomethylated genes (Supplementary Table 5) overlapped by 97%, with K36M 187 hom displaying 1,522 additional hypomethylated genes (Fig. 4G). To determine if a quantitative 188 difference in H3K36me3 level existed between K36M and K36M hom, we plotted library 189 normalized H3K36me3 ChIP-seq read density over the overlapping hypomethylated genes 190 (4,313) and found that the overall H3K36me3 level was further reduced in K36M hom than in 191 K36M (Fig. 4H). Together, K36M mutations exhibit a dosage-dependent dominant negative 192 effect on endogenous H3K36 methylation.

## 194 **K36M hypomethylated genes partially overlap with SDG8 targets**

195 The known role of the SDG8 protein in H3K36me2/3 deposition and the similarity of 196 developmental and molecular phenotypes between *sdg8* and K36M plants prompted us to 197 determine if effects of K36M mutations were dependent on the SDG8 pathway. To test this 198 hypothesis and to minimize variability due to the growth condition, we determined the 199 H3K36me3 profile in 21 DAG *sdg8* plants grown side-by-side with K36M. We identified 3,780 200 genes with significant H3K36me3 reduction in *sdg8* (Supplementary Table 6). Among them, 44% 201 of the hypomethylated genes overlapped significantly with those reported previously (Li et al., 2015) (hypergeometric test *p*-value: 1.38 x 10-297 202 , Supplementary Fig. S5). The different 203 hypomethylated genes in the two datasets are likely due to plant developmental stage (21 DAG 204 vs 14 DAG) and growth conditions (soil vs. MS medium). When comparing the *sdg8* 205 hypomethylated genes (3,780) with the overlapped H3K36me3 hypomethylated genes in K36M 206 and K36M hom (4,313 in Fig. 4G), we noted a significant number of genes are hypomethylated both in K36M and *sdg8* (Fig. 5A, hypergeometric test *p*=7.58×10-315 207 ). This suggests a potential

208 SDG8 dependent mechanism of K36M action. Interestingly, K36M also caused a significant 209 reduction of H3K36me3 in an additional 2,495 genes that did not overlap with *sdg8*  210 hypomethylated genes (Fig. 5A). These data suggest that the K36M mutation may also function 211 through an *SDG8* independent mechanism to inhibit endogenous histone methylation. 212 Interestingly even at the SDG8 unique targets (1,962), there is significant reduction of 213 H3K36me3 in K36M (Fig. 5B), suggesting that these genes are likely the weak K36M targets. 214 Utilizing previously published data (Li et al., 2015), we also plotted the genomic location of 215 SDG8 over its 728 direct target genes and found that SDG8 is specifically enriched at the gene 216 body (Fig. 5C). This is consistent with our observations that the large amount of H3K36me3 is 217 reduced within the gene body in K36M (Fig. 4B), supporting our hypothesis that K36M inhibits 218 endogenous H3K36me3 partially via the SDG8 pathway (Fig. 5D). Given that K36M hom 219 exhibits much stronger developmental defects than the *sdg8* null mutant, our results suggest that 220 K36M may also inhibit an unknown histone methyltransferase(s), which in turn leads to the 221 reduction of H3K36me3 at the transcription start sites (Fig. 5D).

## 223 **Discussion**

224 In multicellular eukaryotes, gene expression is controlled through a hierarchy of mechanisms 225 that function on the scale of individual genes to the entire genome. This study investigates the 226 physiological consequences of disruption of one such regulatory mechanism, histone post-227 translational modifications, using a histone K-to-M missense mutation. Our work revealed that a 228 transgene exogenously expressing H3.3 K36M acts in a dominant negative manner to induce 229 global reduction of H3K36 methylation. Remarkably, this dominant repressive activity causes a 230 strong developmental perturbation by affecting the expression of genes involved in

231 developmental and metabolic processes. Besides its established pathological roles in tumor cells, 232 we demonstrate a dominant negative inhibitory function of K36M in plants that leads to severe 233 developmental defects.

234 When comparing the phenotype of K36M with *sdg8,* we noted the transgenic plants 235 expressing heterozygous K36M transgene show phenotypic similarity to *sdg8* with increased 236 branching, early flowering, and reduced fertility. Interestingly, homozygous K36M transgenic 237 plants show greater expressivity with stunted growth and near infertility (Fig. 1A), suggesting 238 that K36M acts in a gene dosage dependent manner to inhibit plant growth and development. 239 This is consistent with the idea that the concentration of K-to-M oncohistone is important for its 240 oncogenic potential (Jayaram et al., 2016). Although K36M appears phenotypically similar to 241 *sdg8*, we also observed greater reduction of H3K36me3 in K36M mutants in our western blot 242 analysis (Fig. 3A). Our ChIP-seq further reveals that K36M preferentially targets genes with low 243 levels of H3K36me3 when compared to all H3K36me3-enriched genes in WT. Unlike in cell 244 culture models, we note a reduction, but not complete loss of H3K36me3 in K36M (Fig. 3; Fig. 245 4D). The residual H3K36me3 may reflect the activity of other H3K36me3 methyltransferases 246 that are unaffected by K36M inhibition. It may also be the result of utilizing whole plants as 247 experimental material that contains a mixture of different cell types. This is consistent with 248 previous observations of tissue-specific impairment of histone mutation in cancer cells 249 (Schwartzentruber et al., 2012; Behjati et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2014).

250 Multiple lines of evidence suggest that the dominant negative affect of the K36M mutant 251 histone partially acts through SDG8 function. We found a large amount of K36M and SDG8 252 target genes overlap, and the reduction of H3K36me3 in K36M occurs most readily in the gene 253 body where SDG8 is primarily localized (Fig. 4H; Fig. 5C). Furthermore, genes contributing to

254 the stunted phenotype of K36M and *sdg8* were misregulated in both mutants. Specifically, 255 K36M mutants exhibited downregulation of many flowering repressor genes (*MAF1*, *MAF2*, 256 *MAF4, MAF5*) and upregulation of floral homeotic genes (*AG*, *AP1*, *PI*, *FUL* and *SEP3*). Similar 257 alterations in gene expression have been reported for the MAF floral repressors in *sdg8* (Xu et al., 258 2008)*.* These data provide compelling evidence that K36M mutants are developmentally primed 259 for early flowering similarly to *sdg8*. Additionally, K36M leads to significant downregulation of 260 *CRTISO,* a carotenoid biosynthesis enzyme that controls branching inhibitor production*.* The 261 downregulation of *CRTISO* may reduce carotenoid precursors and strigolactone production, 262 which could lead to increased axial branching patterns in K36M. Similar reduction of *CRTISO* 263 expression was noted in *sdg8* (Cazzonelli et al., 2009).

264 Studies of K36M mutation in cell culture models suggest that endogenous H3K36 265 methylation reduction occurred through the inhibition of H3K36me3 specific lysine 266 methyltransferases (Fang et al., 2016; Lu et al., 2016). Similarly, mutations of other histone 267 lysine (K9 and K27) to methionine also cause specific reduction in corresponding methylation 268 levels by inhibiting SET-domain containing methyltransferases (Lewis et al., 2013; Brown et al., 269 2014; Jayaram et al., 2016). Thus, it is possible that K36M may act as a potent inhibitor of SDG8. 270 K36M likely acts via a *trans* mechanism to inhibit SDG8 function because H3.1 K36M which 271 has a distinct genomic localization from H3.3 K36M (Supplementary Fig. S6A and B) also 272 causes similar pleiotropic plant developmental defects (Supplementary Fig. S6C). This is in line 273 with previous studies that the dosage of K36M peptide, but not the genomic location, is critical 274 for its inhibitory function (Lu et al., 2016).

275 Besides the methyltransferase inhibition hypothesis, another possibility is that K36M 276 may enhance the activity of an uncharacterized plant H3K36 demethylase(s) as suggested by a 277 recent study (Herz et al., 2014). A third possibility is that K36M may inhibit K36 from becoming 278 acetylated, which affects H3K36me3, gene expression, and development.

279 Although our data suggest that K36M likely acts through SDG8, it also appears to 280 regulate endogenous H3K36me3 independent of SDG8. We found a large number of genes show 281 reduced H3K36me3 levels in K36M but remain unchanged in *sdg8* (Fig. 5A), suggesting that 282 K36M may function as a potent inhibitor of other H3K36 methyltransferases. Besides SDG8, 283 SDG4 and SDG26 have also been implicated in H3K36 methylation in *Arabidopsis* (Cartagena 284 et al., 2008; Xu et al., 2008; Grini et al., 2009; Berr et al., 2015). However, loss-of-function 285 SDG26 mutant is phenotypically distinct from *sdg8* and cannot induce global reduction of 286 H3K36me3 (Xu et al., 2008). In contrast, *sdg4* mutants have reduced fertility as a result of pollen 287 tube growth suppression and exhibit global reduction of H3K4me2/3 and H3K36me2/3 by 288 western blot (Cartagena et al., 2008). Although the precise genomic locations of H3K36me2/3 289 loss are unknown in *sdg4,* the similar phenotype of K36M and *sdg4* suggests it is possible that 290 K36M may affect SDG4 to cause the reduction of H3K36me3 at SDG8-independent genes.

291 While K36M induces global reduction of H3K36 methylation, we do not observe a 292 significant difference of H3K27me3 in K36M compared to WT (Fig. 3), as has been noted in cell 293 culture models (Lu et al., 2016). Instead, we noted a marked increase of H3K27me1 in K36M 294 based on western blot and mass spectrometry (Fig. 3), suggesting a potential distinct mechanism 295 between plants and animals. Interestingly, we found increased H3K27me1 in both replication-296 independent H3 variant H3.3 and replication-dependent H3.1 in K36M (Fig. 3B; Supplementary 297 Fig. 3A and B). The increase of H3K27me1 in K36M is unlikely due to an increased expression 298 of H3K27me1 methyltransferase *ATXR5* or *ATXR6* because we do not observe a significant 299 change of its expression based on our RNA-seq (Supplementary Fig. S7; Supplementary Table 1,

300 Supplementary Table 2). The precise mechanism for this increase is not known, but it appears 301 possible that the conversion of H3K27me1 to H3K27me2 may be impaired in K36M as we noted 302 a significant decrease of H3K27me2 accompanied with H3K27me1 increase in K36M (Fig. 3B). 303 In *Arabidopsis*, H3K27me1 is strongly associated with heterochromatin (Jacob et al., 2009; 304 Roudier et al., 2011), whereas H3K27me2/3 is more commonly found in euchromatic regions 305 (Roudier et al., 2011). Another possibility is that severe reduction of H3K36 methylation may 306 render the corresponding regions more like heterochromatin and less like euchromatin. This 307 transformation of the local chromatin environment could make it more accessible for *ATXR5*/6 to 308 deposit H3K27me1 at these regions. In line with this notion, a recent study proposes a 309 redistribution model for the increased H3K27me3 in K36M in mammals (Lu et al., 2016). In the 310 future, it will be interesting to uncover how K36M modulates the H3K27 methylation levels and 311 how its activities function together with histone methyltransferases and/or other chromatin 312 modifiers to modulate chromatin landscape.

313 Our study reveals, for the first time, that histone K-to-M mutations act as dominant 314 negative gain-of-function mutations in plants. K36M mutations lead to unique developmental 315 aberrations including premature flowering, increased branching and reduced fertility. Given its 316 highly conserved inhibitory role across both plant and animal kingdoms, K-to-M mutation 317 provides a potentially useful strategy to alter epigenetic landscapes in organisms where histone 318 methyltransferases are uncharacterized or where genetic studies are challenging.

323 **Methods**

#### 324 **Plant growth conditions**

325 All plants were grown in long day growth conditions (16 h light/8 h dark) at 21**°**C. Aerial tissues 326 of 10, 14, 18 and 21 day-old plants were harvested for experiments. *sdg8* (*efs-3*) mutant plants 327 were obtained as a gift from Dr. Richard Amasino (Kim et al., 2005).

328

## 329 **Plasmid construction and transformation**

330 Genomic DNA fragment containing full-length of histone H3.3 (AT4G40040) and H3.1 331 (AT5G10390) with their 1Kb promoters were cloned into a pENTR/D-TOPO vector. The K36M 332 mutation was created by site directed mutagenesis using primers described in Supplementary 333 Table 7. Constructs were recombined into a pEarleyGate302 binary vector (Earley et al., 2006; 334 Chen et al., 2016) to create 3xFLAG fusions and transformed into AGL1 *Agrobacterium*  335 *tumefaciens* and further into Col-0 wild type plants via the floral dip method (Clough and Bent, 336 1998). Transgenic plants were selected against using glufosinate (Basta) treatment.

337

## 338 **Immunoblotting**

339 Extracted histones or plant extracts were separated on SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to PVDF 340 membrane, blocked in 3% BSA in TBS (50mM Tris-Cl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20), 341 incubated with primary antibody, and detected with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated α-mouse 342 or α–rabbit secondary antibodies (PI31430 and PI31460, ThermoFisher). Primary antibodies 343 used included: αH3K27me1 (07-448, Millipore), αH3K27me2 (07-452, Millipore), αH3K36me2 344 (ab9049, Abcam), αH3K36me3 (ab9050, Abcam), αH3 (ab1791, Abcam), and αFLAG (F3165,

345 Sigma-Aldrich). Signal was detected through chemiluminescent imaging utilizing a GE 346 Imagequant LAS 4000.

347

#### 348 **Chromatin immunoprecipitation, library preparation, and sequencing**

349 Histone ChIP was performed as previously described (Lu et al., 2015). ChIP-seq libraries were 350 prepared according to the Ovation Ultralow DR multiplex System (NuGEN, #0330). RNA-seq 351 libraries were constructed using a TruSeq RNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, #RS-122- 352 2002). Libraries were sequenced on a HiSeq2000 in the UW-Madison Biotechnology Center.

353

### 354 **Data analysis**

355 For ChIP-seq, reads were aligned to the *Arabidopsis* TAIR10 reference genome using Bowtie2 356 with default parameters (Supplementary Table 8) (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). H3K36me3 357 peaks were called using SICER (Xu et al., 2014) and significantly hypomethylated regions were 358 defined by using SICER-df-rb.sh with a window size of 200 bp and FDR cutoff <0.01. Regions 359 of hypomethylation were mapped to genes using custom scripts and the Bedtools intersect tool 360 (Quinlan, 2014) requiring 75% overlap of the hypomethylated region and the gene. Genome-361 wide density plots of ChIP signal were generated using custom Perl scripts that normalize each 362 sample to its total aligned reads. Metaplots were generated using ngsplot (Shen et al., 2014) and 363 boxplots were produced using R programming language (http://www.R-project.org). Data on 364 SDG8-HA binding sites in *sdg8-5* were obtained from (Li et al., 2015). Overlap of K36M and 365 *sdg8* hypomethylated regions was performed by Venn-Diagram software 366 (www.bioinformatics.lu/venn.php) after genome alignment, peakcalling and gene mapping.
