# Genome-wide analysis of gene expression of EMS-induced short fiber mutant Ligon lintless-y $(li_y)$ in cotton (*Gossypium hirsutum* L.)

### ABSTRACT

In this work we describe a chemically-induced short fiber mutant cotton line, Ligon-lintless-y ( $li_y$ ), which is controlled by a single recessive locus and affects multiple traits, including height of the plant, and length and maturity of fiber. An RNAseq analysis was used to evaluate global transcriptional changes during cotton fiber development at 3, 8 and 16 days post anthesis. We found that 613, 2629 and 3397 genes were significantly down-regulated, while 2700, 477 and 3260 were significantly uncertain  $li_y$  at 3, 8 and 16 DPA. Gene set enrichment analysis revealed that many metabolic pathways, including carbohydrate, cell wall, hormone metabolism and transport were substantially altered in  $li_y$  developing fibers. We discuss perturbed expression of genes involved in signal transduction and biosynthesis of phytohormones, such as auxin, abscisic acid, gibberellin and ethylene. The results of this study provide new insights into transcriptional regulation of cotton fiber development.

### 1. Background

Cotton is the major renewable source of fibers used in the textile industry. Cotton fibers are highly elongated single-celled trichomes that emerge from the outer epidermis of the ovule. Approximately 25% of the ovule epidermal cells differentiate into the commercially important and spinnable "lint" fibers [1,2]. Lint fibers of the economically important upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) generally grow to about 25–30 mm in length. Cotton fiber development consists of four distinct but overlapping stages, including fiber initiation, elongation, secondary cell wall biosynthesis, and maturation [2]. Fiber elongation starts on the day of anthesis and continues for about 3 weeks before the cells switch to intensive secondary cell wall (SCW) cellulose synthesis. The fiber cells elongate up to 3000 fold during 3 weeks which makes them the fastest growing and longest single cell known in higher plants [3]. Apart from its economic importance, the cotton fiber provides a unique single-celled model system to study cell elongation and cell wall biogenesis in the absence of cell division [2]. Understanding the molecular basis of fiber elongation would provide a means for cotton breeders and researchers to improve the fiber length while maintaining yield and other characteristics.

A number of naturally occurring cotton fiber mutations were discovered decades ago [4]. These fiber mutations include, among others, the fiberless trait mutant lines MD17, SL1-7-1, and XZ142w [5,6]; seeds with only lint fibers and no fuzz fibers in the Naked seed lines  $N_1$  [7] and  $n_2$  [8]; and seeds that are described as extremely short lint fibers, in the Ligon lintless-1 ( $Li_1$ ), -2 ( $Li_2$ ) and -x ( $Li_x$ ) [9–11]. Most of the listed above mutations are yet to be identified, except  $N_1$  and  $Li_1$ . The N1 gene encodes a MYB MIXTA-like transcription factor 3 (MML3)/GhMYB25-like in chromosome 12. The extremely low expression of GhMML3\_A12 in N1 is associated with natural antisense transcripts production, driven by its 3' antisense promoter [12]. The  $Li_1$  mutation is caused by a Gly65Val substitution in an actin gene, which disrupts cell polarity and F-actin organization resulting in dwarf, lintless cotton plants [13].

Naturally occurring or spontaneous mutations are rare events. Therefore to genetically dissect a biological process scientists create artificial mutations by using genome manipulation techniques such as CRISPR/Cas9 technology or treating an organism with a mutagenizing agent. Recently, CRISPR/Cas9 system has been successfully applied for targeted mutagenesis in Cotton [14]. There are three other general approaches to create mutations, using radiation, chemical or transposon insertion [15] methods. It has been shown that chemical mutagenesis can be a valuable tool to create a wide range phenotypic variations and study gene functions in cotton [16–18]. Recently a mutant with

<span id="page-1-0"></span>

short fiber phenotype was found in the M2 population of ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutagenized cotton plants; genetics study revealed the mutation is in a single locus and is recessive [\[19\]](#page-8-0). The mutant was named as Ligon lintless-y (liy). The liy homozygous plants exhibit pleiotropy in the form of stunted growth resulting in shorter plants than the wild type (WT) (Fig. 1A).

Mutant analysis in combination with genome-wide analysis of gene expression provides a powerful approach for elucidating fiber developmental processes by comparing gene expression between the mutant and WT plants [20–[27\].](#page-8-0) The rapid expansion of fiber cells requires high turgor pressure and cell wall relaxation [\[3,28\].](#page-8-0) These two cellular processes involve a variety of biosynthetic pathways and are coordinately regulated by developmental signals [\[29\]](#page-8-0). Phytohormones such as auxins [\[30,31\]](#page-8-0), ethylene [\[20\]](#page-8-0) and brassinosteroids [\[32\]](#page-8-0) are involved in regulation of fiber development. There are number of studies highlighted importance of carbohydrate and lipid metabolisms in cotton fiber development [\[21,25,33,34\]](#page-8-0). Several studies have revealed a critical role of actin cytoskeleton in cotton fiber elongation [\[13,35,36\].](#page-8-0)

The goal of this study was to identify fiber elongation related genes and pathways affected by the liy mutation. We used comparative RNAseq analysis of developing fibers of liy mutants with its WT line MD15. We found large numbers of differentially regulated genes between two lines at 3, 8 and 16 days post anthesis (DPA) of fiber development. Many metabolic pathways and other processes were substantially disturbed in liy developing fibers. These results will facilitate future research in better understanding of cotton fiber elongation processes.

### 2. Results

### 2.1. Morphology of the liy mutant

We did not observe differences in the shape of liy mutant leaves, stems or flowers compared to WT. ### 2.2. Sources of variability in RNAseq data

Genome-wide gene expression was evaluated by RNAseq in elongating cotton fiber cells at 3, 8 and 16 DPA in the liy and MD15. Time point 3 DPA represents early elongation, while 8 DPA represents peak of elongation and 16 DPA - transition to SCW biosynthesis. Approximately 2.38 billion 100 bp reads from 18 libraries, including 3 libraries from each developmental time point of WT and liy, were trimmed with Sickle [\[37\]](#page-8-0) and mapped to the G. hirsutum TM-1 genome transcript sequences [\[38\].](#page-8-0) Over 70% clean reads were mapped to G. hirsutum TM-1 genome, which can be explained by the incompleteness of the draft genomic reference sequence. The results of mapping reads are provided in Supplemental Table 2.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to explore relationships in gene expression among the samples. According to PCA the samples from 3 biological replicates were clustered tightly, however samples were far separated at different time points and between two lines, with greater distance at 8 and 16 DPA (Supplemental Fig. 1A). To further investigate the proportion of variation in gene expression explained by each factor, a principal variance components analysis (PVCA) was run on the same data set. This approach first reduces data dimensionality with PCA, and then fits a mixed linear model to each principal component with variance components analysis (VCA). The largest source of variability in fiber transcriptome was the "time" variance component T (developmental time; weighted average proportion of 66.7%), whereas the "line" variance component L (WT and mutant lines) explained only 9.6% of the total transcriptional variance and interaction between L x T explained 16.8% of total transcriptional variance (Supplemental Fig. 1B). Thus, developmental time was the major source of variability in data.

### 3. Discussion

In this study we compared transcriptomes of developing fibers of an EMS-induced short fiber mutant liy with its WT. Chemically induced mutants have been proved to be an efficient tool for functional studies

<span id="page-6-0"></span>because each individual line can carry single point missense and nonsense substitutions in hundreds of genes [\[40\].](#page-8-0) Concerns about background mutations have been addressed by Henikoff in his publications [\[40,41\]](#page-8-0) and been considered that the two crosses following mutagenesis unlink target and background mutations, since it becomes highly unlikely for both a target and a background lesion to be homozygous in the same plants. Also previous genetics study that tested about 2500 F2 plants, originated from liy x MD15 crosses, observed Mendelian 3:1 distribution, with one quarter of F2 plants showing liy phenotype [\[19\].](#page-8-0) Therefore, the liy phenotype can be linked to a single locus.

We detected a large number of genes significantly differentially expressed between these lines at 3, 8 and 16 DPA with very little overlap between tested time points [\(Fig. 2](#page-2-0)). These three time points represent early elongation, peak of elongation and transition to SCW biosynthesis where different sets of genes were disturbed in the liy. Our observation is consistent with previous studies revealing separation of gene expression at different cotton fiber developmental stages [\[25,34\]](#page-8-0).

Cotton fiber elongation is a complex development involving many metabolic pathways, signal transduction and other processes. We found a large number of genes perturbed in liy developing fibers. To gain insight into biological processes altered in liy mutation we used MapMan ontology for gene set enrichment analysis. DEGs associated with cell wall functional category were significantly over-represented among down-regulated genes in all tested developing stages in liy mutant fibers ([Figs. 4 and 5C](#page-4-0)). Suppression of transcriptional activity of genes involved in cell wall metabolism was also observed in other short fiber mutants, Li1 and Li2 [\[24,25,42,43\]](#page-8-0). We evaluated transcript profiling for six genes involved in cell wall metabolism during fiber developmental time course (Supplemental Fig. 2). One of them is xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase (XTH), an enzyme that cleaves and reattaches xyloglucan polymers. A previous study in cotton has shown that XTH has a significant effect on fiber elongation. Transgenic over-expression of GhXTH1 in cotton increased fiber length up to 20% [\[44\]](#page-8-0). RT-qPCR analysis revealed that Gh\_XTH1 substantially reduced transcript activity during 5-16 DPA in liy developing fibers. The thinner fiber of the liy mutant ([Table 1](#page-2-0)) could be explained by less efficient SCW biosynthesis. We found in liy developing fibers at 16 DPA significant suppression of transcriptional activity of genes involved in synthesis of precursors (nucleotide sugars), cellulose, hemicellulose, cell wall proteins and cell wall loosening enzymes (Fig. 5C). RT-qPCR analysis confirmed dramatic reduction of transcript level of the SCW-related gene cellulose synthase 2 (GhCesA2) [\[45\]](#page-8-0) during transition to SCW biosynthesis, 16–20 DPA (Supplemental Fig. 2).

The hormone metabolism category was significantly enriched among down-regulated and up-regulated genes in liy ([Figs. 3 and 4](#page-3-0)A). Phytohormones, such as auxin [46–[51\],](#page-8-0) GAs [\[46](#page-8-0)–49], cytokinins [\[47,](#page-8-0) [52\]](#page-8-0) and ethylene [\[20\]](#page-8-0) play important roles in fiber development. Normally phytohormones interact with each other to regulate plant growth and development [\[31,53,54\].](#page-8-0) In present study we found that expression of genes involved in the production of multiple phytohormones were altered in liy. Particularly, DEGs involved in ABA and Ethylene were significantly over-represented among up-regulated genes, while cytokinin and GA among down-regulated genes at 3 DPA.

It is known that GA and ABA play antagonistic roles in the regulation of numerous developmental processes. For example, in Arabidopsis, GA promotes and ABA suppress root growth [\[55\].](#page-8-0) GA and/or auxin (IAA) are essential phytohormones for promoting fiber production from unfertilized ovules in culture, whereas ABA can reduce the ability of ovule to produce fibers in the presence of GAs or IAA [\[47,56](#page-8-0)–59]. We observed in liy developing fibers antagonistic correlation of significantly enriched DEGs involved in ABA and GA biosynthesis during early elongation at 3 DPA (Fig. 5A). Studies of GA signal transduction have led to the identification of positive and negative regulators; among these are the DELLA proteins (GRAS family of TFs) that act as suppressors of GA signaling [\[60\]](#page-9-0). We found that GRAS TFs were significantly enriched among up-regulated genes at 3 DPA that could explain significant suppression of GA metabolism genes in liy (Fig. 5B).

Interactions between GA and ethylene (the stress-related gaseous hormone) are more complex. Developmental and environmental circumstances determine whether interactions between GA and ethylene are positive or negative [\[61\]](#page-9-0). It has been shown in Arabidopsis that at least part of the inhibitory effect of ethylene on growth and its interaction with GA is mediated by DELLA proteins [\[62\].](#page-9-0) In liy we found an antagonistic correlation of DEGs involved in GA and ethylene biosynthesis at 3 DPA; however, at 8 DPA developmental circumstances were different. A clear role for ethylene in promoting fiber elongation has been shown before. Application of ethylene stimulated fiber growth on cultured ovules; on the other hand, the ethylene inhibitor AVG inhibited fiber growth [\[20\].](#page-8-0) Gene expression experiments revealed that transcripts from ACC oxidase (ACO or an ethylene-forming enzyme) were specifically enriched in 10 DPA fiber tissue [\[20\].](#page-8-0) Consistent with Shi and co-authors study [\[20\]](#page-8-0), we found that in a short fiber mutant, at 8 DPA, DGEs involved in ethylene biosynthesis were significantly enriched among down-regulated genes, while at the same time transcripts from 5 ACO genes were significantly down-regulated (Fig. 5A, Supplemental data file 1).

Hormones are involved in modulation of gene expression by controlling either the abundance of transcriptional factors or repressors. The predominant naturally occurring auxin indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) has been involved in every aspect of plant growth and development. Auxin regulates transcription through the action of two large families of transcription factors called the auxin/indole-3-acetic acid (Aux/IAA) proteins and the auxin response factors (ARFs). Following studies demonstrated that the Aux/IAA proteins are unstable transcriptional repressors that can interact with ARFs [63–[66\].](#page-9-0) In addition, auxin directly stimulates expression of the genes encoding the Aux/IAAs, which indicates that auxin response is subject to a negative feedback loop [\[67\].](#page-9-0) In liy developing fibers we found that transcriptionally induced Aux/ IAA regulators were significantly enriched at 3 and 16 DPA (Fig. 5B). It is still unclear what trigger and how these transcriptional alterations lead to shorter fiber in liy. However, these results suggest that transcriptional changes in multiple metabolic pathways are a result of altered regulation of phytohormones signal pathways and transcription factors in liy mutant.

### 4. Material and methods

### 4.1. Plant material

Seeds of a cotton line, MD15 [\[68\]](#page-9-0), were chemically mutagenized with ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) in 2008 in Lubbock, TX to produce M0 [\[69\]](#page-9-0). In 2009 the seeds from M1 population were planted in a field at Stoneville, MS to produce the M2 population. One plant from M2 population that had short fiber phenotype was observed and named as Ligon lintless-y (liy). Genetic studies revealed that the liy phenotype is controlled by a single recessive locus [\[19\].](#page-8-0)

In autumn of 2014 the liy seeds from M2 plant were planted and grown in greenhouse at New Orleans, LA. For fiber quality measurements, greenhouse progenies (M4 plants; about 50 plants each) from four homozygous liy and MD15 plants were grown during the summer of 2015 in a field in New Orleans, LA. For RNAseq and RT-qPCR experiments, a total of 100 homozygous liy (M4) and 100 WT MD15 plants were grown during summer 2016 in a field in New Orleans, LA. ### 4.2. Fiber quality measurements

For mature fiber quality measurements the cotton fibers were ginned using a laboratory saw gin. The fiber testing data were determined by the Cotton Fiber Testing Lab, USDA-ARS-SRRC, New Orleans, LA. Micronaire (MIC) values were measured by the Fibronaire instrument (Motion Control Inc., Dallas, TX). A higher MIC value indicates a more mature, coarser fiber. Fiber mean length, short fiber content (SFC), fineness, and maturity ratio were measured by the Advanced Fiber Information System (AFIS) (USTER Technologies Inc.). Fiber mean length is the average length of a fixed weight (w) or number (n) of fibers expressed in mm. SFC is the percent of fibers b12.7 mm in a fixed weight (w) or number (n) of fibers. Fiber fineness is given as millitex, which is a measure of linear density derived by the weight of fibers in micrograms per length of fibers in meters. Maturity ratio indicates the fiber maturity in terms of the degree of thickening of the SCW relative to the diameter or fineness of the fiber. A higher maturity ratio indicates a more mature fiber.

### 4.3. RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Cotton fibers were isolated from developing ovules using a glass bead shearing technique to separate fibers from the ovules [\[70\].](#page-9-0) Total RNA was isolated from detached fibers using the Sigma Spectrum Plant Total RNA Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) with the optional on column DNase1 digestion according to the manufacturer's protocol. The concentration of each RNA sample was determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies Inc., Wilmington, DE). The RNA quality for each sample was determined by RNA integrity number (RIN) using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 and the RNA 6000 Nano Kit Chip (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA) with 250 ng of total RNA per sample. A detailed description of reverse transcription and qPCR for quantification of mRNA transcripts was previously reported [\[25,71\].](#page-8-0) 18S rRNA was used as the endogenous reference gene for relative quantitation of the gene expression data. The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [\[72\].](#page-9-0) Sequences of primers are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

### 4.4. RNAseq and data analyses

RNA samples from liy and MD15 developing cotton fibers at 3, 8 and 16 DPA (in tree biological replicates) were subjected to paired-end Illumina mRNA sequencing (RNAseq). These time points were selected because they represent early elongation, peak of elongation and transition to SCW biosynthesis. Library preparation and sequencing was performed by Novogene Corporation (Chula Vista, CA, USA) as previously reported [\[73,74\]](#page-9-0). Particularly, libraries were prepared using a NEB Next® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit (New England BioLabs Inc., Ipswich, MA, USA). mRNA samples were enriched using oligo(dT) beads. First, the mRNA was fragmented randomly by adding fragmentation buffer, then the cDNA was synthesized by using mRNA template and random hexamers primer, after which a custom second-strand synthesis buffer (Illumina), dNTPs, RNase H and DNA polymerase I were added to initiate the second-strand synthesis. Second, after a series of terminal repair, A ligation and sequencing adaptor ligation, the double-stranded cDNA library was completed through size selection and PCR enrichment. After quality control test the qualified libraries were fed into HiSeq/ MiSeq sequencers. The raw reads data are available at the Sequence Read Archive (accession number PRJNA382717). The raw reads were trimmed with SICKLE ([https://github.com/najoshi/sickle\)](https://github.com/najoshi/sickle) using a quality score cutoff of 20. Mapping the reads to the G. hirsutum TM-1 genome sequence [\[38\]](#page-8-0) was performed using GSNAP software [\[75\]](#page-9-0). Default parameters were used, but with the flags "-n 1 -Q" which means that only a single mapping was reported for each read, and reads with multiple equally good hits were discarded rather than randomly mapped. The JMP/Genomics 7.1 software (SAS, Cary, NC, USA) was used for data normalization and statistical analysis. The data was normalized using TMM (Trimmed Mean of M component) method [\[76\].](#page-9-0) An ANOVA process was conducted as previously described [\[71\].](#page-9-0) Particularly, the ANOVA process was fit to the normalized data, with the data following a Poisson distribution. This was accomplished with a generalized linear mixed model for each gene: Yij = Ti + Lj + TLij + Eijk, where T is the time effect for the ith developmental time point (3, 8 and 16 DPA), L is the genotype effect for the jth line (liy or MD15), their interaction (TL), and the error term (E). The liner model was used to test the null hypothesis that expression of a given gene was not different. Specifically, multiple comparisons were made between liy and MD15 at 3, 8 and 16 DPA of fiber development. We identified genes for which the difference in expression levels within these a priori questions were significantly different (false discovery rate ≤ 0.05) [\[77\]](#page-9-0) at least 2 fold.

Transcript data for each sample were used as continuous variables for Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Principal variance component analysis (PVCA) reduces the dimensionality of the data set with PCA, and then fits a mixed linear model to each principal component to partition variability with variance components analysis (VCA). A summary of variance components across all principal components is constructed as a weighted average of the individual estimates, using eigenvalues as weights [\[78\].](#page-9-0)
