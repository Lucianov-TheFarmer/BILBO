## WRKY70 prevents axenic activation of plant immunity by direct repression of SARD1

### Man Zhou, You Lu, Gerit Bethke, Brian T. Harrison, Noriyuki Hatsugai, Fumiaki Katagiri and Jane Glazebrook

Department of Plant and Microbial Biology, Microbial and Plant Genomics Institute, University of Minnesota, 1500 Gortner Ave., Saint Paul, MN 55108, USA

#### Summary

Received: 17 April 2017 Accepted: 4 September 2017

New Phytologist (2017) doi: 10.1111/nph.14846

Key words: activator, Arabidopsis, calmodulin-binding protein, Pseudomonas syringae, repressor, salicylic acid (SA), SARD1, transcription factors, WRKY70.

 SARD1 is an activator of plant immunity that promotes production of the hormone salicylic acid (SA) and activation of defense gene expression. SARD1 itself is strongly inducible by infection. Here, we investigated the transcriptional control of SARD1.

 We used yeast one-hybrid assays to identify WRKY70. The WRKY70 binding site was defined using electrophoretic mobility shift assays, and its importance was investigated using an Arabidopsis thaliana protoplast system. The effect of wrky70 mutations was studied by measurements of pathogen growth, SA concentrations, and gene expression by RNA-seq.

 WRKY70 binds to a GACTTTT motif in the SARD1 promoter in yeast and Arabidopsis protoplasts. Plants with wrky70 mutations have elevated expression of SARD1 in the absence of pathogens, but not when infected. Expression profiling revealed that WRKY70 represses many pathogen-inducible genes in the absence of pathogens, yet is required for activation of many other pathogen-inducible genes in infected plants. The GACTTTT motif is enriched in the promoters of both these gene sets, and conserved in SARD1 orthologs within the Brassicaceae.

 WRKY70 represses SARD1 by binding the motif GACTTTT in the absence of pathogens. Conservation of the WRKY70 binding among the Brassicaceae suggests that WRKY70 repression of SARD1 is important for fitness.

#### Introduction

Salicylic acid (SA) is a phytohormone that is critical for signaling in both pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI) (Pieterse et al., 2012; Tsuda et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2014; Seyfferth & Tsuda, 2014; Bigeard et al., 2015). SA signaling restricts the growth of virulent pathogens, including Pseudomonas syringae pv. maculicola (Pma) ES4326, in Arabidopsis thaliana. In Arabidopsis, SA production and signaling in response to pathogen attack depends on the genes SA INDUCTION-DEFICIENT2 (SID2), ENHANCED DISEASE SUSCEPTIBILITY5 (EDS5), and NONEXPRESSOR OF PR GENES1 (NPR1) (Vlot et al., 2009). SID2 encodes an isochorismate synthase, an enzyme that converts chorismate to isochorismate, (Wildermuth et al., 2001). Induced expression of SID2 and SA concentrations are affected by three members of the CALMODULIN-BINDING PROTEIN60 (CBP60) gene family, CBP60g, CBP60a and SYSTEMIC ACQUIRED RESISTANCE DEFICIENT 1 (SARD1) (Wang et al., 2009, 2011; Zhang et al., 2010; Truman et al., 2013). CBP60g and SARD1 encode positive regulators of plant immunity that promote the production of SA and affect the expression of SA-dependent as well as SAindependent defense genes (Wang et al., 2009, 2011; Zhang et al., 2010). Mutations in either gene lead to increased Pma ES4326 growth and reduced concentrations of SA (Wang et al., 2011). By contrast, CBP60a is a negative regulator of plant immunity and SA production (Truman et al., 2013). CBP60g and CBP60a require calmodulin (CaM) binding to be active, while SARD1 does not bind CaM (Wang et al., 2009, 2011). Both CBP60g and SARD1 have been reported to bind the SID2 promoter in chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) (Zhang et al., 2010; Sun et al., 2015). Expression of CBP60g and SARD1 is strongly inducible by infection with Pma ES4326 or treatment with a microbe-associated molecular pattern (MAMP) derived from bacterial flagellin, the peptide flg22 (Wang et al., 2009, 2011). Here, we searched for transcription factors responsible for control of SARD1 expression, and identified WRKY70.

The WRKY transcription factor (TF) superfamily has important roles in plant immunity. There are 74 family members in Arabidopsis, each having at least one conserved DNA-binding domain, termed the WRKY domain, consisting of a highly conserved peptide sequence (WRKYGQK) and a zinc finger motif (CX4-7CX22-23HXH/C) (Hu et al., 2013). Based on the number of WRKY domains and the structure of the zinc finger motif, the WRKY superfamily has been subdivided into three groups (Rushton et al., 2010). Members of different subgroups of the WRKY superfamily within a single plant species may differ in their DNA-binding requirements (Ciolkowski et al., 2008). Many plant WRKY TFs bind to a classic W-box motif

(TTGACY) (Ülker & Somssich, 2004; Journot-Catalino et al., 2006; Ciolkowski et al., 2008; Choura, 2015). However, sequences flanking the W-box motif may impart a certain level of DNA-binding selectivity and specificity to WRKY TFs (Ciolkowski et al., 2008). In addition to the stringently defined W-box, the W-box-like hexamer (TTGACA) and the core (TGAC) have also been found in the promoters of many WRKYregulated genes (Maleck et al., 2000). The TGA-bZIP transcription factors, which are also important in immune responses, recognize a motif (TGACG) which is quite similar to the W-box consensus (Maleck et al., 2000). Some WRKYs have been shown to bind to noncanonical motifs, including the sugar-responsive (SURE) cis elements bound by the WRKY SUSIBA2 in barley (Sun et al., 2003), the WK box (TTTTCCAC) bound by WRKY12 in tobacco (van Verk et al., 2008), and the WT box (YGACTTTT) bound by WRKY70 in Arabidopsis (Machens et al., 2014).

Expression of Arabidopsis WRKY70 is pathogen-inducible and controlled by important regulators of immunity. WRKY70, WRKY54, and WRKY53 were found among eight WRKY genes that are direct targets of NPR1 (Wang et al., 2006). The WRKY70 promoter was bound by CBP60g and SARD1 in ChIP-seq experiments, and the overexpression of WRKY70 in the snc2-1D background was reduced by the cbp60g or sard1 mutations (Sun et al., 2015). A number of studies using Arabidopsis wrky70 mutants have demonstrated roles of WRKY70 in the control of immune responses. For example, WRKY70 is a negative regulator of SID2 expression and SA concentrations (Jiang et al., 2016), while wrky70 mutants showed reduced resistance to the oomycete Hyaloperonospora parasitica, indicating that WRKY70 is a positive regulator in this system (Knoth et al., 2007). The mechanistic basis underlying the apparent positive effects of WRKY70 on immunity in some systems, and negative effects in others, is not known.

Here, we demonstrated that WRKY70 is a repressor of *SARD1* through direct binding. We identified the binding site of WRKY70 in the *SARD1* promoter and demonstrated its function in Arabidopsis protoplasts and yeast. We found that this binding site is evolutionarily conserved in the *Brassicaceae* family, suggesting that regulation of *SARD1* expression by WRKY70 contributes to fitness. RNA-seq analysis of *wrky70-1* and wild-type (WT) Columbia (Col-0) plants in uninfected and *Pma* ES4326-infected plants revealed that WRKY70 acts mainly as a repressor of immunity in uninfected plants but as an activator of immunity in infected plants. These dual roles may explain why previous studies of WRKY70 detected positive or negative effects on immune responses in different systems.

#### **Materials and Methods**

#### Plant genotypes, growth conditions, and pathogen cultures

Plants were WT Col-0, *sid2-2* (Wildermuth *et al.*, 2001), *cbp60g-1* (SALK\_023199) (Wang *et al.*, 2009; Zhang *et al.*, 2010), *sard1-2* (SALK\_052422) (Zhang *et al.*, 2010; Wang *et al.*, 2011), *wrky70-1*, and *wrky70-3* (Knoth *et al.*, 2007). Arabidopsis

plants were grown on autoclaved BM2 germinating mix (Berger Inc., Saint-Modeste, QC, Canada) in a growth chamber with a 12:12 h photoperiod under  $100 \,\mu M \,m^{-2} \,s^{-1}$  fluorescent illumination at 22°C and 75% relative humidity. Plants were 4–5 wk old at the time experiments were conducted.

*Pma* ES4326 was cultured at room temperature (RT). The inoculum was prepared as a bacterial suspension in 5 mM  $MgSO_4$  ( $OD_{600} = 0.0002$ ) which was used to infiltrate mature leaves using a needle-less syringe. Pathogen growth was determined as previously described (Tsuda *et al.*, 2008).

# Yeast one-hybrid screening and $\beta\mbox{-}galactosidase$ activity assays

Fragments of the SARD1 and CBP60g promoters were cloned into plasmid pMW3, creating fusions to  $\beta$ -galactosidase ('baits') and integrated into yeast strain YM4271 as described (Deplancke et al., 2006). The primers used for cloning are provided in Supporting Information Table S1. A set of TFs was chosen from the Arabidopsis TF ORFeome collection comprising 1956 Arabidopsis transcription factor clones in vector pDEST22 (Pruneda-Paz et al., 2014) obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (stock no. CD4-89). Yeast strains carrying the promoter baits were transformed with selected TFs. The transformed colonies were grown in selective liquid medium for 2-3 d and then used to perform 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl β-D-galactopyranoside (X-Gal; Gold Biotechnology, St Louis, MO, USA) (Deplancke et al., 2006) or ortho-nitrophenyl-β-Dgalactopyranoside (ONPG; Sigma) assays (Pruneda-Paz et al., 2009).

# Expression and purification of recombinant WRKY70 and WRKY54

Using a protocol modified from Machens *et al.* (2014) and Matallana-Ramirez *et al.* (2013), *Escherichia coli* Rosetta strains harboring the plasmids pDEST15-WRKY70 or pDEST15-WRKY54 were grown to an OD<sub>600</sub> value of 0.6. Expression of WRKY70 (AT3G56400) or WRKY54 (AT2G40750) was induced by adding isopropyl  $\beta$ -D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to a final concentration of 1 mM. After further incubation for 4 h, the cells were harvested, and the total soluble protein was extracted. Then, the recombinant WRKY70 or WRKY54 was partially purified from the supernatant using the Glutathione Sepharose 4 Fast Flow kit (Amersham Biosciences 17-5132-01).

### EMSA assays

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays were performed as previously described (Machens *et al.*, 2014), except that biotin-labeled probes were used with the LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A standard binding reaction was performed for 25 min in 20  $\mu$ l 1× gel shift buffer containing 1  $\mu$ l poly(dI-dC) (1  $\mu$ g  $\mu$ l<sup>-1</sup>), competitor DNA at 1000, 100, 10 or 0 nM, 0.1–0.5  $\mu$ g recombinant protein, and 1 nM biotin-labeled

double-stranded DNA. Biotin-labeled probes were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA).

# Protoplast transformation and dual-luciferase reporter assay

Candidate TF coding sequences were transferred from pENTR/D to the TF-overexpression vector pUGW2 (Nakagawa et al., 2007a,b) via Gateway<sup>®</sup> cloning (Life Technologies, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The internal control pUGW2-Renilla-Venus was constructed by transferring the Renilla gene sequence (Loening et al., 2006) from pENTR/D-TOPO to pUGW2-GW-Venus (Nagai et al., 2002) via Gateway® cloning. Arabidopsis protoplasts were isolated from 4- to 5-wk-old seedlings and transformed according to Yoo et al. (2007). Transformations included three plasmids: (1) a pUGW2 TF-overexpression vector containing the CaMV 35S promoter driving expression of WRKY70, WRKY45 (AT3G01970) or JAZ1 (AT1G19180); (2) pUGW35 (Nakagawa et al., 2007a,b) containing the indicated SARD1 promoter driving expression of the firefly luciferase reporter; and (3) pUGW2-Renilla-Venus to control for transformation efficiency. The experiment shown in Fig. 4(c) (see later) included only plasmids of types (2) and (3). Normalization of protein concentration was performed using Protein Assay Dye Reagent Concentrate (Bio-Rad no. 5000006). Luciferase activity was measured using a dual luciferase reporter kit (Promega) and a luminometer (Centro LB 960, Berthold Technologies, Bad Wildbad, Germany).

# Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from leaves of Arabidopsis plants at 24 h after infiltration with 5 mM MgSO<sub>4</sub> (mock) or *Pma* ES4326 ( $OD_{600} = 0.002$  in 5 mM MgSO<sub>4</sub>) using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies). Transcript abundance was determined by qRT-PCR as described previously (Truman & Glazebrook, 2012), using the SuperScript III Platinum SYBR Green One-Step qRT-PCR kit (Invitrogen) in a LightCycler 480 (Roche). The primers used for *ACTIN2*, *CBP60g*, *SARD1*, *PR1*, and *SID2* were as described (Truman *et al.*, 2013).

#### RNA-seq data analysis

Three independent experiments were conducted. Preparation of RNA-seq libraries from total RNA was performed using the Smart-3SEQ method (J. W. Foley, unpublished), which was adapted from the 3SEQ method (Beck *et al.*, 2010). Molecular identifiers for each library were incorporated during sample preparation (Kivioja *et al.*, 2012). The libraries were multiplexed as pools of 24 and sequenced as 50 bp single reads on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 instrument. For each sequenced library, reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis genome (TAIR10) based on gene models from 'Araport11\_20151202' (Cheng *et al.*, 2017) using STAR aligner v.2.4.2a (Dobin *et al.*, 2013).

Gene expression data were analyzed according to Hillmer *et al.* (2017). Genes were not included in the downstream analysis if no

transcript was detected in >60% of the libraries and the fifth largest transcript count value was < 20. For the remaining 18 535 genes, if transcript counts from all three replicates were 0 for one genotype-treatment combination, then the value from the library with the largest 90<sup>th</sup> percentile value was adjusted to 1. This procedure avoided overestimation of fold-change values for genes with undetectable expression in some genotype-treatment combinations. For each gene, a negative binomial generalized linear model (NBGLM) with the log-link function was applied to estimate mean expression values and standard errors in the R environment (v.3.3.1) using the GLM.NB function in package MASS v.7.3-45 (Venables & Ripley, 2002). Within this model, for each library, the log ratio of the expression value at the 90<sup>th</sup> percentile relative to the maximum 90<sup>th</sup> percentile value among all the libraries was used as an offset to normalize the read counts across the libraries. Comparisons of gene expression between genotypes or between treatments were conducted as Z-tests with mean values and standard errors estimated from the NBGLM. The two-tailed P-values derived from all the comparisons were adjusted to q-values through a false discovery rate-based multiple testing correction procedure (Storey & Tibshirani, 2003).

### Conservation analysis

Brassicaceae genome sequences were obtained from databases TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org), Phytozome (https://phyto zome.jgi.doe.gov), BRAD (https://brassicadb.org/), and Pennycress Genome Project (Dorn et al., 2015). To define the translational start sites for SARD1, the full-length coding sequence of Arabidopsis thaliana SARD1 (AtSARD1) was used as a query to BLAST against all the other genomes. The genome regions that match the full length of the AtSARD1 coding sequence, including the start codon, were considered as SARD1 homologs. This approach identified three SARD1 homologs in Camelina sativa, two in Brassica rapa and Brassica oleracea, and one each in all the other genomes. The 500 bp sequence upstream of the start codon for each SARD1 homolog was used for multiple sequence alignment using the CLC Main WORKBENCH software v.7.6.4 with parameters Gap Open Cost set to 10 and Gap Extension Cost set to 1. The criteria used for defining conserved regions were length > 8 bp (the length of the WRKY70 binding motif) and a minimum conservation percentage of 85% at each base position.

### Accession number

The RNA-seq data described herein can be obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) with accession no. GSE100187.

## Results

WRKY70 interacts with the SARD1 promoter in yeast onehybrid (Y1H) assays

constructed a series of Y1H reporter constructs consisting of various fragments of the *SARD1* promoter fused to *LacZ*, which encodes a  $\beta$ -galactosidase ( $\beta$ -gal, Fig. 1a). Activation of the reporters was detected using X-gal assays for  $\beta$ -galactosidase activity. This screen indicated that WRKY70 can bind to the *SARD1* promoter in yeast. As

# WRKY70 binds to a sequence in the SARD1 promoter in $\ensuremath{\mathsf{EMSAs}}$

A sequence that is bound by WRKY70 in EMSAs - 'GACTTTT' has been described (Machens et al., 2014). The -451 to +1 fragment of the SARD1 promoter contains this sequence (-275 to)-269). A 40 bp biotinylated oligonucleotide containing this sequence was obtained (Fig. 2a). N-terminal glutathione-Stransferase (GST)-tagged WRKY70 was expressed in E. coli and partially purified (Fig. S1). Incubation of this protein with the biotinylated oligonucleotide before electrophoresis greatly inhibited the electrophoretic mobility of the oligonucleotide (Fig. 2b). This mobility shift was inhibited by addition of unlabeled 40 bp or 25 bp competitor oligonucleotides containing the putative WRKY70 binding site. However, a 25 bp competitor containing two nucleotide substitutions in the putative WRKY70 binding site did not inhibit the mobility shift. These results indicated that WRKY70 binds a fragment of the SARD1 promoter in vitro, that binding is specific, and that the sequence of a known WRKY70 binding site is required.

Three other sequences similar to the canonical WRKY box binding site of WRKY TFs were detected in the *SARD1* promoter. We named them 'wlike-1', 'wlike-2' and 'TGAC core'. Three 25 bp oligonucleotides containing these sequences were synthesized (Fig. 2a). None of these oligonucleotides competed for WRKY70 binding in the EMSA assay, indicating that WRKY70 binds to these sequences at much lower affinity or not at all. Similarly, a potential WRKY70 binding site was identified in the *CBP60g* promoter, but an oligonucleotide containing this sequence also failed to compete for WRKY70 binding (Fig. 2b). As WRKY70 also did not activate the *CBP60g* promoter in a Y1H assay, WRKY70 probably does not bind the *CBP60g* promoter.

# The WRKY70 binding site is required for WRKY70 activity *in vivo*

Having found a DNA motif bound by WRKY70 in EMSA assays, we investigated the role of this motif in WRKY70 binding in yeast and plant cells. Using the Y1H system, we created two mutant versions of the -451 to +1 fragment, SARD1p-De, in which the 'TAAATAGGGAGACTTTTGACGTCAA', sequence corresponding to the 25 bp WT oligonucleotide used in EMSA, was deleted, and SARD1p-m, with two nucleotide substitutions in the WRKY70 binding site 'GgtTTTT'. The ability of WRKY70-AD to activate these and the WT -451 to +1 promoter was tested using the ONPG assay. As shown in Fig. 3(a), only the SARD1p WT promoter was activated by WRKY70-AD. The activity of the mutant promoters was no different in the presence of WRKY70-AD than in its absence. This result demonstrates that the WRKY70 binding site, including the 'AC' bases, is required for WRKY70 binding to the -451 to +1 promoter in yeast.

We tested the ability of WRKY70 to regulate *SARD1* transcription in plants using a transient expression system in Arabidopsis protoplasts. Two versions of the -451 to +1 *SARD1* promoter fragment were constructed and used to drive expression of firefly luciferase; one was the WT promoter, *SARD1*p, and the other was *SARD1*p-m, having the mutation in the WRKY70 binding site (AGgtTTTT) used in the EMSA and Y1H assays. #### WRKY70 is a negative regulator of immune responses

We tested wrky70 mutant plants for altered expression of SARD1 and related genes. Mutants wrky70-1 and wrky70-3 carry null mutations which have been described previously (Knoth et al., 2007). WT Col-0, wrky70-1 and wrky70-3 plants were inoculated with the bacterial pathogen Pma ES4326 or mock-inoculated, and gene expression was measured by qRT-PCR 24 h later. The level of SARD1 mRNA was much higher in mock-inoculated wrky70 plants than in mock-inoculated WT Col-0 plants, indicating that WRKY70 acts as a repressor of SARD1 expression under this condition (Fig. 4a). By contrast, there was no difference in SARD1 expression between wrky70 and WT Col-0 plants following inoculation with Pma ES4326. We also measured expression of CBP60g, SID2, and PR-1, a gene that is strongly induced by SA. All of these genes showed similar expression patterns, with elevated expression in *wrky70* plants following mock inoculation, but not following Pma ES4326 inoculation (Fig. 4a). The effect of wrky70 mutations on SARD1 expression could be sufficient to explain the elevated expression of the other three genes, as SA activates expression of *PAD4*, which is required for pathogen-induced expression of *SARD1* and *CBP60g*, which activate *SID2*, which produces SA (Wang *et al.*, 2011).

We hypothesized that *wrky70* plants might be more resistant to *Pma* ES4326 owing to elevated immune responses in the absence of pathogen attack. To test this idea, we inoculated WT Col-0 and *wrky70* plants with *Pma* ES4326 and measured bacterial titers after 1 or 3 d. Both *wrky70* mutants support less growth of *Pma* (Figs 4b, S2). Evidently, the elevated immune responses in *wrky70* plants result in slightly enhanced resistance to this pathogen.

It has been reported that *wrky70-1* plants have elevated free SA concentrations in the absence of infection (Wang *et al.*, 2006). We assayed free and total SA concentrations in *wrky70-3* mutants (Methods S1), and confirmed that these are both elevated in the absence of infection, but in infected plants free SA concentrations are not elevated, and total SA concentrations are only modestly elevated (Fig. S3). ## Research 7

WRKY70 acts as a negative regulator of immune responses in the absence of a pathogen, and as a positive regulator in the presence of a pathogen

The results we described earlier demonstrated that WRKY70 acts as a repressor of immune responses in the absence of pathogen attack, acting at least partly through repression of *SARD1* by direct binding to its promoter. To explore the effect of WRKY70 on immunity more broadly, we conducted an expression profiling experiment using RNA-seq. Plants of genotypes WT Col-0, *wrky70-1*, *cbp60g sard1* and *sid2* were mock-inoculated or inoculated with *Pma* ES4326, and inoculated leaves were collected after 24 h. Details of sample preparation and data analysis are provided in Materials and Methods. The complete set of gene identifiers, annotation, expression levels, ratios, and corresponding *q*-values is provided in Notes S1 and S2.

Many genes displayed altered expression levels in this experiment. Throughout this analysis, the criteria for considering a gene as differentially expressed were a fold-change of 2 or more ( $|\log_2$ fold-change| > 1) and q < 0.05. In WT Col-0 plants, expression of 10 273 genes was altered by *Pma* ES4326 infection compared with mock-treated plants, with 3690 genes up-regulated (termed *Pma*-inducible genes) and 6583 genes down-regulated (termed *Pma*-repressible genes). In *wrky70-1* plants, upon infection, the expression of 9350 genes was altered, 3069 of which were up-regulated and 6281 of which were down-regulated. Sets of 2636 *Pma*-inducible and 5384 *Pma*-repressible genes were common to both genotypes. The expression patterns of *SARD1*, *CBP60g, PR-1*, and *SID2* in Col-0 and *wrky70-1* plants were consistent with the qRT-PCR measurements shown in Fig. 4(a) (Fig. S4).

Comparing the expression profiles of mock-treated *wrky70-1* and WT plants (w70 mock vs WT mock), we identified 275 differentially expressed genes, of which 188 were up-regulated in the mutant (termed WRKY70-repressed genes in mock, and abbreviated as 'W70-R mock') and 87 were down-regulated in the mutant (termed WRKY70-activated genes in mock, and abbreviated as 'W70-A mock') (Fig. 5a). Among these 188 W70-R mock genes, 163 were *Pma*-inducible in WT Col-0 (blue dots in Fig. 5a). Among the 87 W70-A mock genes, 75 were *Pma*-repressible in WT Col-0 (red dots in Fig. 5a). As 88.1% of WRKY70-repressed genes and 86.2% of WRKY70-activated genes in mock were *Pma*-inducible or *Pma*-repressible in WT Col-0, respectively, we concluded that WRKY70 is acting mainly as a negative regulator of immune responses in uninfected plants.

Curiously, the effect of *wrky70* was very different in plants infected with *Pma* ES4326. Comparing the expression profiles of *Pma*-infected *wrky70-1* and WT Col-0 plants (w70 *Pma* vs WT *Pma*), we identified 839 differentially expressed genes, of which 486 were up-regulated in the mutant (termed WRKY70repressed genes upon *Pma* infection, and abbreviated as 'W70-R *Pma'*) and 353 were down-regulated in the mutant (termed WRKY70-activated genes upon *Pma* infection, and abbreviated as 'W70-A *Pma'*) (Fig. 5b). Among the 486 W70-R *Pma* genes, 319 (66%) were *Pma*-repressible (green dots in Fig. 5b), while only 94 (19%) were *Pma*-inducible (blue dots in Fig. 5b). Of the

353 W70-A *Pma* genes, 229 (65%) (purple dots in Fig. 5b) were *Pma*-inducible in WT Col-0, while only 30 (8.5%) (red dots in Fig. 5b) were *Pma*-repressible. These data indicate that WRKY70 acts mainly as a positive regulator of plant immunity during *Pma* infection.

# WRKY70 activated or repressed different sets of genes in the absence of pathogen than during *Pma* infection

Interestingly, the W70-R mock and W70-A mock genes are almost entirely distinct from the W70-R *Pma* and W70-A *Pma* genes (Fig. 5c). Among the W70-R mock genes (yellow in Fig. 5c), only six are in common with W70-R *Pma* (red), and another six are in common with W70-A *Pma* (blue). Among the W70-A mock genes (brown), only three are common with W70-A *Pma*, and none are in common with W70-R *Pma*. This pattern extends our observations using qRT-PCR, in which we found that *wrky70* mutants have elevated expression of *SARD1*, *CBP60g*, *SID2*, and *PR-1* in mock-infected plants, but expression levels of these genes do not differ from WT Col-0 in *Pma* ES4326-infected plants (Figs 4a, S4). As there are many other pathogen-responsive genes that show reduced induction or repression in *wrky70* mutants, WRKY70 must affect different genes in the uninfected state than in the infected state. # WRKY70 could affect gene expression indirectly by affecting expression of *SARD1* and/or *SID2*

As WRKY70 has a profound effect on expression of *SARD1* in the absence of pathogen attack, some genes affected by *wrky70* might be direct targets of SARD1. We tested this idea by comparing the *wrky70* differentially expressed gene set obtained by RNA-seq with the SARD1 target gene set defined by ChIP-Seq (Sun *et al.*, 2015). In the *Pma* infection dataset, this comparison identified 45 WRKY70-activated genes (13%) and 29 WRKY70repressed genes (6%) which were also SARD1 targets, suggesting that during *Pma* infection, most of the effect of WRKY70 is independent of SARD1 (Fig. 5c). In the mock treatment dataset, 59 WRKY70-repressed genes but only four WRKY70-activated genes were direct SARD1 targets. Therefore, among the 188 WRKY70-repressed genes in the mock-infection condition, 31% may be repressed by WRKY70 indirectly through its repression of *SARD1*.

We also explored *wrky70* effects possibly mediated by SARD1 using RNA-seq data from *cbp60g sard1* plants. We postulated that genes expressed at high levels in *wrky70* mock-infected plants as a result of elevated *SARD1* expression might show reduced expression in *cbp60g sard1* plants following *Pma* infection. Among the 163 genes that were repressed by WRKY70 in

mock and *Pma*-inducible in WT Col-0, 38 had reduced expression in *cbp60g sard1* plants upon *Pma* infection (Fig. S5a), consistent with the idea that *wrky70* affects their expression through SARD1. Of these 38 genes, 19 were direct targets of SARD1 defined by SARD1 ChIP (Fig. S5b) (Sun *et al.*, 2015), further supporting the idea that these genes were affected by elevated *SARD1* expression in *wrky70*. The 125 other genes highly expressed in *wrky70* mock that do not require CBP60g or SARD1 for induced expression may be direct targets of WRKY70, or they may be repressed by WRKY70 through effects on other transcription factors.

Some of the genes with high expression in wrky70 mockinoculated plants may have increased expression as a result of the elevated level of SID2 in these plants. Such genes might show reduced expression in sid2 plants upon Pma ES4326 infection. Among the 163 genes that were repressed by WRKY70 in mock and *Pma*-inducible in WT Col-0, 34 genes showed reduced expression in sid2 plants (Fig. S5c). These genes may be high in wrky70 mock because of the increased expression of SID2 (Fig. 4a). This set includes 12 genes overlapping with the set of 38 genes that require CBP60g or SARD1 for Pma-induced expression (Fig. S5d), indicating that these genes may be high in *wrky70* mock because of the increased expression of SID2 resulting from the increased expression of SARD1. It is not surprising that genes affected by *cbp60g sard1* and *sid2* are only partially overlapping, as cbp60g sard1 is known to affect many more Pma-inducible genes than sid2, and sid2 plants have much lower concentrations of SA than do cbp60g sard1 plants (Wang et al., 2011).

## The promoters of W70-R mock and W70-A *Pma* genes are enriched for the WRKY70 binding motif

As WRKY70 binds DNA fragments containing 'GACTTTT', we performed Promoter bootstrapping (POBO) analysis (Kankainen & Holm, 2004) and found that this motif was enriched in the promoters (1.0 kb upstream of the transcriptional start site) of the 188 W70-R mock genes (Fig. 6a), suggesting that without pathogen challenge, WRKY70 may repress a set of genes through binding of this motif. Meanwhile, in the presence of pathogen, this motif is significantly enriched in 353 W70-A *Pma* genes but not in 486 W70-R *Pma* genes, indicating that WRKY70 might

activate some defense-related genes through the 'GACTTTT' motif (Fig. 6b). This result is consistent with a dual action of WRKY70, which appears to serve as a negative regulator of immunity in the absence of a pathogen and a positive regulator in the presence of a pathogen. It also suggests that many of the genes repressed by WRKY70 in the absence of pathogen are directly repressed, while genes activated by WRKY70 in the absence of *Pma* ES4326, the reverse is true – many of the genes activated by WRKY70 are probably directly activated, while the repressed genes are probably indirectly repressed.

# The WRKY70 binding site in the *SARD1* promoter is highly conserved within the *Brassicaceae*

If WRKY70 control of SARD1 expression is an important component of immunity, loss of such control would be expected to carry fitness costs, and therefore the WRKY70 binding site in the SARD1 promoter should be conserved. We tested this idea by aligning 17 SARD1 promoter sequences from 12 Brassicaceae species. While most of the promoter alignment displays various degrees of polymorphisms or gaps, there are three regions that are longer than 8 bp and have greater than 85% conservation at each base. The first conserved region, located from -286 to -266 bp upstream of the SARD1 translation start site in A. thaliana, contains the WRKY70 binding site, which is indeed highly conserved within the Brassicaceae (Fig. 7a). This conserved region includes the WRKY70 binding motif, including the two bases that we showed were essential, and it also includes the two adjacent TGA-bZIP motifs. Conservation of these motifs suggests either that another important transcription factor binds to them or that they are important for WRKY70 binding. The second conserved region is located from -260 to -247 bp, and contains one TGAC core motif. This sequence was included in the TGAC core oligonucleotide used in EMSA, which failed to compete for WRKY70 binding (Fig. 2). The third conserved region is located from -203 to -179 bp, but does not contain any recognizable motifs.

#### Discussion

A model of the role of WRKY70 in control of SARD1 expression is illustrated in Fig. 8. In uninfected plants, WRKY70 directly represses expression of SARD1 and a set of other immuneresponse genes, and indirectly represses expression of CBP60g and a different set of other immune-response genes. The reduced expression of CBP60g and SARD1 reduces expression of SID2. In infected plants, WRKY70 does not affect expression of CBP60g or SARD1, but it does affect expression of other immuneresponse genes, some directly and some indirectly.

#### Immunity-induced expression of WRKY70 is reduced in mutants defective in SA signaling

Here, we found that WRKY70 represses SA signaling via repression of SARD1 expression in the absence of infection. Data from our previous expression profiling experiments showed that expression of WRKY70 was induced following challenge with Pma ES4326 (Fig. S6a). In the RNA-seq data reported here, we found elevated WRKY70 expression in mock-treated cbp60g sard1 plants (Fig. S6b). Compared with Pma ES4326-infected WT Col-0 plants, expression of WRKY70 was reduced in Pma ES4326-infected pad4, sid2, npr1, eds5, pbs3, and cbp60g sard1 plants (Fig. S6c). Consistently, Sun et al. (2015) found that both SARD1 and CBP60g bind the WRKY70 promoter by ChIP, and that that the elevated WRKY70 levels in snc2-1D plants were reduced by cbp60g and sard1 mutations. The relationships between WRKY70 and SARD1 thus appear quite complex, as they each bind the promoter of the other, and they both affect and are affected by SA concentrations.

#### WRKY70 can act as a repressor or activator of SARD1 transcription

We found that WRKY70 binds to a GACTTTT motif in the SARD1 promoter and acts a repressor of SARD1 expression, as wrky70 plants have elevated levels of SARD1 transcripts in the absence of infection. There are previous reports that WRKY70 can be a negative regulator. WRKY70 and WRKY54 are negative regulators of SID2 expression and SA biosynthesis (Wang et al., 2006). They also cooperate as negative regulators of leaf senescence, stomatal closure, and osmotic stress tolerance (Besseau et al., 2012; Li et al., 2013). Nevertheless, WRKY70 activated expression of luciferase from the SARD1 promoter in Arabidopsis protoplasts, consistent with a previous report that WRKY70 can activate a promoter fragment containing the 'AGACTTTT' motif (141 to 107 from the gene At1G76960) in parsley protoplasts (Machens et al., 2014). However, we observed that expression from the longer SARD1p-L was much weaker than from SARD1p, suggesting that another transcription factor(s) binding to a site(s) between 1522 and 451 blocks the activation activity of WRKY70. Similar phenomena may explain other observations of negative regulators displaying activation activity in protoplast assays. For example, the three homologous NAC transcription factors – ANAC019, ANAC055, and ANAC072 – activated expression of a reporter by binding to a 60 bp sequence containing an MYC-like motif 'CATGTG' from the gene EARLY RESPONSIVE TO DEHYDRATION STRESS 1 (ERD1) in Arabidopsis protoplasts (Tran et al., 2004). However, ANAC019, ANAC055, and ANAC072 are repressors of SID2 by direct binding confirmed by ChIP assay (Zheng et al., 2012). In our RNA-seq experiment, we observed that WRKY70 acted

# Other factors also contribute to the induction of *SARD1* in the presence of pathogens

The level of *SARD1* expression in *wrky70* mock-infected plants is lower than in infected WT Col-0 plants (Fig 4a), indicating that simply releasing the repression of WRKY70 is not enough to fully induce *SARD1*. Therefore, other factors must contribute to this induced expression. These could be activators that are recruited to the *SARD1* promoter or other repressors which are removed following pathogen recognition. There are other potential TF binding sites in the *SARD1* promoter, and two regions that are evolutionarily conserved (Fig. 6a). TFs binding to these sites may affect expression. WRKY70 has no detectable effect on *SARD1* expression in *Pma* ES4326-infected plants. This is consistent with the idea that WRKY70 might not bind the *SARD1* promoter, or that its repression activity is somehow blocked, in the presence of pathogens. Repression activity could be blocked by interactions with other proteins, or by modification of WRKY70.

## Regulation of *SARD1* expression by WRKY70 probably contributes to fitness

Promoter sequences not affecting gene expression diverge rapidly over evolutionary time. Our results demonstrated that the GACTTTT motif is evolutionarily conserved within the *Brassicacae* family (Fig. 6a), which strongly suggests that regulation of *SARD1* expression by WRKY70 contributes to fitness, and repression of *SARD1* by WRKY70 might be important for restricting immune responses in the absence of pathogen attack, thus avoiding unnecessary diversion of resources from growth to defense.
