### **Highlight:**

A new pentatricopeptide repeat protein gene *Dek41* was cloned and was confirmed to affect *cis*-splicing of mitochondrial *nad4* intron 3, complex I assembly and seed development in maize.

# Abstract

The splicing of organelle-encoded mRNA in plants requires nucleus-encoded proteins. The mechanism of organelle mRNA splicing and the factors involved in this process are not well understood. Pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins are known to participate in such RNA-protein interactions. Maize defective kernel 41 (dek41) is a seedling-lethal mutant that causes developmental defects. The Dek41 gene was cloned by *Mu* tag isolation and allelic confirmation, and was found to encode a P-type PPR protein that targets mitochondria. Mitochondrial RNA transcript profile analysis revealed that *dek41* mutations cause reduced splicing efficiency of mitochondrial nad4 intron 3. Immature dek41 kernels exhibited severely reduced complex I assembly and NADH dehydrogenase activity. The up-regulated expression of alternative oxidase genes and deformed inner cristae of mitochondria in dek41, as revealed by transmission electron microscopy, indicate that proper splicing of *nad4* is essential for mitochondrial functions and morphology. Consistent with this finding, differentially expressed genes in the *dek41* endosperm included those related to mitochondrial function and activity. Our results indicate that DEK41 is a PPR protein affecting *cis*-splicing of mitochondrial *nad4* intron 3 that is required for mitochondrial function and maize kernel development.

# Key words:

dek41, mitochondria, nad4, pentatricopeptide repeat protein, RNA splicing, Zea mays.

## Introduction

Maize (*Zea mays*) is widely used for genetic research due to its easily observable phenotypes (Neuffer and Sheridan, 1980). *Defective kernel* (*dek*) mutants are a major class of maize kernel mutants which are good tools for investigating kernel development (Neuffer and Sheridan, 1980). To date, many maize *dek* mutants have been identified, for instance: *dek1* exhibits severe growth and development defects (Lid *et al.*, 2002); *reas1* causes a mild developmental delay (Qi *et al.*, 2016); and the mutants of pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins exhibit reduced kernel size and arrested development of embryo and endosperm (Liu *et al.*, 2013; Sun *et al.*, 2015; Cai *et al.*, 2017; Dai *et al.*, 2018). Thus, *dek* mutants offer opportunities to investigate many basic biological processes during kernel development.

In higher plants, the nucleus-encoded PPR proteins primarily localize to chloroplasts and mitochondria, and play critical roles in RNA editing, RNA *cis*- and *trans*-splicing, RNA cleavage and RNA maturation (Barkan and Small, 2014). PPR proteins are defined by tandem repeats of a degenerate 35-amino-acid motif and are classified into two major subgroups: P-type PPR members possess only tandem repeats of the 35-amino-acid PPR motif, and PLS-type PPR members include sequential repeats of P, short (S), and long (L) PPR motifs that often carry an E or E–DYW domain extension at the C terminus (Lurin *et al.*, 2004; Cheng *et al.*, 2016). In plants, the PPR family comprises more than 450 members, and a number of PPR mutants associated with severe growth and development defects have been characterized (Fujii and Small, 2011; Barkan and Small, 2014; Hammani and Giege, 2014; Møller, 2016; Gorchs Rovira and Smith, 2019). However, the regulatory functions of great numbers of uncharacterized PPR proteins are still unknown.

Many P-type PPR proteins function in RNA stabilization, cleavage, translational activation, splicing, and other RNA processes. RNA splicing in mitochondria requires the coordination of PPR proteins because of losing ability of self-splicing (Bonen,

2008; Brown et al., 2014). Group I and group II introns have distinct splicing patterns. More than 20 group II introns have been identified in the mitochondrial genomes of flowering plants, including 15 cis-spliced introns in nad1, nad2, nad4, nad5, nad7, rps3, cox2, and ccmF<sub>c</sub> and seven trans-spliced introns in nad1, nad2, and nad5 (Burger et al., 2003; Berrisford and Sazanov, 2009). In the mitochondrial genome, most introns are in genes encoding subunits of complex I (NADH dehydrogenase), which is located in the inner membrane and transfers electrons from NADH to the primary acceptor FMN followed by the ubiquinone binding site (Sazanov and Hinchliffe, 2006; Berrisford and Sazanov, 2009). Five P-type PPR proteins are required for group II intron splicing of mitochondrial transcripts in Arabidopsis thaliana, including transcripts of nad1 (de Longevialle et al., 2007), nad2 (Liu et al., 2010), nad5 (Colas des Francs-Small et al., 2014) and nad7 (Hsieh et al., 2015). In maize, EMP10, EMP16 and DEK37 are involved in *cis*-splicing of *nad*2 intron 4 and intron 1 (Xiu *et al.*, 2016; Cai et al., 2017; Dai et al., 2018), DEK2 is required for the trans-splicing of nad1 intron 1 (Qi et al., 2017b), and DEK35 affects cis-splicing of nad4 intron 1 (Chen et al., 2017). The important roles of other P-type PPR proteins in *cis*- and *trans*-splicing of mitochondrial RNA can be revealed through studies of maize kernel mutants.

Among the large family of PPR proteins in flowering plants, only a small subset has been studied at the molecular level, particularly for mitochondrial PPRs, in which mutations frequently are embryo-lethal. Determining the molecular functions of mitochondrial PPR proteins could shed light on RNA-processing mechanisms as well as the assembly of the oxidative phosphorylation machinery. In the current study, a maize seed mutant *dek41* corresponding to a defective and seedling-lethal phenotype was characterized. According to the rules in maize genetic field, the corresponding mutant was termed as *dek41*, the candidate gene was termed as *Dek41*, its protein was termed as DEK41, the phenotype of the mutant was termed as dek, and the phenotype of wild type seed was termed as WT. *Dek41* encodes a P-type PPR protein that targets to the mitochondria. The *dek41* showed reduced splicing efficiency of mitochondrial *nad4* intron 3 in developing seeds. Lack of this splicing process affects complex I accumulation and NADH dehydrogenase activity, leading to the arrest of

mitochondrial function and kernel development.

# Materials and methods

### Plant materials

The *MuDR* stock (330I) and UFMu01110 mutant line were obtained from Maize Genetics Cooperation Stock Center. The *MuDR* stock (330I) was crossed into B73 inbred line as male parent to generate F1 population seeds, and then self-crossed to generate the F2 population ears. Phenotypical screening for seed mutants was carried out on F2 ears. The *dek41*-ref was recovered as a *dek* mutant from F2 ear screening, with progenies exhibiting 1:3 segregation of *dek* and wild-type (WT) seed phenotype.

The mature seeds were used for genomic DNA extraction. The immature seeds were harvested for RNA and protein extraction, mitochondria isolation, and paraffin and resin section preparation, as well as transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and RNAseq. Other plant tissues were harvested from one inbred B73 plant for RNA and protein extraction. All plants were cultivated in the experimental field in Shanghai University, Shanghai, China.

## Measurement of protein and starch levels

For the protein measurements, the endosperm of WT and *dek41* mature seeds was separated from the embryo and pericarp by dissection after soaking the seeds in water. The samples were dried to constant weights, pulverized with a mortar and pestle in liquid nitrogen, and then measured according to a previously described protocol (Qi *et al.*, 2016). All the measurements were replicated at least three times. For the starch measurements, five mature seeds of the WT and *dek41* were ground in liquid nitrogen. The resulting powders were dried to a constant weight. Finally, the total starch was measured using amyloglucosidase/ $\alpha$ -amylase starch assay kit (Megazyme). The method referenced a previously described protocol (Qi *et al.*, 2016).

Light microscopy and transmission electron microscopy

For light microscopy analysis, immature WT and *dek41* seeds at 12 days after pollination (DAP), 15 DAP and 18 DAP were collected from the same ear and were cut along the longitudinal axis for paraffin section preparation. The sections were fixed in a formalin–acetic acid–alcohol mixture and were dehydrated in an ethanol gradient series of 50, 60, 70, 85, 95, and 100% ethanol. After replacement of acetone and infiltration with paraffin, the sections were embedded and cut using RM2265 (Leica). The paraffin section was stained by fuchsin basic. The sections were observed using Leica DFC500 at Shanghai University.

For TEM analysis, immature WT and *dek41*-ref seeds at 15 DAP were collected from the same ear and were cut along the horizontal axis into small pieces and fixed in paraformaldehyde. The samples were further processed and stained by osmic acid, and were observed by TEM (FEI Tecnai Spirit G2 BioTWIN).

#### *Mutator* tag isolation

The *Mutator* tag isolation was performed according to Williams-Carrier *et al.* (2010), with some modifications. Genomic DNA was prepared for mechanical shearing at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Company, Ltd (Shanghai, China). Fragments (200–500 bp) were ligated to modified Illumina adapters to mark samples from different individuals. *Mu*-containing DNA fragments were enriched by hybridization to a biotinylated oligonucleotide corresponding to the end of the *Mu* terminal inverted repeat. Two successive hybrid enrichment steps were performed to ensure that most of the sequenced DNA fragments harbor *Mu* sequences. Using primers that bind to the ends of the adapters, 15 and 18 cycles PCR was used to bulk up the recovered DNA after the first and second selection rounds, respectively. PCR products were cloned into vector pMD18-T (Takara). Effectiveness of the enrichment was tested by calculating the percentage of clones containing the *Mu* fragment. Enrichment rate that was above 30% was selected for *Mu* Illumina sequencing at

### RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the samples with TRIzol reagent (Tiangen) according to previous report (Feng *et al.*, 2009), and DNA was removed by treatment with RNase-Free DNase I (Takara). Using ReverTra Ace reverse transcriptase (Toyobo), RNA was reverse transcribed to complementary DNA using the random primers provided in the kit (ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix, Toyobo). Amplification of mitochondrial transcripts was performed using primers as described previously (Chen *et al.*, 2017). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed using three independent sets of RNA with *Ubiquitin* as the reference gene. Primers for the mitochondrial introns were designed as described previously with some modifications (Qi *et al.*, 2017b) (Supplementary data, Table S2). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green Real-Time PCR Master Mix (Toyobo) using a Mastercycler ep realplex 2 (Eppendorf) according to the standard protocol.

### Phylogenetic analysis of DEK41 and its homologs

Sequences were compared with NCBI GenBank entries (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) using the protein-protein BLAST. To align the sequences of DEK41 and its homologs, the software Clustal X 1.81 (Thompson *et al.*, 1997) was used. A Phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbour-joining method of the MEGA 4.0 program (Tamura *et al.*, 2007) with the bootstrap test based on 2000 replicates.

#### Subcellular localization of DEK41

The full-length DEK41 coding region was cloned into pENTR/D-TOPO (Invitrogen), and transferred into binary vector pB7WGY2 to produce the DEK41:EYFP fusion construct through LR site-specific recombination. The DEK41:EYFP fusion was placed under a cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter. The construct was introduced into *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* strain Gv3101 and

infiltrated into *Nicotiana tabacum* leaf epidermal cells as described (van Herpen *et al.*, 2010). The fluorescence signals were detected by laser scanning confocal microscopy LSM710 (ZEISS) 30 h after infiltration.

#### Preparation of mitochondria

Mitochondria were extracted from immature seeds of inbred B73 at 18 DAP as described previously with some modifications (Chen et al., 2017). Approximately 15 g of immature seeds was harvested at 18 DAP and ground into powder in liquid nitrogen with a mortar and pestle. Then 20 ml of 4°C precooled extraction buffer (100 mM tricine, 300 mM sucrose, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 1 mM EDTA-K, 0.1% BSA, 5 mM DTT, pH 7.4) and 60 ml of plant protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich) were added to the ground tissue. All the subsequent steps were performed at 4°C. The samples were centrifuged twice at 2600 g for 15 min after filtration through a Miracloth membrane (Calbiochem, San Diego, CA), then the supernatant was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 25 min to pellet the crude mitochondria. The pellet was resuspended in wash buffer (100 mM tricine, 300 mM sucrose, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 1 mM EDTA-K, 0.1% BSA, pH 7.4) and loaded on sucrose density gradients of 1.5, 2.5, 2.5, 2, and 2 ml containing 1.8, 1.45, 1.2, 0.9, and 0.6 M sucrose diluted in wash buffer respectively, as previously described (Clayton and Shadel, 2014). After 90 min of centrifugation at 24,000 rpm at 4°C, the mitochondria were collected from the 1.2 M/1.45 M interface and washed four times in wash buffer. The purified mitochondria were collected after 20 min of centrifugation at 12,000 rpm at 4°C.

#### BN-PAGE and NADH dehydrogenase activity examination

The isolated mitochondria were resuspended in 50 µl B25G20 solution (25 mM Bis-Tris, 20% glycerin, pH 7.0), adding 20% *n*-Dodecyl  $\beta$ -D-maltoside (DDM) to a final concentration of 1%, and were gently mixed on ice for 1.5 h. After 15 min of centrifugation at 13,000 rpm at 4°C, the supernatant was collected and added to the loading buffer for blue native polyacrylamide electrophoresis (BN-PAGE). The concentration of the separation gel was from 5 to 13.5%. At first, the electrophoresis

was run at 50 V, and 25 V was added every 20 min to the final 150 V until the loading dye migrated to the edge of the gel. The gel was stained by coomassie brilliant blue. Measurement of NADH dehydrogenase activity was performed as described previously (Meyer *et al.* 2009). Briefly, the gel was washed three times for 5 min with distilled water and incubated in the reaction solution (0.14 mM NADH, 1.22 mM NBT, 0.1M Tris-HCl, pH 7.4). When the dark blue stain was strong enough, the reaction was stopped by transferring the gel to 40% (v/v) methanol /10% (v/v) acetic acid.

#### Immunoblot analysis

Total proteins were extracted from WT and *dek41* seeds, then were separated by SDS-PAGE. Separated protein samples were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (0.45 µm; Millipore, Bedford, MA). The membrane with the protein sample attached was incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. The signal was visualized according to the manufacturer's instructions using the SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate kit (Pierce). To produce polyclonal antibodies against NAD4, the 55-71 amino acid sequence (PRIQFDPSTANSQFVESC) of NAD4 was synthesized. This peptide was synthesized and dissolved in PBS solution (1.37M NaCl, 0.1M Na<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub>, 0.018M KH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>, KCl 0.027M) containing 8M of urea, then was coupled to KLH (Keyhole limpet hemocyanin) protein for immunization in rabbits according to the standard protocols of Shanghai Ying Ji Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The polyclonal antibodies against NAD4 recognize a protein of an apparent molecular weight of 70 kDa (Fig. S1). The antibodies against Cyt c (Agrisera) and  $\alpha$ -tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich) were used at a dilution of 1:5000.

### **RNA-seq** analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the developing WT and *dek41* seeds at 12 DAP. Library construction was performed according to Illumina standard instructions. Reads were aligned to the maize B73 genome using Bowtie 2 v2.1.0 (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). Data were normalized as reads per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (RPKM), as the sensitivity of RNA-seq depends on both molar concentration and transcript length (Mortazavi *et al.*, 2008). Significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified as those with a fold change and *P* value of differential expression above the threshold (fold change >2.0, P<0.001).

The RNA-seq data are available from the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) under the series entry GSE117091. Sequence data from this article can be found in the GenBank/EMBL data libraries under the following accession numbers: DEK41, ONM56221, GRMZM2G127015; NAD4, AAR91196.

### Results

The *dek41* displays defective seed development

The *dek41* mutant is a *defective kernel* (*dek*) mutant isolated from F2 populations containing active *MuDR* (see Materials and methods). F2 ears that displayed a 3:1 segregation (589:207, *P*<0.05) of WT to *dek* phenotypes were named *dek41*-ref. The *dek41*-ref mutant had white seeds that were smaller than WT during seed development (12 DAP) (Fig. 1A). Mature homozygous *dek41* kernels were small and flat, along with empty pericarps at the top of kernels (Fig. 1B and 1C). The hundred-kernel weight of mutant seeds was only 13% that of the WT (Fig. 1G). The total starch content was reduced by 20% per weight in *dek41* endosperm vs. WT (Fig. 1H). The zein protein and total protein contents per weight of mature endosperm were lower in mutant kernels than WT (Fig. S2A, S2C and S2D).

Detailed phenotypic analysis of WT and *dek41* seeds at different developmental stages showed that embryo and endosperm development in the *dek41*-ref mutant was delayed at least six days compared to WT (Fig. 1D). The endosperm cells of *dek41* seeds at 12 and 15 DAP were less cytoplasmically dense with fewer starch granules

compared to the WT (Fig. 1E). Analysis of paraffin sections of 12 and 15 DAP WT and *dek41* endosperm showed that the development of basal endosperm transfer layer (BETL) was arrested in *dek41* kernels (Fig. 1F). The *dek41* seeds showed very low germination rates, and no seedlings survived after germination. Together, these results indicated that the growth and development of *dek41* seeds were severely affected and that the homozygous *dek41* mutation was seedling-lethal.

### Cloning of Dek41 gene

We used F2 seeds of *dek41*-ref exhibiting a 3:1 segregation ratio (589:207, *P*<0.05) for genetic mapping. After characterizing 170 individuals, *Dek41* was mapped between InDel markers InDel-6 and InDel-7, a region at ~9 Mb on chromosome 7 (maize B73 RefGen\_v3) (Fig. 2A). As *dek41*-ref was isolated from an active *Mutator* line, *Mu* tags were isolated and sequenced (Williams-Carrier *et al.*, 2010). Only one unique *Mu* insertion was found in the mapped interval. The *Mu* insertion was located 632 bp downstream of the start codon of *GRMZM2G127015* (Fig. 2B). Thus, *GRMZM2G127015* was the candidate gene for *dek41*.

In addition, a *UniformMu* insertion line (UFMu01110), carrying a *Mu* insertion at 1047 bp (named *dek41*-mu in this study) downstream of the start codon in *GRMZM2G127015*, was obtained from Maize Genetic Stock Center (Fig. 2B). To determine whether the mutation in *GRMZM2G127015* is indeed the cause of *dek41*, an allelic test was performed with mutant allele *dek41*-mu. The test yielded predicted 3:1 segregation ratio of WT to *dek* seed phenotypes (188:66 and 165:55 respectively, P<0.05) (Fig. 2C). Twenty randomly selected seeds (WT and *dek*, ten for each) from a test-cross ear (*dek41*-ref × *dek41*-mu) were analyzed to detect the *Mu* insertion sites by co-segregation (Fig. S3). Using the 1s and *Mutir* primers, three types of fragments (394 bps, 809 bps and no fragment, respectively; see Fig. S3D) were amplified from WT seeds, and identical bands (two bands per seed, 394 bps and 809 bps; see Fig. S3D) were amplified from *dek* seeds. Using 1a and *Mutir* primers, three types of fragments (342 bps, 757 bps and no fragment, respectively; see Fig. S3E) were amplified from WT seeds, and identical bands (two bands per seed, 757 bps and

11

342 bps; see Fig. S3E) were amplified from the *dek* seeds. Both the phenotypes of ears in the allelic test and the detection of *Mu* insertion sites by co-separation confirmed that *dek41-mu* is allelic to *dek41-ref*. Therefore, it is concluded that *GRMZM2G127015* is the causal gene for *dek41*.

#### *Dek41* encodes a P-type PPR protein

Sequence analysis of *Dek41* revealed that this gene consisted of a single exon (without any introns) with a 1371-bp coding sequence encoding a putative PPR protein of 456 amino acids (Fig. 2B). Based on annotation in FLAGdb (http://tools.ips2.u-psud.fr/projects/FLAGdb++/HTML/index.shtml), DEK41 was classified as a P-type PPR protein with seven P repeat domains (Fig. 2B).

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted based on full-length maize DEK41 protein sequence and homologous protein sequences from other organisms. The result showed that DEK41 homologs were highly conserved in angiosperms (Fig. S4A). DEK41 was closely related to Sb02g029280 from *Sorghum bicolor*, Os05g11700 from *Oryza sativa japonica*, and homologs from *Setaria italica* (XP\_004960609) and *Brachypodium distachyon* (XP\_003566429). A detailed sequence alignment with homologs from sorghum, rice, *S. italica* and *B. distachyon* indicated that DEK41 shared highly conserved P repeat domains with homologs in other monocotyledons (Fig. S4B).

### Dek41 is constitutively expressed in various tissues

To examine *Dek41* expression, quantitative RT-PCR was perfomed using total RNA extracted from immature WT and *dek41* kernels at 12 DAP, 15 DAP and 18 DAP. *Ubiquitin* was used as an internal control. Surprisingly, increased transcript level was detected at 12 and 18 DAP in *dek41* vs. WT (Fig. S5). The full-length *Dek41* transcripts from WT and *dek41* seeds were also detected by reversed transcription PCR using specific primers Dek41-F1/R1 matching the 5'- and 3'-UTR regional sequences of *Dek41*, respectively (Table S2). Full-length (1650-bp) *Dek41* transcripts were detected in WT seeds, but no target bands were amplified from either *dek41* 

mutant (Fig. 2D). These results indicated that the expression of full-length *Dek41* was completely arrested in *dek41* mutants.

The expression pattern of *Dek41* in WT was examined by quantitative RT-PCR. *Dek41* was constitutively expressed in all tested tissues, with higher expression level in the ear than in other tissues (Fig. 3A). During seed development, *Dek41* was expressed before 3 DAP, until after 30 DAP, with a peak level at 30 DAP (Fig. 3A).

#### DEK41 targets to the mitochondria

PPR proteins are predominantly targeted to plastids or mitochondria (Colcombet *et al.*, 2013). To determine the subcellular localization, full-length DEK41 was fused to EYFP in a binary vector pB7WGY2. The fusion was transiently expressed in tobacco leaf epidermal cells by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Confocal laser scanning microscopy revealed that the EYFP signals colocalize with the mitochondria-mCherry marker pBIN20-MT-RK (Nelson *et al.*, 2007), indicating that DEK41 is targeted to mitochondria (Fig. 3B).

The *dek41* mutation affects *cis*-splicing of mitochondrial *nad4* intron 3

As DEK41 is a mitochondrion-targeting P-type PPR protein, the transcripts of all the mitochondrial protein-coding genes from immature WT and *dek41* seeds at 12 DAP were amplified by RT-PCR using specific primers (Table S2). Among the 35 genes, only the level of mature *nad4* transcript was reduced in *dek41*, and the transcripts of several genes (*nad6*, *rps4*, *rps13*, *atp9*, *ccmF<sub>N</sub>*, *mat-r*, *rpl16*, etc.) were increased in *dek41* (Fig. 4A and Fig. S6). These results suggested that DEK41 was involved in regulating the expression of *nad4* in developing maize kernels. To further examine the *nad4* precursor transcript, three specific primers were designed to amplify fragments containing each of the three introns in the *nad4* transcript in both *dek41*-ref and *dek41*-ref/*dek41*-mu compared to WT, whereas the splicing efficiency of *nad4* intron 2 was not affected (Fig. 4C).

P-type PPR proteins are involved in the splicing of group II introns (Cheng et al.,

2016; Xiu *et al.*, 2016). Maize mitochondria contain 22 group II introns, including three introns from *nad4* transcripts. To investigate the splicing alterations in *dek41* mutants, specific primers were designed for qRT-PCR to inspect the 22 mitochondrial group II introns in both *dek41*-ref and *dek41*-mu. The quantitative differences in spliced exons between *dek41* mutants and WT were compared by PCR using primers across adjacent exons. The results showed distinctly reduction of *nad4* spliced exon 3-4 fragment and splicing efficiency of *nad4* intron 3 in both *dek41* mutants compared to WT (Fig. 5A and 5B). A slight reduction in splicing efficiency of *nad4* intron 1 was also observed in both *dek41* mutants (Fig. 5B). These results indicated that the splicing efficiency of *nad4* intron 3 was decreased in the *dek41* mutants, suggesting that DEK41 is required for the *cis*-splicing of mitochondrial *nad4* intron 3.

Mutations in *Dek41* affect mitochondrial complex I assembly and NADH dehydrogenase activity

NAD4 is a subunit of the mitochondrial complex I, NADH dehydrogenase. Blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN-PAGE) was used to detect the potential impact on mitochondrial complexes in *dek41* mutants. The mitochondria were isolated and enriched from immature WT and *dek41* seeds at 18 DAP, then equivalent amounts of mitochondrial proteins were separated by BN-PAGE. The levels of complex I, as well as super complex I+III<sub>2</sub>, were reduced in both *dek41*-ref and *dek41*-mu, compared to WT (Fig. 6A). An in-gel NADH dehydrogenase activity test was conducted to examine the activity of complex I (Meyer *et al.*, 2009). The *dek41* mutants showed significantly reduced complex I and super complex I+III<sub>2</sub> NADH dehydrogenase activity, indicating that complex I and super complex I+III<sub>2</sub> assembly were impaired in *dek41* mutants (Fig. 6B). A smaller complex I with reduced activity was detected in *dek41* mutants (Fig. 6B), suggesting that a partially assembled complex I maintained residual NADH dehydrogenase activity.

To detect the influence of *dek41* mutation on oxidation respiratory chain complexes, the levels of mitochondrial complex-related proteins in *dek41* vs. WT plants were analyzed by Western blot using antibodies against NAD4 (subunit of

complex I) and Cyt c (cytochrome c). The NAD4 protein was less abundant in the *dek41* mutants, while the amount of Cyt c was increased (Fig. 6C). The defect of NAD4 was in accordance with the changes on BN-PAGE. These results suggested that *dek41* affected the abundance of NAD4 and the assembly of complex I, which probably also affected the accumulation of other electron transport chain (ETC)-related proteins (such as Cyt c) in *dek41*.

### The dek41 mutant exhibits abnormal mitochondrial morphology

To detect the effects of *dek41* mutation on mitochondrial structure, the endosperms of WT and *dek41* at 15 DAP were observed by TEM analysis. Normal activation of the electron transport chain (ETC) was required for the proper formation of the inner envelope cristae in mitochondria (Logan, 2006). The mitochondria in the WT endosperm formed distinct inner envelope cristae surrounded by a dense matrix. By contrast, the internal structure of mitochondria in *dek41* was vague without typical cristae but only small dots (Fig. 6E). The result showed morphological changes of mitochondria occurred in *dek41*, suggesting that mitochondrial function was severely arrested in the *dek41* during seed development.

The *dek41* mutation affects the expression of genes related to mitochondrial function

In plant, alternative oxidases (AOXs) are activated to maintain the tricarboxylic acid cycle and electron transport when there is an electron transport defect in the cytochrome c pathway (Vanlerberghe and Ordog, 2002). The alternative pathway was examined by analyzing the expression levels of AOX genes in *dek41*. Quantitative RT-PCR showed that both *Aox2* and *Aox3* were up-regulated in *dek41* compared to WT (Fig. 6D), indicating the expression level of genes in alternative respiratory pathway was upregulated due to inefficient mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in the mutant.

The transcript profiles of 12 DAP *dek41* and WT endosperm were compared using RNA-seq. Among the 41406 gene transcripts detected by RNA-seq, significant DEGs were identified as those with a threshold fold change >2 and *P*-value <0.001.

Based on these criteria, 1823 genes showed significant altered expression between dek41 and WT. There were 1542 genes with increased transcription, while 281 genes showed decreased transcription (Table S1). Of these DEGs, 1124 genes could be functionally annotated (BLAST analysis against the GenBank database at http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Gene ontology (GO; http://bioinfo.cau.edu.cn/agriGO) analysis indicated that DEGs were mostly related to ten GO terms. Among them, six GO terms were closely related to mitochondrial function: mitochondrial membrane, protein targeting to mitochondria, GTP binding, NAD or NADH binding, purine monophosphophate nucleoside biosynthetic process. and hydrogen ion transmembrane transporter activity (Fig. 6F). In addition, DEGs involved in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, glucose catabolic process and nucleosome assembly were also up-regulated in the dek41. The nutrient reservoir activity, which is mainly attributed to the storage proteins, was down-regulated in *dek41* vs. WT (Table S1). This finding is in accordance with the reduced total protein content in mature *dek41* seeds compared to WT (Fig. S2D). RNA-seq data were further validated by quantitative RT-PCR on some of the most significant DEGs from each GO category (Fig. S7), and four BETL marker genes (BAP-1A, BAP-1B, BETL3, and BETL9) were also detected using qPCR (Fig. S8), all these results were in accordance with the RNA-seq data.

# Discussion

DEK41 is a P-type PPR protein required for *cis*-splicing of mitochondrial *nad4* intron 3 in maize

In this study, we identified a novel P-type PPR protein DEK41 which affects the *cis*-splicing of *nad4* intron 3 in maize. The functional characterization of DEK41 expanded the list of P-type PPR proteins that function as splicing factors in organelle RNA metabolism. The P-type PPR proteins with splicing effect have been studied in *Arabidopsis* mitochondria, including OTP43, ABO5, OTP439 and TANG2, SLO3, and

MTL1 (see these proteins in Table 1). These PPRs are required for the splicing of mitochondrial nad1, nad2, nad5 and nad7 introns. In addition to PPRs, the Arabidopsis mutants affecting complex I also include RNA maturase (nMAT1, nMAT2, nMAT4) mutants (Keren et al., 2009, 2012; Cohen et al., 2014), and RNA helicase (PMH2 and ABO6) mutants (Köhler et al., 2010; He et al., 2012). Mutation of these genes (PPR and non PPR genes) in Arabidopsis usually causes slow-growth phenotype of plants. In maize, the mutants affecting complex I are majorly PPR mutants. The P-type PPRs (EMP8, EMP10, EMP11, EMP16, DEK2, DEK35 and DEK37) are reported affecting the splicing of mitochondrial nad1, nad2 and nad4 introns (see Table 1). These P-type PPRs together with the PLS-type PPRs (Smk1, EMP5, EMP7) which are required for the transcript editing of mitochondrial nad7, *rpl16* and *ccmF<sub>N</sub>* (see Table 1), affect the assembly of mitochondrial complex I and the correspondent mutants all demonstrate abnormal morphology of mitochondria. The maize dek10 mutant affects complex IV, the mutant Emp9 affects complex I and III, and the correspondent genes of these mutants both encode mitochondrial E-subgroup PPRs (Qi et al., 2017a; Yang et al., 2017). The mutants affecting complex I, III and IV all demonstrate abnormal morphology of mitochondria, indicating that the mitochondrial structure and function are affected. In maize, Mutation of these genes usually causes lethal phenotype of plants (Liu et al., 2013; Qi et al., 2017b; Yang et al., 2017; Dai et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2018).

The maize *dek41*-ref is a defective kernel mutant with arrested endosperm and embryo development (Fig. 1A and 1D). Full-length *Dek41* transcript was completely repressed in *dek41*-ref mutant (Fig. 2D), indicating that loss-of-function of DEK41 is the intrinsic causation for arrested development of endosperm and embryo in *dek41* seed. The splicing efficiency of *nad4* intron 3 was affected in both *dek41*-ref and *dek41*-mu mutants, this alteration of splicing efficiency was specific in all 22 mitochondrial group II introns (Fig. 5B). Although splicing of *nad4* intron 3 was not completely abolished in the *dek41* mutants, the levels of mature *nad4* transcript were reduced (Fig. 4A) which was probably due to a loss of stabilization resulting from a splicing defect. We also noticed that the transcripts of several genes were found to be

increased in the *dek41* (such as *nad6*, *rps4*, *rps13*, *atp9*, *ccmF<sub>N</sub>*, *mat-r*, *rpl16*; see Fig. 4A). This could be due to feedback response to the *Dek41* mutation. Similar feedback response in transcription of mitochondrial genes was also observed in other P-type PPR gene mutations (Xiu *et al.*, 2016; Qi *et al.*, 2017b). Slight reduction in splicing efficiency of *nad4* intron 1 was also observed in both *dek41* mutants (Fig. 4C and 5B). However, the reduction in the levels of spliced *nad4* exon1-2 was not found (Fig. 5A), suggesting that *nad4* intron 1 might not be the unique splicing target of DEK41. Our previous work reported that DEK35 also affects the splicing of *nad4* intron 1 (Chen *et al.* 2017), and that EMP8 affects the splicing of mitochondrial *nad4* intron 1, *nad2* intron 1 and *nad1* intron 4 (Sun *et al.*, 2018), so it is possible that the splicing of *nad4* intron 1, *nad2* intron 1 requires DEK41, DEK35 and EMP8. Further analysis of mitochondrial complexes showed significant deficiency of complex I and super complex I + III<sub>2</sub> (Fig. 6A), suggesting that mitochondrial function is severely defective in *dek41* mutants due to reduced *nad4* transcript levels.

NAD4 is one of nine mitochondria-encoded subunits (NAD1-4, NAD4L, NAD5-7, and NAD9) constituting NADH dehydrogenase (Clifton et al., 2004). NADH dehydrogenase is the major entry point for transporting electrons in plants (Braun et al., 2014). Previous studies have revealed that different types of nuclear-encoded proteins are involved in the diverse RNA processing of *nad4* transcript: MTSF1 is essential for the 3'-processing of mitochondrial nad4 mRNA and its stability in Arabidopsis thaliana (Haïli et al., 2013), RPF1 is required for the efficient generation of a 5'-end 228 nucleotides upstream of mitochondrial nad4 gene in A. thaliana (Hölzle et al., 2011), AHG11 has potential roles in nad4 RNA editing of mitochondria in A. thaliana (Murayama et al., 2012), CSS1 is a splicing factor of mitochondrial nad4 intron in A. thaliana (Nakagawa and Sakurai, 2006), the EMP8 is required for the splicing of mitochondrial nad4 intron 1 in maize (Sun et al., 2018), and MS1 is involved in splicing of mitochondrial nad4 intron 1 in Nicotiana sylvestris (Brangeon et al., 2000). These studies report severe effects of faulty regulating nad4 transcript, indicating that NAD4 plays a crucial role in mitochondrial function. In *dek41* mutants. nad4 intron 3 exhibits affected and reduced splicing processes, resulting in severe deficiency of mitochondrial complex I assembly and activity as well as seed development. This study demonstrated that splicing of the *nad4* transcript is another crucial posttranscriptional process required to maintain the normal function of mitochondrial complex I.

The *dek41* mutation results in defective mitochondrial function and kernel development

Mitochondrial complex I is the primary entry point for electrons into the electron transport chain (ETC), inhibition of which by inhibitors or by loss of subunits leads to problems with primary metabolism in the affected plants (Noctor et al., 2007). Complex I is embedded in the inner membrane and mediates the transfer of electrons from NADH to ubiquinone (Lee et al., 2013). BN-PAGE and in-gel NADH dehydrogenase activity analysis revealed that the assembly and activity of complex I and super-complex I + III<sub>2</sub> was severely impaired in *dek41* mutants (Fig. 6A and 6B). A smaller complex I with partial activity was observed in the in-gel NADH dehydrogenase activity assay (Fig. 6B), suggesting that dek41 mutants can still synthesize proteins in mitochondria at probably reduced capability and/or with increased errors. Such partially assembled complex I has also been observed in other PPR mutants, including *emp9*, *ppr78* and *dek35*, which exhibit arrested mitochondrial complex I activity and seed development (Chen et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2017). Similar to EMP16 (Xiu et al., 2016) and Dek2 (Qi et al., 2017b), increased Aox2 and Aox3 level was also observed in dek41. AOXs can reduce the reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in situations when ETC complexes are unable to function properly for the maintenance of electron flux. There is rapid increase in transcriptional level of Aoxs in previously reported PPR mutants (Sun et al., 2015; Xiu et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2017; Qi et al., 2017b). The cytochrome c is located in the mitochondrial intermembrane/ intercristae spaces and functions as electron shuttle in the respiratory chain and interacts with cardiolipin (Garrido et al., 2006). Therefore, in addition to the reduction of NAD4 and increase of Cyt c in *dek41* mutants (Fig. 6C), it was suggested that the dek41 mutation affected the assembly and NADH dehydrogenase activity of mitochondrial complex I, which probably promotes the accumulation of other complex-related protein (such as cytochrome c).

ETC biogenesis is required for the proper morphology of the cristae in mitochondria (Logan, 2006). In maize PPR mutants, the formation of mitochondrial cristae by the inner membrane is strongly impaired, these structurally altered mitochondria were likely less functional than mitochondria with a normal inner structure (Sosso *et al.*, 2012; Chen *et al.*, 2017; Qi *et al.*, 2017b; Dai *et al.*, 2018). Voided internal structure with abnormal morphology of mitochondria was also observed in the *dek41* (Fig. 6E), suggesting that mitochondrial function was severely disordered in *dek41*. The loss of DEK41 function results in a defect of ETC biogenesis, which affects not only respiratory metabolism but also the inner structure of mitochondria.

The mitochondrial function defect in dek41 also leads to changes in the expression level of several important genes in GO categories associated with energy-consuming biological process. Among ten GO classifications, six GO classifications that were highly related to mitochondrial structure and activity exhibited extensive up-regulated DEGs, which indicated that the oxidation respiratory chain was severely affected in *dek41* (Fig. 6F). The critical effects on mitochondrial function were in accordance with the increased mitochondrial membrane (GO: 0031966), protein targeting to mitochondria (GO: 006626), GTP binding (GO: 0005525), NAD or NADH binding (GO: 0051287), hydrogen ion transmembrane transporter activity (GO: 0015078) and purine nucleoside monophosphate biosynthetic process (GO: 0009127) in transcriptome analysis. The tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (GO: 0006099) is a series of enzyme-catalyzed chemical reactions that form a key part of aerobic respiration in mitochondria, and the glucose catabolic process (GO: 0006007) determines the reaction rate of TCA cycle. Nucleosome assembly is essential for a variety of biological processes, such as cell cycle progression, development, and senescence (Gal et al., 2015), which are also energy-consuming processes. The dek41 mutation causes abnormal mitochondrial function, which might result in the feedback up-regulation of nucleosome assembly-related genes, leading to

developmental delay (Table S1). Apart from nine up-regulated GO classifications, only one classification "nutrient reservoir activity" (GO: 0045735) showed decreased DEGs (Table S1). Immature *dek41* seeds showed delayed embryo and endosperm development (Fig. 1A and 1D). The mature *dek41* seeds exhibited significantly decreased seed dry weight, starch content and total protein contents (Fig. 1G, 1H and S2D). These phenotypic data were in accordance with the decreased DEGs in transcriptome analysis (Table S1). It was postulated that the widely down-regulation of genes encoding zein proteins might explain the reduced total protein content in seeds (Fig. S2D). These results highlight the necessity of mitochondrial activity for nutrient generation and accumulation (Law *et al.*, 2014).

BETL develops extensive cell wall ingrowths supporting an enlarged plasma membrane surface that promotes primary nutrient uptake in the endosperm (Pate and Gunning, 1972; Thompson et al., 2001; Offler et al., 2003), which requires high metabolic rates. Therefore, transfer cells are typically rich in mitochondria. In this study, the development of BETL was dramatically arrested in *dek41* endosperm (Fig. 1F), and the transcriptional levels of BETL marker genes were decreased in *dek41* (Fig. S8). Absence of a properly formed transfer cell layer is always correlated with reduced rates of grain filling and seed abortion (Brink and Cooper, 1947; Charlton et al., 1995). These mutants with arrested BETL development exhibit small and hollow kernels. Mutations of the maize EMP4 gene, EMP16 gene, DEK35 and DEK37 gene encoding PPR proteins also result in a defective transfer cell layer (Gutierrez et al., 2007; Xiu et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2017; Dai et al., 2018). Therefore, the arrested BETL development in *dek41* might be the consequence of reduced energy for nutrient transport. In summary, maize *dek41* mutation affects the mitochondrial structure and function, resulting in arrested BETL formation and embryo morphogenesis, which ultimately affects kernel development.
