# **Plant-specifc Dof transcription factors VASCULAR-RELATED DOF1 and VASCULAR-RELATED DOF2 regulate vascular cell diferentiation and lignin biosynthesis in Arabidopsis**

**Vasagi Ramachandran1 · Yuki Tobimatsu2  [·](http://orcid.org/0000-0002-7578-7392) Yamamura Masaomi2 · Ryosuke Sano1 · Toshiaki Umezawa2,3 · Taku Demura[1](http://orcid.org/0000-0002-2499-4738) · Misato Ohtani1,[4](http://orcid.org/0000-0001-5429-3310)**

Received: 3 September 2019 / Accepted: 23 July 2020 © Springer Nature B.V. 2020

### **Abstract**

*Key message* **Plant-specifc Dof transcription factors VDOF1 and VDOF2 are novel regulators of vascular cell differentiation through the course of a lifetime in Arabidopsis, with shifting their transcriptional target genes.**

**Abstract** Vascular system is one of critical tissues for vascular plants to transport low-molecular compounds, such as water, minerals, and the photosynthetic product, sucrose. Here, we report the involvement of two Dof transcription factors, named VASCULAR-RELATED DOF1 (VDOF1)/VDOF4.6 and VDOF2/VDOF1.8, in vascular cell diferentiation and lignin biosynthesis in Arabidopsis. *VDOF* genes were expressed in vascular tissues, but the detailed expression sites were partly diferent between *VDOF1* and *VDOF2*. Vein patterning and lignin analysis of *VDOF* overexpressors and double mutant *vdof1 vdof2* suggested that VDOF1 and VDOF2 would function as negative regulators of vein formation in seedlings, and lignin deposition in inforescence stems. Interestingly, efects of *VDOF* overexpression in lignin deposition were diferent by developmental stages of inforescence stems, and total lignin contents were increased and decreased in *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* overexpressors, respectively. RNA-seq analysis of inducible *VDOF* overexpressors demonstrated that the genes for cell wall biosynthesis, including lignin biosynthetic genes, and the transcription factor genes related to stress response and brassinosteroid signaling were commonly afected by *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* overexpression. Taken together, we concluded that VDOF1 and VDOF2 are novel regulators of vascular cell diferentiation through the course of a lifetime, with shifting their transcriptional target genes: in seedlings, the *VDOF* genes negatively regulate vein formation, while at reproductive stages, the VDOF proteins target lignin biosynthesis.

**Keywords** *VASCULAR-RELATED DOF1* · *VASCULAR-RELATED DOF2* · Vascular cell diferentiation · Lignin · Vein patterning · Dof transcription factor · *Arabidopsis thaliana*

## **Introduction**

Vascular tissues in plants are composed of xylem, phloem, and cambium. Xylem transports water and nutrients from roots to aboveground tissues and provides mechanical support to the plant body, while phloem transports biosynthesis products, i.e., photosynthetic product sucrose and proteins, throughout the plant (Myburg et al. [2001;](#page-18-0) Ye [2002](#page-18-1); Shuetz et al. [2013\)](#page-18-2). Vascular cells are all generated from vascular stem cells, called (pro)cambial cells. Recent advances in the molecular genetics studies with *Arabidopsis thaliana* (Arabidopsis) have revealed many classes of transcription factors critical for (pro)cambium formation (Miyashima et al. [2013;](#page-17-0) Furuta et al. [2014;](#page-17-1) Rybel et al. [2016\)](#page-18-3). The formation of (pro)cambium is considered to be initiated by auxin signals, which are mediated by the auxin response transcription factor MONOPTEROS (MP)/AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR5 (ARF5) (Hardtke and Berleth [1998](#page-17-2); Rybel et al. [2016;](#page-18-3) Othani et al. [2017](#page-18-4)). MP/ARF5 regulates *PIN* genes encoding auxin efux carriers and the homeodomain-leucine zipper (HD-Zip) III type transcription factor AtHB8. AtHB8 is known to be involved in the stabilization of PIN1 expression, to regulate the acquisition of (pro)cambial cell fate (Baima et al. [2001](#page-16-0); Donner et al. [2009](#page-17-3)).

Precursors of xylem and phloem cells are generated from (pro)cambium cells by orientated cell division (Miyashima et al. [2013](#page-17-0); Furuta et al. [2014](#page-17-1); Rybel et al. [2016](#page-18-3)), however, processes of cell fate determination from (pro)cambium to xylem or phloem precursors are still largely unknown. For phloem cell development, ALTERED PHLOEM DEVEL-OPMENT (APL), a MYB coiled-coil transcription factor, was identifed as a critical regulator of phloem cell diferentiation, because the *apl* mutant could not produce sieve tube elements or companion cells (Bonke et al. 2003). For xylem cell diferentiation, a specifc group of NAC transcription factor, containing VASCULAR-RELATED NAC-DOMAIN1 (VND1) to VND7 (Kubo et al. [2005;](#page-17-4) Yamaguchi et al. [2008,](#page-18-5) [2010](#page-18-6); Nakano et al. [2015;](#page-18-7) Tan et al. [2018](#page-18-8)) and NAC SECONDARY WALL THICKENING PROMOT-ING FACTOR1 (NST1), NST2, and NST3/SECONDARY WALL-ASSOCIATED NAC DOMAIN PROTEIN1 (SND1) (Zhong et al. [2006](#page-18-9); Mitsuda et al. [2007;](#page-17-5) Zhong and Ye [2014](#page-18-10); Nakano et al. [2015](#page-18-7)), have been proposed to act as the master regulators of xylem cell diferentiation (Nakano et al. [2015](#page-18-7); Ohtani et al. [2017](#page-18-4); Ohtani and Demura [2019\)](#page-18-11). In Arabidopsis, VND and NST proteins function in the diferentiation of tracheary elements and fbers, respectively (Kubo et al. [2005](#page-17-4); Zhong et al. [2006](#page-18-9); Mitsuda et al. [2007;](#page-17-5) Zhong and Ye [2014](#page-18-10); Nakano et al. [2015](#page-18-7); Ohtani and Demura [2019](#page-18-11)). Water is conducted through tracheary elements and xylem fbers support the plant body. These cells can be characterized based on their lignifed, thick secondary cell walls (SCWs) (Myburg et al. [2001](#page-18-0); Shuetz et al. [2013\)](#page-18-2).

Lignin, a phenolic biopolymer, is the second most abundant biopolymer on Earth (after cellulose) (Boerjan et al. [2003\)](#page-17-6). Lignin is a critical molecular factor that helps increase the stifness and strength of the plant cell, and provides waterproofng to the cell wall. Three types of monolignols contribute as lignin precursors in dicot plants: mainly sinapyl alcohol and coniferyl alcohol, giving rise to syringyl (S) and guaiacyl (G) lignins, respectively, with traces of *p*-coumaryl alcohol, giving rise to *p*-hydroxyphenyl (H) lignin. These monolignols are transported to the cell wall where they are polymerized by laccases and/or peroxidases via oxidative radical coupling (Vanholme et al. [2010](#page-18-12); Weng and Chapple [2010](#page-18-13); Acker et al. [2013](#page-18-14); Tobimatsu and Schuetz [2019](#page-18-15)). Moreover, the molecular structures of the constituent monolignols afect the chemical properties of lignin as well as of biomass as a whole; thus, the S/G/H composition of lignin has been a major target of cell wall bioengineering studies (Chen and Dixon [2007](#page-17-7); Bonawitz et al. [2014](#page-17-8); Umezawa [2018\)](#page-18-16). Many MYB transcription factors have been reported to function as key regulators controlling lignin biosynthesis (Grima-Pettenati et al. [2012\)](#page-17-9). In Arabidopsis, MYB46 and MYB83 are considered to be master regulators of SCW formation including lignin biosynthesis (Zhong et al. [2007](#page-18-17); McCarthy et al. [2009](#page-17-10); Nakano et al. [2015](#page-18-7); Ohtani and Demura [2019](#page-18-11)). In addition to MYB46 and MYB83, other MYB proteins, such as MYB58, MYB63, and MYB85, directly regulate lignin biosynthesis either positively or negatively (Zhou et al. [2009](#page-18-18); Zhong and Ye [2014;](#page-18-10) Nakano et al. [2015](#page-18-7)). Thus, complex transcriptional networks would be involved in lignin biosynthesis for functional xylem cell formation.

Accumulating evidence also suggests that Dof transcription factors are key regulators of vascular development. Gardiner et al. [\(2010\)](#page-17-11) showed that the expression regions of three Dof genes, *Dof2.1*, *Dof4.6* and *Dof5.3*, partially overlap with those of *AtHB8*, which encodes a critical factor in determining procambial cell fate, during the early stages of vascular development in leaves (Gardiner et al. [2010](#page-17-11)). Another Dof gene expressed in procambial cells is *Dof5.8* (Konishi and Yanagisawa [2007;](#page-17-12) Konishi et al. [2015](#page-17-13)). MP/ARF5 was found to directly activate *Dof5.8* expression through binding to its promoter sequence, and the functional inhibition of Dof5.8 afected root and cotyledon development, as well as vein patterning in the cotyledon (Konishi et al. [2015](#page-17-13)). These fndings, together with the observations by Gardiner et al. ([2010\)](#page-17-11), suggest that *Dof2.1*, *Dof4.6*, *Dof5.3*, and *Dof5.8* play important roles in vascular development under the control of auxin. The last cambium cell-related Dof gene identifed was *Dof5.6*, also known as *HIGH CAMBIAL ACTIVITY2* (*HCA2*) (Guo et al. [2009](#page-17-14)). In the gain-of-function mutant *hca2*/*dof5.6*, the continuous ring of vascular tissues in fower stems forms early due to the early formation of interfascicular cambium (Guo et al. [2009](#page-17-14)). Recently, Miyashima et al. [\(2019](#page-18-19)) reported that the two Dof proteins Dof2.4 and Dof5.1, newly named PHLOEM EARLY DOF 1 (PEAR1) and PEAR2, respectively, regulate the radial growth of roots, with 4 other Dof proteins, Dof3.2/DOF6, Dof5.3/TMO6, Dof1.1/OBP2 and Dof5.6/HCA2. These 6 *Dof* genes are expressed in protophloem sieve elements under the control of auxin and cytokinin (Miyashima et al. [2019](#page-18-19); Smetana et al. [2019](#page-18-20)), but the proteins can move across short ranges of cell fles via plasmodesmata (Miyashima et al. [2019\)](#page-18-19). Moreover, multiple mutants of these *Dof* genes showed the reduction of radial cell proliferation in primary roots, whereas single or double mutants did not display any phenotypes (Miyashima et al. [2019](#page-18-19)). These results collectively suggest that cambial growth and subsequent vascular cell development are modulated by the function of multiple Dof transcription factors in Arabidopsis (Miyashima et al. [2019](#page-18-19); Smetana et al. [2019](#page-18-20)). Moreover, the other Dof gene, *Dof4.2*, is involved in regulating phenylpropanoid-related genes (Diaz et al. [2002\)](#page-17-15). Dof4.2 has been suggested to be a negative regulator of favonoid biosynthesis, and notably, lignin depositions in hypocotyl fbers were not observed in a *Dof4.2* overexpressor (Skirycz et al. [2007](#page-18-21)). Therefore, Dof4.2 could modulate phenylpropanoid metabolism in a tissue- and stress-dependent manner (Skirycz et al. [2007\)](#page-18-21).

In the present work, we report molecular functions of two Dof genes, *VASCULAR-RELATED DOF1* (*VDOF1*) (also known as *Dof4.6*) and *VDOF2* (also known as *Dof1.8*) in the regulation of vascular cell diferentiation and lignin biosynthesis in Arabidopsis. These genes were contained in the set of genes that are upregulated during xylem vessel cell diferentiation in transciptome data of Arabidopsis *in vitro* system (Kubo et al. [2005\)](#page-17-4). Enzymatic saccharifcation analysis of seedlings and fower stem samples from these overexpressor plants led to the successful identifcation of several genes encoding positive regulators of saccharifcation efciency (Ohtani et al. [2017\)](#page-18-4). VDOF1 and VDOF2 are included in the list of positive regulators (Ohtani et al. [2017](#page-18-4)), suggesting that VDOF1 and VDOF2 regulate SCW properties associated with saccharifcation efciency. VDOF1 and VDOF2 were expressed in vascular tissues, including cambial regions, in accordance with the previous observation of *Dof4.6* reporter (Gardiner et al. [2010\)](#page-17-11). Overexpressors of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2*, as well as the double knock-out mutant *vdof1 vdof2*, showed abnormalities in vein patterning in seedling cotyledons, as well as lignin deposition in fower stems. In addition, transcriptome analysis using inducible *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* overexpressors identifed developmental-related transcription factor and lignin biosynthesis-related genes as target genes of VDOF1 and VDOF2. These fndings indicate that VDOF1 and VDOF2 are novel regulators of vascular cell diferentiation.

### **Materials and methods**

### **Plant materials and growth conditions**

*Arabidopsis thaliana* (Arabidopsis) ecotype Columbia (Col-0) was used as the wild type. T-DNA insertion lines of SALK\_025618, SALK\_039188, SALK\_045207, SALK\_008152, SALK\_004243, SALK\_079468, and SALK\_152104 for *VDOF1*, and of SALK\_130584 for *VDOF2* were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Research Center. No visible phenotype was observed in any T-DNA lines, thus we selected SALK\_152104 (*vdof1*) and SALK\_130584 (*vdof2*) for the generation of double mutant. The *vdof1 vdof2* double mutant was established by the reciprocal crossing between *vdof1* and *vdof2*. The sterilized seeds were placed on germination medium (GM) and incubated at 4 °C for 2−3 d in darkness. The plates were transferred to a growth chamber with continuous light at 22 °C. Fourteenday-old seedlings were transferred to soil and allowed to continue growing in the growth chamber or in a greenhouse, with long-day conditions (16-h light/8-h dark) at 22 °C.

### **Plasmid construction and plant transformation**

Promoter sequences were obtained as approximately 2-kb upstream regions of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2*. The *VDOF* promoters (*VDOFpro*) and regions containing promoter sequences and open reading frames (ORFs) were amplifed by PCR using genomic DNA as template. The coding sequences (CDSs) of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* were amplifed by PCR using cDNA as template. The amplifed fragments were subcloned into the pENTR/D-TOPO vector (Invitrogen), and then the cloned fragments were integrated into the GATE-WAY destination vectors using LR Clonase (Invitrogen). To generate constitutive and estrogen-inducible overexpressors for *VDOF* genes (*VDOF1ox* and *VDOF2ox*), the CDS fragments of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* were integrated into the binary vectors pH35GS (Kubo et al. [2005](#page-17-4)) and pHXGS (Endo et al. [2015](#page-17-16)), respectively. Reporter constructs were generated by inserting the *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* promoter fragments with or without coding sequences into pBGGUS (Kubo et al. [2005](#page-17-4)) to produce *VDOF1pro:GUS, VDOF2pro:GUS, VDOF1pro:VDOF1 CDS-GUS* and *VDOF2pro:VDOF2 CDS-GUS*. The regions containing *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* promoters and ORF fragments were inserted into pHGY (Yamaguchi et al. [2008\)](#page-18-5) to produce *VDOF1-YFP* and *VDOF2-YFP*. To generate efector plasmids for the dual luciferase (LUC) transient expression assay, the CDSs of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2*

were inserted into the *Sma*I/*Sal*I site of the p35S-GAL4DB plasmid (Yamaguchi et al. [2008](#page-18-5)). The reporter and reference plasmids containing frefy LUC and *Renilla reniformis* LUC, respectively, were prepared as described in Endo et al. [\(2015\)](#page-17-16).

The plasmid constructs were electroporated into *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* strain *GV3101:pMP90*. Agrobacteria carrying the selected constructs were transformed into Col-0 plants using the foral-dip method (Clough and Bent [1998](#page-17-17)). Seeds of transformed plants were screened on GM plates supplemented with 20 µg mL−1 hygromycin or 10 µg mL−1 bialaphos (Bar) under continuous light at 22 °C as described by Yamaguchi et al. ([2008\)](#page-18-5).

#### **Dual luciferase transient expression assay**

Transient expression assays were performed according to the methods of Endo et al. [\(2015\)](#page-17-16). The efector, reporter, and reference plasmids were delivered into 14-day-old Arabidopsis plants by particle bombardment (Biolistic PDS-1000/ He Particle Delivery System, Bio-Rad). After bombardment, plants were incubated in darkness for 18 h at 22 °C. Then, leaves were excised to determine LUC activity using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) and the Tristar LB941 Multimode Microplate Reader (Berthold).

### **Localization analysis of VDOF proteins**

The subcellular localization of VDOF proteins was examined in 4-day-old seedlings (T2 generation) harboring *35S:VDOF1-YFP* or *35S:VDOF2-YFP*. Seedlings were fxed overnight at 4 °C in fxative solution [1.5% (v/v) formaldehyde and 0.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in PEMT bufer containing 50 mM PIPES, 2 mM EGTA, 2 mM MgSO4, 0.05% (v/v) Triton X-100, pH 7.2] (Sugimoto et al. [2000\)](#page-18-22). Then, the samples were washed with PEMT bufer at room temperature three times for 10 min each, followed by washing with PBS bufer three times for 10 min each at room temperature. The samples were stained with 1 mM DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) for 10 min at room temperature, and then washed with PBS bufer three times for 10 min each (Ishida et al. [2009\)](#page-17-18). The samples were mounted in PBS bufer before imaging. Samples were observed using an automated confocal laser-scanning microscope (FluoView FV10i-LIV, Olympus) equipped with flters for YFP (460–495 nm) and DAPI (410−585 nm). Images were processed and analyzed using ImageJ software (<https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/>).

The localization of VDOF proteins was also examined in 4-day-old seedlings (T2 generation) of *VDOF1pro:VDOF1- YFP* and *VDOF1pro:VDOF2-YFP* plants. The seedlings were washed briefy in distilled water, and the roots were cut and counterstained in 10 µg mL−1 propodium iodidesolution for 2 min. The samples were mounted on a glass slide with a few drops of water. YFP fuorescence was observed using an automated laser-scanning confocal microscope.

#### **GUS reporter assay**

The T2 or T3 generation of GUS reporter lines were aseptically grown on GM plates, and 1, 2, 3, and 7-day-old seedlings were sampled for the GUS reporter assay according to the method of Pyo et al. ([2007](#page-18-23)) with slight modifcations. Samples were fixed with pre-chilled 90% (v/v) acetone overnight at −30 °C, and then washed three times at room temperature with 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). Then, samples were incubated in substrate solution (1 mM 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl glucuronide, 0.5 mM potassium ferricyanide, 0.5 mM potassium ferrocyanide, and 10% Triton-X-100) for 2 to 4 h at 37 °C. Samples were washed with sodium phosphate bufer and then mounted on glass slides with a few drops of clearing solution (8 g chloral hydrate, 1 mL glycerol, and 2 mL distilled water). Inforescence stem samples were embedded in 7% (w/v) agar and sectioned into thin sections of 90−100 µm using the DTK-Zero1 microslicer (Ted Pella, Inc.). Samples were observed using a microscope equipped with Nomarski optics (BX51, Olympus), and images were photographed with a digital camera (DP70, Olympus). More than three independent reporter lines with fve technical replicates were examined.

#### **Histochemical observations of inforescence stems**

The inforescence stems of 40-day-old *VDOF1ox* and *VDO-F2ox* plants (homozygous T3 generation) and the *vdof1 vdof2* double mutant were cut into apical, middle, and basal samples, and fxed with freshly prepared fxative solution [ethanol:acetic acid, 9:1 (v/v)] overnight at 4 °C. Then, the fxed stems were dehydrated and rehydrated with an ethanol series [30, 50, 70, 95, and 100% ethanol (v/v)] and subjected to cross-sectioning. The sections were incubated in 20% (w/v) CaCl2 as described previously by Herr ([1992](#page-17-19)). Samples were viewed using a microscope equipped with Nomarski optics (BX51, Olympus), and images were photographed with a digital camera (DP70, Olympus).

#### **Observations of cotyledon vein patterns**

Seven-day-old and 14-day-old seedlings of *VDOF1ox* and *VDOF2ox* (homozygous T3 generation) and the *vdof1 vdof2* double mutant were fxed in pre-chilled 90% (v/v) acetone overnight at 4 °C. Samples were washed with distilled water and then mounted on glass slides with clearing solution [8:1:2 (w/v/v) mixture of chloral hydrate, glycerin, and water]. Samples were observed with a microscope equipped with Nomarski optics (SZX16, Olympus) and were photographed with a digital camera (DP72, Olympus). Vein networks were analyzed according to the method of Verna et al. [\(2015\)](#page-18-24).

#### **Chemical analysis**

The inforescence stems of 40-day-old *VDOF1ox* and *VDO-F2ox* (homozygous T3 generation) and *vdof1 vdof2* doublemutant plants were sampled for lignin analysis. Extractivefree cell wall residue (CWR) samples for chemical analyses were prepared as previously described (Bonawitz et al. [2014](#page-17-8)). In brief, the basal 10 cm of each stem was harvested, pre-ground, and sequentially extracted with distilled water and 80% aqueous ethanol. Thioglycolic acid lignin assay was performed as described previously (Suzuki et al. [2009\)](#page-18-25) except beech milled wood lignin was used as a calibration standard. Thioacidolysis was performed according to Yamamura et al. [\(2012](#page-18-26)) and the released S/G/H lignin monomers were derivatized with *N*,*O*-bis(trimethylsilyl)acetamide and quantifed by gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (GC/ MS) using 4,4´-ethylidenebisphenol as an internal standard (Yue et al. [2012](#page-18-27)).

### **2D NMR analysis**

For NMR analysis, CWRs were further ball-milled and digested with crude cellulases (Cellulysin, Calbiochem) as described previously (Tobimatsu et al. [2013](#page-18-28)) to aford ligninenriched CWRs. The lignin-enriched CWRs were subjected to solubilization and acetylation in dimethylsofoxide/*N*methylimidazole/acetic anhydride (2:1:1, v/v/v) and completely dissolved in 0.6 mL of chloroform-*d* for solutionstate NMR analysis (Lu and Ralph [2003;](#page-17-20) Wagner et al. [2011](#page-18-29); Tobimatsu et al. [2013](#page-18-28)). The 2D HSQC NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker Biospin Avance III 800US spectrometer ftted with a cryogenically cooled 5-mm TCI gradient probe. Adiabatic HSQC NMR experiments were carried out using standard implementation (hsqcetgpsp.3, Bruker Biospin) with parameters described in literature (Wagner et al. [2011](#page-18-29)), and data processing and analysis were as described previously (Tobimatsu et al. [2013](#page-18-28)). For quantifcation of lignin aromatic distributions by volume integration (Fig. [1](#page-5-0)), only the carbon/proton-2 correlations from G units (G2) and the carbon/proton-2/6 correlations from H and S units (S2/6 and H2/6) were used, and the G integrals were logically doubled. For lignin inter-unit linkage types (Fig. [1\)](#page-5-0), the wellresolved side-chain contours (Aα, Bα, Cα, Dα, and Eα) were integrated.

### **RNA‑seq analysis of inducible** *VDOF1* **and** *VDOF2* **overexpression lines**

Six-day-old seedlings of estrogen-inducible *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* overexpression lines were transferred into liquid MS medium with or without 10 µM of 17-β-estradiol for 24 h at 22 °C. A total of 15 seedlings from three independent lines of estrogen-inducible overexpressors of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* were treated with estrogen, washed briefy, and then collected for total RNA extraction using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). The quality of each total RNA sample was examined using an Agilent RNA 6000 Nano Kit and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Inc.), and samples that scored a high RNA integrity number (RIN≥8) were selected for library preparation. Library preparation and short-read sequencing were performed by BGI Japan (https://www.bgi. com/jp/); ca. 2 µg of total RNA samples were used for the preparation of non-directional, Illumina TruSeq-compatible libraries. The resultant libraries were sequenced using Illumina HiSeq™ 4000 (Illumina), to obtain raw read data (single read; 49-nucleotide) including 24~33 Million reads.

After the pretreatment of trimming adaptors and poorquality parts of reads by trimmomatic program v0.32 (Bolger et al. [2014\)](#page-17-21) and depleting rRNA-derived reads by Tagdust2 program v2.33 (Lassmann [2015\)](#page-17-22), the read data of each library was mapped independently to Arabidopsis genome (TAIR10) along with the gene annotation information (GFF fle from Ensembl Plant Release 29, as of October 2015). Tophat2 program (v2.1.1; Kim et al. [2013\)](#page-17-23) was employed to map, with settings as follows: "-p 8 --segmentlength 16 --segment-mismatches 1 --min-anchor-length 4 --max-multihits 20 -x 20 --report-secondary-alignments -i 30 -I 6000 --min-segment-intron 30 --max-segment-intron 6000 --min-coverage-intron 30 --transcriptome-index<precalculated-index-with-Genome-and-GFF>". For the normalized-expression value calculation, the cufdif program from Cufinks suite v2.2.1 (Trapnell et al. [2012\)](#page-18-30) was exploited with settings as follows: "-p 12 -c 1 -u -q --max-bundle-frags 2000000". As the relative expression level, the fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (FPKM) value were calculated by cufdif, and genes with a q-value of <0.05 were regarded as diferentially-expressed genes (DEGs).

# **Results**

# **Molecular characteristics of VDOF1 and VDOF2 proteins**

*Arabidopsis thaliana* (Arabidopsis) has 36 Dof transcription factors (Yanagisawa [2002\)](#page-18-32). Previous transcriptome data indicated that VDOF1/DOF4.6 and VDOF2/ DOF1.8 were upregulated during early stages of xylem vessel cell diferentiation (Kubo et al. [2005](#page-17-4); Ohtani et al. [2017](#page-18-4)). A Maximum-Likelihood phylogenetic tree based on Dof domains of Arabidopsis 36 Dof proteins (Fig. [1](#page-5-0)a) demonstrated that VDOF1 and VDOF2 proteins are relatively similar in amino acid sequences among Dof proteins, while the C-terminal regions are rather variable (Fig. [1](#page-5-0)b). Subcellular localization analysis of VDOF1 and VDOF2 by expressing the coding sequences of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* fused with the *YFP* coding sequence driven by the 35S promoter showed that VDOF1 and VDOF2 localize to the nucleus, since the YFP signals overlapped with the DAPI signals indicating nucleus in both the *VDOF1* and *VDOF2-YFP* transgenic seedlings (Supplementary Fig. S1). These observations supported molecular functions of VDOF1 and VDOF2 as transcription factors. To determine whether VDOF1 and VDOF2 have transcriptional activator activity or transcriptional repressor activity, we carried out transient expression assays with true leaves of 14-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings (Fig. [2\)](#page-6-0). The efector constructs, expressing the GAL4-binding domain (BD) fused with VDOF1 and VDOF2 driven by the CaMV 35S promoter, were delivered into Arabidopsis leaves with a reporter construct containing frefy luciferase (LUC) linked to the GAL4-binding site with or without CaMV 35S promoter (Yamaguchi et al. [2008;](#page-18-5) Fig. [3\)](#page-7-0). The results suggested that neither VDOF1 nor VDOF2 has a transcriptional activator domain (Fig. [2b](#page-6-0)), whereas VDOF2 would have a transcriptional repressor domain (Fig. [2c](#page-6-0)).

# **Efects of** *VDOF1* **and** *VDOF2* **overexpression and** *vdof1 vdof2* **mutations on vein patterning in cotyledons**

Several Dof genes are involved in vein formation in cotyledons and true leaves (Gardiner et al. [2010](#page-17-11); Konishi and Yanagisawa [2007](#page-17-12); Konishi et al. [2015\)](#page-17-13). Therefore, we examined the vein patterning in *VDOF1ox*, *VDOF2ox*, and *vdof1 vdof2* plants using 7- and 14-day-old cotyledons (Fig. [5\)](#page-9-0). In 7-day-old cotyledons of *VDOF1ox* and *VDOF2ox* plants, unconnected veins were often observed, while the secondary veins of *vdof1 vdof2* cotyledons tended to resemble closed loops (Fig. [5](#page-9-0)a). To quantitatively characterize vein formation in the cotyledons, we measured three descriptors from Verna et al. ([2015\)](#page-18-24) and calculate the cardinality, continuity, and connectivity of vein network indexes, which refect the number of veins in a network, the number of vein fragments (i.e., isolated veins that do not have contact with other veins) in a network, and how closed a vein network is, respectively (Verna et al. [2015](#page-18-24)). In 7-day-old cotyledons, the cardinality and connectivity indexes of *vdof1 vdof2* tended to be higher than those of the wild type, whereas the connectivity index was signifcantly reduced in *VDOF1ox* and *VDOF2ox* (Fig. [5c](#page-9-0)). These results suggest that VDOF1 and VDOF2 have negative efects on vein development (Fig. [5](#page-9-0)a, c). In the cotyledons of 14-day-old plants, only the cardinality index of *vdof1 vdof2* difered signifcantly from that of the wild type (Fig. [5b](#page-9-0), d), indicating that the reduced connectivity detected in the cotyledons of 7-day-old *VDOF1ox* and *VDOF2ox* plants was recovered in the cotyledons of 14-day-old plants, and that enhanced vein development continued to occur in *vdof1 vdof2* cotyledons. Taken together, these results suggest that VDOF1 and VDOF2 function as negative regulators of vein formation in seedlings.

# **Efect of** *VDOF1* **and** *VDOF2* **overexpression and** *vdof1 vdof2* **mutation on vascular cell development and secondary wall formation in fower stems**

Based on the expression patterns of VDOF1 and VDOF2 in vascular tissues of inforescence stems, the structures of inforescence stems in *VDOF1ox*, *VDOF2ox*, and *vdof1 vdof2* plants were examined, paying special attention to SCW formation, because SCW polymers such as lignin are considered to be a promising sustainable bioresource (Boerjan et al. [2003;](#page-17-6) Ohtani and Demura [2019](#page-18-11)). The inforescence stems of 40-day-old plants were separated into three regions (apical, middle and basal), and subjected to histological examination of the cell walls. Observations of cross-sections revealed no structural diferences among *VDOF1ox*, *VDOF2ox*, and *vdof1 vdof2* plants in any parts of the inforescence stem (Fig. [6](#page-12-0)). The phloroglucinol-HCl staining, which stains lignin red, revealed the presence of lignin in the apical regions of *vdof1 vdof2* stems, whereas no clear deposition of lignin was found in the apical regions of wild-type stems (Fig. [6a](#page-12-0), j). In the middle regions of the *VDOF1ox* and *VDOF2ox* stems, the lignin signal tended to be reduced in the interfascicular fbers compared to the wild type control (Fig. [6](#page-12-0)b, e, h), while no clear diference in lignin deposition was observed in the basal regions of the stems for any genotype examined (Fig. [6c](#page-12-0), f, i, l).

These histological observations prompted us to examine the lignin content and structure in the inforescence stems of the transgenic plants. To determine the total lignin content and chemical structure of lignin in the inforescence stems of transgenic plants, the extractive-free cell wall residues (CWRs) prepared from the lower halves of inforescence stems, which contained mainly the basal parts (Fig. [6](#page-12-0)c, e, i, and l), were subjected to the thioglycolic acid assay (Suzuki et al. [2009\)](#page-18-25), analytical thioacidolysis (Yamamura et al. [2012\)](#page-18-26) and 2D HSQC NMR analysis (Tobimatsu et al. [2013](#page-18-28)). The total lignin content determined by the thioglycolic acid assay was signifcantly higher in *vdof1 vdof2* stems than in wild-type stems (Fig. [6m](#page-12-0)). However, the lignin contents in *VDOF1ox* stems were tended to be increased whereas those in *VDOF2ox* stems were appeared to be conversely reduced compared to the wild-type stems (Fig. [6m](#page-12-0)). The earlier observation made by the histochemistry which suggested that lignin was reduced in the middle part of *VDOF1ox* inforescence stem (Fig. [6e](#page-12-0)), thus, it is possible that the *VDOF1* overexpression may afect lignin biosynthesis diferently by the developmental stages of the inforescence stems. Both analytical thioacidolysis and 2D NMR analysis determined no change in S/G/H lignin composition ratio among these plants (Fig. [7](#page-14-0)a; Supplementary Fig. S6). Our HSQC NMR footprint data further showed no noticeable diference in the distributions of the major lignin inter-monomeric linkage types among the wild type, *VDOF1ox*, *VDOF2ox*, and *vdof1 vdof2* cell wall samples (Fig. [7](#page-14-0)b). These lignin profle data collectively indicate that the *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* overexpression and *vdof1 vdof2* mutation afect primarily the lignin quantity, but not the lignin quality in the inforescence stem cell walls.

## **Discussion**

## **Roles for VDOF1 and VDOF2 in vascular development**

Dof transcription factors are unique to plant species, and more than half of the members of the Dof family are expressed in vascular tissues (Hir and Bellini [2013\)](#page-17-28). It has been proposed that all Arabidopsis Dof genes could be involved in vascular development, although many Dof genes have not been characterized to date. In the present paper, we show that *VDOF* genes are expressed in vascular regions throughout the plant's life cycle, from the early post-embryonic developmental stages, suggesting that VDOF proteins are always involved in vascular development (Figs. 3 and 4; Supplementary Fig. S2). This view was supported by the observations that overexpression and knockout mutations of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* afected cotyledon vein formation (Fig. [5](#page-9-0)) and lignin deposition in the inforescence stems (Fig. [6](#page-12-0)).

Overexpression of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* reduced vein connectivity, whereas vein cardinality was enhanced in the *vdof1 vdof2* double mutant (Fig. [5\)](#page-9-0). Therefore, *VDOF* genes could negatively regulate cotyledon vein formation. In the current model of vein formation, auxin is a frst cue of provascular cell diferentiation from undiferentiated leaf meristem cells (Rybel et al. [2016](#page-18-3)). Auxin signals are mediated by the transcription factor MP/ARF5 (Mattsson et al. 2003), leading to the expression of AtHB8, the key regulator of (pro)cambial cell establishment, in provascular cells. Previous studies have suggested that several Dof proteins, including VDOF1/Dof4.6, may function after MP/ARF5 mediated auxin signaling but before AtHB8 functions (i.e., during vascular cell development) (Gardiner et al. [2010](#page-17-11); Konishi and Yanagisawa [2015\)](#page-17-29). One of these Dof proteins is Dof5.8, which is a direct target of MP/ARF5 (Konishi et al. [2015\)](#page-17-13). Dof5.8 does not alter the expression of the genes that are preferentially expressed in provascular cells, such as AtHB8; instead, Dof5.8 infuences the expression of auxinassociated transcription factor genes (Konishi and Yanagisawa [2015](#page-17-29)). Thus, Dof5.8 is presumed to be a modulator of the auxin response for vein formation (Konishi and Yanagisawa [2015\)](#page-17-29). Similarly, we found that the expression levels of well-known vascular-related genes including *AtHB8* were not changed by overexpression of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* (Supplementary Table S6), suggesting that VDOF1 and VDOF2 are not directly involved in the MP/ARF5-AtHB8 pathway.

Instead, our transcriptomic data indicated that the inducible overexpression of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* commonly afected *BEE2*, encoding the transcription factor for brassinosteroid (BR) signaling (Friedrichsen et al. [2002](#page-17-30)), and *HBI1*, encoding a positive regulator of cell elongation downstream of BR (Bai et al. [2012,](#page-16-1) Fan et al. 2014). These genes were both downregulated by the overexpression of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* (Supplementary Table S3), suggesting the possibility of VDOF functions in the BR signaling pathways. BR is a crucial phytohormone regulating vascular cell diferentiation; previous studies of in vitro xylem induction (Yamamoto et al. 1997) and BR-related mutants (Clay and Nelson [2002;](#page-17-31) Caño-Delgado et al. [2004](#page-17-32)) indicate that BR is involved in xylem development (Kondo et al. [2014](#page-17-33); Nieminen et al. [2015](#page-18-38)). Public gene expression data show that VDOF1 and VDOF2 can be upregulated by BR (Arabidopsis eFP browser, Winter et al. [2007](#page-18-39)), therefore, VDOF1 and VDOF2 may function as modulators of the BR response during vascular development.

The *vdof1 vdof2* mutant did not show severe inhibition of seedling growth (Supplementary Fig. S4), and the negative efects of *VDOF* overexpression in seedling development were restored at later stages of development (Supplementary Fig. S5). These observations strongly suggested that other DOF proteins can function redundantly with VDOF1 and VDOF2 for vascular development. Further analysis of BRrelated events during vascular development in *VDOF1ox*, *VDOF2ox*, and *vdof1 vdof2*, with special attentions to the interaction with other DOF proteins, is expected to reveal mode of actions of VDOF1 and VDOF2 proteins.

#### **Roles for VDOF1 and VDOF2 in lignin biosynthesis**

In addition to the efects on vein formation, we also observed the altered lignin deposition in *VDOF1ox, VDOF2ox* and *vdof1 vdof2* (Fig. [6](#page-12-0)). No changes were detected in the structures of vascular tissues in the inforescence stem under normal growth condition; however, a reduction in lignin was detected in the middle region of the *VDOF1ox* and *VDO-F2ox* stems (Fig. [6](#page-12-0)e, h), and conversely, enhanced lignin deposition was observed in the *vdof1 vdof2* double mutant inforescence stems (Fig. [6](#page-12-0)j, m). These results suggested that VDOF1 and VDOF2 may work cooperatively as negative regulators of lignin deposition. The detailed analysis of amino acid sequence by SMART (Letunic and Bork [2018\)](#page-17-34) indicated that only VDOF2 has putative internal repeats, and that the number and location of putative low complexity regions are diferent between VDOF1 and VDOF2 (Supplementary Fig. S11), suggesting that the VDOF1 and VDOF2 proteins would have diferent molecular characteristics not only in transcription factor activity, but also in protein-protein interaction (Boija et al. [2018;](#page-17-35) Martin and Mittag [2018\)](#page-17-36). We observed the opposite phenotype on lignin accumulation in stems between *VDOF1ox* and *VDOF2ox*, i.e. the increased and decreased lignin contents in *VDOF1ox* and *VDOF2ox*, respectively (Fig. [6](#page-12-0)), and a much greater impact of *VDOF2* overexpression on transcriptome than that of *VDOF1* (Fig. [8](#page-15-0)). These results can be explained by the molecular diferences between VDOF1 and VDOF2 as transcription factors, especially derived from the diference of C-terminal regions.

Notably, however, the overexpression of *VDOF1* and *VDOF2* can afect the expression of lignin-related genes positively as well as negatively (Fig. [8](#page-15-0); Supplementary Table S2, S3), although VDOF1 and VDOF2 themselves do not possess any transcriptional transactivation domains (Fig. [2\)](#page-6-0). Moreover, *CAD5*, *MYB63*, and *LAC12* genes associated with lignin biosynthesis were upregulated in both the overexpressor and mutant of *VDOF* genes (Fig. [8c](#page-15-0)). Such discrepancy between the *VDOF* overexpressors and mutants made it difcult to generate a simple conclusion for VDOF functions in lignin biosynthesis; we have to consider additional layers regulating lignin deposition mediated by VDOF proteins. One of possible schemes for VDOF-mediated regulation of lignin biosynthesis would be the interactions among multiple Dof proteins and/or other classes of transcription factors, as previously proposed for Dof4.2 (Skirycz et al. 2014) and PEAR proteins (Miyashima et al. [2019\)](#page-18-19). Based on the results that Dof4.2 could alter the expression levels of genes for phenylpropanoid metabolism in environmental and tissue-specifc manner, Skirycz et al. (2014) proposed the stress- and/or tissue-specifc unknown factors to modulate the activity of Dof4.2 at the protein level. Miyashima et al. ([2019\)](#page-18-19) clearly demonstrated the high redundancy among 6 Dof functions for (pro)cambial activity. A feedback system between Dof proteins and HD-ZIP III proteins were proposed for auxin- and cytokinin-mediated regulation of radial growth of roots (Miyashima et al. [2019](#page-18-19); Smetana et al. [2019\)](#page-18-20). Similarly, VDOF-mediated regulation of lignin biosynthesis may involve interaction(s) with other transcription factors; no drastic changes for known vascularrelated genes as well as Dof genes in *VDOF* overexpressors (Supplementary Table S7) suggests such interaction(s) can be at the protein levels. We found that VDOF proteins contains multiple but diferential low complexity regions, which are thought to contribute to phase separation to form biomolecular condensates (Martin and Mittag [2018](#page-17-36)). Future study may test this aspect through protein-protein interaction analyses of VDOF proteins.

## **Conclusions**

In summary, our data indicate that VDOF1 and VDOF2 are novel regulators of vascular cell diferentiation through the course of a lifetime. VDOF1 and VDOF2 negatively regulate vein formation in cotyledons and lignin deposition in inforescence stems, possibly by modulating BR signaling and transcriptional alteration of lignin-related genes. Future molecular analysis on VDOF proteins, especially focusing on the interaction with other Dof proteins as well as BRrelated transcription factors would provide novel clues into the regulatory system of vascular development in plants.
