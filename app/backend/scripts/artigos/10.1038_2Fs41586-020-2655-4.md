## Quinone perception in plants via leucine-rich-repeat receptor-like kinases

Anuphon Laohavisit<sup>1,4</sup>, Takanori Wakatake<sup>1,2,4</sup>, Nobuaki Ishihama<sup>1</sup>, Hugh Mulvey<sup>1</sup>, Kaori Takizawa<sup>1</sup>, Takamasa Suzuki<sup>3</sup> & Ken Shirasu<sup>1,2</sup>

Ouinones are produced and sensed in all kingdoms of life<sup>1-4</sup>. Plants are primary producers of quinone<sup>1,2</sup>, but the role of quinone as a signalling agent in plants remains largely unknown. One well-documented role of quinone is in the induction of haustoria (specialized feeding structures) in plants that parasitize roots, which occurs in the presence of the host-derived quinone compound 2,6-dimethoxy-1,4-benzoquinone (DMBQ)<sup>5</sup>. However, how parasitic plants sense DMBQ remains unclear, as is whether nonparasitic plants are capable of sensing quinones. Here we use Arabidopsis thaliana and DMBQ as a model plant and quinone to show that DMBQ signalling occurs in Arabidopsis via elevation of cytosolic Ca<sup>2+</sup> concentration. We performed a forward genetic screen in Arabidopsis that isolated DMBQ-unresponsive mutants, which we named cannot respond to DMBQ1(card1). The CANNOT RESPOND TO DMBQ1(CARD1; At5g49760, also known as HPCA1) gene encodes a leucine-rich-repeat receptor-like kinase that is highly conserved in land plants. In Arabidopsis, DMBQ triggers defence-related gene expression, and *card1* mutants show impaired immunity against bacterial pathogens. In *Phtheirospermum japonicum* (a plant that parasitizes roots), DMBQ initiates Ca<sup>2+</sup> signalling in the root and is important for the development of the haustorium. Furthermore, CARD1 homologues from this parasitic plant complement DMBQ-induced elevation of cytosolic Ca<sup>2+</sup> concentration in the card1 mutant. Our results demonstrate that plants-unlike animals and bacteria-use leucine-rich-repeat receptor-like kinases for quinone signalling. This work provides insights into the role of quinone signalling and CARD1 functions in plants that help us to better understand the signalling pathways used during the formation of the haustorium in parasitic plants and in plant immunity in nonparasitic plants.

Given their sessile lifestyle, plants sense and respond to multiple stimuli with various signalling components. Although quinones are produced across all kingdoms of life<sup>1,2</sup> (with plants as the primary producers), they are often neglected in plant signalling research. Quinone sensing is well-studied in bacteria and animals<sup>3,4</sup>, but no equivalent mechanism has yet been identified in plants. In animals and bacteria, quinone sensing often involves cysteine modifications of sensor proteins that influence cytoprotective gene expression<sup>3,4</sup>. In general, plants do not possess homologues of known quinone receptors<sup>6</sup> and thus are likely to use sensing strategies different to those of animals and bacteria.

Orobanchaceae plants that parasitize roots—including *Striga* species that cause considerable losses in agricultural yield worldwide<sup>5,7</sup>— develop specialized feeding organs known as haustoria in the presence of the host-derived quinone compound DMBQ (Fig. 1a). Although DMBQ is a potent haustorium-inducing factor<sup>8</sup> in various members of the Orobanchaceae, the molecular events that underlie perception and that precede the formation of the haustorium are unclear; the identification of DMBQ signalling components in parasitic plants is challenging, as key research tools (such as transgenerational genetic

transformations) are unavailable<sup>3,4</sup>. Understanding plant quinone sensing and the molecular events that follow DMBQ perception would provide a better understanding of plant signalling in general, and offer a molecular target to combat parasitic plants.

To identify components of plant perception of DMBQ, we used *A. thaliana*, owing to its ease of manipulation. First, we clarified whether *Arabidopsis*–a nonparasitic plant–can sense DMBQ by measuring hallmarks of a signalling event, including increases in cytosolic calcium concentrations ( $[Ca^{2+}]_{cyt}$ ) and the activation of mitogenactivated protein kinases. We observed DMBQ-induced  $[Ca^{2+}]_{cyt}$  elevation (DICE) in *Arabidopsis* plants that express aequorin, a  $Ca^{2+}$  indicator based on bioluminescence (hereafter,  $WT^{AEQ}$  plants) (Fig. 1b). Imaging *Arabidopsis* plants that express R-GECO1 (a  $Ca^{2+}$  sensor based on fluorescence intensity)<sup>9</sup> revealed that the spatiotemporal patterns in the  $[Ca^{2+}]_{cyt}$  elevations induced by DMBQ and flg22 differ (Fig. 1c, Extended Data Fig. 1a–c, Supplementary Videos 1–3). DMBQ induced a rapid and transient (2–4 min) activation of mitogen-activated protein kinases (Fig. 1d), but not apoplastic production of reactive oxygen species (Extended Data Fig. 1d). CARD1 is highly conserved in land plants, but not in chlorophytes (Extended Data Fig. 5a, b). CARD1 protein alignments revealed a unique cysteine region (C395, C405, C421, C424, C434 and C436 (based on CARD1 numbering in Arabidopsis)) at the C-terminal end of the ectodomain that is specific to subfamily VIII-1 of the LRR-RLKs (Extended Data Fig. 5c, d). As quinone perception in animals and bacteria acts through the cysteine modification of sensor proteins<sup>3,4</sup>, we generated CARD1 single-cysteine mutants and attempted complementation of DICE in card1-2 plants using each variant. Despite detectable CARD1 in plasma-membrane-enriched fractions, CARD1(C395S) and CARD1(C405S) did not complement DICE in card1-2 plants (Fig. 2a, b, Extended Data Fig. 2i). Thus, C395 and C405-which were not isolated in the genetic screen (potentially owing to screening limitations)-may have an important role in the CARD1-dependent perception of DMBQ, possibly by contributing to protein structural integrity through disulfide bond formation or by reacting with quinone  $\alpha$ ,  $\beta$ -unsaturated carbonyl groups to form stable quinone adducts via Michael-type addition<sup>6</sup>. To

address this possibility, we used tetrafluoro-1,4-benzoquinone (TFBQ) (Fig. 2c), a stronger Michael acceptor than DMBQ that reacts more readily with cysteine thiol groups (because of its electron-withdrawing fluorine) and has previously been shown to inhibit the DMBQ-induced formation of the haustorium in parasitic plants<sup>13</sup>. Although TFBQ did not induce Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx, cotreatment with DMBQ and TFBQ inhibited DICE in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2d, e). TFBQ may competitively target the same CARD1 sites as DMBQ, or may bind to putative allosteric sites in CARD1 and thus induce conformational changes in its active site. Alternatively, TFBQ may target other proteins that interact with CARD1 and that are required for CARD1-dependent quinone signalling.

To shed light on the downstream events of CARD1-dependent signalling, we confirmed the absence of the activation of mitogen-activated protein kinases in card1 plants (Fig. 3a), and performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on WTAEQ, card1-1 and card1-2 plants 10 min after treatment with 5 µM DMBQ (Fig. 3b). We observed 35 genes (listed in Supplementary Data 1) that are differentially expressed in WTAEQ plants, but not in the two *card1* mutants ( $\log_2$ -transformed fold change  $\geq 1$ , false-discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05) and confirmed differential expression of a subset of these genes by quantitative (q)PCR (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Analysis of Gene Ontology terms revealed that these CARD1-dependent genes are involved in responses to wounding and stress (Fig. 3c). To investigate the longer-term effects of DMBQ, we performed a separate RNA-seq experiment between WT<sup>AEQ</sup> plants and the card1-2 mutant at 30 min, 1 h and 6 h after treatment and identified 61, 60 and 28 genes ( $\log_2$ -transformed fold change  $\geq 1$ , FDR < 0.05), respectively, that were induced only in WTAEQ plants. These genes were enriched for Gene Ontology terms for responses to fungi, stress and defence (Extended Data Fig. 6b, Supplementary Data 1).

Given these results, we considered whether CARD1-dependent quinone signalling is important for plant immunity. We challenged WT<sup>AEQ</sup> plants and card1 mutants with Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 or a less-virulent strain that lacks coronatine, a modulator of plant immune signalling. In both cases, card1 mutants were more susceptible than WT<sup>AEQ</sup> plants to pathogen infection (Fig. 3d). Previous reports have suggested that guinol-like molecules can induce stomata closure<sup>14</sup>, and can be rapidly oxidized to guinones in highly oxidized apoplastic environments<sup>15</sup>. These responses may be induced by DMBQ, influencing stomatal immunity<sup>16</sup>. DMBQ notably induced stomata closure in card1-2/pCARD1::CARD1-but not card1-2-plants (Fig. 3e), consistent with higher leaf surface temperatures in card1-2/ pCARD1::CARD1 plants treated with DMBQ (Extended Data Fig. 6c). We tested whether treatment with DMBQ primes plant immunity, but found no difference between mock-treated plants and plants pretreated with DMBQ when challenged with Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 (Extended Data Fig. 6d). As bacteria-derived effectors and immune modulators (such as coronatine) may overcome the effects of DMBQ<sup>16</sup>, we challenged plants that were pretreated with DMBQ with the Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 strain that lacks coronatine, and observed CARD1-dependent induction of resistance (Fig. 3f). Overall, CARD1 contributes to immunity by the induction of defence- and/or stress-related genes, and by influencing stomatal immunity in response to quinone-related compounds during bacterial infection.

Immune signalling through LRR-RLKs (such as FLS2) often involves BRASSINOSTEROID INSENSITIVE 1 ASSOCIATED RECEPTOR KINASE 1 (BAK1). To see whether BAK1 is involved in DMBQ signalling, we compared DMBQ responses in plants of WT<sup>AEQ</sup> and *bak1-5*, which is a mutant allele specifically impaired in immunity responses and not in the brassinosteroid-related functions of BAK1<sup>17</sup>. DMBQ treatment of *bak1-5* plants resulted in a reduced activation of mitogen-activated protein kinases (Fig. 3g) and reduced expression of DMBQ marker genes (selected from a qPCR-validated list) (Fig. 3c, h, Extended Data Fig. 6a). This suggests possible crosstalk between CARD1 and pattern-triggered immunity signalling.

As CARD1 signalling in *Arabidopsis* influences plant immunity, quinone-related molecules could function as a pathogen- or a danger-associated molecular pattern. Microorganisms often produce diverse quinone compounds in large amounts<sup>1</sup>, which may explain why CARD1 responds to a variety of quinone compounds (Extended Data Fig. 3). Some fungi produce quinone compounds (for example, 2,5-DMBQ), along with laccases and peroxidases, to stimulate biodegradation<sup>20–22</sup>. Quinone detection may therefore represent a warning system against invading pathogens. However, 2,6-DMBQ was also isolated from extracts from sorghum roots, and is widespread in different plant families<sup>23</sup>; plasma-membrane-localized phylloquinones<sup>24</sup> could be released upon cell breakdown. These compounds, or their metabolites, could influence CARD1-dependent quinone signalling in neighbouring cells and function as danger-associated molecular patterns. In summary, we showed that CARD1 and homologous CADL proteins are required for quinone signalling in plants. We have revealed the role of CARD1 in immunity in a nonparasitic plant, and further characterized quinone signalling in the formation of the haustorium in parasitic plants. It will be interesting to delineate the underlying molecular differences between parasitic and nonparasitic plants. Furthermore, quinones are implicated in nonparasitic interactions between plants, such as rhizosphere allelopathic interactions (for example, with black-walnut-derived juglone)<sup>2,25</sup>. CARD1 was recently shown to sense external reactive oxygen species<sup>26</sup>: more work is needed to determine the relationship between CARD1, quinones and reactive oxygen species. This study paves the way to further elucidate quinone signalling and CARD1 functions in plants, which can be further used to explore the signalling pathways that underlie plant–microorganism and plant–plant interactions.

#### **Methods**

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. The experiments were not randomized and investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.

#### **Chemicals and reagents**

All chemicals were purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical unless otherwise specified. A list of chemicals that were bought from other suppliers is given in Supplementary Table 1. The flg22 peptide (QRLSTGSRINSAKDDAAGLQIA, 95.3% purity) was custom-synthesized by Medical & Biological Laboratory, and dissolved in endotoxin-free water.

#### **Plant materials**

All *A. thaliana* lines are in the Col-0 background. Seeds of *card1-12* (SALK\_118906) and *card1-13* (SALK\_063284) plants were obtained from the *Arabidopsis* stock centre (NASC) and were confirmed for homozygosity using allele-specific primers (Supplementary Table 2). Transgenic *Arabidopsis* lines expressing (apo)aequorin[27](#page-9-0) and R-GECO1[9](#page-5-8) were previously described, and were given by J. M. Davies and M. Krebs, respectively. *S*eeds were surface-sterilized and stratified for 2 days before growing in liquid medium (1/2 MS, 0.25% sucrose, pH 5.7) at 20–22 °C, 16-h photoperiod, 80–100 μE. Soil-grown *Arabidopsis* plants were grown individually in a pot (1:1 mixture of vermiculite and soil) under controlled conditions (20– 22 °C, 8-h photoperiod, 80–100 μE). Experiments using liquid-grown plants were performed 1 week post-germination and soil-grown plants were used 4–5 weeks post-germination, unless otherwise stated. *Phtheirospermum japonicum* (Thunb.) Kanitz seeds were handled as previously described[18](#page-5-17),[19.](#page-5-18) Soil-grown *Nicotiana benthamiana* plants were grown individually in a pot (1:1 mixture of vermiculite and soil) under controlled conditions (25 °C, 16-h photoperiod, 100–150 μE).

#### **Whole-seedling aequorin luminometry**

Liquid-grown *Arabidopsis* seedlings were individually placed in wells of white 96-well plates (Lumitrac Flat bottom white; Greiner Bio-One) containing 100 μl of 10 μM coelenterazine (Biosynth) and aequorin, and then allowed to reconstitute overnight. The next day, samples were transferred to a Tristar[2](#page-5-2) LB942 modular multimode microplate reader (Berthold Technology). After baseline luminescence values were recorded for 1 min, 100 μl of stimuli was injected (2 times the final concentration). Luminescence values were recorded every 5 s for 1,000 s. Discharge solutions (10% (v/v) ethanol, 1 M CaCl2) were injected and the luminescence was recorded for a further 70 s. Calibration to convert luminescence values to [Ca2+]cyt was performed using the following previously published equation[28](#page-9-1), assuming 25-°C experimental conditions: pCa = 0.3326(−log(*k*)) + 5.5593, in which *k* is a rate constant at each time point (*k* = luminescence count per s/total luminescence counts).

For inhibitor experiments, inhibitor solutions were added to samples to give the desired final concentration and incubated for 30 min. Total volumes in sample wells were adjusted to 100 μl before stimuli treatment and sample measurement.

#### **Reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurement**

Liquid-grown *Arabidopsis* seedlings were individually placed in wells of white 96-well plates (Lumitrac Flat bottom white; Greiner Bio-One) containing 100 μl of 100 μM L-012. Seedlings were washed once with sterile water before addition of DMBQ (5 μM final concentration). Luminescence values were recorded every 1 min for 30 min with a Tristar[2](#page-5-2) LB942 modular multimode microplate reader. Flg22 (1 μM final concentration)-induced ROS bursts were performed as positive controls.

#### **Mutagenesis**

*Arabidopsis* seeds (approximately 12,500 seeds) expressing aequorin were soaked briefly in 0.05% (v/v) Triton X-100 solution and washed briefly 3 times with water. The seeds were then mutagenized in 0.2% (v/v) ethylmethanesulfonate solution for 16 h and washed with water using the following regime: 3 × 5 min; 3 × 30 min; 1 × 60 min. Mutagenized seeds were resuspended in 0.1% (w/v) agar and were sown directly onto soil. The chlorotic sector in the M1 population was 0.57% and plants were pooled into 108 different pools. Seeds were collected from each pool and used for subsequent screening.

#### **Forward genetic Ca2+ screen**

Approximately 54,000 M2 lines were screened (500 lines per pool) for loss of DMBQ-induced Ca2+ increases. Individual seeds were sown directly into 96-well white plates containing 1/2 MS, 0.25% (w/v) sucrose, pH 5.7. Plates were stratified for 2 d and incubated for 7 d under long-day conditions (16 h:8 h, light:dark, 22 °C, 50 μE). Before Ca2+ screening, the solution in the plates was replaced with 10 μM coelenterazine and the plates were incubated overnight in the dark at room temperature. On the following day, plants were screened for DMBQ-induced Ca2+ increases using a Tristar[2](#page-5-2) LB942 modular multimode microplate reader. Putative mutants were isolated and their phenotypes were confirmed in the M3 generation. Mutants were grouped according to Ca2+ phenotype in response to DMBQ. These mutants were backcrossed to the parental aequorin lines to test for dominant or recessive traits, as well as to create mapping populations for next-generation sequencing. Allelism tests were performed in groups of mutants that showed the same Ca2+ phenotype.

#### **Mutant mapping by sequencing**

Genomic DNA (gDNA) of *card1-1* and *card1-2* plants was extracted from approximately 100 seedlings of the BC1F2 homozygous population with Qiagen DNeasy Plant Mini kits (Qiagen). For DNA library preparation, gDNA was sheared to the size of around 450–500 bp using Covaris S2 system (Covaris) and DNA libraries were prepared using the Ovation Rapid DR Multiplex system (NuGEN Technology) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA sequencing was performed with NextSeq500 (Illumina) and casual mutations of *card1-1* and *card1-2* plants were identified as previously described[29](#page-9-2). Other alleles of *card1* identified by allelism tests—were Sanger-sequenced to confirm the presence of mutations in the *CARD1* gene.

#### **In vivo Ca2+ imaging with R-GECO1**

Confocal microscopy and subsequent image analysis of *A. thaliana* roots were performed as previously describe[d9](#page-5-8) . Plants were grown on 1/2 MS plates (1% sucrose and 0.8% agar) under long-day conditions (20–22 °C, 16-h photoperiod, 80–100 μE). Five-day-old seedlings were transferred to glass-bottom dishes (Iwaki) and incubated under a thin layer of 1/2 MS agar. Seedlings were returned to a growth chamber (20–22 °C, 16-h photoperiod, 80–100 μE) overnight. *P. japonicum* with R-GECO1-transformed hairy roots were transferred to 0.7% agar and were grown for 2 d under long-day conditions (24–25 °C, 16-h photoperiod, 80–100 μE). Freshly elongated hairy roots were transferred to glass-bottom dishes (Iwaki) and placed under a thin layer of 0.7% agar. Hairy roots were returned to a growth chamber (24–25 °C, 16-h photoperiod, 80–100 μE) overnight. The following day, plants in glass-bottom dishes were imaged using an inverted confocal microscope SP5 (Leica). R-GECO1 was excited at 543 nm and emission spectra were collected at 570–640 nm with the pinhole set to 5 airy units. Images were taken for 11 min at intervals of 2 s. Stimuli in 1/2 MS solution were added directly to the thin agar layer 1 min after starting measurements. Acquired images were processed using ImageJ software ([https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/\)](https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). Background noise was calculated as the mean intensity of the signal within a manually defined rectangular box (450 × 900 μm) outside of the root

tissue. This value was then subtracted from all images. After background correction, intensity values of R-GECO1 were normalized as (*F* − *F*0)/*F*0, as previously described[9](#page-5-8) , in which *F*0 indicates the mean of fluorescence intensity before stimuli were added from the first 30 images.

#### **Anti-CARD1 antibody production**

A peptide from the CARD1 C terminus (QYSGNFPASKLEPQ) with an N-terminal cysteine was chemically synthesized (Scrum). The antigen peptide was conjugated to a keyhole limpet haemocyanin carrier via SS-linkage through the N-terminal cysteine, and polyclonal antisera were raised in rabbits (Scrum). CARD1 antibodies were purified from the antisera by affinity chromatography using antigen-immobilized beads (HiTrap NHS-activated HP; GE Healthcare).

#### **Gene cloning, plasmid construction and transformation**

All primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 2. To create a complemented line, native *CARD1* promoter (2.7 kb 5′ regulatory sequences of *CARD1*; *pCARD1*) and full-length gDNA sequence of *CARD1* (*At5g49760*; start to stop codon) were cloned as separate fragments using KOD plus neo (Toyobo). The binary vector pGWB[430](#page-9-3) was linearized with Bsp1407I (Takara), and combined with the cloned promoter and gene fragments to yield *pCARD1::CARD1* using In-Fusion HD cloning kit (Takara) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

For site-directed mutagenesis, PCR primers were designed with 15 bp overlapping sequence at their 5′ ends and cysteine-coding codons were replaced with serine-coding codons. PCR reactions were performed with full-length gDNA *CARD1* sequence to yield mutagenized *CARD1* gDNA fragments using KOD plus neo. These fragments, together with the *pCARD1* promoter fragment and Bsp1407I-linearized pGWB4 binary vector, were combined using In-Fusion HD cloning. All sequences were verified by Sanger sequencing.

For the cross-species complementation experiment, full-length gDNA sequences of Pj*CADL1*, Pj*CADL2* and Pj*CADL3*, and Sa*CADL1*, Sa*CADL2* and Sa*CADL3* (start to stop codon; see Supplementary Table 3 for accession identifiers) were cloned from *P. japonicum* gDNA (extracted from 20 × 10-day-old seedlings using Qiagen DNeasy Plant Mini kits) and *Striga asiatica* gDNA (provided by T. Spallek), respectively, using KOD plus neo. These fragments were individually combined with the promoter fragment (*pCARD1*) and the Bsp1407I-linearized pGWB4 to yield *pCARD1::PjCADL1*, *pCARD1::PjCADL2*, *pCARD1::PjCADL3*, *pCARD1::SaCADL1*, *pCARD1::SaCADL2* and *pCARD1::SaCADL3* using In-Fusion HD cloning kit.

Plasmids were electroporated into *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* AGL1 strains. *Arabidopsis* plants were transformed with the constructs by floral dipping and T3 homozygote plants were selected under hygromycin.

To create R-GECO1 construct for *P. japonicum* experiments, we used the Golden Gate system[31.](#page-9-4) R-GECO1 was PCR-amplified from a previously described R-GECO1 vector[9](#page-5-8) (a gift from M. Kreb) and inserted into the CDS module (pICH41308). Two synonymous point mutations were introduced to R-GECO1 sequence to remove restriction sites during this step. The *Lotus japonicus* ubiquitin promoter (Lj*UBQ1*) was PCR-amplified from the pUB-GW-GFP vector (a gift from M. Hayashi) and inserted into the promoter and 5′ untranslated region module (pICH41295[\)32](#page-9-5). These two plasmids and the terminator module (*Arabidopsis HSP18.2* terminator sequence)[18](#page-5-17),[19](#page-5-18) were assembled into a level-1 module (pICH47802), yielding *pLjUBQ1::R-GECO1*, and further subcloned into a level-2 vector (pAGM4723) to change the antibiotic resistance for *Agrobacterium* selection.

For expression analysis, promoter sequences (1.9–2.4 kbp) of the *Pj*CADL genes were PCR-amplified from *P. japonicum* genomic DNA. The Pj*HB8* promoter sequence in *PjHB8::3×Venus-SYP* plasmid[19](#page-5-18) was replaced with these PCR products by Gibson Assembly[33](#page-9-6). Accession numbers for these plasmid sequences are given in Supplementary Table 3.

The hairy-root transformation method was previously describe[d18,](#page-5-17)[19.](#page-5-18) In brief, 6-day-old *P. japonicum* seedlings were submerged under *Agrobacterium rhizogenes* AR1193 suspension. Plants were sonicated for 10 s and vacuum infiltration for 5 min. This was followed by co-incubation on Gamborg's B5 plate, (1 × Gamborg's B5 salts, 1% sucrose, 0.8% agar and 450 μM acetosyringone, for 2 d (22 °C in the dark). Plants were then transferred to selection plates (1×x Gamborg's B5 salts, 1% sucrose, 0.8% agar and 300 μg/ml cefotaxime (Tokyo Chemical Industry)) and incubated at 24–25 °C, 16-h photoperiod, 80–100 μE.

#### **MAPK activation and CARD1 immunoblot**

Seven-day-old *Arabidopsis* seedlings or 10-day-old *P. japonicum* seedlings were treated with 5 μM DMBQ or DMSO control (mock). Samples were collected and snap-frozen in liquid N2 immediately after treatment for the indicated times and stored at −80 °C. Samples were ground to a fine powder with zirconia beads (3-mm diameter) with a FastPrep-24 sample shaker (M.P. Biomedicals). Proteins were extracted in extraction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 2 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT, 1 × EDTA-free complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche), 0.1% IGEPAL CA-630, 0.5 mM phenylmethane sulfonyl fluoride), supplemented with phosphatase inhibitors (1 mM Na2MoO4, 1 mM NaF, 0.5 mM Na3VO4, 20 mM β-glycerophosphate). Samples were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min and insoluble debris was pelleted by centrifugation (20,000*g*). Protein concentrations in the supernatant were determined using Bradford Reagent (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Equal amounts of proteins were loaded onto an acrylamide gel and subjected to SDS–PAGE. Proteins were then electroblotted onto a PVDF membrane using a trans-blot turbo transfer system (Bio-Rad Laboratories) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Membranes were blocked overnight at 4 °C in TBS-T (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20, pH 7.5) containing 5% (w/v) skimmed milk powder. To detect phosphorylated MAP kinases, we incubated blots with an anti-phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2 ; Thr202/Tyr204; D13.14.4E) rabbit monoclonal antibody (1:2,000; Cell Signaling Technology; product number 4370) for 1 h at room temperature with shaking. To detect CARD1, we incubated the blot with an anti-CARD1 peptide antibody as described in 'Anti-CARD1 antibody production' (1:5,000) for 1 h at room temperature with shaking. Blots were then further incubated for 1 h with peroxidase-linked anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000; NA934; GE Healthcare). Antibody-bound proteins were detected using SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions with a LAS 4000 system (GE Healthcare). After protein detection, membranes were stained with Coomassie blue solution (0.1% (w/v) CBB R250 in 5:4:1 (v:v:v) mixture of H2O, methanol, acetic acid) to verify equal loading.

#### **CARD1 cellular localization**

*Arabidopsis* seedlings of different mutant alleles were grown for 7 days in liquid medium before being collected (100 mg). Samples were snap-frozen in liquid N2 and were stored at −80 °C until required. Samples were processed using Minute Plant Plasma Membrane Protein Isolation Kit (Invent Biotechnologies) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Each sample was separated into three fractions (plasma membrane, organelle membrane and soluble). The presence of CARD1 protein was detected with CARD1 antibody as described in 'Anti-CARD1 antibody production'. Anti-H+ -ATPase 1 (anti-AHA1; AS07 260, Agrisera), anti-sterol methyltransferase 1 (anti-SMT1; AS07 266, Agrisera) and anti-RUBISCO large subunit (anti-RbcL; AS03 037, Agrisera) were used as control for plasma membrane, organelle membrane and soluble fractions, respectively. Blots were incubated with anti-AHA1 (1:5,000), anti-SMT1 (1:5,000) or anti-rbcL (1:20,000) for 1 h at room temperature with shaking and were further incubated for 1 h with peroxidase-linked anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000). Antibody-bound proteins were detected using SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate for AHA1 and SMT1 or Clarity Max Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad Laboratories) for RbcL, according to the manufacturer's instructions, with a LAS 4000 system.

#### **CARD1 data mining and phylogenetic analysis**

Sequences of CARD1 homologues from other plant species were mined from Phytozome (database version 12.1). Full-length CARD1 amino acid sequence was used as a query for blastP against the database with the cut-off *E*-value of 1 × 10−100. Hit sequences were filtered further by selecting sequences with ≥ 80% query coverage. Amino acid sequence alignments were performed on full-length sequences using MUSCLE (default parameters)[34](#page-9-7). A phylogenetic tree was inferred with RAxML[35,](#page-9-8) using the PROTGAMMAAUTO model, and rapid bootstrapping with the autoMRE bootstopping, using a random number seeds for a full maximum-likelihood tree search. The resulting estimated phylogeny was viewed and edited using iTOL[36.](#page-9-9) The tree was rooted to *Marchantia polymorpha* 0097s0050.

#### **Transcriptome analysis**

For the first RNA-seq experiment, liquid-grown *Arabidopsis* seedlings (WTAEQ, *card1-1* and *card1-2*) were treated with 5 μM DMBQ for 10 min. Samples were prepared in four biological replicates. Full-transcript breath adapter directional sequencin[g37](#page-9-10) libraries were prepared as previously described, and sequenced using 50-bp single-end reads on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform by the Vincent J. Coates Genomics Sequencing Laboratory. Quality filtering was performed using the Fastx toolkit [\(http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx\\_toolkit/\)](http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/). Low-quality nucleotides (quality score <30) were removed from the 3′end. Short reads (<40 bp) and reads that showed at least 95% nucleotides with a low-quality score (quality score <20) were removed. Filtered reads were mapped onto the *Arabidopsis* genome (TAIR10) with Tophat2 v2.1.1[38](#page-9-11) (<http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/tophat>) with the following settings: -i 20 -I 5000. Mapped reads were counted using HTseq v.0.6.[139](#page-9-12) ([https://](https://htseq.readthedocs.io/) [htseq.readthedocs.io/\)](https://htseq.readthedocs.io/) with default settings. Downstream analysis was performed using the EdgeR package (3.24.3[\)40](#page-10-0). Only genes with at least 10 counts per million in at least 4 samples were considered to be expressed and analysed further. Trimmed mean of *M* values normalization was applied to filtered read counts. Gene-wise dispersion was estimated over all expressed genes. Quasi-likelihood dispersions were estimated with the glmQLFit function. Differentially expressed genes (FDR ≤ 0.05, log-transformed fold change ≥ 2) were calculated using the glmQLFtest and glmTreat functions in edgeR[41](#page-10-1).

For the second RNA-seq experiment, liquid-grown *Arabidopsis* seedlings (WTAEQ and *card1-2*) were treated with 5 μM DMBQ for 30 min, 1 h or 6 h. Samples were prepared in three biological replicates. Total RNA was extracted using Qiagen RNeasy Plant mini kit (Qiagen) with DNaseI-digestion. Libraries were then prepared using KAPA mRNA HyperPrep Kit (Roche) according to the manufacturer's instructions, except for the adaptor ligation steps, for which we used NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (NEB), to produce a library size of approximately 400–500 bp. Libraries were sequenced using NextSeq500/550 High Output v2 kit (Illumina) with a NextSeq500 (Illumina). Quality filtering was performed as described here. Filtered reads were mapped onto the *Arabidopsis* genome (TAIR10) with HISAT v.2.1.[042](#page-10-2) [\(https://](https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml) [ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml](https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml)) with a following setting: –max-intronlen 5000. Mapped read count and downstream analyses were performed as described here. Differentially expressed genes (FDR ≤ 0.05, log-transformed fold change ≥ 2) were calculated using the glmQLFtest function in edgeR. All RNA-seq data are deposited in NCBI's Gene Expression Omnibus[43](#page-10-3), and are accessible through GEO Series accession number GSE128510.

#### **qPCR analysis**

Total RNA samples were obtained from plant materials using Qiagen RNeasy Plant Mini kit (Qiagen) with DNaseI digestion. RNA was quantified with a NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and reverse-transcribed with ReverTra Ace qPCR RT master mix (Toyobo) or Superscript IV master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for *A. thaliana*

or *P. japonicum* haustorium samples, respectively. qPCR primers are given in Supplementary Table 2. Melt-curve analysis and amplicon sequencing were performed to validate the specificity of primers. qPCR was performed using a Thunderbird SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo) and the amplicons were detected with a Stratagene Mx3000p real-time thermal cycler (Agilent). Relative transcripts between treatments were calculated from a standard curve for each transcript, and normalized against the *SAND* (At2g28390) gene[44](#page-10-4) or Pj*UBC2*[18](#page-5-17) for *A. thaliana* or *P. japonicum* respectively.

#### **Bacterial growth assays**

Assays were performed as previously describe[d45](#page-10-5). In brief, *Pst* DC3000 and *Pst* DC3000 cor− were grown on King's B plates 1 d before growth on liquid King's B overnight at 28 °C (supplemented with appropriate antibiotics in all cases). *Pst* bacterial cultures were centrifuged and the pellets were resuspended in 10 mM MgCl2 to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) = 0.02 for *Pst* DC3000 and OD600 = 0.2 for *Pst* DC3000 cor− . Silwet L-77 was added (0.02% (v/v)) to bacterial suspensions before spraying. Suspensions were generously sprayed onto leaf surfaces of 5–6-week-old plants and plants were covered during the course of infection. Leaf discs were taken 3 days post-inoculation using a 4-mm diameter disposable biopsy punch (Kei Medical) from 3 leaves per plant, with 6 plants per genotype per independent trial. Leaf discs were ground in sterile water, diluted and plated onto LB agar plates with appropriate antibiotics. Plates were inoculated at 28 °C before counting colonies 1–2 days later. For DMBQ pretreatment, plants were sprayed with 50 μM DMBQ or DMSO equivalent supplemented with 0.02% (v/v) Silwet L-77 and were returned to normal growth conditions for 3 h. After 3 h, the plants were challenged with *Pst* DC3000 cor− as described.

#### **Stomata assays**

Stomata assays were performed following a previous method[45,](#page-10-5) with slight modification. Leaf discs from *card1-2* plants, or complemented line (*card1-2/pCARD1::CARD1*), were incubated in stomata solution (concentration in mM: 10 MES, 50 KCl, 0.01 CaCl2, 0.01% Tween-20, pH 6.1) under white light for 4 h to induce stomata opening. Stimuli (5 μM DMBQ final concentration or DMSO) were added to the buffer and samples were further incubated under the same conditions for 3 h. Stomata were observed under a light microscope (Zeiss) and stomatal apertures were measured with ImageJ.

#### **Thermal imaging**

Soil-grown five-week-old plants were pretreated with 50 μM DMBQ and thermal images were captured 3 h after treatment using a thermal video system (TVS-8500; Nippon Avionic). The experiments were always carried out in the morning.

#### **Confocal microscopy**

Freshly elongated hairy roots were placed under a thin agar (0.7%) layer in small Petri dishes (glass slides are on the bottom side (IWAKI)) and were further incubated for another day and subsequently subjected to confocal microscopy (Leica SP5).

#### **Haustorium assay**

Ten-day old liquid-grown *P. japonicum* seedlings (in 1/2 MS, 0.25% sucrose, pH 5.7, at 25 °C, 16-h photoperiod, 80–100 μE) were placed in wells of 6-well plates (Corning), containing 10 seedlings per well in sterile water in dark at 25 °C overnight. The following day, seedlings were treated with solutions containing either control (DMSO) or the compound-of-interest (final concentration indicated in figures and figure legends) and were left for 5 days in the dark at 25 °C (for Extended Data Fig. 7d–f). Haustorium formation was scored and captured with a Leica M165FC dissection microscope equipped with a Leica DFC7000T digital camera (Leica). For inhibitor treatments, inhibitor solutions were added together with DMBQ solution to give the desired final

concentration. Seedlings were left for 5 days in the dark at 25 °C and haustorium formation was scored. Twenty biologically independent samples (10 seedlings per sample) were analysed.

For DMBQ and/or inhibitor pulse-treatment experiments (Fig. 4d, e, Extended Data Fig. 7g), *P. japonicum* seedlings were grown and prepared as described in 'Plant materials'. For chemical treatment, *P. japonicum* seedlings were treated with DMBQ for a specific time period before copiously rinsed with sterile water and transferred to new containers with sterile water. They were left for 5 days in the dark at 25 °C before haustoria were scored and captured as described here. For inhibitor treatments, inhibitors were added together with DMBQ with a desired final concentration. Treatment was performed as described here for a specific period of time, before seedlings were rinsed with sterile water. Fifteen biologically independent samples were analysed.

#### RNA interference in P. japonicum

RNA interference was performed using pHG8-YFP, as previously described<sup>18,46</sup>. Target sequences were PCR-amplified from *P. japonicum* cDNA or gDNA, and cloned into the pENTR vector before being subcloned into pHG8-YFP vector using LR clonase II plus (Invitrogen). Plasmid was transformed into *A. rhizogenes* AR1193 and hairy root transformation was performed as described in 'Gene cloning, plasmid construction and transformation'. YFP-positive hairy roots were transferred to glass-bottom dishes and examined for haustorium formation after 24 h DMBQ application. Root tips including haustoria were collected and subjected to reverse-transcription qPCR to verify expression levels of the *Pj*CADL genes.

## $\label{eq:constraint} Expression and purification of the ectodomain of the CARD1 \\ protein$

The ectodomain of CARD1 (residues 1-546; hereafter ectoCARD1) containing a 2-amino-acid (KG) spacer was generated from Arabidopsis gDNA by PCR. The fragment was cloned into pEAQ binary vector<sup>47</sup>, which has been linearized by Agel (NEB) and Xhol (NEB), using In-fusion HD cloning as previously described in 'Gene cloning, plasmid construction and transformation', and sequences were verified by Sanger sequencing. The plasmid was electroporated into A. tumefaciens C58C1 strains. The bacterial suspension ( $OD_{600} = 0.2$ , in 10 mM MES-NaOH, 10 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, pH 5.6) was infiltrated into leaves of 5-weeks post-germination soil-grown N. benthamiana with a needleless syringe and infected plants were left for 4 days. After 4 days, infected leaves were collected and vacuum-infiltrated with extraction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl. 0.01% Tween 20,1×EDTA-free complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche), pH 7.5) for 10 min. Remaining water residues were removed and the apoplastic fluid were collected by centrifugation (1,500g, 10 min, 4 °C). The apoplastic fluid was subjected to anion exchange column (HiTrap Q, GE Healthcare) in buffer containing 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, under 1 M NaCl gradient. Fractions with the protein-of-interest were verified by SDS-PAGE and pooled before being subjected to size exclusion chromatography (Superdex200 Increase 10/300 GL, GE Healthcare) in buffer containing 20 mM MES, 100 mM NaCl, pH 5.5. Fractions with ectoCARD1 were verified by SDS-PAGE and were pooled before being subjected to buffer exchange in 20 mM MES, 50 mM NaCl, pH 5.5 using a dialysis cassette (Slide-A-Lyzer Dialysis Cassette 10K MWCO, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Proteins were kept at -80 °C, in aliquots, until required. Approximately 1 mg ectoCARD1 was obtained from 30 leaves.

#### Analytical size exclusion chromatography

A solution of protein standards (thyroglobulin, 669 kDa; ferritin, 440 kDa; aldolase, 158 kDa; conalbumin, 75 kDa; ovalbumin, 44 kDa; carbonic anhydrase, 29 kDa; RNase A, 13.7 kDa; from GE Healthcare) was subject to size exclusion chromatography (Superdex200 Increase 10/300 GL) with the same condition as ectoCARD1 protein purification.

Elution volumes for each protein standard were determined and were used to construct a standard curve.

#### Dynamic light scattering experiments

All solutions and samples were filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane before experiments. EctoCARD1 protein quality was analysed on a Zetasizer Nano-S (Malvern Panalytical) at 20 °C, equipped with a laser at 633 nm and a scattering angle of 173°. Protein sample (1–2 mg/ml) was loaded into a Hellma Quartz cuvette Type 105.252.005-QS (Hellma Analytics). The light path and centre were 3 × 3 mm and 9.65, respectively. Samples were recorded in triplicates to ensure reproducibility. Data acquisition, the polydispersity index, the average hydrodynamic radius were recorded and analysed using Zetasizer software (v.6.20; Malvern Panalytical,).

#### Isothermal titration calorimetry experiment

Binding events were analysed using MicroCal iTC200 (GE Healthcare) at 25 °C in buffer containing 20 mM MES, 50 mM NaCl, pH 5.5. DMBQ solution ( $0.2 \mu$ M) was injected ( $1 \times 0.4 \mu$ l, followed by  $18 \times 2.0 \mu$ l) at 150-s intervals into the calorimeter cell containing  $0.02 \mu$ M ectoCARD1. Data analysis was performed with ORIGIN7 software (OriginLab).
