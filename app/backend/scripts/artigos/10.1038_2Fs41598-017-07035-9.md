# **Molecular bases for diferential aging programs between fag and second leaves during grain-flling in rice**

**Shinyoung Lee1, Hyobin Jeong1, Sichul Lee1, Jinwon Lee1, Sun-Ji Kim1, Ji-Won Park<sup>1</sup>, Hye Ryun Woo2, Pyung Ok Lim2, Gynheung An3, Hong Gil Nam1,2 & Daehee Hwang1,2**

**Flag leaves (FL) and second leaves (SL) in rice show diferential aging patterns during monocarpic senescence. Coordination of aging programs between FL and SL is important for grain yield and quality. However, the molecular bases for diferential aging programs between FL and SL have not been systematically explored in rice. Here, we performed mRNA-sequencing of FL and SL at six time points during grain-flling and identifed four molecular bases for diferential aging programs between FL and SL: phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, photosynthesis, amino acid (AA) transport, and hormone response. Of them, photosynthesis (carbon assimilation) and AA transport (nitrogen remobilization) predominantly occurred in FL and SL, respectively, during grain-flling. Unlike other molecular bases, AA transport showed consistent diferential expression patterns between FL and SL in independent samples. Moreover, long-distance AA transporters showed invariant diferential expression patterns between FL and SL after panicle removal, which was consistent to invariant diferential nitrogen contents between FL and SL after panicle removal. Therefore, our results suggest that the supplies of carbon and nitrogen to seeds is functionally segregated between FL and SL and that long-distance AA transport is an invariant core program for high nitrogen remobilization in SL.**

In cereal crops, nutrients are predominantly transported to grains from leaves. In rice, leaves provide grains with carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) mainly through assimilation and remobilization, respectivel[y1](#page-12-0)[–3](#page-12-1) . Terefore, senescence of leaves greatly afects nutrient supply to grains[4](#page-12-2)[–7](#page-13-0) . During leaf senescence, photosynthesis (C assimilation) is down-regulated, whereas N remobilization is up-regulate[d6,](#page-12-3) [7](#page-13-0) . Tus, delayed senescence can result in increased grain yields by delaying down-regulation of C assimilation[8](#page-13-1) . On the other hand, delayed senescence can decrease protein content (grain quality) in grains by delaying up-regulation of N remobilization, whereas accelerated senescence can increase protein content[9–](#page-13-2)[11](#page-13-3). Collectively, these data indicate that leaf senescence is crucial for determining both yield and quality of grains during the grain-flling period.

To investigate leaf senescence at the molecular level in rice, several studies have identifed a broad spectrum of genes that are associated with leaf senescence using genetic approaches. Tese include the following: (1) transcription factors (TFs), such as *OsWRKY42*[12,](#page-13-4) *OsDOS*[13](#page-13-5), *OsTZF1*[14,](#page-13-6) *OsNAP*[8](#page-13-1) , and *OsNAC106*[15;](#page-13-7) (2) hormonal factors, such as the JA receptor CORONATINE INSENSITIVE 1[b16;](#page-13-8) (3) defense-related proteins, such as lesion mimic and early senescence 1 (*OsLMES1*), spotted leaf3 (*OsSPL3*), and UDP-N-acetylglucosamine pyrophosphorylase 1 (*OsUAP1*)[17](#page-13-9)[–19](#page-13-10); (4) phytochrome B (*OsPhyB*[\)20;](#page-13-11) and (5) the chlorophyll (Chl) degradation genes (*OsNYC1*, *OsPPH1*, *OsNYC4*, *OsRCCR1*, and *OsSGR1*)[21](#page-13-12)[–26](#page-13-13). Moreover, time-course gene expression profling of leaves during the grain-flling period can provide a more comprehensive understanding of leaf senescence. Tese studies were performed mostly in FL only. However, senescence in rice does not occur independently in individual leaves and is a systemic process. To characterize this monocarpic senescence, Choudhuri's *et al*. Comparisons of FL and SL showed that FL and SL have diferential senescence patterns (i.e., rates of decreases in Chl and protein levels) during monocarpic senescence and that these diferential senescence patterns were altered by panicle removal[28,](#page-13-15) [30](#page-13-18). Tus, we performed mRNA-sequencing of FL and SL at six time points during the grain-flling period and compared gene expression profles between FL and SL. Tese analyses revealed SAGs with shared and diferential patterns of expression changes during grain-flling. Mapping of these SAGs into cellular processes identifed key cellular processes underlying the shared and diferential senescence programs between FL and SL. Furthermore, among diferential senescence programs, changes afer panicle removal were used to distinguish invariable core senescence programs from variable senescence programs.

#### **Results**

**Diferential decreases in Chl levels and N contents between FL and SL during grain flling.** In preliminary analyses of the Dongjin (*Oryza sativa* L. ssp. japonica) variety, which has been widely used for genetic analyses[34](#page-13-19), diferential patterns of Chl degradation were observed between FL and SL during grain-flling. Because this is a hallmark feature of leaf senescence, we used this variety for further experiments in this study. To systematically examine temporal characteristics of diferential senescence patterns, we initially measured grain weights during the entire grain-flling period. Temporal grain weight patterns revealed that grain-flling was completed at around 32 days afer heading (DAH) under our growth conditions (Fig. [1a\)](#page-2-0). Accordingly, we selected six diferent time points (4, 12, 20, 28, 36, and 44 DAH) to cover the entire grain-flling period and examined Chl levels in FL and SL. Chl levels decreased during grain-flling and tended to be higher in FL than in SL (P<0.01, two-way AVOVA with Bonferroni correction; Fig. [1b](#page-2-0)). To confrm this tendency, we measured mRNA expression levels of *OsSGR1*, *OsPPH1*, and *OsRCCR1*, which are reportedly associated with Chl degradatio[n22,](#page-13-20) [24–](#page-13-21)[26](#page-13-13), and found higher levels in SL than in FL (P<0.01, two-way AVOVA with Bonferroni correction; Fig. [1c](#page-2-0)), thus consistent with a higher Chl level in FL than in SL. Moreover, rice grains obtain N from leaves through N remobilization, which is another representative senescence feature[4,](#page-12-2) [35,](#page-13-22) [36.](#page-13-23) Tus, we measured total N contents in FL and SL at the six time points. N contents decreased during grain-flling and were higher in FL than in SL (P<0.01, two-way AVOVA with Bonferroni correction; Fig. [1d\)](#page-2-0). To confrm this diference, we measured mRNA expression levels of *OsNAP* and *OsAAP5*, which are reportedly associated with N remobilizatio[n8](#page-13-1), [37](#page-13-24), and found higher levels in SL than in FL (P<0.01, two-way AVOVA with Bonferroni correction; Fig. [1e](#page-2-0)), consistent with a higher N content in FL than in SL. However, expression levels of these genes decreased afer peaking at 28 (*OsSGR1*, *OsPPH1*, and *OsRCCR1* in FL and SL; *OsNAP* and *OsAAP5* in SL) or 36 DAH (*OsNAP* and *OsAAP5* in FL), whereas Chl levels and N contents continuously decreased (see Discussion). Taken together, these data indicate that FL and SL have diferential decreases in Chl levels and N contents, which are representative senescence phenotypes during the grain-flling period.

**SAGs with mRNA expression changes in FL and SL during grain-flling.** To investigate the molecular nature of diferential patterns of Chl levels and N contents between FL and SL during the grain-flling period, we performed mRNA-sequencing of FL and SL at the six time points used in the analyses described above. Subsequently, we used time-course mRNA-sequencing data and identifed a total of 6365 SAGs that showed signifcant (P<0.05, ANOVA with Bonferroni correction) mRNA expression changes in FL (5179 SAGs) and/ or SL (4421 SAGs) during grain-flling (Methods; Supplementary Table [1\)](http://1). Comparisons of the SAGs in FL and SL showed that 3235 SAGs were common in both, whereas 1944 and 1186 SAGs were uniquely identifed in FL and SL, respectively (Fig. [2a](#page-3-0)). However, this comparison was limited because the common SAGs in FL and SL show distinct temporal expression patterns during grain-flling. Tus, we categorized SAGs into two groups based on the similarities of temporal expression patterns of the 6365 SAGs between FL and SL (Methods): (1) 3047 genes that showed shared temporal expression patterns between FL and SL during grain-flling (Shared\_SAGs, log2-fold-changes between FL and SL<0.58 (1.5-fold) at all six time points), and (2) 3058 genes that showed diferential temporal expression patterns between FL and SL during grain-flling (Dif\_SAGs, log2-fold-changes ≥0.58 and P<0.05 from t-test at one or more of the six time points) (Fig. [2b;](#page-3-0) Supplementary Table [1\)](http://1). Of the 6365 SAGs, 260 did not belong to Shared\_SAGs or Dif\_SAGs because, although they showed larger than 1.5-fold differences between FL and SL at one or more time points, the differences were not statistically significant (P>0.05, t-test). Tese data show that FL and SL have shared and diferential transcriptional programs during the grain-flling period.

**Cellular processes associated with Shared\_SAGs in FL and SL during grain-flling.** Several studies have shown that FL and SL perform similar roles to provide nutrients to grain[s3](#page-12-1), [33,](#page-13-17) suggesting the presence of Shared\_SAGs (3047 SAGs). To systematically explore the Shared\_SAGs, we classifed them into seven patterns (SP1-7) (Supplementary Fig. [1a\)](http://1a) using k-means clustering followed by hierarchical clustering (Methods). Te seven patterns of Shared\_SAGs included four (SP1-4) up-regulated and three (SP5-7) down-regulated patterns (Supplementary Fig. [1a\)](http://1a). To understand cellular processes associated with Shared\_SAGs, we performed the enrichment analysis of gene ontology biological processes (GOBPs) for the genes in SP1-7 (Supplementary Fig. [1b](http://1b) and Table [2\)](http://2). First, the transport process was represented by SP1, which had a peaked pattern during grain-flling. SP1 included four major facilitator superfamily genes (*Os01g0268100*, *Os03g0795000*, *Os04g0573000*,

<span id="page-2-0"></span>and *Os12g0133100*), two ABC transporters (*Os08g0167000* and *Os04g0209200*), and a putative copper transporter (*Os08g0455900*), suggesting that mobilization of molecules using these transporters occurred commonly in both FL and SL during the grain-flling period. Second, DNA methylation/replication/repair, RNA processing, and production of ta-siRNAs/siRNAs involved in RNA interference were represented by SP2 and/or SP3 with early increases during grain-flling. Tird, stress responses (responses to salt stress, cadmium ion, and phosphate starvation) were represented by SP4, with gradual increases during grain-flling. Finally, photosynthesis and Chl biosynthetic processes were represented by SP5 and/or SP6, which had early or gradual decreases during grain-flling. Tese data suggest that DNA repair, epigenetic gene silencing (DNA methylation and siRNA generation), and stress responses were up-regulated with shared temporal patterns in FL and SL during the grain-flling period, whereas photosynthesis-related processes were down-regulated with shared temporal patterns in FL and SL.

**Distinct associations of Shared\_SAGs and Dif\_SAGs with transport and photosynthesis.** Te above analyses show that several cellular processes are represented by both Shared\_SAGs and Dif\_SAGs as follows: (1) Te transport process by SP1 of Shared\_SAGs (Supplementary Fig. [1b\)](http://1b) and by G3 of Dif\_SAGs (Fig. [3c\)](#page-4-0) and (2) photosynthesis and Chl biosynthetic processes by SP3, SP5, and SP6 of Shared\_SAGs (Supplementary Fig. [1b\)](http://1b) and also by G2 of Dif\_SAGs (Fig. [3c](#page-4-0)). Shared\_SAGs and Dif\_SAGs included mutually exclusive sets of SAGs, suggesting that Shared\_SAGs and Dif\_SAGs were distinctly associated with diferent sets of transporters or with diferent subnetworks of photosynthesis and Chl biosynthesis. To confrm these distinct associations of Shared\_SAGs and Dif\_SAGs with transport processes, we examined the relative enrichment of subfamilies of transporters in SP1 of Shared\_SAGs and G3 of Dif\_SAGs. G3 of Dif\_SAGs was signifcantly (P<0.01, hypergeometric test) enriched with AATs (Fig. [4a](#page-5-0); Supplementary Fig. [3](http://3) and Table [4\)](http://4). To confrm associations of Shared\_SAGs and Dif\_SAGs with photosynthesis, we mapped the genes in SP3 and SP5-6 of Shared\_SAGs and in G2 of Dif\_SAGs into the photosynthesis complexes: photosystem I (PS I), photosystem II (PS II), cytochrome b6f, ATPase, and RuBisCO (Fig. [4b](#page-5-0)). Among these, PS I and II were prominently enriched with the Shared\_SAGs and/or Dif\_SAGs. PS I was predominantly enriched with Shared\_SAGs (8 SP5-6 SAGs), whereas PS II was enriched with both Shared\_SAGs (9 SP5-6 SAGs) and Dif\_SAGs (5 G2 SAGs). Te fve G2 Dif\_SAG in PS II comprised the mobile light harvesting components (*OsLhcB1-3*)[41](#page-13-27), the adaptor component (*OsLhcB5*), and the assembly/repair component (*OsPsb28*[\)42](#page-13-28) (Fig. [4b,c\)](#page-5-0). In contrast, the nine SP5-6 Shared\_SAG in PS II comprised lumen-exposed Psb components of oxygen-evolving complexes (*OsPsbO*/*P*/*Q*/*R*), which are conserved or specifcally present in higher plants[43](#page-13-29) (Fig. [4b](#page-5-0)). Finally, we mapped G2 (Dif\_SAGs) and SP5-6 (Shared\_SAGs) into the Chl biosynthetic pathway (Fig. [4d](#page-5-0)). Tus, we investigated the relative efects of grain-flling on senescence of FL and SL in panicle removal experiments and measured temporal expression patterns of the following representative genes (Panicle Removal in Fig. [5a,b and d,e](#page-7-0)) for Bases 1–4 (G1–4 of Dif\_SAGs; Fig. [3c](#page-4-0)): (1) ferulate 5-hydroxylase (*OsF5H*), phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (*OsPAL*), and favonoid 3′-monooxygenase (*OsF3*′*H*) for phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Basis 1 for G1); (2) light harvesting complex Chl a/b binding protein (*OsLhcB1*), glutamyl-tRNA reductase (*OsGluTR*), and magnesium-chelatase subunit H (*OsChlH*) for photosynthesis and Chl biosynthesis (Basis 2 for G2); (3) UMAMIT19 homolog (*OsUMAMIT19*), *OsATL9*, and *OsCAT4* for AA transport (Basis 3 for G3); and (4) *OsERF75*, *OsERF77*, and *OsERF96* for hormone responses (Basis 4 for G4). In addition to panicle removal experiments, we confrmed diferential expression patterns of these representative Dif\_SAGs in mRNA sequencing experiments (mRNA-seq panels in Supplementary Fig. [4a–d](http://4a�d)) using qRT-PCR analyses of independent cohorts of FL and SL grown under diferent feld and environmental conditions (Control panels in Fig. [5a,b and d,e](#page-7-0) or Supplementary Fig. [4a–d](http://4a�d)).

#### **Discussion**

Leaf senescence signifcantly afects yield and quality of grain[s4](#page-12-2)[–7](#page-13-0) . Previously, FL and SL in rice have been shown to have shared decreases in photosynthesis but diferent decreasing rates of Chl levels during grain-flling[28](#page-13-15), [30](#page-13-18), [31.](#page-13-37) However, the molecular bases underlying shared and diferential aging patterns have rarely been explored. In this study, we performed time-dependent transcriptome analysis of FL and SL during grain-flling and identifed 6365 SAGs with aging-dependent expression changes in FL and/or SL. Subsequently, we categorized these into 3047 Shared\_SAGs and 3058 Dif\_SAGs according to temporal expression patterns in FL and SL. Shared\_ SAGs were mainly associated with DNA repair, epigenetic gene silencing (DNA methylation and siRNA generation), and stress responses, whereas Dif\_SAGs were mainly associated with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Basis 1), PS II-based photosynthetic and Chl biosynthetic processes (Basis 2), transport of AAs (Basis 3), and senescence-associated hormone responses (Basis 4). Tese data indicated that Shared\_SAGs and Dif\_SAGs mediate shared and diferential molecular aging patterns between FL and SL during grain-flling.

Leaf senescence increases N remobilization and decreases C assimilation. Because leaves are the major source of N and C for grains, it would be difcult to satisfy the needs of N and C simultaneously in grains during leaf senescence, referred to as the "dilemma of senescence["53,](#page-14-2) [54](#page-14-3). According to our analyses of Bases 1–2 of the Dif\_SAGs, FL showed higher expression levels of genes involved in photosynthesis (G2 in Fig. [3](#page-4-0); light harvesting components, their adaptor molecules, and damage repair molecules of PS II in Fig. [4b](#page-5-0)) and in protection from ROS-generating environmental stresses (G1 in Fig. [3;](#page-4-0) favonoid, phenylpropanoid, lignin synthesis-related genes[\)38–](#page-13-25)[40](#page-13-26). Tese data suggest higher capacities of light collection and recovery in PS II during photosynthesis and thus higher C assimilation in FL than in SL, as shown previousl[y30](#page-13-18), [31,](#page-13-37) [55](#page-14-4). However, compared with FL, SL showed higher levels of genes involved in AA transport (G3 in Fig. [3;](#page-4-0) AATs in Fig. [4a\)](#page-5-0) and had lower total N contents (Fig. [1d\)](#page-2-0), suggesting higher N remobilization than in FL. Tis functional segregation of C assimilation and N remobilization to FL and SL, respectively, during grain-flling provides C and N to seeds and overcomes the dilemma of senescence. Although the dilemma of senescence was initially addressed in examinations of single leaves during the grain-flling period[53,](#page-14-2) [54,](#page-14-3) our fndings suggest that these issues may be resolved by examining multiple leaves simultaneously.

Chl and N levels decreased continuously during grain-flling, but expression levels of their associated genes, *OsSGR1*, *OsPPH1*, *OsRCCR1*, *OsNAP*, and *OsAAP5*, peaked prior to the end of grain-flling (Fig. [1c and e](#page-2-0)). In this study, we measured temporal expression patterns of these genes because previous studies have reported the association of these genes with Chl degradation (*OsSGR1*[25,](#page-13-38) [26](#page-13-13), *OsPPH1*[22](#page-13-20), and *OsRCCR1*[24\)](#page-13-21) or N remobilization (*OsNAP*[8](#page-13-1) and *OsAAP5*[37\)](#page-13-24) during grain-flling. In contrast with previous studies that report expression levels of these genes up to 28 DAH[8](#page-13-1) , we continued to measure their expression levels up to 44 DAH and showed peaks in temporal expression patterns at 28 DAH. In agreement with our data, the RiceXPr[o56](#page-14-5) database includes the time-course gene expression profles up to 35 DAH and shows that *OsSGR1*, *OsPPH1*, *OsRCCR1*, and *OsNAP* expression levels peak at 28 DAH (Supplementary Fig. [6\)](http://6). However, the mechanisms behind these peaks remain unclear, and potentially refect unknown factors that prevent excessive decreases of Chl degradation and N remobilization at the late phase of grain flling.

In this study, we proposed four molecular bases (Bases 1–4) based on the GOBPs represented by the Dif\_ SAGs in G1–4 identifed using the following criteria (Criteria 1): log2-fold-changes ≥0.58 and P<0.05 from t-test at one or more of the six time points. However, the GOBPs for G1–4 (Bases 1–4) can vary depending on the cutofs used to identify the Dif\_SAGs. Tus, we applied the following four additional stringent criteria to identify Dif\_SAGs: Criteria 2–3) log2-fold-changes ≥0.58 and P<0.05 from t-test in at least two (Criteria 2) or three (Criteria 3) of the six time points; and Criteria 4–5) log2-fold-changes ≥1 and P<0.05 in at least one (Criteria 4) or two (Criteria 5) of the six time points. Using Criteria 1–5, 3058, 1934, 1185, 960, and 518 Dif\_SAGs were identifed, respectively (Supplementary Fig. [7a\)](http://7a), suggesting that log2-fold-changes are a more stringent cutof for selection of Dif\_SAGs than numbers of time points with expression diferences between FL and SL. To examine the efects of these stringent cutofs on the GOBPs represented by Dif\_SAGs, GOBP enrichment analyses were performed for the Dif\_SAGs identifed using Criteria 2–5. Te results showed that the GOBPs for G1–4 were still signifcantly (P<0.05) represented by the Dif\_SAGs identifed using Criteria 1–4. In contrast, the GOBP (defense response) for G5 was not signifcantly represented by the Dif\_SAGs identifed using Criteria 2–5 while the GOBP (photosynthesis) for G2 was not signifcantly represented by the Dif\_SAGs identifed using the most stringent criteria (Criteria 5). Tese data suggest that defense responses for G5 are sensitive to difering cutofs, but that the GOBPs for G1–4 are relatively robust. Moreover, the GOBPs for G1–4 can vary depending on the tool used to identify the Dif\_SAGs. Tus, we applied the timecourse Package developed by Tei and Speed[57](#page-14-6) to our data and identifed 2136 Dif\_SAGs (P<0.05; Methods). Among these, 969 (45.37%) overlapped with our Dif\_ SAGs, and 336, 180, 186, 248, and 132 belonged to G1-5, respectively. Te GOBP enrichment analysis for these genes in G1-5 showed that the GOBPs for G1–4 were signifcantly represented by the corresponding Dif\_SAGs (Supplementary Fig. [7b](http://7b)). Accordingly, we focused on the GOBPs for G1–4 (Bases 1–4), which are relatively robust against the criteria and the method used for identifcation of Dif\_SAGs, and investigated diferential expression patterns between FL and SL during grain-flling.

Dif\_SAGs of Basis 3 representing AA transport showed invariant diferential expression patterns between FL and SL afer panicle removal (Fig. [5c,d\)](#page-7-0) and between independent cohorts grown under diferent light and temperature conditions (Supplementary Fig. [4c,e](http://4c,e)). In contrast, Dif\_SAGs of Bases 1, 2, and 4 showed variable diferential expression patterns between FL and SL in independent cohorts and/or afer panicle removal (Fig. [5](#page-7-0)). Tese three variable bases are consistent with fndings from a number of previous studies. In particular, photosynthesis (Basis 2) is reportedly afected signifcantly by environmental factors, such as light, temperature, and states of nutrient sinks such as fruits and grains. Accordingly, (1) photosynthetic activity is inhibited under sub-optimal growth conditions involving non-optimal light or temperature[58](#page-14-7)[–60](#page-14-8); and (2) photosynthetic activity is reduced when nutrient sinks are removed and the need for C assimilation decreases[61](#page-14-9)[–65](#page-14-10). Similarly, the bases representing phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Basis 1) and senescence-associated hormone responses (Basis 4) are reportedly sensitive to various environmental factors: (1) favonoids can be induced by UV-[B66](#page-14-11); and (2) amounts of senescence-associated hormones, such as abscisic acid, ethylene, and jasmonic acid, can be varied in response to various types of stimuli[67.](#page-14-12) Unlike the variable bases, however, no previous reports have shown that AA transport (Basis 3), particularly long-distance AA transport (Fig. [6](#page-8-0)), is invariant under diferent environmental conditions or in the absence of panicles (nutrient sinks).

Variable and invariant bases of diferential expression patterns between FL and SL likely refect frequently observed negative genetic relationships between grain yields and grain protein contents in crops, such as soybean, wheat, maize, and ric[e68–](#page-14-13)[71](#page-14-14). Tese negative relationships have been interpreted according to (1) competition between C and N for captured energy during grain development[72](#page-14-15) and (2) N dilution by C-based compounds[73.](#page-14-16) Based on the present observations of variable and invariant bases, we suggest that the negative relationship between grain yield and protein content refects diferential sensitivity of photosynthesis (variable Basis 2) and AA transport (invariant Basis 3) to senescence-inducing stresses, such as drought, salinity, and diseases or pest[s74.](#page-14-17) Specifcally, photosynthetic activity was sensitively down-regulated during stress-induced senescence, resulting in reduced grain yield. In contrast, AA transport activity was relatively stable, leading to stable protein contents in grains. Given the stable protein content, under stress conditions, photosynthetic activity can be further decreased, resulting in a yield relatively lower than can be explained by protein content in grains. Under non-stress conditions, however, photosynthetic activity can be less decreased, resulting in a yield relatively higher than indicated by protein contents.

Variable and invariant components are ofen observed in biological systems and in physical and chemical systems. Tese systems have a general structure wherein invariant components are located at the core and variable components are peripheral and interact with core components and external factors. For example, circadian clocks have the core clock components (LHY and TOC1), which are relatively stable under environmental variations. Tese core clock components constantly interact with variable components, including CCA1, ELFs, PRRs, GI, ZTL, and PIFs, at outer layers, which respond to changes in various environmental factors. Tus, the transcriptional programs for the regulation of diferential aging in FL and SL likely form a similar structure to ensure the stability of core components, such as stable protein contents in grains through diferential long-distance AA transport for N remobilization from SL, and to cope with environmental changes through variable bases (phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, photosynthesis, and hormone defense responses. Tese hypotheses are supported by observations that (1) photosynthesis and plant growth are down-regulated until favorable conditions are recovered, and (2) when the need for C in newly developing sinks increases, photosynthesis increases in source leaves. Furthermore, analyses of other systems indicate close interactions of the core basis (AA transport) with variable bases.

Finally, our fndings can be useful in developing transgenic plants and farming practices (e.g., fertilizers) that optimize rice grain yields and protein contents under given conditions. Our results suggest that leaves ensure frst the limiting N resource in grains through diferential long-distance transport of AAs between FL and SL regardless of environmental conditions. Moreover, invariant supplies of AAs to grains likely maintain minimal plant growth when photosynthesis is severely blocked, because AAs can be used as the C source. For example, glutamate dehydrogenase produces the TCA cycle intermediate 2-oxoglutarate from glutamat[e75–](#page-14-18)[78](#page-14-19). Hence, invariant supplies of AAs can also facilitate seed germination even in N limiting soil. Taken together, these data suggest that the stable supply of AAs to grains is of high priority and is met by diferential aging regulation between FL and SL. Concomitantly, variable bases accommodate environmental variations. Terefore, transgenic rice or farming practices that enhance long-distance AA transport in SL can be developed to maximize the benefts of diferential aging regulation between FL and SL.

#### **Methods**

**Plant materials and sampling.** Rice seedlings of the Asian rice cultivar Dongjin (*Oryza sativa L*. *ssp*. *Japonica*) for mRNA-sequencing were transplanted in a paddy feld at Daegu, South Korea (approximately 35 °N) in early June in 2013. Seedlings for qRT-PCR analyses (control and panicle removal) were transplanted in a different feld at the same time in 2014. In both years, heading dates were around August 20, and stems with similar heading dates were marked for sample preparation and physiological analyses. Samples were prepared between 7 and 8 AM at six time points (4, 12, 20, 28, 36, and 44 days afer heading). Middle parts[16](#page-13-8) of nine independent leaves were obtained as a biological replicate and three replicates were sampled at the same time.

**Library construction and mRNA-sequencing.** Rice leaf tissues were ground using a mortar and pestle, and total RNA was extracted from tissue samples of approximately 100mg using RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA integrity was confrmed using an Agilent Technologies 2100 BioAnalyzer (RNA integrity number >8.0). Poly(A) mRNA was then isolated from total RNA, and fragmentation reactions were performed using Illumina Truseq Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit with poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads according to the manufacturer's instructions. Reverse transcription of RNA fragments was performed using Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Life Technologies, Grand Island, USA). Adaptor-ligated libraries were generated for the resulting samples and were then sequenced using Illumina HiSeq 2500. Paired-end sequencing was performed to 101bp from both ends for three independent replicates at each time point under individual conditions.

**Alignment and quantifcation of sequenced reads to the** *Oryza sativa* **genome.** For the read sequences obtained from mRNA-sequencing, adapter trimming was performed using cutadapt sofwar[e79](#page-14-20) (version 1.2.1) with the adapter sequences (TruSeq Universal Adapter and indexed TruSeqTM Adapters, provided by Illumina). Read alignment was performed using TopHat aligner[80](#page-14-21) (version 2; -r 100–library-type fr-frststrand– mate-std-dev 100–solexa-quals -p 24) with two mismatches and up to 10 multiple alignments allowed based on *Oryza sativa* reference genome (*Oryza sativa* IRGSP-1.0.22). We then assembled the aligned reads into transcripts based on gene features (GTF fle of *Oryza sativa* IRGSP-1.0.22. gtf) and used Cufink[s81](#page-14-22) (version 2.0.2;– library-type fr-frststrand -N–max-bundle-frags 1000000000 -F 0–min-frags-per-transfrag 1–max-bundle-length 5500000) to calculate the fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads (FPKM) values for the assembled transcripts. Quantitated transcripts whose FPKMs are larger than 1 in at least one time point were regarded as expressed transcripts as previously describe[d82.](#page-14-23) For the statistical test and pattern analysis of the transcripts, we obtained the aligned read counts using HTSeq[.83](#page-14-24) (version 0.6.1) based on gene features and then performed a two-stage normalization as previously described[84](#page-14-25), [85](#page-14-26). Briefy, we frst normalized the aligned read counts into count per million (CPM) using Te trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) normalization metho[d86](#page-14-27) in edgeR package[87](#page-14-28) to remove the bias of RNA composition and the diference of library sizes across the samples and then performed the quantile normalizatio[n88](#page-14-29) on log2(CPM+1) to remove systematic variation in the CPM values across the samples. Note that one was added to CPM before taking the log to avoid negative infnity.

**Identifcation of SAGs.** Te normalized log2-CPM values were divided into time-course expression profles of FL and SL. Te time-course expression profles of FL or SL were smoothed using the Savitzky-Golay metho[d89](#page-14-30) with polynomial order=2 and frame size=5. For each transcript, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefcients (PCCs) between the smoothed expression profles from three possible pairwise combinations of the three biological replicates (replicate 1 vs. 2, 2 vs. 3, and 3 vs. 1). Te transcripts with the median PCCs ≥0.7 were selected as the ones with reproducible age-dependent expression patterns. Te cutof of 0.7 was determined from the following random experiments: (1) for *n*×*m* time-course mRNA expression data matrix (X) for *n* transcripts and *m* time points in each replicate of FL or SL, we randomly permuted the columns of X; (2) for each transcript, we calculated PCC between temporal expression profles using the randomly permuted data matrixes (Xrand) of the replicates; (3) steps 1 and 2 were repeated 100 times; and (4) we estimated an empirical distribution for the null hypothesis (a transcript showed no correlation between the temporal expression profles) using the PCCs obtained from the random permutations. Te 95th percentile of PCC in the empirical null distribution was 0.71. Based on this fnding, we determined the PCC cutof to be 0.7. Te distribution of the number of transcripts with respect to PCC with an interval of 0.7 from zero to one revealed that for FL, there were 17696 (46.5%) with significant (P<0.05) correlations (PCC≥0.7), 4079 (10.7%) with marginal correlations (0.4≤PCC≤0.7), and 8945 (23.5%) with low correlations (PCC<0.4), and for SL, there were 13681 (36.0%) with signifcant (P<0.05) correlations with (PCC≥0.7), 5671 (14.9%) with marginal correlations (0.4≤PCC≤0.7), and 10956 (28.8%) with low correlations (PCC<0.4). For FL, we selected the transcripts with the median PCC≥0.7 based on the time-course expression profles of FL and then identifed FL\_SAGs as the selected transcripts with P<0.05 (ANOVA with Bonferroni correction as a post-hoc test) and the maximum absolute log2-fold-changes ≥1 over six time points. Using the same approach, we selected the transcripts with the median PCCs ≥0.7 based on the time-course expression profles of SL, and then identifed SL\_SAGs. Finally, we defned overall SAGs as the union of FL\_SAGs and SL\_SAGs. For the overall SAGs, log2-fold-changes were calculated by subtracting the average log2-CPM values at 4 DAH in FL from those at individual time points. Tese log2-fold-changes for FL and SL were displayed in Fig. [2b](#page-3-0) (heat map in red and green colors).

**Identification of Shared\_SAGs and Diff\_SAGs.** For each of the aforementioned overall SAGs, log2-fold-changes between FL and SL were calculated by subtracting the median log2-CPM values of the three replicates of SL from those of FL at individual time points. Of the overall SAGs, Shared\_SAGs were identifed as the SAGs with log2-fold-changes <0.58 (1.5-fold) for all six time points. We also applied the two-sample t-test to log2-CPM values of the three replicates of FL and SL at each time point to evaluate the signifcance (P-value) of the diference in expression levels between FL and SL at the corresponding time point. Of the overall SAGs, Dif\_SAGs were then identifed as the SAGs with P-values<0.05 from the t-test and absolute log2-fold-changes ≥0.58 (1.5-fold) in one or more of the six time points. Moreover, to examine whether the method used to identify Dif\_SAGs can afects the GOBPs represented by Dif\_SAGs, we also identifed Dif\_SAGs by applying the timecourse Package developed by Tei and Speed[57](#page-14-6) to our data. For each gene, the Package provides no P-values, but only Hotelling T2 statistic values representing the signifcance of the diference between its temporal expression profles of FL and SL. Tus, to estimate *P*-values for the Hotelling T2 values, we performed the following random permutation experiments: (1) for  $n \times m$  time-course mRNA expression data matrix (X) for n genes and m time points in each replicate of FL or SL, we randomly permuted the columns of X; (2) for each gene, we calculated a Hotelling T<sup>2</sup> value by applying the timecourse Package to the randomly permuted data matrixes (Xrand) of the replicates of FL and SL; (3) steps 1 and 2 were repeated 1000 times; (4) we estimated an empirical distribution for the null hypothesis (a gene showed no difference in the temporal expression profiles of FL and SL) using Hotelling T<sup>2</sup> values obtained from the random permutations; and (5) for the Hotelling T<sup>2</sup> value of each gene, we calculated an adjusted P-value by one-sided test using the empirical distribution. We then selected 2136 Diff\_SAGs with P  $\leq$  0.05 and of these, 336, 180, 186, 248, and 132 belonged to G1-5, respectively.

**Pattern analysis of Shared SAGs and Diff SAGs.** Shared\_SAGs were clustered using their  $log_2$ -fold-change profiles by the k-means clustering method with correlation as a similarity measure and the number of clusters (k) = 30. The 30 clusters (SC1-30) of Shared\_SAGs were categorized into 16 up-regulated and 14 down-regulated clusters. The up- and down-regulated clusters were further grouped into SP1-4 and SP5-7, respectively, by applying hierarchical clustering (complete linkage method and Euclidean dissimilarity measure) to the average  $log_2$ -fold-change profiles of the up- and down-regulated clusters. Also, Diff\_SAGs were clustered using their  $log_2$ -fold-change profiles and also additionally the differences of  $log_2$ -fold-changes between FL and SL by the same k-means clustering method. The 30 clusters (DC1-30) of Diff\_SAGs were then categorized into 15 up-regulated and 15 down-regulated clusters. The up- and down-regulated clusters were grouped into DP1-10 and DP11-16, respectively, by applying the same hierarchical clustering used for the Shared SAGs. Finally, DP1-16 were further categorized into five major groups (G1-5) based on the similarity in the difference patterns of  $log_2$ -fold-changes between FL and SL.

**Enrichment analysis of GOBPs.** GOBP annotations of *Oryza sativa* were collected from gramene (release 41)<sup>90</sup>, NSF45K annotation<sup>91</sup>, and RGAP all GO slim<sup>92</sup>. GOBPs enriched by a group of the genes in SP1-7 and G1-5 were identified using curated GOBP annotations by the EASE scoring method used by DAVID software<sup>93</sup>. For each group, we selected the GOBPs with P-values  $\leq 0.05$  and the numbers of genes with the GOBPs  $\geq 5$ .

**qRT-PCR analyses.** After DNase (AM1906, ambion) treatment, cDNA was synthesized from approximately 400 ng of total RNA using an ImProm-II Reverse Transcription System (A3800, Promega). Subsequently, qRT-PCR analyses were performed using CFX96 or CFX384 Touch<sup>™</sup> Real-Time PCR Detection Systems (Bio-Rad) with cDNA from 10–15 ng of total RNA. Actin mRNA expression was used as an internal standard, and normalized cycle threshold (Ct) values were used in statistical analyses (Supplementary Table 6). All primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 6.

**Comparison of differences between expression profiles of FL and SL with and without panicle removal.** Mean relative expression levels of three replicates in FL and SL measured from qRT-PCR analysis were first computed at individual time points, and the differences between the mean relative expression levels of FL and SL were then calculated at individual time points. The integrated difference with  $[Diff(FL-SL)_{PR}]$  or without panicle removal  $[Diff(FL-SL)_{Con}]$  was then calculated by integrating the differences of the mean expression levels at individual time points over time using the trapezoidal integration method.  $[Diff(FL-SL)_{PR} - Diff(FL-SL)_{Con}]/Diff(FL-SL)_{Con}$  were calculated. The same method was used for long-distance AATs and the negative control AATs.

**Correlation between mRNA-sequencing and qRT-PCR data.** For each of the representative genes for G1-5,  $\log_2$ -CPM values from mRNA-sequencing and the expression levels from qRT-PCR in FL or SL were smoothed using the Savitzky-Golay method with polynomial order = 2 and frame size = 5 as previously described<sup>82</sup>. The correlation coefficients between the smoothed mRNA-sequencing and qRT-PCR data were calculated using three different methods (Pearson, Spearman and Kendall's methods). In each method, the correlation coefficients for FL and SL were weighted summed. For mRNA-sequencing or qRT-PCR data, the weights for FL and SL were calculated as the areas under the smoothed expression profiles of FL and SL, respectively. For FL or SL, the final weight was then computed as the geometric mean of the weights of the corresponding leaf for mRNA-sequencing and qRT-PCR data. The weighted summed correlation coefficients from the three methods were finally combined into their geometric mean.

**Analyses of chlorophyll and total N content.** Chls were extracted from approximately 50 mg of ground tissue samples using 95% ethanol. Chl contents were calculated after measuring optical densities using a 96 well plate reader (Infinite M200 PRO, TECAN)<sup>94</sup>. Ground leaf tissues were then dried and total N contents were measured from approximately 2 mg tissue samples using an Elemental analyzer (Elementar).

**Data Availability.** The mRNA sequencing data were deposited into the Gene Expression Omnibus<sup>95</sup> with the accession number GSE89233.
