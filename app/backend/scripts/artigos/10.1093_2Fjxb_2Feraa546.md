### <u>Abstract</u>

Under low iron (Fe) availability, plants transcriptionally induce various genes responsible for Fe uptake and translocation to obtain adequate amounts of Fe. Although transcription factors and ubiquitin ligases involved in these Fe-deficiency responses have been identified, the mechanisms coordinating these pathways have not been clarified in rice. Recently identified Fe-deficiencyinducible IMA/FEP peptides positively regulate many Fe-deficiency-inducible genes for Fe uptake in Arabidopsis. Here, we report that the expressions of two IMA/FEP genes in rice, OsIMA1 and OsIMA2, are strongly induced under Fe-deficiency, positively regulated by the transcription factors IDEF1, OsbHLH058, OsbHLH059, as well as OsIMA1 and OsIMA2 themselves, and negatively regulated by HRZ ubiquitin ligases. Overexpression of OsIMA1 or OsIMA2 in rice conferred tolerance to Fe-deficiency and accumulation of Fe in leaves and seeds. These OsIMA-overexpressing rice exhibited enhanced expression of all of the known Fe-deficiency-inducible genes involved in Fe uptake and translocation, except for OsYSL2, a Fe-nicotianamine transporter gene, in roots but not in leaves. Knockdown of OsIMA1 or OsIMA2 caused minor effects, including repression of some Fe uptake- and translocation-related genes in OsIMA1 knockdown roots. These results indicate that OsIMA1 and OsIMA2 play key roles in enhancing the major pathway of the Fe-deficiency response in rice.

**Keywords:** gene regulation; IMA/FEP peptides; iron deficiency response; iron translocation; iron uptake; rice.

### **Introduction**

Iron (Fe) is an essential element for virtually all living organisms. However, its reactivity also causes cytotoxic effects when present in high levels (Guerinot and Yi, 1994; Marschner, 1995). Therefore, to acquire adequate amounts of Fe while avoiding toxicity, living organisms have evolved tight regulatory mechanisms for the expression of genes involved in Fe uptake and translocation in response to altered Fe availability.

Plants utilize two major mechanisms for the uptake of Fe from the rhizosphere, where Fe is abundant but only slightly soluble (Römheld and Marschner, 1986; Marschner, 1995). Nongraminaceous plants, such as the model species Arabidopsis thaliana, utilize a reduction mechanism, designated Strategy I. Central processes in this mechanism are reduction of ferric Fe by ferric chelate reductase FRO (Robinson et al., 1999) and the subsequent uptake of ferrous Fe (Fe<sup>2+</sup>) by the divalent metal transporter IRT (Eide *et al.*, 1996). This mechanism is supported by the excretion of protons by HA (Santi et al., 2005) and various Fe-chelating or Fe-reducing substances, such as coumarins and flavins (Sisó-Terraza et al., 2016; Rajniak et al., 2018; Stringlis et al., 2019). On the other hand, graminaceous plants, including important crop species such as rice, wheat, and maize, utilize a chelation mechanism designated Strategy II. This mechanism relies on the production and secretion of high-affinity ferric Fe chelators, the mugineic acid family phytosiderophores (MAs) (Takagi, 1976). The secreted MAs solubilize Fe(III) in the rhizosphere, and the resulting Fe(III)-MA complexes are taken up by the roots through YS1/YSL transporters (Curie et al., 2001, 2009). Rice utilizes deoxymugineic acid (DMA) as a type of MA for Strategy II, whose key genes encode enzymes for DMA biosynthesis, including nicotianamine (NA) synthases OsNAS1 and OsNAS2 (Higuchi et al., 2001; Inoue et al., 2003), NA aminotransferase OsNAAT1 (Inoue et al., 2008), DMA synthase OsDMAS1 (Bashir et al., 2006), DMA efflux transporter TOM1 (Nozoye et al., 2011), and Fe(III)-DMA uptake transporter OsYSL15 (Inoue et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2009). Expression of these genes is regulated spatiotemporally in response to Fe availability (Inoue et al., 2003, 2008, 2009; Kobayashi et al., 2005, 2014; Bashir et al., 2006; Nozove et al., 2011; Itai et al., 2013). In addition, rice possesses a partial Strategy I that takes up Fe<sup>2+</sup> by OsIRT1 and OsIRT2 transporters (Ishimaru et al., 2006). Chelators including DMA and its precursor NA, as well as transporters of these chelators and their metal complexes, are also important for the internal transport of Fe and other divalent metals (Kobayashi and Nishizawa, 2012; Kobayashi et al., 2019a).

In response to low Fe availability, plants transcriptionally induce the expression of the genes mentioned above involved in Fe uptake and translocation. This type of Fe-deficiency response is most obvious in the roots, but induction of a large subset of these genes also occurs in the leaves (Higuchi *et al.*, 2001; Kobayashi *et al.*, 2009; Schwarz and Bauer, 2020). Various transcription factors (TFs) mediating this response have been characterized, mainly in rice and *Arabidopsis*, and this has shown that both species share key TFs belonging to the lb, Illa, IVb, and IVc subgroups of the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) family (Kobayashi, 2019; Wang *et al.*, 2020b; Schwarz and Bauer, 2020). In rice, subgroup IVc bHLH TFs OsbHLH058/OsPRI2, OsbHLH059/OsPRI3, and OsbHLH060/PRI1 are constitutively expressed and situated upstream of Fe deficiency-inducible bHLH TFs of <u>the subgroups</u> Ib (OsIRO2), Illa (OsbHLH156/OsFIT) and IVb (OsIRO3) (Zhang *et al.*, 2017, 2020; Kobayashi, 2019; Kobayashi *et al.*, 2019b; Liang *et al.*, 2020). <u>OsIRO2 and</u> OsbHLH156/OsFIT form heterodimers to induce the genes responsible for Strategy II Fe uptake and translocation (Ogo *et al.*, 2007, 2011; Wang *et al.*, 2020b; Liang *et al.*, 2020). In contrast, OsIRO3 represses the expression of many genes involved in Fe uptake and translocation (Zheng *et al.*, 2010; Wang *et al.*, 2020a). Rice also possesses other constitutive TFs, including IDEF1, belonging to the ABI3/VP1 TF, and IDEF2, belonging to the NAC TF, both of which induce the expression of partially overlapping subsets of Fe deficiency-inducible genes (Kobayashi *et al.*, 2007, 2009; Ogo *et al.*, 2008; Wang *et al.*, 2020a). In *Arabidopsis*, a subgroup IVb bHLH TF, AtbHLH121/URI, interacts with subgroup IVc bHLH TFs to activate regulation mediated by the latter subgroup (Kim *et al.*, 2019; Gao *et al.*, 2020; Lei *et al.*, 2020).

In addition to these bHLH TFs, rice and Arabidopsis possess homologous negative regulators of the Fe-deficiency response, which are the ubiquitin ligases OsHRZ1 and OsHRZ2 in rice and BTS, BTSL1, and BTSL2 in Arabidopsis (Long et al., 2010; Kobayashi et al., 2013; Hindt et al., 2017). OsHRZ1, OsHRZ2 and BTS bind Fe and Zn (Kobayashi et al., 2013; Selote et al., 2015), which potentially sense and transmit Fe-deficiency/sufficiency signals (Kobayashi et al., 2013; Hindt et al., 2017). Some studies have suggested that OsHRZ1 and BTS mediate ubiquitination of the subgroup IVc bHLH proteins OsbHLH058/059/060 and AtbHLH105/115, respectively, for subsequent 26S proteasome-mediated degradation (Selote et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017, 2020), but another in vitro study showed no ubiquitination of OsbHLH058, 059, and 060 by OsHRZ1 (Kobayashi et al., 2019b). Similarly, Arabidopsis BTS homologs, BTSL1 and BTSL2, are suggested to ubiquitinate FIT (Rodríguez-Celma et al., 2019), a subgroup IIIa bHLH TF that plays a central role in inducing the Fedeficiency response via Strategy I (Colangelo and Guerinot, 2004; Jakoby et al., 2004; Schwarz and Bauer, 2020). These observations suggest that these ubiquitin ligases act upstream of the transcriptional network via bHLH TFs. Moreover, transgenic rice lines with repressed expression of both OsHRZ1 and OsHRZ2, designated as HRZ knockdown lines, exhibit markedly enhanced expression of most of the Fe-deficiency-inducible genes involved in Fe uptake and translocation (Kobayashi et al., 2013), suggesting that HRZs might target unknown master regulator(s) of the Fedeficiency response.

<u>Recently</u>, Grillet *et al.* (2018) and Hirayama *et al.* (2018) reported that *Arabidopsis IRON MAN* (*IMA*)/*FE UPTAKE-INDUCING PEPTIDE (FEP)* genes, encoding peptides of ca. 23–86 amino acids, are strongly upregulated under Fe-deficiency, and their overexpression in *Arabidopsis* plants results in upregulation of many Fe-deficiency-inducible genes involved in Fe uptake and translocation, and the accumulation of Fe, Mn, and zinc (Zn) in shoots and seeds. Grillet *et al.* (2018) also reported the presence of Fe-deficiency-inducible *IMA/FEP* homologs in various plant species, including rice and soybean. Moreover, overexpression of *Arabidopsis AtIMA1* in tomato and rice *OsIMA1* in *Arabidopsis* also resulted in Fe accumulation, suggesting that IMA/FEPs are conserved among plant species. *Arabidopsis* IMA/FEPs are suggested to bind divalent metals, and to be involved in shoot-toroot Fe signaling (Grillet *et al.*, 2018), although the molecular mechanisms underlying their function have not been identified. In addition, the function of IMA/FEPs has yet to be examined in graminaceous species.

In the present study, we aimed to clarify possible roles of the rice *IMA/FEP* genes *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* as master regulators for the Fe-deficiency response in rice. To this end, we explored the expression and function of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* in rice plants. Our results show that expressions of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* are strongly induced under Fe-deficiency and are regulated by known regulators. Overexpression of *OsIMA1* or *OsIMA2* conferred tolerance to Fe-deficiency and Fe accumulation in shoots and seeds. Plants showing *OsIMA* overexpression had markedly enhanced expression of all of the known Fe-deficiency-inducible genes for Fe uptake and translocation except

# *OsYSL2,* indicating that OsIMAs positively regulate the main pathway of the Fe-deficiency response in rice.

### **Materials and Methods**

### Plant materials and growth conditions

Rice (*Oryza sativa* L.) cultivar Tsukinohikari and its transformants described below were germinated and cultivated hydroponically or in normal soil, based on the description in Kobayashi *et al.* (2019b) with a slight modification in the culture length. The *OsIMA* transformants and corresponding non-transformants (NTs) were germinated and cultivated for 18 days and 12–15 days, respectively, prior to acclimation. *HRZ* knockdown plants and the corresponding NT were cultivated according to Kobayashi *et al.* (2013). *OsbHLH058* and *OsbHLH059* overexpression and knockdown lines and the corresponding NTs were cultivated according to Kobayashi *et al.* (2019b).

# Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analyses

Total RNA was extracted from hydroponically grown roots and leaves of 3–6 plants per replicate using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), treated with DNase I and reversetranscribed using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). The quantitative PCR was performed using a StepOnePlus<sup>TM</sup> Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Forster City, CA, USA) with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan) or with the TaqMan Gene Expression Assay (Applied Biosystems), using the primers listed in Supplementary Table S1. For quantification of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2*, <u>open reading frames (ORFs) of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* starting from the first start codon were amplified by PCR using the primers listed in Supplementary Table S1 and a complementary DNA (cDNA) pool of Nipponbare rice cultivar roots and leaves (Kobayashi *et al.*, 2013) as a template, and cloned into pENTR/D-TOPO (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA); then the sequences were verified, the copy numbers in the solutions were estimated from the absorbance at 260 nm, and the solutions were used as the standards for quantification. After normalization with rice  $\alpha$ -2 tubulin expression, transcript abundance was expressed either as the number of transcript copies per 1 µg total RNA using *OsIMA* standard solutions, or a ratio relative to the levels in Fe-sufficient NT roots or leaves in each experiment.</u>

# Generation of transgenic rice lines

To produce rice overexpression lines, an LR Clonase reaction (Invitrogen) was conducted using pH7FWG2 (Karimi *et al.*, 2002) as the destination vector and the aforementioned *OsIMA* ORF-pENTR/D-TOPO as the entry vector, to construct an in-frame fusion of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* downstream of the cauliflower mosaic virus *35S* promoter. To produce the knockdown rice lines by RNA interference (RNAi), a 299 bp fragment corresponding to the 3'-untranslated region (3'UTR) of *OsIMA1*, and a 302 bp fragment corresponding to the 292 bp 3'UTR and a 10 bp flanking coding region of *OsIMA2*, were amplified by PCR using the primers listed in Supplementary Table S1 and a

cDNA pool of the Nipponbare rice cultivar roots and leaves <u>(Kobayashi *et al.*, 2013) as a template,</u> <u>and</u> were inserted into pENTR/D-TOPO; then the sequence was verified. Using the LR Clonase reaction, the fragment was transferred into the destination vector pIG121-RNAi-DEST (Ogo *et al.*, 2007), to construct the RNAi vector. Transformation of the rice cultivar Tsukinohikari was performed as previously described (Hiei *et al.*, 1994; Kobayashi *et al.*, 2001), and T<sub>1</sub> seeds were used for further analyses.

### Metal concentration analyses

Leaf-blade segments and  $T_1$  brown seeds were used to measure Fe, Zn, Mn, and copper (Cu) concentrations by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICPS-8100; Shimadzu, Japan) as described in Kobayashi *et al.* (2019b).

### RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses

RNA-seq analyses were carried out using one sequenced library per plant organ per transgenic line, or NT replicates for Fe deficiency responsiveness as indicated below. The extracted total RNA was used for RNA-seq library preparation, sequencing, mapping, and quantification of each gene expression as reads per million (RPM), following the Lasy-Seq v1.1 protocol described in Kamitani *et al.* (2019) (https://sites.google.com/view/lasy-seq/). The sequencing for Fe-sufficient roots and leaves of IMA1OX-7, 9, IMA2OX-2, 5, and the corresponding NT was conducted using HiSeq X (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) (designated Round 1), resulting in ca. 16–32 M reads each, while the sequencing for Fe-sufficient roots of IMA1OX-4, IMA2OX-1, IMA1i-2, IMA2i-3, the corresponding NT, as well as two biological replicates of Fe-sufficient and Fe-deficient roots of NT, was conducted using HiSeq 2500 (Illumina) (designated Round 2), resulting in ca. 3–9 M reads each. Whole data sets are accessible through the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) record GSE151941. Calculation of the expression ratio using the RPM data and further analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel. For the correlation analyses, the log<sub>2</sub> ratio of two transgenic line replicates or two NT biological replicates was averaged, and data with RPM = 0 for at least one replicate of either the control or treatment or those with RPM < 0.2 for all of the control and treatment replicates, were omitted.

# Results

Expression of two rice OsIMA genes is strongly induced under Fe-deficiency and is controlled by known regulators

We first examined the amino acid sequences of two putative *IMA/FEP* genes in rice, *OsIMA1* (Os01g0647200) and *OsIMA2* (Os07g0142100), designated by Grillet *et al.* (2018) (Supplementary Fig. S1). Both OsIMA1 and OsIMA2 possessed 17 conserved acidic amino acids at the C-terminus, which are critical for IMA/FEP functioning in *Arabidopsis* (Grillet *et al.*, 2018). According to the RAP-DB database (https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/), the OsIMA2 peptide was predicted to start from the 5'-end of the transcript without a start codon until the 30th amino acid (underlined in Supplementary

Fig. S1). This region may not be translated and might not be required for functioning because this region was not conserved in other *IMA* genes, and it lacked the conserved acidic motif.

Because Fe deficiency-inducible expression pattern of IMA/FEP appeared to be a key for their function (Grillet *et al.*, 2018), we quantified the expression of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* transcripts in hydroponically grown rice plants under Fe-sufficiency and deficiency by RT-qPCR. We also examined the effects of known regulators on *OsIMA* expression using transformants with altered expression of specific regulator genes (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S2). In NT rice, expression of both *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* was strongly induced by Fe-deficiency treatments in both the roots and leaves (Fig. 1A), as suggested by Grillet *et al.* (2018) based on previous microarray results. While *OsIMA1* was moderately expressed under Fe-sufficiency in the roots and leaves (9.0 ×  $10^3$ -fold) than in the roots (58-fold), resulting in higher expression in Fe-deficient leaves than in Fe-deficient roots (Fig. 1A). On the other hand, expression of *OsIMA2* was not detected in this experiment (Fig. 1A). Under Fe-deficiency, *OsIMA2* expression was dramatically induced in both the roots and leaves ( $5.8 \times 10^6$ -fold), resulting in very high expression in Fe-deficient leaves but relatively low expression in Fe-deficient roots.

Expression of *OsIMA1* tended to be increased in the *HRZ* knockdown (HRZi) lines in Fe-sufficient roots and leaves, while *OsIMA2* expression tended to be increased in the *HRZ* knockdown line 2 but not in line 1 (Fig. 1B). Under Fe-deficiency, expression of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* tended to be lower in the *HRZ* knockdown lines than in NT in the roots and more obviously in the leaves (Fig. 1B). These effects of *HRZ* knockdown are similar to the typical Fe-deficiency-inducible genes involved in Fe uptake and localization (Kobayashi *et al.*, 2013).

We also analyzed the *OsIMA* expression in *OsbHLH058* overexpression (b058OX), *OsbHLH058* knockdown (b058i), and *OsbHLH059* knockdown (b059i) lines (Fig. 1C, D). Expression of both *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* was strongly increased in the b058OX lines in both Fe-sufficient and Fe-deficient roots (Fig. 1C). By contrast, *OsIMA1* expression was strongly repressed in both the b058i and b059i lines in Fe-sufficient roots, except in b058i line 1 (Fig. 1D). These results indicate that OsbHLH058 and OsbHLH059 positively regulate the expression of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2*.

We further examined *OsIMA* expression using our previous microarray results (Ogo *et al.*, 2007, 2008, 2014; Kobayashi *et al.*, 2009, 2013; Supplementary Table S2). *OsIMA1* expression was strongly induced, not only in whole roots and shoots, but also in the discrimination center, root vascular bundle, cortex, and epidermis/exodermis, under Fe-deficiency. HRZ-dependent *OsIMA1* expression was similar to the RT-qPCR results (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Table S2). *OsIMA1* expression was also positively regulated by IDEF1, particularly in an early stage (1 day) of Fe-deficiency (Supplementary Table S2). *OsIMA1* expression was not strongly affected by IDEF2 or OsIRO2, except for negative regulation by IDEF2 in Fe-deficient roots (Supplementary Table S2). On the other hand, *OsIMA2* expression was strongly induced in shoots and new discrimination centers, but not in root tissues, where expression levels were very low (Supplementary Table S2). *OsIMA2* expression was positively regulated by IDEF1 in leaves, particularly at early Fe-deficiency, but not by IDEF2 (Supplementary Table S2). The dependence of *OsIMA2* expression on OsIRO2 was not discernible because of the absence of *OsIMA2* in the array slide. In summary, both *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* expression was linked

to major Fe-deficiency-inducible regulatory pathways, except for very low *OsIMA2* expression in the roots.

According to the microarray database RiceXPro (Sato *et al.*, 2011, 2013; http://ricexpro.dna.affrc.go.jp/index.html), under standard growth conditions, *OsIMA1* was expressed in various organs including leaf blade, leaf sheath, stem, lemma, palea and embryo, whereas *OsIMA2* expression was very low except in leaf blades (Supplementary Fig. S2). In vegetative and reproductive leaves, expression of both *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* showed strong daily fluctuations that typically peaked late in the daytime (Supplementary Fig. S3). Expression of both *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* was similarly and strongly induced in the shoots by treatment with auxin, brassinosteroid, cytokinin, and jasmonic acid, and was repressed by abscisic acid (Supplementary Fig. S4).

# OsIMA overexpression in rice confers tolerance to Fe-deficiency and Fe accumulation, while its knockdown causes minor effects

To identify the function of the *OsIMA* genes in rice, we produced transgenic rice lines constitutively overexpressing *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* under the control of the cauliflower mosaic virus *355* promoter (IMA1OX and IMA2OX lines, respectively), as well as lines with repressed expression of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* using the RNAi method (IMA1i and IMA2i lines, respectively). For *OsIMA2* overexpression, the region upstream of the first start codon (underlined in Supplementary Fig. S1) was not included. Overexpression or repression of *OsIMA1* or *OsIMA2* in independent lines was confirmed by RT-qPCR analyses (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S5). We mainly used IMA1OX lines 7, 9, and IMA2OX lines 2 and 5 for further analysis. Notably, *OsIMA1* overexpression resulted in the induction of *OsIMA2* expression in Fe-sufficient roots but its repression in Fe-sufficient roots and leaves (Fig. 2), revealing a positive feedback loop of *OsIMA* expression in Fe-sufficient roots.

The IMA1OX and IMA2OX lines showed retarded growth at the seedling stages, whereas the IMA1i and IMA2i lines grew similarly to the NT. These transgenic lines and the NT rice were transferred to hydroponic culture and then subjected to Fe-deficiency treatment (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. S6). With the progression of Fe-deficiency, new leaves became chlorotic, which is a typical Fe-deficiency symptom. However, all of the IMA1OX and IMA2OX lines retained relatively greener leaves than the NT, as substantiated by significantly higher SPAD values (relative chlorophyll level) in the newest leaves typically during days 4–7 of Fe-deficiency (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. S7). The IMA1OX and IMA2OX lines had reduced shoot heights compared to the NT at the beginning of the Fe-deficiency treatment due to slowed seedling growth, but grew more vigorously during the treatment, and some of them caught up to or even passed the NT shoot height at the end of the Fe-deficiency treatment (Fig. 3B, C, Supplementary Fig. S7). The overexpression lines still retained relatively green leaves even at the end of the Fe-deficiency treatment except IMA1OX line 7 (Fig. 3A, D, Supplementary Fig. S6).

By contrast, the IMA1i and IMA2i lines showed similar or moderately lower SPAD values than NT during the Fe-deficiency treatment (Supplementary Fig. S6). Some of these lines tended to show lower growth rates during the treatment (Supplementary Fig. S6). Occasionally, brown spots

appeared in the newest leaves of IMA1i line 2 and IMA2i line 3, but not in other lines, after 6 days of Fe deficiency treatment (Supplementary Fig. S6M). The overexpression and knockdown lines showed no clear phenotypes under Fe-sufficient conditions, except for the smaller sizes of the IMA1OX and IMA2OX lines (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Fig. S6N).

We measured the metal concentrations in the leaves of these lines after hydroponic culture under either Fe-sufficient or -deficient conditions. When grown with sufficient Fe, the IMA1OX and IMA2OX leaves showed a marked increase in Fe concentration, and a moderate increase in Zn and Cu concentrations, compared to the NT (Fig. 4A). The IMA2OX lines particularly showed higher concentrations of these metals than the IMA1OX lines. The concentration of Mn did not differ from NT in the IMA1OX lines but was moderately lower in IMA2OX line 2, and higher in IMA2OX line 5 (Fig. 4A). After Fe-deficient culture, metal accumulation in the overexpression leaves was less obvious (Fig. 4B). The Fe concentration only tended to increase in the IMA1OX lines and was moderately increased in the IMA2OX lines. The concentrations of Zn, Mn, and Cu tended to be lower in the overexpression lines than in the NT.

By contrast, IMA1i and IMA2i leaves showed moderate decrease in Fe and Cu concentrations compared to the NT under Fe-sufficient conditions, while Zn and Mn concentrations were similar between these lines and NT, except for decrease in Mn concentration in IMA2i line 3 (Fig. 4C). Similar but less obvious trends were also observed after Fe-deficient culture, in which significant decrease was only seen in Fe concentration in IMA1i line 2 (Fig. 4D).

We also measured the concentrations of the metals in brown seeds after pot culture in normal soil (Fig. 4E, F). Notably, all of the overexpression lines showed increased Fe concentrations compared to the NT, increasing by 2.5- to 3.4-fold in the IMA1OX lines, and 2.3- to 4.2-fold in the IMA2OX lines. The Zn concentration also increased moderately in IMA1OX line 9 (1.4-fold) and all of the IMA2OX lines (1.3- to 1.7-fold), but not in IMA1OX lines 4 and 7. The Cu concentration also increased slightly in many lines, but only showed a significant increase in IMA2OX line 2. The Mn concentration was similar to that of the NT in the IMA1OX lines 3 and 5 showed higher Fe, Zn, and Cu concentrations than in the NT, while no difference was observed in IMA1i lines 7 and 9 (Fig. 4F). The Mn concentration was lower in all of the IMA1i and IMA2i lines compared to the NT. Grain yields of the analyzed lines tended to be moderately lower than NT (data not shown). Collectively, these results show that overexpression of *OsIMA1* or *OsIMA2* confers tolerance to Fe deficiency and strong Fe accumulation in rice leaves and seeds, while knockdown of *OsIMA1* or *OsIMA2* causes relatively minor effects on Fe deficiency tolerance and Fe accumulation.

# OsIMA overexpression induces an almost total Fe-deficiency response involved in Fe homeostasis in the roots but not in the leaves

To clarify the downstream pathways of OsIMAs in rice plants, we carried out RNA-seq analyses (designated Round 1) using hydroponically grown Fe-sufficient roots and leaves of *OsIMA* overexpression lines compared to the NT. We calculated the log<sub>2</sub> expression ratios of the IMA OX lines against the NT for each gene and compared them with those of Fe- deficiency responsiveness in the NT roots. We first examined the expression of known Fe-related genes, which are involved in

Fe uptake, translocation, regulation, subcellular sequestration, and storage (Kobayashi and Nishizawa, 2012; Kobayashi *et al.*, 2014, 2019a, 2019b; Bashir *et al.*, 2015; Wang *et al.*, 2020b). A majority of these genes clearly showed increased expression in both the IMA1OX and IMA2OX lines compared to the NT in the roots, and almost all of these genes were induced under Fe-deficiency in the NT (Table 1). This tendency was also obvious in the scatter plot between the log<sub>2</sub> expression ratio of Fe-deficiency responsiveness in the NT and that of the IMA OX lines/NT (Fig. 5A). Both these expression ratios showed a strikingly strong positive correlation with a Pearson's correlation coefficient (R) of 0.924 and 0.801 for IMA1OX and IMA2OX, respectively. The correlation between the IMA1OX/NT and IMA2OX/NT ratios was also strongly positive in Fe-sufficient roots (R = 0.969; Supplementary Fig. S7A). IMA2OX tended to show higher expression than IMA1OX. These results suggest conserved functions for OsIMA1 and OsIMA2 in rice, while the impact of *OsIMA2* overexpression appears to be stronger than *OsIMA1*.

Curiously, the expression of *OsYSL2*, encoding a Fe(II)-NA transporter (Koike *et al.*, 2004), was located outside of the correlation tendencies (Fig. 5A); this gene showed reduced expression in the IMA1OX and IMA2OX lines compared to the NT, although it showed strong induction under Fedeficiency in NT (Table 1), consistent with previous reports (Koike *et al.*, 2004; Ishimaru *et al.*, 2010; Kobayashi *et al.*, 2014). Another obvious outlier was *OsFer1*, encoding the Fe storage protein ferritin (Stein *et al.*, 2009), which showed only moderate repression in the IMA1OX and IMA2OX lines, but showed strong repression under Fe-deficiency in the NT (Table 1), consistent with a previous report (Itai *et al.*, 2013).

In contrast to this overall regulation of Fe-related genes except for *OsYSL2* and *OsFer1* in the roots by OsIMA, the leaves showed a distinct expression pattern (Table 1). *OsIMA*-overexpressing leaves showed enhanced expression of a minority of these genes, such as *TOM2*, and repressed the expression of another small subset, including *OsNAAT1*. Expression of the *OsFer1* and *OsFer2* ferritin genes was moderately repressed (Table 1). The correlation between the IMA1OX/NT and IMA2OX/NT expression ratios in the leaves was positive (R = 0.533) but weaker than that in the roots (Supplementary Fig. S7B). We confirmed the expression of representative genes by RT-qPCR (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S8). The results indicated high consistency in the expression levels detected by RNA-seq and RT-qPCR in the roots and leaves, except when the expression was very low (generally RPM < 0.2; italics and "nd" in Table 1), in which case only the RT-qPCR showed clear results (Supplementary Fig. S8). Expression of *OsNAS1*, *OsYSL2*, and *OsIRO2* was repressed in *OsIMA*-overexpressed leaves, except for *OsIRO2* in the OsIMA2OX lines (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Another round of RNA-seq analyses (Round 2) were conducted using Fe-sufficient roots of a representative line of IMA1i and IMA2i each, another line of IMA1OX and IMA2OX each, and corresponding NTs, to gain further insights into the effects of OsIMAs on the expression of Fe-deficiency-responsive genes (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Table S3, Fig. S9). This analysis indicated that knockdown of *OsIMA1*, but not *OsIMA2*, resulted in the reduced expression of many Fe-related genes, including *OsNAAT1*, *TOM1*, *OsYSL15*, *OsIRT1*, *OsIRT2*, *OsNRAMP1*, and *OsOPT7* (Fig. 5B). Accordingly, the IMA1i-2/NT expression ratio showed a clear negative correlation with the Fe-deficiency response in the NT (R = -0.531), whereas IMA2i-3/NT showed almost no correlation (R = 0.010) (Fig. 5B). These results suggest that OsIMA1, but not OsIMA2, contributes to the induction of the typical Fe-deficiency-responsive genes in Fe-sufficient roots, although these results need to be confirmed by additional experiments using more knockdown lines.

This Round 2 analysis also confirmed the positive effects of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* overexpression on known Fe-related genes (Supplementary Table S3), showing a strong positive correlation with the Fe-deficiency response in the NT (R = 0.929 and 0.894, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. S9A). The Round 2 analysis failed to detect low expression levels (such as *OsYSL2* in some lines) due to shallower sequencing than in Round 1.

We further investigated the effects of *OsIMA* overexpression and knockdown on the expression of other Fe-deficiency-induced genes. Scatter plots of these genes in the NT roots revealed a slight positive correlation between the Fe-deficiency response in the NT and the *OsIMA* overexpression/NT ratios (R = 0.328 and 0.278 for IMA1OX-4 and IMA2OX-1, respectively; Supplementary Fig. S9B). The *OsIMA* knockdown/NT showed almost no correlation using these genes (Supplementary Fig. S9D). Similar analyses using the Fe-deficiency-repressed genes in the NT roots showed almost no correlation with the *OsIMA* transformants, except for a slight positive correlation for OsIMA1OX-4/NT (R = 0.149) (Supplementary Fig. S9C, E). In summary, these expression analyses revealed that the *OsIMA* expression levels positively affected the root expression of overall Fe-deficiency-inducible genes for known Fe-related functions except *OsYSL2*, and a relatively small subset of other Fe-deficiency-responsive genes.

#### Discussion

#### OsIMAs positively regulate major, but not entire, pathways of the Fe-deficiency response in rice

In the present study, we aimed to clarify the regulatory roles of OsIMA1 and OsIMA2. We showed that *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* expression was strongly induced under Fe-deficiency in both the roots and leaves (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, the expression of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* was positively regulated by key TFs OsbHLH058, OsbHLH059, and IDEF1, as well as OsIMA themselves, and negatively regulated by the ubiquitin ligases OsHRZ1 and OsHRZ2 at the transcript level (Fig. 1, 2; Supplementary Table S2). Our results showed that OsbHLH058 and OsbHLH059, subgroup IVc bHLH TFs, are especially important for *OsIMA* expression (Fig. 1C, D). The involvement of subgroup IVc bHLH TFs for *IMA/FEP* gene expression has not been reported in *Arabidopsis*. Recently, Kim *et al.* (2019) and Gao *et al.* (2020) reported that AtbHLH121, a subgroup IVb bHLH that interacts with subgroup IVc bHLH TFs, is essential for induction of *AtIMA1* and *AtIMA2*, but not *AtIMA3*, under Fedeficiency. However, another report showed no requirement of AtbHLH121 for *AtIMA1*, *AtIMA2*, and *AtIMA3* expression (Lei *et al.*, 2020). Future investigation of OsbHLH064, the closest homolog of AtbHLH121 in rice, would be important to clarify the OsIMA-mediated pathways fully.

We also showed that overexpression of either *OsIMA1* or *OsIMA2* resulted in strong induction of all of the known Fe uptake- and translocation-related Fe-deficiency-inducible genes, except for *OsYSL2* in Fe-sufficient roots (Table 1; Fig. 5A). In addition, recent report by Wang *et al.* (2020a) suggests that the expression of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* is repressed by OsIRO3. Based on these results, we propose possible position of the OsIMAs in the regulatory pathway of the Fe-deficiency response in rice roots, as shown in Fig. 6.

We noticed a strong correlation between the OsIMA-mediated induction ratio and the Fedeficiency-mediated induction ratio of known Fe-related genes, except for OsYSL2 and OsFer1 (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. S9A). This suggests a possible function for OsIMA as a central determinant of the Fe-deficiency responsiveness of these genes. We recently reported that repression of a rice aconitase gene *OsACO1* results in decreased expression of many Fe deficiency-inducible genes including *OsIMA1* in rice roots, whereas overexpression of *OsACO1* causes the opposite effects (Senoura *et al.* 2020). This raises the possibility that OsACO1 acts upstream of OsIMA to regulate Fe deficiency response. *OsACO1* expression is repressed under Fe-deficiency (Senoura *et al.* 2020), in contrast to strong induction of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* (Fig. 1A). Considering the positive regulation of *OsIMA1* by *OsIMA2* overexpression and *vice versa* (Fig. 2), and the moderately repressed expression of *OsIMA1* by *OsIMA2* knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S5), OsIMAs are thought to amplify the Fe-deficiency response by feedforward loops to induce downstream genes (Fig. 6).

In contrast to the strong phenotypes of the *OsIMA* overexpression lines, including Fe-deficiency tolerance and Fe accumulation in the leaves and seeds (Fig. 3, 4), the *OsIMA* knockdown lines only showed minor phenotypes (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S6). This result supports the possibility that OsIMA1 and OsIMA2 are functionally redundant, as suggested by similar effects of *OsIMA1* and *OsIMA2* overexpression. This observation is similar to the absence of a phenotype of *AtIMA1* or *AtIMA2* knockdown *Arabidopsis* (Grillet *et al.*, 2018), although the single knockout mutant of *AtFEP1/AtIMA3* shows some defects in Fe translocation (Hirayama *et al.*, 2018). Our RNA-seq analyses indicated that knockdown of *OsIMA1*, but not *OsIMA2*, results in impaired expression of some Fe-related Fe-deficiency-inducible genes in Fe-sufficient roots (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Table S3, Fig. S9). This phenomenon could be explained by the extremely low expression of *OsIMA2* in Fe-sufficient roots (Fig. 1A), where *OsIMA1* might play a dominant role in the basal expression of Fe-related genes. Under Fe-deficiency, a strong induction of another *OsIMA* would complement the knockdown effect, which could account for only small alterations in susceptibility to Fe-deficiency and Fe accumulation (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S6).

Positive regulation of the Fe-related Fe-deficiency-inducible genes by OsIMAs was observed almost exclusively in the roots, not in the leaves (Table 1), although the expression of *OsIMAs* was induced more vigorously in the leaves than in the roots (Fig. 1A). These marked characteristics are also reported in *Arabidopsis* (Grillet *et al.*, 2018), along with the dominant expression of *AtIMA1* in the phloem cells of shoots and roots (Grillet *et al.*, 2018). Based on these results and grafting experiments, Grillet *et al.* (2018) proposed that IMA/FEPs are phloem-mobile peptides that positively regulate the Fe-deficiency response in the roots. They also reported that the AtIMA1 protein could bind divalent metals such as Fe<sup>2+</sup> and Mn<sup>2+</sup>, presumably at the acidic residues in the C-terminal conserved motif, raising the possibility for IMA/FEP to function as a Fe-sensing and signaling molecule in the phloem. Our results support such IMA function in rice, in which the C-terminal motif is fully conserved in OsIMA1 and OsIMA2 (Fig. S1), and overexpression of partial *OsIMA2* possessing the C-terminal motif was sufficient for enhancing its function (Table 1; Fig. 3–5).

### OsIMAs are responsible for plant species conserved- and grass-specific pathways and functions

As discussed above, the effects of OsIMAs resemble those of *Arabidopsis* IMA/FEPs (Grillet *et al.*, 2018; Hirayama *et al.*, 2018), suggesting functional <u>conservation of IMA/FEPs among plant species</u>. This is further supported by the Fe accumulation in *OsIMA1*-overexpressing *Arabidopsis* shoots and *AtIMA1*-overexpressing tomato fruits, in addition to the presence of Fe-deficiency-inducible *IMA/FEP* homologs with a C-terminal conserved motif in soybean (Grillet *et al.*, 2018). Therefore, <u>IMA/FEPs</u> are thought to have emerged before the divergence of graminaceous species, which utilize distinct mechanisms of Fe-acquisition (Strategy II). Nevertheless, our results indicate that rice OsIMAs positively regulate not only genes conserved among rice and non-graminaceous species, but also Poaceae-specific Strategy II genes for Fe(III)-DMA biosynthesis, uptake, and translocation (Table 1, Supplementary Table <u>S3</u>). This suggests that graminaceous plants have a newly acquired induction system of these genes via IMA/FEPs, presumably thorough the optimization of *cis*-acting elements for as yet-unknown IMA/FEP functions, in addition to the functions of the encoding proteins for Strategy II.

In contrast to the almost complete induction of Fe-related Fe-deficiency-inducible genes in *OsIMA* overexpression rice, *AtIMA* overexpression *Arabidopsis* plants show the induction of a subset, including the *subgroup Ib bHLH* genes, *AtIRT1* and *AtFRO2*, for Strategy I Fe uptake (Grillet *et al.*, 2018; Hirayama *et al.*, 2018). RNA-seq analyses of *AtIMA1*-overexpressing *Arabidopsis* roots suggest that the genes outside the AtIMA1 regulon include *PYE*, *BTS*, *AtIMA3/4/5/6/7/8*, and *AtOPT3* (Grillet *et al.*, 2018), although their close rice homologs *OsIRO3*, *OsHR21*, *OsIMA1/2*, and *OsOPT7* are strongly upregulated in *OsIMA*-overexpressing rice roots (Table 1, Supplementary Table S3). Therefore, it appears that rice has acquired an expanded range of IMA/FEP functions to induce almost all of the Fe-related genes. Moreover, *OsIMA*-overexpressing rice also showed strong induction of *MIR* (data not shown), an *Oryza* species-specific Fe-deficiency-inducible mitochondrial protein gene with an unknown function (Ishimaru *et al.*, 2009; de Oliveira *et al.*, 2020). Meanwhile, our correlation analyses using other Fe-deficiency-inducible genes (Supplementary Fig. S9), similar to *AtIMA1*-overexpressed *Arabidopsis* (Grillet *et al.*, 2018). Future investigation will clarify the possible roles and range of IMA/FEP functions outside the known Fe-related genes.

We noticed one outlier in this OsIMA regulation, *OsYSL2*, which was not upregulated by *OsIMA* overexpression, irrespective of its strong induction under Fe-deficiency (Table 1, Fig. 5A). OsYSL2 transports Fe(II)-NA and Mn(II)-NA and is involved in Fe and Mn translocation in the shoots towards the seeds, but not Fe uptake in the roots (Koike *et al.*, 2004; Ishimaru *et al.*, 2010). Functionally related homologs of *OsYSL2* are also present in non-graminaceous plants, such as *AtYSL1*, *AtYSL2*, and *AtYSL3* in *Arabidopsis*, but their expression is repressed under Fe-deficiency (DiDonato *et al.*, 2004; Le Jean *et al.*, 2005; Schaaf *et al.*, 2005; Waters *et al.*, 2006). It appears that rice has acquired the Fe-deficiency-inducible expression of *OsYSL2* by IDEF1, IDEF2, and subgroup IVc bHLH TFs for efficient Fe translocation under Fe limitation (Fig. 6; Kobayashi *et al.*, 2009, 2019b; Ogo *et al.*, 2008; Zhang *et al.*, 2017, 2020), but this system has not been equipped with the link to the IMA/FEP system, in contrast to all of the other known Fe-deficiency-inducible genes for Fe translocation.

Our results also showed that expression of Fe-deficiency-repressed genes, including ferritin genes for Fe storage (Briat *et al.*, 2009; Stein *et al.*, 2009) and VIT/VTL genes for possible Fe

sequestration in the vacuole (Gollhofer et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2012), are regulated differently between IMA-overexpressing rice and Arabidopsis. Expression of AtFer1, AtFer3, AtVTL1, AtVTL2, and AtVTL5 genes is induced in the roots of AtIMA1-overexpressing Arabidopsis (Grillet et al., 2018), but the expressions of their Fe-deficiency-repressed rice homologs OsFer1, OsFer2, and OsVIT2 are repressed in OsIMA overexpressing roots and induced in OsIMA1 knockdown roots (Table 1, Supplementary Table S3). Induction of Arabidopsis ferritin genes is also observed in AtIMA1overexpressing leaves, possibly due to excess Fe accumulation (Grillet et al., 2018). Indeed, AtIMAoverexpressing Arabidopsis plants suffer from severe Fe-toxicity (Grillet et al., 2018). Strikingly, the expressions of OsFer1, OsFer2, and OsVIT2 are repressed even in the leaves of OsIMA overexpression lines (Table 1, Supplementary Table S3), although they highly accumulate Fe (Fig. 4). These lines do not show apparent Fe-toxicity symptoms under normal Fe conditions, except for slowed seedling growth, which might be related to Fe-toxicity (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. S6). Rice plants are highly tolerant of Fe-excess conditions (Aung et al., 2018, 2019), which might affect the quantity or quality of the Fe-excess signals in *IMA* overexpression plants. In addition, the regulatory roles of IMA/FEPs on Fe-deficiency-repressed genes for Fe-sequestration might not be conserved between rice and Arabidopsis.

<u>AtIMA-overexpressing Arabidopsis shoots accumulate not only Fe but also other metals</u> including Mn and Zn, as reflected in the IMA nomenclature (IRON MAN; Grillet *et al.*, 2018). By contrast, metal accumulation of *OsIMA*-overexpressing rice shoots appears more specific to Fe, with only a moderate accumulation of Zn and Cu, but not Mn (Fig. 4). This difference in metal accumulation might be due to the differences in substrate specificity of Fe-deficiency-inducible chelators and transporters, such as the divalent cation transporter IRT.

Rice is a major staple crop, particularly in Asian countries, but is highly susceptible to low Fe availability (Mori et al., 1991) and its seeds contain a low concentration of Fe compared to other crops (Connorton and Balk, 2019), which makes it disadvantageous as a major supply of Fe in the human diet. Our results indicate that overexpression of either OsIMA1 or OsIMA2 confers an approximately 3-fold increase in the Fe concentration in brown seeds (Fig. 4E), accompanied by Fedeficiency tolerance (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. S6). Such phenotypes are advantageous for Fefortified crops grown in fluctuating conditions in neutral to alkaline soil, where Fe-deficiency symptoms occasionally appear. Because similar phenotypes are also achieved by HRZ knockdown or mutation (Kobayashi et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2017), a combination of IMA overexpression and HRZ repression would potentially confer further Fe accumulation, with improved tolerance to low Fe availability. Moreover, HRZ knockdown plants tolerate high Fe accumulation without any growth penalty under normal Fe conditions (Kobayashi et al., 2013; Aung et al., 2018), presumably because of the highly induced expression of OsYSL2 and NAS and the resultant NA-based protection from Fetoxicity (Kim et al., 2005; Aung et al., 2019). Therefore, a combination of HRZ manipulation, along with consideration of promoters for driving OsIMA, would represent promising avenues to overcome growth retardation and potential Fe-toxicity of OsIMA overexpression lines.

In conclusion, our results provide evidence for deciphering the pathways of the Fe-deficiency responses in rice, and will aid in the production of Fe-fortified crops with improved traits.
