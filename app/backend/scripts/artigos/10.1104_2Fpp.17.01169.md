26 **Abstract** 

28 Increasing grain yield is the most important subject of crop breeding. Here, we report 29 the elevated expression of a conserved microRNA, OsmiR408, could positively 30 regulate grain yield in rice by increasing panicle branches and grain number. We 31 further showed that OsmiR408 regulates grain yield by downregulating its 32 downstream target, *OsUCL8*, which is an uclacyanin (UCL) gene of the phytocyanin 33 family. The knock down or knock out of *OsUCL8* also increases grain yield, while the 34 overexpression of *OsUCL8* results in an opposite phenotype. Spatial and temporal 35 expression analyses showed that *OsUCL8* was highly expressed in pistils, young 36 panicles, developing seeds and inflorescence meristem, and was nearly 37 complementary to that of OsmiR408. Interestingly, the OsUCL8 protein was localized 38 to the cytoplasm, distinctive from a majority of phytocyanins which localize to the 39 plasma membrane. Further studies revealed that the cleavage of *OsUCL8* by miR408 40 affects copper homeostasis in the plant cell, which in turn affects the abundance of 41 plastocyanin proteins and photosynthesis in rice. This is the first report of the effects 42 of miR408-*OsUCL8* in regulating rice photosynthesis and grain yield. Our study 43 further broadens the perspective of miRNAs and UCLs and provides important 44 information for breeding high-yielded crops through genetic engineering. 45 46 47 48 49 50 51 52

62 **Introduction** 

64 Increasing grain yield is the most important subject of crop breeding. To date, a 65 couple of genes related to grain yield have been identified and functionally 66 characterized. For example, *PTB1* regulates rice seed setting rate by affecting pollen 67 tube growth (Li et al., 2013). *Ghd7* and *Ghd8* regulate the number of rice grains per 68 panicle (Xue et al., 2008; Yan et al., 2011). *DEP1* controls panicle architecture and 69 grain number by promoting cell division (Huang et al., 2009). *GW2* and *GS3* are the 70 two major QTLs that regulate grain size (Fan et al., 2006; Song et al., 2007; Mao et al., 71 2010). Notably, most of the reported genes encode proteins and are identified in QTLs. 72 Despite the knowledge of these protein coding genes, our understanding of the 73 regulating networks that control rice grain yield remains limited, especially that 74 noncoding RNAs modulated agronomic traits.

75 MicroRNAs (miRNAs), a class of abundant small noncoding RNAs, have been 76 identified as important regulators of gene expression in plants, affecting many aspects 77 of plant development (Bartel, 2004). Recently, several miRNAs have been reported to 78 regulate rice grain yield. The perturbed OsmiR156-mediated regulation of a 79 transcription factor gene, *OsSPL14*, reduces the tiller number and increases the 80 number of panicle branches, resulting in an increasing of grain yield (Jiao et al., 2010; 81 Miura et al., 2010). We previously demonstrated that miR397 can improve rice yield 82 by increasing the grain size and promoting panicle branching (Zhang et al., 2013). 83 More recently, a rare mutation in *GS2* has been reported to affect the binding of 84 OsmiR396c, which causes elevated expression of *OsGRF4*, a gene encodes 85 growth-regulating factor 4 (OsGRF4), leading to an increase in grain weight and yield 86 (Li et al., 2016). These results illustrate the potential roles of miRNAs in controlling 87 rice yield-related traits.

88 Given the importance of crop yield improvement, we asked whether other 89 miRNAs in the rice genome could positively regulate rice grain size or other 90 yield-related traits. Our previous study has revealed that miR397 regulates rice grain 91 yield by affecting a blue copper protein, laccase. This mechanism is conserved 92 between monocots and dicots (Zhang et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2014), suggesting that

93 miRNA mediation of blue copper protein could be a novel method for improving rice 94 yield. More interestingly, we performed a genome-wide screening of miRNAs 95 expression during embryogenesis and post embryogenesis and found that, among all 96 the known miRNAs, only one conserved miRNA, miR408, showed the same 97 expression pattern with that of miR397 during embryogenesis. Coincidently, miR408 98 could also targets the blue copper protein (Luo et al., 2006; Zhu et al., 2008; Xue et al., 99 2009; Chen et al., 2011). Thus, we asked whether miR408 could regulate rice grain 100 yield or other agronomic traits.

101 MiR408 has been reported to target a variety of blue copper protein members, 102 including those in the phytocyanin family (Jones-Rhoades and Bartel, 2004; Sunkar et 103 al., 2005). Both phytocyanin and laccase are blue copper proteins, but belong to 104 different groups (Ryden and Hunt, 1993). Blue copper proteins could be divided into 105 three groups, including small blue proteins, blue oxidases and coagulation factors. 106 Laccase belongs to blue oxidases, and combines four coppers. Phytocyanin and 107 plastocyanin both belong to small blue proteins, and combine only one copper. 108 However, the functions of phytocyanin members in plant developmental processes, 109 especially in rice, are not fully understood. Notably, it has been revealed that a 110 member of the phytocyanin family is the target of miR408 in Arabidopsis and 111 responds to copper deficiency and light (Abdel-Ghany and Pilon, 2008). Another 112 member of the phytocyanin family was also reported as a target in wheat (Zhao et al., 113 2016), suggesting that miR408 is a conserved miRNA that regulates pathways that are 114 involved in phytocyanin.

115 In this study, we analyzed the functions of OsmiR408 and its target gene in rice. 116 We revealed that a high expression level of OsmiR408 or a knockout of its target gene, 117 *OsUCL8*, an uclacyanin (UCL) gene in the phytocyanin family, could promote 118 panicle branches and increase grain number, leading to higher grain productivity. We 119 further elucidated the molecular mechanisms of OsmiR408 activity, finding that the 120 cleavage of *OsUCL8* by miR408 affects copper homeostasis in plant cells, which in 121 turn affects the abundance of plastocyanin (PC) proteins and photosynthesis in rice. 122 Our study provides the first functional evidence of rice uclacyanins in regulating crop

- 123 agronomic traits and further broadens the function of miRNAs in breeding
- 124 high-yielded crops through genetic engineering.

#### 129 **OsmiR408 promotes panicle branching and increases grain weight**

130 To investigate the function of OsmiR408, we generated OsmiR408-overexpressing 131 (OXmiR408) lines (**Fig. 1A**) to evaluate the effect of OsmiR408 on rice development. 132 RNA blot hybridization was performed to analyze the expression levels of OsmiR408 133 in the OxmiR408 lines (**Fig. S1A**). The OXmiR408 transgenic plants grew slightly 134 faster than the wild-type (WT) plants and the heights of OXmiR408 plants (plant 135 height, ~112.3cm, p<0.001, n=100) were slightly taller than that of WT plants (plant 136 height, ~109.0cm, n=80) at the mature stage (**Fig. 1A**), indicating that miR408 might 137 positively regulate plant growth.

138 To further determine whether OsmiR408 positively regulates grain yield, we 139 measured yield-related traits in the OXmiR408 plants. Intriguingly, compared with the 140 wild-type plants, the OXmiR408 transgenic lines showed a significant increase in the 141 number of panicle branches and have slightly longer grains (**Fig. 1B**). The numbers of 142 primary and secondary branches in the OXmiR408 lines were approximately 14% and 143 22% higher than those in the WT plants, respectively, and the effective grains per 144 main panicle in the OXmiR408 plants were approximately 18% higher than those in 145 the WT plants (**Fig. 1C**). In addition, the 1,000-grain weight of the OXmiR408 was 146 also significantly increased (**Fig. 1C**). It has been reported that the number of vascular 147 bundles in the peduncle corresponds to the panicle branches and the grain number, 148 thus, we analyzed the peduncle vascular bundles. Consistently, the peduncle diameter 149 and the number of peduncle vascular bundles were also increased in the OXmiR408 150 main panicles (**Fig. 1D-E**). These results demonstrate the positive regulation of 151 OsmiR408 on increasing panicle branches and grain weight.

# 153 **MiR408 exert its functions through its downstream targets,** *uclacyanin-like* 154 *protein 8*

155 It is well known that miRNAs exert their functions on the development of 156 organisms through their downstream targets (Bartel, 2004; Jones-Rhoades et al., 157 2006). To determine the molecular mechanism by which of OsmiR408 regulates rice

158 development *in vivo*, we further investigated its downstream targets. In Arabidopsis,

- 159 three laccase genes, *LAC3*, *LAC12* and *LAC13*, and one plantacyanin gene have been
- 160 validated to be the targets of miR408 and respond to light and copper conditions

161 (Abdel-Ghany and Pilon, 2008). In rice, two members of the uclacyanin (UCL) family, 162 LOC\_Os03g50140 (*UCL8*) and LOC\_Os08g37670 (*UCL30*), were identified as the 163 targets of OsmiR408 (Zhou, 2010). However, a recent study on the variety-specific

164 regulatory schema for OsmiR408 expression in rice showed that the transcript 165 accumulation of *OsUCL30* does not correspond to the variation of miR408 expression 166 levels (Mutum et al., 2013). Further analysis of the expression pattern of *OsUCL30* 167 showed that it was relatively abundantly expressed in most of the examined tissues 168 (Ma et al., 2011). In contrast, the spatial expression pattern of OsmiR408 and 169 *OsUCL8* by qRT-PCR detection showed that OsmiR408 is highly expressed in flag 170 leaf, root and embryo, whereas the expression pattern of *OsULC8* is approximately 171 complementary with that of OsmiR408 (**Fig. 2A**). Thus, in this study, we mainly 172 focus on the regulatory schema of OsmiR408 and *OsUCL8*.

173 To further confirm the expression of OsmiR408 and *OsUCL8*, we analyzed their 174 spatial expression patterns of by β-glucuronidase (GUS) activity analysis and *in situ* 175 hybridization. Both OsmiR408 and *OsULC8* are expressed in inflorescence meristem, 176 young panicle and seeds of 5 days after pollination, but the expression levels of 177 *OsUCL8* in young panicle and pistil are higher than that of OsmiR408 (**Fig. 2B-C**). 178 Moreover, the mRNA abundant of *OsUCL8* is reduced in the OXmiR408 179 inflorescence meristem (**Fig. 2C**). We further validated the cleavage of *OsUCL8* 180 mRNA by OsmiR408 using RNA ligase–mediated rapid amplification of cDNA ends 181 (RLM-RACE), and found that the cleavage was between 11th and 12th base pairs of 182 the OsmiR408 target site (**Fig. S1B**). These results implied that OsmiR408 might play 183 a role in regulating panicle and seed development through cleaving *OsUCL8* mRNA.

184 To identify the effect of *OsUCL8* on grain yield in rice, we generated transgenic 185 plants that ectopically expressed *OsUCL8* (OXUCL8) and investigated their 186 phenotype (**Fig. 1A**). We used qRT-PCR to analyze the expression levels of *OsUCL8* 187 mRNA in these OXUCL8 transgenic lines (**Fig. S1C**). Because OsmiR408 was found 188 to promote panicle branching, to increase grain number per panicle and grain weight, 189 we then evaluated the effects of *OsUCL8* on the traits of panicles and grains. We 190 hypothesized that the overexpression of *OsUCL8* might have a negative phenotype 191 compared with that of the overexpressing OsmiR408 plants. Notably, OXUCL8 plants 192 showed semi-dwarf phenotypes (plant height, ~98.3cm, p<0.001, n=90), the number 193 of panicle branches and effective grains per panicles decreased dramatically (**Fig.**  194 **1C-F**), and the 1,000-grain weight of the OXUCL8 plants was also significantly 195 decreased compared with the WT plants (**Fig. 1B-C**). The peduncle diameter and the 196 number of peduncle vascular bundles were also decreased in the OXUCL8 main 197 panicles (**Fig. 1D-E**).These results indicated that the phenotypes observed in the 198 plants with high expression levels of OXUCL8 were opposite to those observed in the 199 OXmiR408 plants, which expressed lower levels of *OsUCL8* than the wild-type 200 plants.

201

202 To further validate the function of *OsUCL8*, we constructed transgenic plants that 203 knock down *OsUCL8* by RNAi (*UCL8*-RNAi) and plants that knock out *OsUCL8* by 204 CRISPR-Cas9 (*ucl8*) (**Fig. 1A, Fig. S1C-D**). Consistently, the plants that knock down 205 or knock out *OsUCL8* had more panicle branches and effective grains than those of 206 the WT plants, and their 1,000-grain weight and the grain yield per plant were higher 207 than those of the WT plants (**Fig. 1B-C**). To observe the actual grain yield in the 208 OXmiR408, OXUCL8, *ucl8* and *UCL8*-RNAi plants, we conducted plot field 209 experiment of different transgenic lines with different OsmiR408 and *OsUCL8* 210 expression levels. Overall, compared with WT plot yield, overexpression of 211 OsmiR408 increased plot yield by ~16.2%, and knockout of *OsUCL8* increased plot 212 yield by ~21.4% (**Table. 1**). Moreover, the expression level of OsmiR408 is positively 213 correlated with grain yield, and the level of *OsUCL8* is negatively correlated with 214 grain yield (**Table. 1, Fig. S1C**). The observations above suggest that miR408 215 regulates rice panicle branching and grain weight via *OsUCL8*.

# 217 **Subcellular localization of OsUCL8 protein and its effects on plastocyanin** 218 **abundance and photosynthesis**

219 We then asked about the mechanism by which *OsUCL8* exerts its effect. *UCLs* 220 are members of the ancient and plant-specific phytocyanin family, which is a 221 subfamily of blue copper proteins (Nersissian et al., 1998; Ma et al., 2011). Thus far, 222 most of the research on phytocyanin has been on their biochemical characteristics and 223 redox properties (Sato et al., 2007; Guzzi et al., 2008; Ma et al., 2011; Cao et al., 224 2015). Recent studies showed that phytocyanin is a pollen tube growth-controlling 225 protein (Dong et al., 2005) . However, the subcellular location and function of UCL8 226 remain largely unknown.

227 We firstly evaluated the subcellular location of OsUCL8. Bioinformatic analysis 228 suggested that lots of phytocyanins are predicted to have 229 glycosylphosphatidylinisotol-anchor signals responsible for plasma membrane 230 localization, and several members have been identified to be plasma membrane 231 localized (Ma et al., 2011). To determine the subcellular localization of *OsUCL8*, we 232 fused GFP to the C-terminus and N-terminus of OsUCL8. These plasmids were then 233 transiently transformed into rice protoplasts. The fluorescence signal in the protoplasts 234 showed that the *OsUCL8* protein localized in the cytoplasm but not the plasma 235 membrane (**Fig. 2D**), indicating that the subcellular localization of OsUCL8 is different 236 from other phytocyanins reported.

237 The observations above indicated that OsUCL8 is specifically located in the 238 cytoplasm, suggesting that it might have a different function with UCLs located in the 239 plasma membrane. Although miR408 has different target genes in different species, its 240 targets all encode copper proteins. Moreover, miR408 responds to Cu deficiency and is 241 involved in Cu dynamics through its target genes. Copper is an essential micronutrient 242 in plants, and copper proteins, including UCLs and plastocyanin (PC), competitively 243 bind copper ions. Previous studies showed that under conditions of Cu deficiency, Cu is 244 preferentially supplied to PC, which is one of the most abundant copper proteins and is 245 essential to photosynthetic electron transport. We speculated that OsmiR408 might 246 regulate copper homeostasis through its target, *OsUCL8*. We then determined the total 247 copper content and the chloroplastic copper content in OXmiR408, OXUCL8 and WT 248 plants. We found that copper uptake in chloroplast is affected when *OsUCL8* levels are 249 altered (**Fig. 3A-B**).

250 In Arabidopsis, increased levels of miR408 under Cu deficiency could repress its 251 target laccase to maintain PC abundance (Zhang et al., 2014). Thus, we asked if

252 OsUCL8 affected the function of PC in rice. To address this question, we collected 253 proteins from the panicles and flag leaves of the OXmiR408, OXUCL8 and WT plants 254 for western blot analysis of PC. In Arabidopsis there are two PC proteins (AtPETE1 and 255 AtPETE2), and they have only one ortholog in rice (LOC\_Os06g01210), which is 55.4% 256 identical to AtPETE1 and 61.8% identical to AtPETE2. We transiently transformed 257 OsPC-Flag into rice protoplasts and detected OsPC by both anti-Flag antibody and 258 anti-PC antibody, and the result showed that the PC antibody is specific for OsPC 259 protein (**Fig. S1E**). We then used PC antibody to detect rice endogenous PC protein. As 260 expected, high expression levels of OsUCL8 decreased PC abundance, while 261 over-expression of OsmiR408 caused a slight increase in the amount of PC, both in the 262 panicles and flag leaves (**Fig. 3C**).

263 PC is an electron carrier for the photosynthetic machinery and is located in the 264 thylakoid lumen of the chloroplast. Up-regulation of PC proteins in the OXmiR408 265 plants suggested that miR408 might affect the photosynthesis. We next measured the 266 light-responsive curves and chlorophyll florescence of the flag leaves in transgenic 267 plants. The results showed that the photosynthetic rate and the photosynthetic electron 268 transport rate were indeed upregulated in the OXmiR408 plants and downregulated in 269 the OXUCL8 plants (**Fig. 3D-F**). We further analyzed the sugar (fructose, glucose, 270 sucrose, and starch) content of the transgenic plants because the photosynthetic rate 271 directly regulates carbon assimilation. Consistently, the OXmiR408 plants accumulate 272 more sugars in the leaves and panicles than the WT plants, while the sugar content in 273 the OXUCL8 plants was less than that of the WT plants (**Fig. 3G-H**). Consistently, 274 OsmiR408 was highly expressed in the flag leaf(**Fig. 2C**), which suggests that the 275 expression of OsmiR408 in the flag leaf might be necessary for the abundance of PC 276 and photosynthesis. The results show that the OXmiR408 plants were more efficient at 277 saving and converting light energy into sugars, suggesting that OsmiR408 might 278 promote grain yield through regulating the PC content and photosynthesis by 279 downregulating *OsUCL8*.

## 281 **Transcriptome analysis further showed that OsmiR408-mediated** *OsUCL8* **acts**

### 282 **through the photosynthesis pathway**

283 The results indicated that miR408 regulates rice grain yield by down-regulating

284 *OsUCL8* and controlling photosynthesis. To further reveal the biological pathways 285 that uclacyanin members might regulate to control rice grain yield-related traits, we 286 performed RNA-seq on the seedlings and young panicles of wild-type, OXmiR408 287 and OXUCL8 plants to screen for differentially expressed genes. We generated an average of 3.1 x 10<sup>7</sup> 288 reads per sample, and the gene expression profiles derived from 289 RNA-seq were estimated using FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per 290 Million reads mapped). To investigate the functions of the differentially expressed 291 genes, we performed gene ontology (GO) analyses and KEGG pathway analysis. The 292 differentially expressed genes could be divided into several groups, including the 293 photosynthesis group, and two photosynthesis-related pathways (starch and sucrose 294 metabolisms, photosynthesis - antenna proteins) (**Fig. 4A**). These were included in the 295 top ten enriched pathways, and the photosynthesis - antenna proteins pathway was the 296 most significantly enriched pathway (**Fig. 4B**). We further confirmed the expression 297 of the photosynthesis-related genes by qRT-PCR to validate the RNA-seq data (**Fig.**  298 **4C**). Moreover, the genes that were differentially expressed between the OXmiR408 299 and WT plants were significantly enriched in photosynthesis and sugar metabolism 300 GO terms, including photosynthesis light harvesting, starch catabolic process and 301 sucrose catabolic process (**Fig. 4D**,**Fig. S2**). These results are consistent with the 302 effects of miR408 and *OsUCL8* on photosynthesis, sugar content and grain yield*.*

#### 305 **Discussion**

# 307 **Novel function of phytocyanin proteins**

308 Plant phytocyanins are blue copper proteins and function as electron transporters. 309 Most of the phytocyanins are expected to have glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchor 310 signals (Ma et al., 2011). Phytocyanins can be categorized into three families, 311 including uclacyanins (UCLs), stellacyanins (SCLs) and nodulin (ENOD)-like 312 proteins (ENODLs) (Ma et al., 2011). Previous studies have indicated that 313 phytocyanin proteins are involved in various plant activities, including pollen tube 314 germination and anther pollination (Kim et al., 2003; Dong et al., 2005), reproductive 315 potential determination (Khan et al., 2007), and apical buds organ development 316 (Yoshizaki et al., 2000). However, the role of uclacyanins in plant development is 317 poorly identified.

318 In this study, we revealed the functions of *OsUCL8*, which belongs to the 319 uclacyanin family. *OsUCL8* is subcellularly located in the cytoplasm, not in plasma 320 membrane or chloroplast as most other phytocyanin family members. We 321 demonstrated that *OsUCL8* is regulated by OsmiR408 and is associated with panicle 322 branching and grain weight by regulating plastocyanin content and photosynthesis. 323 This is the first report on the effects of UCLs on photosynthesis and grain yield 324 regulation.

325

# 326 **miR408 and miR397 are two miRNAs that positively regulate rice grain yield**

327 Identifying new genes that modulate agronomic traits, especially grain yield, 328 would facilitate the breeding of new varieties with increased yields. MiRNAs have 329 been identified as important regulators of gene expression and are involved in many 330 aspects of plant development. For example, OsmiR156 negatively controls the 331 number of panicle branches and grain yield. Down-regulation of OsmiR396c causes 332 elevated expression of OsGRF4, leading to increased grain weight and yield (Li et al., 333 2016). However, miRNAs that positively regulate grain size and grain yield are very 334 few, and currently, only one such miRNA, miR397, was reported in both rice and Arabidopsis11,13 335 **.**

336 In this study, we found another conserved miRNA, miR408, which positively 337 regulates grain yield. Intriguingly, miR408 shows the same expression pattern as 338 miR397 (Luo et al., 2006; Zhu et al., 2008; Xue et al., 2009; Chen et al., 2011) and 339 also targets a blue copper protein, as does miR397. We have previously reported that 340 OsmiR397 increases grain yield by downregulating its target, *OsLAC*, a gene 341 encoding a laccase-like protein (Zhang et al., 2013). *OsLAC* does not affect PC 342 protein content; instead, it was found to be involved in the sensitivity of plants to 343 brassinosteroids (Zhang et al., 2013). In contrast, miR408 regulates rice grain yield by 344 regulating a uclacyanin protein and affecting PC abundance, which in turn affects 345 photosynthesis, suggesting that the regulatory networks of blue copper proteins 346 involved are complicated. Altogether, our results indicate that miRNA-regulation of 347 blue copper proteins may be a novel method for improving rice yield.

348

#### 349 **Multiple methods for controlling copper ion homeostasis in plant cells**

350 Homeostasis of copper ions is essential for plant development and physiology 351 (Bashir et al., 2016). Copper is a cofactor for various enzymes and plays roles in 352 photosynthetic and respiratory electron transport, oxidative stress protection, ethylene 353 perception and cell wall metabolism. Additionally, Cu deficiency leads to abnormal 354 root, stem and leaf development. Thus, it is necessary to control copper ion 355 homeostasis in plant cells.

356 The plant utilizes chelating agents to solubilize metals, and transporters actively 357 participate in the regulation of cellular metal homeostasis. However, compared with 358 other metals, such as Fe, Zn and Mn, Cu homeostasis is poorly understood. Cu enters 359 the plant cell via a family of high-affinity copper transporter proteins (COPT) (Grotz 360 and Guerinot, 2006). Cu is used as a cofactor by plant proteins involved in 361 neutralizing reactive oxygen species, lignification of the cell wall, ethylene perception 362 and formation of phenolic compounds (Burkhead et al., 2009). Plants can maintain 363 copper homeostasis and prioritize the use of cellular copper by regulating the 364 abundance of these proteins.

365 Interestingly, some of these copper proteins are targeted by several miRNAs, 366 including miR397, miR398, miR408, miR528 and miR857, and the pathways 367 mediated by these miRNAs are important in controlling copper ion homeostasis 368 (Abdel-Ghany and Pilon, 2008)**.** It has been shown that miR408 targets laccases and 369 regulates Cu homeostasis in Arabidopsis (Zhang et al., 2014)**.** In this study, we found 370 that in rice, miR408 targets another copper protein, *OsUCL8* and affects Cu 371 homeostasis, suggesting that miR408 is important in regulating blue copper protein 372 pathways and Cu homeostasis in both monocots and dicots.

373

### 374 **Specific regulatory schema of** *UCL* **regulation by miR408**

375 miR408-mediated regulation of specific plant blue copper proteins has been 376 reported widely conserved across distant plant species. In Arabidopsis, miR408 377 targets laccase genes *LAC3*, *LAC12 LAC1*3 and *plantacyanin1* (Abdel-Ghany and 378 Pilon, 2008). In rice, two *OsUCLs* have been predicted to be the targets of miR408 379 (OsUCL8 and OsUCL30), which have been validated by 5′ RACE (Zhou, 2010). 380 However, a previous study made an interesting observation that miR408 levels are not 381 always inversely related with that of its target genes, although the target mRNAs were 382 cleaved by miR408 experimentally. For example, *OsUCL30* was confirmed to be the 383 target of miR408 (Zhou, 2010). However, its transcript accumulation is stable in 384 most tissues and does not match the expression pattern of miR408, indicating the 385 complex nature of the relationship between miR408 and its target genes. Thus, it is 386 necessary to examine the expression patterns of miRNAs and the target genes before 387 functional analysis.

## 390 **Conclusions**

391 Increasing grain yield is the most important aspect of crop breeding. In this study, we 392 report a conserved miRNA, OsmiR408, that is a positive regulator of grain yield in 393 rice. OsmiR408 regulates grain yield by downregulating its downstream target, 394 *OsUCL8*, which is an uclacyanin (UCL) gene of the phytocyanin family. We further 395 elucidated the molecular mechanisms of OsmiR408 activity, finding the abundance of 396 *OsUCL8* affects copper homeostasis in the plant cell, which in turn affects the 397 abundance of plastocyanin (PC) proteins and photosynthesis in rice. This is the first 398 report of the effects of miR408-UCLs on photosynthesis and grain yield regulation. 399 Our study provides important information for breeding high-yield crops through 400 genetic engineering.

### 431 **Northern blot analysis**

432 Total RNAs were isolated from seedlings of rice with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). 433 Northern blot analysis was performed as described with some modifications (Zhang et 434 al., 2013). A 100-µg aliquot of total RNA from different transgenic plants was 435 separated on a denaturing 10% polyacrylamide gel at 300 V for 3.5 h, then RNA was 436 transferred to Hybond-N+ membranes (Amersham, GE Life Sciences) using a 437 semi-dry blotting apparatus (Bio-Rad). After electroblotting, the RNAs were fixed to 438 the membrane by UV cross-linking for 4 min and then baked at 80°C for 50 min. 439 DNA oligonucleotides complementary to the miRNA sequence were synthesized (Sangon, Shanghai). The 5′ ends of the DNA probes were labeled with [γ-32P 440 ]ATP 441 (Yahui Co.) using T4 polynucleotide kinase (TaKaRa). ## 475 **Plot field experiment**

476 Plants of WT, OXmiR408, OXUCL8, *ucl8* and UCL8-RNAi were grown under 477 natural condition in Guangzhou and Zhuhai respectively in China. The planting 478 density was 15cm×25cm, with one plant per hill. Each plot contained 8x8 plants. We 479 chose 4 OXmiR408 lines and 4 OXUCL8 lines with different levels of OsmiR408 or 480 OsUCL8 respectively, and each line has been divided into 8 plots. WT, ucl8 and 481 UCL8-RNAi plants were also been divided in to 8 plots. The grain yield per plant and 482 grain yield per plot were determined when the seeds were harvested. Data are from 483 randomized complete block design with eight replications. Values are means ± s.d.

#### 485 **Histochemical GUS staining**

486 A binary vector, pCAMBIA1303 was used to generate the GUS-OsmiR408 and 487 GUS-OsUCL8 transgenic lines. The ~2000 bp regions upstream of the OsmiR408 or 488 OsUCL8 were used as the promoter region to control the expression of GUS gene. 489 The GUS activity in the transgenic plants was localized by histochemical staining 490 with 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-β-D-glucuronic acid (X-Gluc). Transgenic plants 491 were cut and incubated overnight at 37°C in staining buffer (1 mM X-Gluc, 100 mM 492 sodium phosphate [pH 7.0], 10 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM K4Fe(CN)6, 0.5 mM K3Fe(CN)6, 493 and 0.1% [v/v] Triton X-100) and then destained in 70% ethanol before 494 photographing.

#### 496 **In situ hybridization**

497 RNA in situ hybridization was performed as described previously with minor 498 modifications (Kouchi and Hata, 1993). Briefly, plant materials were fixed in FAA 499 fixative for 8 hr at 4 °C after vacuum infiltration dehydrated using a graded ethanol 500 series followed by a xylene series, and embedded in Paraplast Plus (Sigma-Aldrich). 501 Microtome sections (8 μm) were mounted on Probe-OnTM Plus microscope slides 502 (Fisher). The 214-bp region of OsUCL8 was amplified with the primers 5'- 503 TGTTCAGGTACTTGCCGTGG -3' and 5'- TGTTTCTCCCTCAGTCACACG -3' 504 and then subcloned into the pEASYTM-T3 (TransGen Biotech) vector and used as the 505 template to generate sense and antisense RNA probes. The antisense probe was 506 transcribed using T7 RNA polymerase, and the sense probe was synthesized using 507 SP6 RNA polymerase. Digoxigenin-labeled RNA probes were prepared using a DIG 508 RNA Labeling Kit (SP6/T7) (Roche) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The 509 custom 5' and 3' double-labeled LNA-modified oligonucleotides (EXIQON) 510 (5'-CUGCACUGCCUCUUCCCUGGC-3') was used to detect OsmiR408-3p 511 produced from pre-miRNAs (miRNA precursors). A scrambled miRNA control probe 512 was used as a negative control. Photomicrographs were taken using a bright-field 513 microscope (Leica DM5000B).

#### 515 **Subcellular localization**

516 Two-week-old rice shoots were used to isolate protoplast. A bundle of rice plants 517 (approximately 30 seedlings) was cut together into approximately 0.5 mm strips with 518 propulsive force using sharp razors. The strips were incubated in an enzyme solution 519 (1.5% cellulose RS, 0.75% macerozyme R-10, 0.6 M mannitol, 10 mM MES at pH 520 5.7, 10 mM CaCl2 and 0.1% BSA) for 4-5 h in the dark with gentle shaking (40-50 521 rpm). After the enzymatic digestion, an equal volume of W5 solution (154 mM NaCl, 522 125 mM CaCl2, 5 mM KCl and 2 mM MES at pH 5.7) was added, followed by 523 shaking (60-80 rpm) for 30 min. Protoplasts were released by filtering through 40-μm 524 nylon mesh into round bottom tubes, washing the strips with W5 solution 3-5 times. 525 The pellets were collected by centrifugation at 800 rpm for 3 min in a swinging 526 bucket. After washing once with W5 solution, the pellets were then resuspended in 527 MMG solution (0.4 M mannitol, 15 mM MgCl2 and 4 mM MES at pH 5.7) at a concentration of 2×106 cells mL-1 528 . PEG-mediated transfections were carried out as 529 previously described (Zhang et al., 2011). Protoplasts were observed using a confocal 530 laser scanning microscope (Zeiss 7 DUO NLO) at 488 nm and 561 nm excitation. All 531 manipulations described above were performed at room temperature.

532

#### 533 **Measurement of chloroplast copper content**

534 Leaves from plants of different genotypes were harvested and weighed, then washed 535 twice with double-distilled water. The plant material was then digested in 1% nitric 536 acid (Sigma-Aldrich) and used directly for copper analysis with inductively coupled 537 plasma-atomic emission spectroscopy as previously described (Zhang et al., 2014).

538 Chloroplasts were isolated from rice leaves as previously described (Kubis et al., 539 2008). Plant tissues were homogenized in chloroplast isolation buffer (0.3 M sorbitol, 540 5 mM MgCl2, 5 mM EGTA, 5 mM EDTA, 20 mM HEPES/KOH, pH 8.0, and 10 mM 541 NaHCO3). The extract was filtrated through two layers of Miracloth to remove cell 542 debris. Then, 6 mL of the filtrate was layered on a discontinuous Percoll density 543 gradient solution (upper layer, 3 ml of 40%; bottom layer, 1 ml of 80%) containing 544 isolation buffer and centrifuged at 8,000 x *g* for 10 min at 4°C. A band at the 40–80% 545 interface, which contains intact chloroplasts, was collected. Afterward, intact 546 chloroplasts were measured using 40 mg chlorophyll. The intact chloroplasts were 547 digested in 1% nitric acid and used directly for copper analysis with inductively 548 coupled plasma mass spectrometry.

549

### 550 **Photosynthesis**

551 Photosynthesis rates of flag leaves were measured using an infrared gas analyzer (Li-6400XT, LICOR, USA) with a 2 cm2 552 leaf chamber fluorescent light head 553 (6400-40, LICOR, USA). Light response curves were constructed at 28°C at relative 554 humidity > 60% in a saturating chamber with 400 ppm CO2 under a light density of 555 2200, 2000, 1800, 1600, 1400, 1200, 1000, 800, 600, 400, 200, 150, 100, 50, 20, 10 and 0 µmol photons m−2 s−1 556 . The investigation of the ETR and ФPSII were measured 557 in a fluorescence chamber with CO2 of 400 ppm under the light density of 600µmol photons m−2 s−1 558 . The data were collected with 30 individuals for each genotype.

559

#### 560 **Measurement of the glucose, fructose and starch content**

561 The glucose and fructose content was measured by GC-MS as previously described 562 (Lisec et al., 2006). Generally, 100 mg (fresh weight) flag leaves and spikelets were 563 harvested and ground into powder in liquid nitrogen. Then, 700 µL methanol and 200 564 µL of 0.2 mg/mL ribitol (Sigma-Aldrich) were added to the powder. The mixture was 565 shaken at 950 RPM at 70°C for 10 min. After centrifugation at 11,000 x *g* for 10 min, 566 the supernatant was transferred to a new tube and dried. Forty microliters of 567 methoxyamination reagent was added at 37°C and shaken for 2 h. Afterward, 40 µL of 568 N-methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide was added, and the mixture was 569 incubated at 37°C for 30 min. GC-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 570 7890A series. The gas chromatograph was combined with a quadrupole mass selective 571 detector (Agilent). Starch was measured using a starch assay kit (product number 572 SA20-1KT; Sigma-Aldrich).
