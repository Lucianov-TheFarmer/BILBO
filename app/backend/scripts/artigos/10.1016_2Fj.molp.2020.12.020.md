# Asymmetric epigenome maps of subgenomes reveal imbalanced transcription and distinct evolutionary trends in Brassica napus

# ABSTRACT

The complexity of the epigenome landscape and transcriptional regulation is significantly increased during plant polyploidization, which drives genome evolution and contributes to the increased adaptability to diverse environments. However, a comprehensive epigenome map of Brassica napus is still unavailable. In this study, we performed integrative analysis of five histone modifications, RNA polymerase II occupancy, DNA methylation, and transcriptomes in two B. napus lines (2063A and B409), and established global maps of regulatory elements, chromatin states, and their dynamics for the whole genome (including the An and Cn subgenomes) in four tissue types (young leaf, flower bud, silique, and root) of these two lines. Approximately 65.8% of the genome was annotated with different epigenomic signals. Compared with the Cn subgenome, the An subgenome possesses a higher level of active epigenetic marks and lower level of repressive epigenetic marks. Genes from subgenome-unique regions contribute to the major differences between the An and Cn subgenomes. Asymmetric histone modifications between homeologous gene pairs reflect their biased expression patterns. We identified a novel bivalent chromatin state (with H3K4me1 and H3K27me3) in B. napus that is associated with tissue-specific gene expression. Furthermore, we observed that different types of duplicated genes have discrepant patterns of histone modification and DNA methylation levels. Collectively, our findings provide a valuable epigenetic resource for allopolyploid plants.

# INTRODUCTION

The complexity of the epigenome landscape and transcriptional regulation is significantly increased during plant polyploidization, which is associated with genome evolution and increased adaptability to diverse environments [\(Cheng et al., 2018a\)](#page-14-0). *Brassica napus* (rapeseed) is an allotetraploid species that is cultivated as a major oil crop, and also serves as a model plant for studying polyploid morphology, evolution, and genomic characteristics. It originated some 7500 years ago from interspecific hybridization between an A-genome species, *Brassica rapa* (2n = 20, AA), and a C-genome species, *Brassica*

*oleracea* (2n = 18, CC). The release of the *B. napus* reference genome in recent years has accelerated research on *B. napus*functional genomics and breeding programs ([Chalhoub](#page-14-1) [et al., 2014;](#page-14-1) [Sun et al., 2017;](#page-15-0) [Zou et al., 2019](#page-15-1); [Song et al., 2020\)](#page-15-2).

Epigenetic modifications are an important facet of transcriptional regulation controlling diverse biological processes, including plant development and stress resistance, and yield and quality. Epigenetic-based research has led to considerable advances in the study of diploid plants, particularly *Arabidopsis thaliana* and rice [\(Hollister and Gaut, 2009;](#page-14-2) [Chen and Zhou, 2013;](#page-14-3) [You et al.,](#page-15-3) [2017\)](#page-15-3). Many studies in the model plant *A. thaliana* have revealed the functions of various chromatin modifications ([Zhang et al., 2009;](#page-15-4) [Roudier et al., 2011;](#page-15-5) [Sequeira-Mendes](#page-15-6) [et al., 2014](#page-15-6)). According to the accessibility and modification of chromatin, a large number of *cis*-regulatory elements have been identified in plants, which helped better understanding of their genomic characteristics [\(Zhang et al., 2012](#page-15-7); [Sullivan et al.,](#page-15-8) [2014;](#page-15-8) [Wang et al., 2017](#page-15-9); [Maher et al., 2018](#page-14-4); [Ricci et al., 2019;](#page-14-5) [Zhao et al., 2020](#page-15-10)).

Studies on polyploid cotton plants have shown that epigenetic modifications can regulate the expression imbalance between the allopolyploid cotton subgenomes, and that the D subgenome contains a large number of active histone marks and is characterized by lower levels of DNA methylation [\(Zheng et al., 2016\)](#page-15-11). Moreover, it has been observed that the basic gene expression level of the D subgenome is higher than that of the A subgenome, and non-CG DNA methylation has been found to reflect the expression bias between homeologous gene pairs ([Song et al., 2015](#page-15-12)). Dosage-dependent and -independent genes in *A. thaliana* tetraploids have been shown to be labeled and distinguished by different types of histone modifications and DNA methylation ([Shi et al., 2015](#page-15-13)). Research on the epigenomic map of wheat has indicated that evolutionary selection on gene regulation targets sequence and chromatin features and that *cis*-regulatory elements are highly conserved across subgenomes ([Li et al., 2019](#page-14-6)). The change in DNA methylation is also observed in synthetic allopolyploid *B. napus* ([Lukens et al.,](#page-14-7) [2006;](#page-14-7) [Xu et al., 2009](#page-15-14)). Collectively, the results from these studies indicate that epigenetic modifications are widely associated with the expression states of the complex genomes of polyploid plants. However, we still lack comprehensive epigenomic maps and dynamic landscapes of allopolyploids plants, from which we can determine the epigenetic basis for subgenomic imbalance and epigenetic modifications involved in dynamic gene expression in allopolyploids.

The polima (*pol*) system was the first recognized cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS) system to be effectively used in *B. napus* and has since been used in the breeding of hybrid cultivars worldwide ([Robbelen, 1991](#page-15-15) € ). At present, hybrid varieties account for 70% of the total *B. napus* cultivated area in China, and approximately 60% of these hybrids have been generated using the *pol* CMS system [\(An et al., 2014](#page-14-8); [L'Homme et al.,](#page-14-9) [1997;](#page-14-9) [Liu et al., 2012\)](#page-14-10). Of these, 2063A is an excellent *pol* CMS line of *B. napus*, and B409 is an excellent conventional variety that can be used as a 2063A restorer line. Together, 2063A and B409 have been hybridized to generate the HZ62 variety, which is widely cultivated in China. We aim to develop epigenome resources for *B. napus* as part of its ENCODE (Encyclopedia of DNA Elements) project. We used the chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) method in combination with other approaches to construct high-quality epigenome maps for four tissues (young leaf, flower bud, silique, and root) of the two lines 2063A and B409. We comprehensively analyzed and summarized the epigenomic landscape and differences between the An and Cn subgenomes of *B. napus*, and we characterized the epigenomic dynamics in different tissues. In the course of the study, we identified a novel bivalent chromatin state (CS) (with H3K4me1 and H3K27me3) in *B. napus*. The epigenomic information in this study can serve as a valuable reference for functional genomics research and the breeding of *B. napus*, as well as reference datasets for epigenetic research on other polyploid plants.

# RESULTS

### Mapping epigenome marks from four tissues of two B. napus lines

To examine epigenome features and identify *cis*-regulatory elements in *B. napus*, we generated 124 epigenetic datasets, including the genome-wide enrichment profiles of five histone modification marks (H3K4me3, H3K27ac, H3K4me1, H3K27me3, and H3K9me2), RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) occupancy, whole-genome DNA methylation, and the transcriptomes of four tissues (young leaf, flower bud, silique, and root) from *B. napus* lines 2063A and B409 ([Supplemental](#page-13-0) [Figure 1](#page-13-0) and [Supplemental Table 1\)](#page-13-0). We evaluated the quality of these datasets by calculating the number of uniquely mapped reads, the fraction of mapped reads in peaks, and inter-replicate correlation. The datasets satisfying quality controls were used for downstream analysis. Consistent with previous studies in other species [\(Zhang et al., 2009;](#page-15-4) [He et al., 2010;](#page-14-11) [Du et al., 2013;](#page-14-12) [Li et al., 2019](#page-14-6)), we found that the active histone marks H3K4me3 and H3K27ac were mainly located (and showed high co-occupancy) around the transcription start sites (TSSs) of active genes. The repressive histone mark H3K27me3 and transcribed gene mark H3K4me1 were mainly distributed in the gene body. RNAPII occupancy was enriched throughout the gene body, with a higher abundance near the transcription termination sites (TTSs) ([Supplemental Figure](#page-13-0) 2A and 2B). H3K9me2 was mainly located on transposable elements (TEs), particularly on Gypsy and Copia retrotransposons, whereas TEs harbored a smaller proportion of other histone marks ([Figure 1B](#page-2-0) and 1C; [Supplemental Figure 2C](#page-13-0)). These datasets provide global views of the epigenome landscape in multiple tissues of *B. napus*.

We further investigated the relationships between epigenetic modifications and the expression levels of homeologous gene pairs and subgenome-unique genes in the two subgenomes. Previous studies have shown that orthologous gene pairs in their respective progenitor genomes (*B. rapa* and *B. oleracea*) remain homeologous gene pairs in *B. napus*; the term An/Cn subgenome-unique genes refers to missing syntenic genes that have only a single copy in the An or Cn subgenome ([Chalhoub](#page-14-1) [et al., 2014\)](#page-14-1). Despite detecting no significant differences in the average expression levels of the homeologous gene pairs in the An and Cn subgenomes, we observed that 28% of the homeologous gene pairs showed expression bias (log2 fold change > 1.5, *q* value <0.05), which was associated with a difference in the signal intensity of the epigenetic marks ([Figure 1](#page-2-0)D and 1E). We found that the asymmetrical transcription of the An and Cn subgenomes could primarily be attributed to the differential expression of the subgenomeunique genes ([Figure 1](#page-2-0)D). As shown in [Figure 1](#page-2-0)E, similar gene expression levels correspond to similar levels of epigenetic modification signals in the two subgenomes, whereas a higher gene expression level is associated with lower levels of DNA methylation and H3K27me3, and higher levels of H3K4me3, H3K27ac, and RNAPII occupancy [\(Figure 1](#page-2-0)D–1G). Interestingly, we found that the average expression level of An subgenomeunique genes was higher than that of Cn subgenome-unique genes. The average level of H3K4me1 on An subgenomeunique genes was higher than that on Cn subgenome-unique genes, whereas the average enrichment level of H3K4me1 was similar for all three categories of homeologous gene pairs ([Figure 1](#page-2-0)E). These results indicate a relationship between epigenetic modifications and asymmetrical transcription of the An and Cn subgenomes, as well as the differential functions of H3K4me1 with respect to the transcription of homeologous gene pairs and An/Cn subgenome-unique genes.

We also characterized the expression patterns of genes modified by various epigenetic marks using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from 30 representative *B. napus* tissues [\(Supplemental](#page-13-0) [Table 2\)](#page-13-0). Here, a gene was defined as a housekeeping gene if the transcripts of that gene could be detected in more than 25 out of the 30 RNA-seq tissue datasets, whereas a gene was defined as tissue-specific if the transcripts could be detected in fewer than six tissues. Genes associated with RNAPII occupancy and active histone marks (H3K4me1, H3K4me3, and H3K27ac) tended to be expressed in a majority of the examined tissues. Conversely, most genes associated with the repressive histone mark H3K27me3 tended to be expressed in only a small number of tissues, whereas the bivalent modification (H3K4me3 and H3K27me3)-marked genes covered all categories of expression breadth [\(Figure 1H](#page-2-0)). A similar proportion of homeologous gene pairs in the Cn and An subgenomes were housekeeping genes; however, a larger proportion of subgenome-unique genes in the Cn subgenome were tissue-specific genes compared with those in the An subgenome ([Figure 1](#page-2-0)I). Although our analysis overestimates slightly the representativeness of gene classification from the 30 RNA-seq samples based on epigenomic data from one tissue, we can still see the overall trend that subgenome-unique genes were more tissue-specific than homeologous gene pairs in *B. napus*. Consistent with this, the proportions of homeologous gene pairs modified by different histone marks and occupied by RNAPII were similar in the two subgenomes, and the subgenome-unique genes harbored a smaller proportion of active marks in the Cn subgenome ([Figure 1J](#page-2-0)). Furthermore, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the homeologous gene pairs from the An and Cn subgenomes had similar GO terms: the homeologous genes defined as housekeeping genes were significantly enriched in terms related to essential function processes, including biosynthetic processes, structural molecule activity, and structural constituents of ribosomes ([Figure 1K](#page-2-0)). The tissuespecific-expressed subgenome-unique genes of the An and Cn subgenomes were enriched in different GO terms such as endopeptidase inhibitor activity, peptidase regulator activity stimulus in the An subgenome, and responses to stress in the Cn subgenome ([Supplemental Figure 2G](#page-13-0)). These results indicate that the homeologous pairs of housekeeping genes in the An and Cn subgenomes, which are mainly marked with active histone marks, tend to have similar functions, whereas the subgenomeunique genes in these two subgenomes tend to have tissuespecific functions and are mainly marked with H3K27me3.

DNA methylation, histone marks, and RNAPII occupancy are related to the expression bias of homeologous gene pairs between subgenomes, and they are also related to the asymmetry of the activity of genes specific to the An and Cn subgenomes. The asymmetric distribution of epigenetic marks may be one of the reasons for the asymmetric gene expression between the subgenomes of *B. napus*.

### Chromatin states, DNA methylation, genome function, and transcription in allotetraploid B. napus

We further characterized CSs according to their combinatorial patterns in multi-epigenomic datasets. Constructed from integrative analysis of epigenomic profiling data, CS maps can be used as foundational representations of genome annotation and transcription activity ([Ernst and Kellis, 2010](#page-14-13); [Lee et al., 2010;](#page-14-14) [Sequeira-Mendes et al., 2014](#page-15-6)). In the present study, we trained a 15-CS model using ChromHMM software at a 200-bp resolution along the genomes obtained for four tissues (young leaf, flower bud, silique, and root) from *B. napus* lines 2063A and B409.

We found that active states cover, on average, 21.3% of the reference epigenome and consist of promoter-associated states (weak active TSS/CS1, active TSS without RNAPII/CS2, and active TSS with RNAPII/CS3), transcription-associated states (flanking active TSS1-3/CS5-7, weak transcription/CS8, and strong transcription/CS9), an active TTS and flanking state (CS10), and an active intergenic state (CS12) [\(Figure 2](#page-4-0)A). The active promoter-associated states commonly have a higher frequency of H3K4me3, as well as low enrichment for TEs, with extremely low levels of DNA methylation, and cover 5.7% of the genome. These states differed with respect to the presence and levels of other active marks (H3K27ac and RNAPII occupancy), leading to varying expression levels of the marked genes ([Figure 2](#page-4-0)A). The transcription-associated states were predominantly associated with H3K4me1 and RNAPII occupancy and accounted for 12.9% of the genome. The strong transcription states

The heterochromatin state (Het/CS14) was defined by enrichment of H3K9me2 and covered 14.9% of the *B. napus* genome. We found that regions associated with CS14 consistently have the highest DNA methylation levels, highest TE abundance, and the lowest level of transcription. Most CS14 regions colocalized preferentially with TEs, mainly comprising retrotransposons (Gypsy, Copia, and SINE) and DNA transposons (CACTA and Mutator). The Quies state (Quiescent state/CS15) was devoid of any examined epigenetic marks and covered annotated intergenic regions of the genome, showing high enrichment in the SINE retrotransposon and DNA transposons [\(Figure 2](#page-4-0)A). Our results revealed that the *B. napus* epigenome can be partitioned into four categories: active states, repressive states, inactive state, and Quies state. With the exception of the Quies state, approximately 65.8% of the *B. napus* genome was annotated with at least one type of epigenomic mark. Different states have distinct levels of genome coverage, gene transcription, TE enrichment, and DNA methylation ([Figure 2](#page-4-0)A and 2B).

Previous studies have shown that allopolyploid plants may exhibit genome dominance [\(Garsmeur et al., 2013](#page-14-15)). We observed that,

although the CSs of the two subgenomes were not significantly different from the overall CSs, the proportions of some states were different [\(Figure 2](#page-4-0)C and [Supplemental Figure 3B](#page-13-0)). We compared the genome coverage of different CSs, and found that genomic coverages of the An subgenome with respect to active states (TssWk/CS1, TssA/CS2, TssAP/CS3, TssAFlnk1/ CS5, TssAFlnk2/CS6, TxWk/CS7, Tx/CS8, strong transcription/ CS9, and active TTS and flanking/CS10) and the intergenic repressed state (ReprPC/CS13) were higher than those of the Cn subgenome. In contrast, the genomic coverage of the heterochromatin state (Het/CS14) in the Cn subgenome was higher than that in the An subgenome [\(Figure 2C](#page-4-0)), which could be attributable to the large number of TEs in the Cn subgenome ([Figure 1B](#page-2-0)). Notably, we observed that genomic coverages of the active TSS without RNAPII state (CS2) and weak transcription state (CS8) were significantly higher in the An subgenome than in the Cn subgenome [\(Figure 2C](#page-4-0)). Accordingly, this observation suggests that the An subgenome is more active than the Cn subgenome at the CS level.

Meanwhile, we found that the CS models were similar between 2063A and B409 tissues ([Figure 2](#page-4-0)A; [Supplemental Figure 3A](#page-13-0) and 3B; [Supplemental Figure 4A](#page-13-0) and 4B). To evaluate the epigenetic differences between 2063A and B409, we calculated the differences in histone marks and RNAPII occupancy between these two lines. We found high levels of variation in H3K9me2, H3K27me3, and RNAPII between the two lines ([Supplemental Figure 5A](#page-13-0)). Although the CS models of 2063A and B409 are similar, the genome coverage in each state is different, especially for CS6, CS9, and CS13 [\(Supplemental](#page-13-0) [Figure 5B](#page-13-0)). We next examined the profiles of different epigenomic marks in 2063A and B409. There was no significant difference in histone mark intensities between the four assessed tissues of 2063A and B409, especially in the active histone marks [\(Supplemental Figure 5C](#page-13-0)). These results indicate that, although there are some proportional differences in some epigenetic marks (H3K9me2, H3K27me3, and RNAPII) between 2063A and B409, there is no significant difference at the epigenetic level.

Overall, these results reveal two different types of bivalent states (H3K4me3-H3K27me3 and H3K4me1-H3K27me3) in the *B. napus* genome, and show that the An subgenome is more active than the Cn subgenome at the CS level.

### The dynamic epigenomic landscape between B. napus tissues

To examine *B. napus* epigenomic dynamics and their potential relationships with respect to gene expression states, we systematically characterized the relationship between various epigenetic marks and gene expression in 2063A four tissue types (young leaf, flower bud, silique, and root). For both the An and Cn subgenomes, we observed that active genes associated with H3K4me3, H3K27ac, and RNAPII occupancy as well as repressed genes associated with H3K27me3 showed greater dynamics than those associated with H3K4me1 and H3K9me2 ([Figure 3A](#page-6-0)). Moreover, the intensities of H3K4me3 and H3K27ac were found to be strongly positively correlated with changes in gene expression among the four tissues, whereas the intensity of H3K27me3 showed a weak negative correlation with gene expression in the four tissues ([Figure 3](#page-6-0)B and 3D; [Supplemental Figure 6A](#page-13-0)). In addition, we observed a strong positive correlation between the H3K4me3 and H3K27ac fold changes, and for most genes with changes in both H3K4me3 and H3K27ac, the changes in these marks were positively correlated with changes in gene expression, whereas a negative correlation between H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 and fold change in gene expression was detected in the four assessed tissues ([Figure 3](#page-6-0)C and 3D; [Supplemental Figure 6](#page-13-0)B).

Furthermore, we found that the amplitude of gene expression and variability of chromatin marks in the An subgenome were higher than those in the Cn subgenome [\(Supplemental Figure 7A](#page-13-0)). However, this difference in gene expression amplitude can be ascribed to differences in the genes unique to the two subgenomes, rather than to differences between homeologous gene pairs ([Figure 3](#page-6-0)E). Correspondingly, we found that the variability of these histone marks (H3K4me3, H3K27ac, H3K4me1, and H3K27me3) and RNAPII occupancy of genes unique to the An subgenome were higher than those of genes unique to the Cn subgenome [\(Figure 3E](#page-6-0)). These results thus indicate that the imbalanced expression of genes between the An and Cn subgenomes in *B. napus* might be related, at least in part, to the differential epigenetic status of subgenome-unique genes.

We then examined the trends and epigenetic basis of the asymmetric expression of homeologous gene pairs between the An and Cn subgenomes in the four assessed tissues, and detected 2852 homeologous gene pairs characterized by asymmetric expression in the four assessed tissues ([Figure 3F](#page-6-0)). We found that signal intensities of the active marks (H3K4me3, H3K27ac, H3K4me1, and RNAPII) were strongly positively correlated with the biased expression of homeologous genes in the four assessed tissues, including 1463 genes with An-biased expression and 1389 genes with Cn-biased expression ([Figure 3](#page-6-0)G and [Supplemental Figure 7](#page-13-0)B).

By comparing young leaf and root tissues, we investigated the relative frequency of switches from one CS to another CS in different tissues. With the exception of the weak transcription state/CS8 defined only by H3K4me1, we detected high levels of switching within promoter regions (CS1-3) and transcribed regions (CS5–10). A high frequency of CS switching was also apparent between the H3K27me3-marked states, such as switching from the bivalent promoter state (CS4) and the bivalent genic state (CS11) to the intergenic repressed state (CS13). In contrast, low levels of switching were detected for the intergenic repressed (CS13), heterochromatin (CS14), and Quies (CS15) states [\(Figure 3H](#page-6-0)). These results are consistent with the high variability of active and repressive marks, and the low variability of inactive marks in tissues ([Figure 3A](#page-6-0)), and indicate the robust dynamics of promoters and gene transcription versus the relative stability of heterochromatin and intergenic regions across *B. napus* tissues. Furthermore, we found that the switching patterns of DNA methylation were similar to those of the histone marks ([Supplemental](#page-13-0) [Figure 7](#page-13-0)C), thereby indicating the close correlation between histone marks and DNA methylation. #### A novel bivalent chromatin state associated with H3K4me1 and H3K27me3 in B. napus

Our *B. napus* epigenomic maps reveal a new bivalent CS characterized by H3K4me1 and H3K27me3 co-modification (the bivalent genic state/CS11), which differs from the classic H3K4me3-H3K27me3 bivalent state ([Hammoud et al., 2009\)](#page-14-16). H3K27me3 is a typical mark of repressive chromatin, and H3K4me1 is enriched in transcribed gene bodies in plants. This CS covers 25.12% of the H3K27me3 peaks and encompasses 8551 genes in 2063A young leaf tissue. We first verified the authenticity of the CS11 by forward and reverse ChIP-reChIP experiments using 2063A young leaf tissue (H3K4me1-ChIP/ H3K27me3-reChIP and H3K27me3-ChIP/H3K4me1-reChIP), and found that 89.05% of new bivalent CS regions was overlapped ([Figure 4](#page-8-0)A and 4B), thereby indicating that a genuine H3K4me1-H3K27me3 bivalent CS (CS11) exists in the *B. napus* genome. CS11 is mainly enriched in gene body regions, which is consistent with the distribution of H3K4me1 and H3K27me3 ([Figure 4](#page-8-0)C). Subsequent analysis indicated that the lengths and exon lengths of genes marked by CS11 were similar to those of genes marked by H3K4me1 only. The exon number and expression levels of CS11-covered genes were comparable with those of genes marked by H3K27me3 only [\(Supplemental](#page-13-0) [Figure 8](#page-13-0)A–8D). In addition, we observed that CS11-marked genes showed highly tissue-specific expression patterns, which were higher than those of genes marked by H3K27me3 alone ([Figure 4D](#page-8-0)), thereby indicating the potential regulatory roles of the H3K4me1-H3K27me3 bivalent CS in gene-specific expression in *B. napus*.

We further examined the dynamics of genes marked by CS11 across the four tissue types (young leaf, flower bud, silique, and root), and found that a large percentage of genes modified by CS11 had changed in the four assessed tissues [\(Figure 4E](#page-8-0)). When comparing the young leaf to the root, we found that 15.9% of the total CS dynamics were caused by the change of CS11, and 62% of CS11 regions were changed between two tissues. Among the observed changes in CS11, we found that 21.8% and 22.5% of the transitions were from CS11 to active states and silenced states, respectively, whereas the proportion of transitions from active states to the CS11 state was 15.3%. We also detected rare (2.4%) transitions from silenced states to the CS11 state ([Figure 4F](#page-8-0)). Our results revealed similar CS transitions between CS11 and other states in the four assessed tissues [\(Supplemental Figure 8E](#page-13-0)–8I). Furthermore, we found that CS11 was sometimes converted to bivalent promoter/CS4, weak transcription/CS8, or intergenic repressed state/CS13 in the CS transitions between the four assessed tissues ([Figure 4](#page-8-0)G).

We observed 14119 genes characterized by new bivalent CS (CS11) in the 2063A young leaf, 2267 of which switched to active states (CS1–CS3 and CS5–CS10) in the root. GO enrichment analysis revealed that these genes are mainly involved in transmembrane transport, cell wall organization or biogenesis, and responses to oxidative stress [\(Figure 4H](#page-8-0)). We also characterized the CS dynamics of the *RSL2* gene, a positive regulator of root hair growth in *A. thaliana* ([Yi et al., 2010\)](#page-15-17). There are six copies of this gene in the *B. napus* genome ([Supplemental](#page-13-0) [Table 3](#page-13-0)) and all are modified by new bivalent CS, and gene silencing is maintained in the young leaf, flower bud, and silique. In the root of *B. napus*, however, the active marks H3K4me3 and H3K27ac and RNAPII were present on these six *Bna.RSL2* genes, and gene expression levels were found to be higher [\(Figure 4I](#page-8-0); [Supplemental Figure 9](#page-13-0)A–9E). These results thus indicate that the new bivalent CS (CS11) is related to the tissue-specific gene expression in *B. napus* and may play an important role in plant growth.

#### Epigenetics, evolution, and dosage characteristics of the duplicated genes in B. napus

Given the complex genome duplication events that have occurred during the evolutionary history of *B. napus* [\(Chalhoub](#page-14-1) [et al., 2014\)](#page-14-1), we sought to characterize the epigenetic properties underlying the different types of duplicated genes in the *B. napus*. We classified the duplicated genes of *B. napus* into five categories: whole-genome duplication (WGD), tandem duplication (TD), proximal duplication (PD), transposed duplication (TRD), and dispersed duplication (DSD), using an analysis method similar to that in a previous study ([Supplemental Figure 10A](#page-13-0)) [\(Qiao et al., 2019\)](#page-14-17). Genes from WGD originate from genome duplication. Genes from TD arise through one or two genes that duplicate in tandem, which are closely adjacent to each other in the same chromosome ([Freeling, 2009\)](#page-14-18). Genes from PD may arise through localized transposon activities or originate from ancient tandem duplicates interrupted by other genes, which are near each other but separated by several genes ([Zhao et al., 1998;](#page-15-18) [Freeling et al., 2008](#page-14-19)). Genes from TRD are presumed to arise through distantly transposed duplications [\(Cusack and Wolfe,](#page-14-20) [2007](#page-14-20); [Wang et al., 2012\)](#page-15-19). Genes from DSD arise through unpredictable and random patterns of duplication without clear mechanisms ([Ganko et al., 2007](#page-14-21)).

Dosage dependent/independent is an essential evolutionary mechanism that influences the expression and fate of duplicated genes ([Scannell et al., 2007\)](#page-15-20). Gene expression changes would be caused by gene dosage balance alteration ([Thomas et al., 2006](#page-15-21); [Schnable et al., 2011](#page-15-22)). On the basis of the coefficients of determination (R<sup>2</sup> ) between dosage and expression level ([Shi](#page-15-13) [et al., 2015](#page-15-13); [Tan et al., 2016\)](#page-15-23), a previous study divided the *B. napus* genes in the An and Cn subgenomes into dosagedependent expression genes (AdCd, R2 > 0.59) and dosageindependent expression genes (AiCi, R<sup>2</sup> < 0.59) ([Tan et al.,](#page-15-23) [2016\)](#page-15-23). We characterized the differences in epigenetic features for these genes and found that the AdCd genes had higher levels of active marks (H3K4me1, H3K4me3, and H3K27ac), whereas the AiCi genes had higher levels of H3K27me3. Interestingly, we found that the RNAPII occupancy level of these two types of genes are similar ([Figure 5](#page-9-0)H and 5I; [Supplemental Figure 10G](#page-13-0)). These results indicate that epigenetic modifications may contribute to the differentiation of dosage-dependent and dosage-independent duplicated genes in the *B. napus* genome.

# DISCUSSION

Epigenetic modifications play an important role in the regulation of transcription, growth, and development, and systematic epigenomic analysis represents a novel approach to gaining a more comprehensive understanding of complex genomic information. A number of epigenomics research programs for human and other model organisms have been implemented to fully annotate functional genomic elements and generate reference epigenomic maps for cells and tissues of interest ([The ENCODE Project](#page-14-23) [Consortium, 2012](#page-14-23); [modENCODE Consortium, 2010](#page-14-24); [Shen et al.,](#page-15-24) [2012;](#page-15-24) [Yue et al., 2014](#page-15-25)). *B. napus* is a typical allopolyploid plant with two subgenomes and a highly complex genomic architecture. However, the *B. napus* research community is still lacking comprehensive epigenetic information. In this work, we have generated the largest library of epigenetic resources for *B. napus* to date and constructed comprehensive epigenomic maps, which will provide a valuable basis for studying the epigenetic features of allopolyploid genomes, the imbalance between subgenomes, regulation of the dynamic expression of genes, and the evolution of duplicated genes.

From an epigenetics perspective, we have identified the An subgenome as the dominant one in *B. napus*. Previous studies have found that there is an imbalance between the An and Cn subgenomes in *B. napus*, which can presumably be attributed to differences in the respective proportions of TEs [\(Chalhoub et al., 2014\)](#page-14-1). We found that H3K9me2 was mainly located on TEs, particularly on Gypsy and Copia retrotransposons [\(Figure 1B](#page-2-0) and 1C). Most heterochromatin state (Het/CS14) regions (defined by enrichment of H3K9me2) colocalized preferentially with TEs, mainly comprising retrotransposons (Gypsy, Copia, and SINE) and DNA transposons (CACTA and Mutator). The coverage of CS14 in the Cn subgenome was 17.1%, which was higher than that, 10.4%, in the An subgenome ([Figure 2](#page-4-0)C). The higher levels of H3K9me2 and DNA methylation in the Cn subgenome may be important factors contributing to the significantly lower levels of average gene expression in the Cn subgenome compared with that in the An subgenome [\(Figure 1B](#page-2-0); [Supplemental Figure 2](#page-13-0)D–2F). These results suggest that the An and Cn subgenome imbalance might be attributed to the combination of different epigenetic states and proportions of TEs in subgenomes.

Our data show that the most significant epigenomic differences between the An and Cn subgenomes are in the subgenomeunique genes/regions, and the bias of homeologous genes/regions to An or Cn is balanced. Subgenome-unique genes/regions in the An subgenome exhibit significantly higher intensities of active marks and lower intensities of inactive marks and DNA methylation levels than those in the Cn subgenome ([Figure 1](#page-2-0)D and 1E). We observed that the An subgenome is characterized by a higher proportion of active CSs than the Cn subgenome, whereas the opposite was found for the heterochromatin state ([Figure 2C](#page-4-0)). We found that subgenome-unique genes/regions in the An subgenome show greater dynamics than those in the Cn subgenome among the four assessed tissues [\(Figure 3](#page-6-0)E). These results are consistent with the fact that homeologous regions/gene pairs with similar proximal *cis*-regulated regions do not show obvious differences in gene expression, whereas the An/Cn subgenome-unique genes/regions showed imbalanced in gene expression.

Further, we identified and subsequently confirmed a novel H3K4me1-H3K27me3 bivalent CS in *B. napus* that was strongly associated with tissue-specific gene expression in *B. napus* ([Figure 4](#page-8-0)). We believe that these findings will inspire follow-up studies on the biological functions of this CS in the future.

Our results indicate that histone modifications and DNA methylation are widely related to maintaining the specific features of different types of duplicated genes in *B. napus*. The *B. napus* genome is characterized by a large number of duplicated genes, among which those in different classes show distinct patterns of temporal and functional evolution [\(Qiao et al., 2019](#page-14-17)). Our data show that TD- and PD-derived duplicated genes have higher levels of H3K27me3 and DNA methylation and are subject to higher selection pressure ([Figure 5](#page-9-0)A–5C). Given that H3K27me3-marked genes have been identified to be important

for plant growth and development [\(Yue et al., 2014](#page-15-25); [Cheng et al.,](#page-14-25) [2018b\)](#page-14-25), we speculate that TD- and PD-derived duplicated genes may exert their important roles during polyploidization in *B. napus*. Moreover, histone marks and DNA methylation are involved in the differentiation of PSGs and NSGs [\(Figure 5E](#page-9-0)–5G).

Overall, we have compiled the most comprehensive epigenetic datasets to date and undertaken the comprehensive analysis of *B. napus*. We hope that these epigenome maps of *B. napus* will provide a valuable reference for the gene annotation of polyploid plants, coordinated utilization of subgenomes, and the genetic regulation of traits for crop improvement.

### METHODS

#### Plant material and tissue collection

For the purposes of this study, we used young leaf, root, flower bud, and silique tissues from lines 2063A and B409 of *B. napus*, the parents of the elite *B. napus* hybrid HZ62 in China. For the collection of root and young leaf tissues, germinated seeds were grown in a phytotron with a day/night cycle of 16 h/8 h and a temperature of 22C/18C, respectively [\(Wan et al.,](#page-15-26) [2017](#page-15-26)). The roots were obtained 7 d after planting on moist gauze, and the young leaves were obtained from 2-week-old plants cultured hydroponically as described previously ([Wan et al., 2017\)](#page-15-26). For the other tissues, seeds were grown in soil under natural conditions at the experimental farm of Huazhong Agricultural University, Wuhan, China. Flower buds (%2 mm) and siliques (3–5 cm) were harvested for the investigation.

#### ChIP-seq library preparation

Tissues were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde and quenched with 0.2 M glycine. Approximately 0.5 g of samples were used for each experiment. Samples were ground in liquid nitrogen to a fine powder and then lysed in a 1.53 volume of buffer S (50 mM HEPES-KOH, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 1% SDS, and protease inhibitor cocktail). Each sample was supplemented with a 63 volume of buffer F (buffer S without SDS), and chromatin fragmentation was performed using a Bioruptor (Diagenode). After spinning at maximum speed for 10 min at 4C, the supernatant (containing most of the fragmented chromatin) was transferred to a new tube for immunoprecipitation (IP). The ChIP sample was incubated with 1–5 mg of antibodies against H3K4me1 (ABclonal, A2355), H3K4me3 (Millipore, 07-473; ABclonal, A2357), H3K9me2 (Abcam, ab1220), H3K27me3 (ABclonal, A2363), H3K27ac (ABclonal, A7253), and RNAPII (BioLegend, 920102) overnight with rotation at 4C. Validation of all antibodies is provided on the respective manufacturers' websites and in our previous publications ([Peng et al.,](#page-14-26) [2019](#page-14-26); [Zhao et al., 2019,](#page-15-27) [2020](#page-15-10)). After reverse cross-linking of the DNA and protein, ChIP-DNA was extracted using phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (Sigma-Aldrich, P3803), precipitated with ethanol, and resuspended in TE buffer. ChIP-DNA libraries were prepared using an NEBNext Ultra II DNA library prep kit for Illumina (New England BioLabs, E7645) in accordance with the manufacturer's guidelines. Library fragments of 300–500 bp were selected using AMPure XP beads (Beckman, A63881). Finally, the DNA fragments were sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq X Ten system (paired-end 150-bp reads). ChIP-seq libraries for each histone mark and RNAPII had two biological replicates.

#### ChIP-reChIP-seq library preparation

Tissue fixation, chromatin sonication, and IP with the first antibody in the primary ChIP procedure were performed as described above. After washing with low-salt ChIP buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) three times, high-salt ChIP buffer (low-salt ChIP buffer with 150 mM NaCl replaced with 350 mM NaCl) twice, ChIP wash buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 250 mM LiCl, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate) once, and TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 1 mM EDTA) twice, the protein-DNA complexes were eluted from beads using ChIP elution buffer for 15 min at 65C, with agitation at 900 rpm. The supernatant (eluted chromatin) was then transferred to a new tube. Five percent of the eluted chromatin was retained for primary ChIP-DNA purification. The remaining eluted chromatin was then used for reChIP. The reChIP with the secondary antibody, elution, DNA purification, library construction, and sequencing were performed as described above. Each experiment had two biological replicates.

### RNA-seq library preparation

Total RNA was isolated using an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (QIAGEN, 74904). The RNA (2 mg) thus isolated was used for library construction using an Illumina TruSeq RNA kit according to the manufacturer's recommendations. Sequencing was performed using an Illumina HiSeq X Ten system. RNA-seq data from each tissue were obtained for two biological replicates.

#### Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing library preparation

Genomic DNA was extracted using a DNeasy Plant Mini Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (QIAGEN, 69104). Bisulfite conversion of the DNA was conducted using the EZ DNA Methylation-GoldTM kit (ZYMO, D5005), and the bisulfite-treated DNA libraries were constructed using the Illumina TruSeq DNA sample prep kit, following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 2 mg of DNA for each sample was fragmented into 200–400-bp pieces by sonication in a Bioruptor (Diagenode). The fragmented DNA was processed by end repair, 3<sup>0</sup> -end adenylation, and adapter ligation. The adapter-ligated DNA fragments were then treated with bisulfite, followed by eight cycles of PCR amplification. Finally, the bisulfite-treated PCR products were purified using AMPure XP beads. Paired-end sequencing of the bisulfite-treated DNA libraries was performed using an Illumina HiSeq X Ten system.

### Analysis methods

#### RNA-seq analysis

The quality of RNA-seq data was examined using FastQC, and low-quality reads were removed from the raw data using Trimmomatic ([Bolger et al.,](#page-14-27) [2014\)](#page-14-27). The clean reads were mapped to the Darmor-bzh ([Chalhoub et al.,](#page-14-1) [2014\)](#page-14-1) reference genome using TopHat2 [\(Kim et al., 2013](#page-14-28)), and HTSeq [\(Anders et al., 2015](#page-14-29)) was used to count the number of reads per gene in each sample. Differentially expressed genes were evaluated using the DESeq2 package in R based on threshold criteria of an adjusted *q* value <0.05 and log2 fold-change >1.5 [\(Love et al., 2014](#page-14-30)). If there were differences in the expression of homeologous gene pairs, we considered this a bias in the expression of the homeologous gene pairs. Then, the high-quality replicates were merged and the aligned reads with mapping quality scores greater than 30 were used for calculation of gene expression using Cufflinks [\(Trapnell et al., 2010](#page-15-28)). Genes with fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM) values >1 were defined as expressed genes.

In order to evaluate the mapping bias of RNA-seq reads, we used RNASeqReadSimulator (READLEN = 130, PAIREDEND = 200,20, error rate = 0.01; other parameters were the defaults) to simulate the RNAseq data of *B. napus*. We set the read length parameter to 130 in the simulation since the average read length of our RNA-seq data is 130 bp. Out of 119 586 046 simulated reads, 114 798 287 (96.0%) were uniquely mapped to the reference genome, and 4 689 463 (4.08%) reads aligned to multiple regions. To ensure that the alignment was correct, we selected 106 740 585 reads with mapping quality scores over 30 and found that 105 593 126 (98.93%) reads were mapped correctly. For 1 147 459 (1.07%) mis-mapped reads, only 3778 (3778/1 147 459 = 0.33%) reads were wrongly mapped to homeologous gene pairs. These results showed that most of the simulated reads could be uniquely and correctly aligned to the reference genome of *B. napus*, and that the mapping bias is marginal in this study.

#### ChIP-seq and ChIP-reChIP-seq analysis

The 150-bp paired-end ChIP-seq sequences were mapped to the Darmor-bzh reference genome using the bwa mem algorithm ([Li and](#page-14-31) [Durbin, 2009\)](#page-14-31). We filtered the mapped reads with map quality (MAPQ) for quality scores below 30 and PCR duplicates, using samtools to ensure the high-quality alignment of data. For H3K4me3, H3K27ac, H3K27me3, and RNAPII, the MACS2 ([Zhang et al., 2008](#page-15-29)) peak caller was used to compare ChIP-seq signals with the corresponding wholecell extract sequenced controls to identify the narrow peaks (macs2 callpeak –t < input file > -c < control file > -f BAM –n < output peak file > -g 8.6e8). For H3k4me1 and H3K9me2, we used the broad-peaks mode (macs2 callpeak –t < input file > -c < control file > -f BAM –n < output peak file > -g 8.6e8 –broad). All ChIP-seq data (H3K4me3, H3K27ac, H3K4me1, RNAPII, H3K27me3, and H3K9me2) were visualized using an integrative genomics viewer (IGV). The Pearson correlation coefficient of the two biological replicates was calculated using 10-kb bin sizes for the whole genome and signal correlation for all ChIP-seq data determined from the four tissues. The histone signals after normalization with input were displayed in the 2-kb regions up- and downstream of each gene TSS, and all ChIP-seq signals were sorted based on gene expression levels [\(Supplemental Figure 2B\)](#page-13-0). If there was a 1-bp overlap between the gene and our histone marks and RNAPII signals, this gene was considered to correspond to the peaks. For ChIP-reChIP-seq analysis, the processing was the same as for the previous ChIP-seq data, but different parameters (macs2 callpeak–t < input file > -c < control file > -B –broad –broad-cutoff 0.01 -q 0.01 -g 8.6e8 –fe-cutoff 4.0 –nomode) were used to call peaks.

#### DNA methylation analysis

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) data were evaluated using FastQC, and adapter and low-quality reads were filtered using Trimmomatic. The clean reads were mapped to the Darmor-bzh genome using BatMeth2 ([Zhou et al., 2019](#page-15-30)). The mapped reads with mapping quality scores higher than 20 were used in subsequent analyses. The methylation level of each cytosine site was determined by dividing the number of methylated reads by the coverage of the site, with the resulting methylation level value at any cytosine site lying between 0 and 1. For those locations where coverage was %3, we considered the data to be missing. To calculate the methylation status of a region, the sum of the number of methylated reads in this region was divided by the sum of the echo cytosine site coverage. The methylation profile was calculated using 40 bins in the gene body and on both sides (within 2 kb), and the profile was generated by an R script. We divide the whole genome into 10 kb bins for comparisons of the methylation levels between the An and Cn subgenomes [\(Supplemental Figure 2](#page-13-0)D).

We evaluated mapping bias of WGBS data and thus simulated the bisulfite sequencing (BS-seq) datasets of *B. napus* using wgsim (v0.3.1) and FASTX-mutate-tools ([https://www.githubcom/nicolaprezza/](https://www.githubcom/nicolaprezza/fastx-mutate-tools) [fastx-mutate-tools\)](https://www.githubcom/nicolaprezza/fastx-mutate-tools) software. The average read length of DNA methylation data in this study is 136, which is the read length parameter in the simulation. After filtering out the reads with mapping quality scores less than 20, the correct mapping rate of the remaining reads was 92.29% (1 170 984/ 1 268 855). For misaligned reads (7.71%), the majority (99.62%) were incorrectly mapped to the same chromosome and 0.38% (4828) of reads were wrongly aligned to homeologous genes. These results indicate that most of the BS-seq reads can be uniquely and correctly aligned to the reference genome of *B. napus*.

#### Differential methylation region analysis

To identify the differential methylation regions (DMRs), the whole genome was divided into 200-bp bins. The DMRs between two samples were examined using a binomial test and defined by two conditions: (1) differences in DNA methylation between the two samples were 0.6 or above; and (2) the adjusted *q* values between the averages of the region's methylation levels were 0.05 or less.

#### TE analysis

The information for TE annotation is available at the Genoscope Genome Database ([www.genoscope.cns.fr/brassicanapus](http://www.genoscope.cns.fr/brassicanapus)). The overall distribution of TEs close to histone modifications was visualized if 1-bp overlaps with peaks were observed, and different preferences were noted.

#### Gene expression breadth analysis

RNA-seq data for 30 tissues [\(Supplemental Table 2](#page-13-0)) that were downloaded from a public database were used to evaluate the expression breadth of histone-marked genes. If there was a 1-bp overlap between the gene and an epigenomic signal, this epigenomic signal was considered to be a corresponding peak of this gene. All RNA-seq datasets were mapped to the Darmor-bzh reference genome and the expressed genes were defined as those with an FPKM value >1. A gene expressed in %5 tissues was considered to be a tissue-specific gene, whereas a gene expressed in R25 tissues was considered to be a housekeeping gene. GO enrichment analyses for tissue-specific and housekeeping genes were performed using agriGO2 ([http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.](http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/agriGOv2/index.php) [cn/agriGOv2/index.php\)](http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/agriGOv2/index.php), and the background was from a previous researchers' article ([Chalhoub et al., 2014\)](#page-14-1) [\(https://wwwdev.genoscope.](https://wwwdev.genoscope.cns.fr/brassicanapus/data/GO/) [cns.fr/brassicanapus/data/GO/](https://wwwdev.genoscope.cns.fr/brassicanapus/data/GO/)).

#### Homeologous gene pairs and classification of duplicated genes

Annotation of homeologous gene pairs was based on the Darmorbzh reference genome, and the list of homeologous gene pairs used in the analysis was derived from published data ([Chalhoub et al., 2014](#page-14-1)). The classification of duplicated genes (WGD, TD, PD, TRD, and DSD) was conducted as previously described ([Qiao et al., 2019](#page-14-17)). Initially, the protein sequence of the gene was extracted based on the coding sequence of the annotated gene, and then an all-against-all blastp comparison (E value %5) was used to identify the gene pairs as duplicated genes.

#### Characterization of CSs and assignment of genomic features.

To determine the significance of the combinatorial information for the different histone marks, we used ChromHMM v1.12 ([Ernst and Kellis,](#page-14-32) [2012](#page-14-32)), which is based on a multivariate Hidden Markov Model. For all histone marks (H3K4me3, H3K27ac, H3K4me1, H3K27me3, and H3K9me2) and RNAPII, read counts were computed in non-overlapping 200-bp bins across the whole genome, and thus the resolution of the CS was 200 bp. The number of states ranging from 10 to 20 was trained, and we selected a 15-state model for subsequent analyses. The 15-state model was selected based on the consideration that it captured all of the key information between the histone marks, and that a smaller number of states could not adequately reflect the combinatorial information of the whole genome. Conversely, a larger number of states did not sufficiently capture the distinct information.

#### Relationships between CSs, DNA methylation, and gene expression

Analysis of the enrichment of histone marks across the whole genome was conducted using ChromHMM. The distribution of DNA methylation (CG, CHG, and CHH methylation from the WGBS datasets) was computed using regions associated with the echo of the 15 CSs. The average expression level of the genes to which CS overlap is considered to be the expression level of this CS. The levels of CHH-type methylation are generally low in *B. napus*, and therefore we only present data from 0 to 0.25 in the box plot [\(Figure 2](#page-4-0)A).

### CS variability and switching

The whole genome was divided into bins of 200 bp, and 15 CSs were used. The frequency of any pair of states in the four tissues was computed for all histone marks using the 2063A line. For example, for a given pair of states A and B, we counted the number of genome bins that were labeled as (A, B) or (B, A) in all pairs of epigenomes. To obtain a symmetric matrix,

a 15 3 15 matrix of the switching frequencies between tissues was transposed and summed with the original matrix. To avoid the preference for different tissues in the calculation process, the symmetric matrix was then raw-normalized to switching probabilities, and we thereafter calculated the switching probabilities of each two-tissue combination and merged them. Using the same method, we determined the switching probabilities of the An and Cn subgenomes. To observe differences between the An and Cn subgenomes, the switching probabilities of the Cn subgenome were removed from the switching probabilities matrix of the An subgenome.

#### DNA methylation variability and switching

The resolution of the CSs and DMR was 200 bp. To observe CS switching in the DMR, we counted the frequency for any pair of states, and the same method used to determine CS variability and switching was used to calculate the switching probabilities matrix. To evaluate the variation in DNA methylation of each CS across the epigenomes, we initially calculated the DNA methylation levels of the 200-bp bins based on the 15 states of the whole genome. Thereafter, we evaluated the DNA methylation density of each CS with n = 1000 points, a Gaussian kernel, and the default nrd0 bandwidth from the R stats package density function, and using these bins we determined the methylation levels between 0% and 100%. Finally, the density values were normalized by calculating log10 (density +1), and the R package pheatmap was used to show the heatmap of variation with the color scale set to white as the minimum log10 (density+1) and red as the maximum log10 (density +1).

#### Analysis of differences in each histone mark

The histone mark signals were called and the peaks of duplicates between tissues were merged using bedtools [\(Quinlan and Hall, 2010\)](#page-14-33) as histone modification regions. Differences in the histone mark signals were evaluated using the DESeq2 package in R. The peaks showing a significant difference were filtered based on the criteria of an adjusted p < 0.05 and log2 fold-change > 1.5. The same method was used for determining the differences between peaks for tissues and lines. With regard to the number of variable regions ([Figure 3A](#page-6-0)), we summed the regions of each pair of tissues, although, if a region was repetitively calculated, this region was only counted once.

#### <span id="page-13-0"></span>Relationship between histone marks and gene expression in tissues

Fold changes between gene expression and histone mark signals were determined by comparing two tissues using DESeq2. If there was a 1-bp overlap between the histone mark peaks and the genes, the changes in the histone marks and gene pairs between any two tissues were identified. These changes were shown in a scatter plot ([Figure 3B](#page-6-0)), with different colors representing different densities. On the basis of the profile of RNAPII and all histone marks ([Supplemental Figure 2](#page-13-0)A), we established that H3K4me3 and H3K27ac are mainly distributed in the promoter region, H3K4me1 and H3K27me3 are mainly distributed in the gene body region, and RNAPII is mainly distributed in the TTS region. In order to observe the relationship between gene expression and histone mark signals in homeologous gene pairs, we counted the read numbers to represent the signal in the promoter region for H3K4me3 and H3K27ac, in the gene body for H3K4me1 and H3K27me3, and in the TTS region for RNAPII. Using this method, we could avoid the situation in which one of the homeologous genes overlaps with a peak and the other does not. To avoid the influence of sequencing on the signals of marks, the number of reads was normalized using the DESeq2 package, and a heatmap ([Figure 3G](#page-6-0)) was generated using the pheatmap package.

#### Variability of histone marks, RNAPII, and transcription in the four tissues

To evaluate the variability of transcription, we calculated the variance in gene expression levels (FPKM) to represent the stability of gene expression in the four tissues assessed in the present study. We then compared the stability of gene expression between homeologous gene pairs and subgenome-unique genes in the An and Cn subgenomes. The same method was used to evaluate the variability of histone marks and RNAPII. As described earlier, we counted the read numbers and normalized these using the DESeq2 package to represent the signal of a given gene in the promoter region for H3K4me3 and H3K27ac, in the gene body for H3K4me1 and H3K27me3, and in the TTS region for RNAPII.

### Tissue-specific histone mark analysis

We initially selected regions with different peaks in any two tissues and then merged these. For comparisons of the four tissues, the signals of different peaks in the four tissues were row-normalized, and then k-means clustering based on the Euclidean distance was used to cluster the signals of the genes with each histone mark. All the corresponding genes were sorted based on the order of the histone marks. Using agriGO2, we conducted GO enrichment analysis of the corresponding genes and showed some of the significant terms.

#### Analysis of PSGs and NSGs

To calculate Ka/Ks values of the homeologous gene pairs, the protein sequences of each gene and the corresponding coding sequence (CDS) were obtained by annotating the gene. Parallel Alignment and back-Translation (ParaAT: <http://bigd.big.ac.cn/tools/paraat>), a tool that constructs protein-coding DNA alignments in parallel for a large number of homeologous genes, was used to align the CDS sequences and output the results in axt format (perl ParaAT.pl -h <homeologous gene pairs > -n < CDS sequence > -a <protein sequence > -p < number of physical cores > -o < output folder > -f <axt>). All the gene pairs from the alignment results were merged. The NG methods for estimating Ka and Ks and their references were used to calculate Ka/Ks values using the kaks\_calculator (<https://sourceforge.net/projects/kakscalculator2/>). We defined gene pairs with Ka/Ks < 0.02 as PSGs and those with Ka/Ks > 1.4 as NSGs.
