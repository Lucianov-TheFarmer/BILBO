## ABSTRACT

Light and the heterotrimeric G-protein are known to antagonistically regulate photomorphogenesis in Arabidopsis. However, whether light and G-protein coordinate the regulation of photomorphogenesis is largely unknown. Here we show that the blue light photoreceptor cryptochrome 1 (CRY1) physically interacts with the G-protein b subunit, AGB1, in a blue light-dependent manner. We also show that AGB1 directly interacts with HY5, a basic leucine zipper transcriptional factor that acts as a critical positive regulator of photomorphogenesis, to inhibit its DNA-binding activity. Genetic studies suggest that CRY1 acts partially through AGB1, and AGB1 acts partially through HY5 to regulate photomorphogenesis. Moreover, we demonstrate that blue light-triggered interaction of CRY1 with AGB1 promotes the dissociation of HY5 from AGB1. Our results suggest that the CRY1 signaling mechanism involves positive regulation of the DNA-binding activity of HY5 mediated by the CRY1–AGB1 interaction, which inhibits the association of AGB1 with HY5. We propose that the antagonistic regulation of HY5 DNA-binding activity by CRY1 and AGB1 may allow plants to balance light and G-protein signaling and optimize photomorphogenesis.

Key words: *Arabidopsis*, cryptochrome, heterotrimeric G-protein b subunit AGB1, HY5, photomorphogenesis

## INTRODUCTION

As an energy source and informational signal, light profoundly influences plant growth and development throughout the plant's life cycle [\(Fankhauser and Chory, 1997; Deng and Quail, 1999\)](#page-13-0). Photomorphogenesis, one of the most critical and best studied light-controlled processes of plants, is characterized by the inhibition of hypocotyl elongation, enhancement of cotyledon expansion, and accumulation of chlorophyll and anthocyanin, and is mediated by multiple photoreceptors including the blue light photoreceptors, the cryptochromes (CRYs, CRY1 and CRY2) and phototropins (PHOT1 and PHOT2), the UV-B light photoreceptor UVR8, and the red/far-red light photoreceptors, the phytochromes (PHYs, phyA to phyE) [\(Cashmore et al.,](#page-13-1) [1999; Briggs and Christie, 2002; Quail, 2002; Rizzini et al.,](#page-13-1) [2011](#page-13-1)). CRYs are not only present in plants but in other organisms, including archaebacteria and mammals, as well. They are known to play a critical role in the entrainment of the circadian clock in *Drosophila* and to be essential components of the circadian clock in mammals ([Emery et al., 1998; Kume](#page-13-2) [et al., 1999; van der Horst et al., 1999; Busza et al., 2004](#page-13-2)). Moreover, CRYs are also involved in the regulation of the

DNA-damage response, cancer progression, and glucocorticoid signaling in mammals, and act as magnetoreceptors perceiving magnetic fields in migratory birds (Gegear et al., 2008; Ozturk et al., 2009; Kang et al., 2010; Lamia et al., 2011).

In Arabidopsis thaliana, CRY1 plays a major role in mediating blue light inhibition of hypocotyl elongation, whereas CRY2 inhibits hypocotyl growth mainly under low-intensity blue light (Ahmad and Cashmore, 1993; Lin et al., 1998). CRYs are structurally divided into an N-terminal domain related to photolyase and a C-terminal extension domain. It has been shown that the C-terminal domains of Arabidopsis CRY1 and CRY2, CCT1 (C terminus of CRY1) and CCT2 (C terminus of CRY2), respectively (Yang et al., 2000), also known as CCE (CRY C-terminal extension) (Yu et al., 2010), mediate CRY signaling by directly interacting with COP1 (Yang et al., 2000, 2001; Wang et al., 2001), a RING-finger E3 ubiquitin ligase (Deng et al., 1992) that interacts with the transcription factorsHY5/ HYH and CONSTANS (CO) and targets them for degradation to regulate photomorphogenesis and photoperiodic flowering, respectively (Putterill et al., 1995; Oyama et al., 1997; Osterlund et al., 2000; Lee et al., 2007; Jang et al., 2008; Liu et al., 2008). HY5 is a basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor, and acts as a major positive regulator of photomorphogenesis by directly regulating the expression of numerous G-boxcontaining light-responsive genes (Oyama et al., 1997; Lee et al., 2007). HYH is a close homolog of HY5. They form heterodimers or homodimers to regulate the expression of overlapping as well as distinct target genes (Holm et al., 2002). Moreover, CRY1 and CRY2 interact with the COP1 enhancer, SPA1 (Lian et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2011; Zuo et al., 2011), which interacts with COP1 and enhances its E3 ligase activity (Seo et al., 2003). The outcome of CRY1/CRY2 interactions with COP1/SPA1 is disruption of the COP1-SPA1 core complex, and thus the stabilization and increased accumulation of the HY5/HYH/CO proteins. The N-terminal domains of CRY1 and CRY2 (CNT1 and CNT2, respectively) mediate CRY dimerization (Sang et al., 2005; Yu et al., 2007), and it has been shown that CRY2 dimerization is inhibited by blue light inhibitor of cryptochromes 1 (BIC1) (Wang et al., 2016a, 2016b). CNT1 is able to mediate CRY1 signaling independently of CCT1, and it was demonstrated recently that CRY1 interacts with AUX/ IAA proteins through its N terminus to inhibit auxin-induced degradation of these proteins and attenuate auxin signaling (Xu et al., 2018).

The heterotrimeric guanine-nucleotide-binding G-protein (G-protein) complex, which is composed of the G $\alpha$ , G $\beta$ , and G $\gamma$  subunits, is an integral membrane signal transduction component that mediates intracellular responses to external stimuli in all eukaryotes (Gilman, 1987). In the canonical model of G-protein signaling, the G-protein heterotrimers are activated by G-protein-coupled receptors that induce the exchange of guanosine diphosphate for guanosine triphosphate (GTP) at a binding site on G $\alpha$ . The binding of GTP to G $\alpha$  allows the release of G $\beta\gamma$  dimers and the subsequent engagement of effectors. The free G $\beta\gamma$  dimers can also activate downstream signaling cascades to mediate a variety of critical signaling pathways for light, hormones, odorants, and neurotransmitters (Wettschureck and Offermanns, 2005; Rosenbaum et al., 2009; Syrovatkina et al., 2016). Animals contain many diverse copies of heterotrimeric G-proteins. For example, there are 23 Ga, 5 G $\beta$ , and 12 G $\gamma$  subunits in humans (Wettschureck and Offermanns, 2005). By contrast, Arabidopsis contains only one canonical Ga subunit, GPA1, three non-canonical extra-large Ga subunits, XLG1, XLG2 and XLG3, a single Gβ subunit, AGB1, and three  $G_{\gamma}$  subunits, AGG1, AGG2, and AGG3 (Ullah et al., 2001, 2003; Heo et al., 2012; Thung et al., 2012). GPA1 and AGB1 function to repress photomorphogenesis, as the gpa1 and agb1 mutants display a partial de-etiolation phenotype characterized by a short hypocotyl (Jones et al., 2003; Ullah et al., 2003). Moreover, GPA1, AGB1, AGG1, and AGG3 have been shown to regulate stomatal development (Zhang et al., 2008; Nilson and Assmann, 2010; Chakravorty et al., 2011; Li et al., 2012). In rice, G proteins play roles in regulating key agronomic traits, nitrogen use efficiency, and resistance to low temperature (Fan et al., 2006; Huang et al., 2009; Sun et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2015).

Although CRY1 and G proteins are involved in the antagonistic regulation of photomorphogenesis, whether and how they coordinately regulate this process is not known. Here we demonstrate that CRY1 and CRY2 physically interact with AGB1 in a blue light-dependent manner, and that AGB1 interacts directly with HY5. We also show that CRY1 acts partially through AGB1, and AGB1 acts partially through HY5 to regulate photomorphogenesis. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the interaction of AGB1 with HY5 inhibits the DNA-binding activity of HY5, and that blue light-induced interaction of CRY1 with AGB1 promotes the dissociation of HY5 from AGB1. We interpret these findings to indicate that AGB1 inhibition of photomorphogenesis is mediated, at least in part, through the repression of the DNA-binding activity of HY5 by the physical AGB1-HY5 interaction, and that the mechanism of CRY1 control of photomorphogenesis involves positive regulation of the DNA-binding activity of HY5 by the CRY1--AGB1 interaction, which prevents the association of AGB1 with HY5. The antagonistic regulation of HY5 DNA-binding activity by AGB1 and CRY1 may allow plants to balance different signaling pathways and optimize photomorphogenesis.

# CRY1 Physically Interacts with AGB1 through Its N Terminus in Yeast Cells

We identified one candidate, the G-protein  $\beta$  subunit AGB1. This protein contains the coiled-coil region and WD40 domain similar to COP1 and the SPAs, and the loss-of-function mutant shows a partially de-etiolated phenotype in darkness, similar to the phenotype of weak *cop1* mutants (Schwechheimer and Deng, 2000; Ullah et al., 2003). ## CRY1 Physically Interacts with AGB1 in a Blue Light-Dependent Manner through Its N Terminus in Plant Cells

To investigate whether CRY1 interacts with AGB1 in plant cells, we made constructs expressing the N- and C-terminal halves of yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) fused to the N terminus of AGB1 and C terminus of CRY1, respectively, and performed bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays with tobacco leaf epidermal cells. As shown in [Figure 1](#page-2-0)F, YFP fluorescence was observed in tobacco cells coexpressing CRY1-cYFP and nYFP-AGB1, but not in those coexpressing CRY1-cYFP and nYFP or cYFP and nYFP-AGB1, suggesting that CRY1 and AGB1 interact. To further confirm the interaction between CRY1 and AGB1 *in vivo*, we performed coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays with transgenic *Arabidopsis* plants co-overexpressing FLAG-AGB1 and Myc-CRY1 (*FLAG-AGB1-ovx*/*Myc-CRY1-ovx*). The FLAG-AGB1 fusion protein is physiologically functional, because its expression in the *agb1* mutant rescued the short hypocotyl phenotype in blue, red, and far-red light (Supplemental Figure 2). As shown in [Figure 1G](#page-2-0), Myc-CRY1 was co-immunoprecipitated with FLAG-AGB1 from seedlings exposed to blue light, but not from those kept in darkness or exposed to red light, indicating that the interaction of CRY1 with AGB1 is blue light-dependent. Furthermore, we performed semi*-in vivo* pull-down assays using the His-AGB1 fusion protein expressed in *E*. *coli* as the bait and protein extracts containing Myc-CRY1 prepared from *Myc-CRY1-ovx* seedlings adapted in the dark and exposed to increasing intensities of blue light as the prey. These assays showed that the interaction of CRY1 with AGB1 was enhanced by higher-intensity blue light [\(Figure 1H](#page-2-0)).

To further confirm that CNT1 mediates the interaction of CRY1 with AGB1 in plant cells, we transiently coexpressed FLAG-AGB1 and CNT1 fused to a nuclear localization signal (NLS) and YFP (YFP-NLS-CNT1), which confers enhanced blue light inhibition of hypocotyl elongation when expressed in the *cry1* mutant ([He et al.,](#page-13-10) [2015](#page-13-10)), or FLAG-AGB1 and CCT1 fused to b-glucuronidase (GUS), an NLS, and YFP (YFP-NLS-GUS-CCT1), which confers a constitutive light response when expressed in *Arabidopsis* [\(Yang](#page-15-0) [et al., 2000](#page-15-0)), in tobacco cells. A Co-IP assay was performed with cells that were adapted in darkness and exposed to blue or red light or kept in darkness. YFP-NLS-CNT1 interacted with AGB1 in blue light, but not in the dark or red light, whereas NLS-GUS-CCT1 did not interact with AGB1 under any condition (Supplemental Figure 1E), indicating that the interaction of CNT1 with AGB1 is blue light-dependent. We then performed a BiFC assay and found that CNT1 interacted with AGB1 (Supplemental Figure 1F). Since we observed that expression of CNT1-YFP but not CRY1-YFP in tobacco cells produced nuclear bodies (NBs), we further performed a protein colocalization assay with tobacco cells coexpressing CFP-tagged AGB1 (CFP-AGB1) and YFP-NLS-CNT1. We found that CFP-AGB1 localized to the same NBs as YFP-NLS-CNT1 (Supplemental Figure 1G). Taken together, these results suggest that CNT1 mediates the interaction of CRY1 with AGB1 in plant cells.

## CRY2 Physically Interacts with AGB1 in a Blue Light-Dependent Manner

Given that the signaling mechanism of both CRY1 and CRY2 involves direct interactions with COP1, SPAs, and phytochromeinteracting factors (PIFs) ([Wang et al., 2001; Yang et al., 2001;](#page-15-9) [Lian et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2011; Ma et al., 2016; Pedmale](#page-15-9) [et al., 2016\)](#page-15-9), we examined whether CRY2 also interacts with AGB1 by performing a yeast two-hybrid assay. We found that CRY2 interacted with AGB1 in the dark and under blue light (Supplemental Figure 3A–3C). We then carried out BiFC assays and found that CRY2 interacted with AGB1 in the nucleus (Supplemental Figure 3D). Moreover, we performed a semi*in vivo* pull-down assay using protein extracts containing Myc-CRY2 prepared from *Myc-CRY2*-overexpressing (*Myc-CRY2-ovx*) *Arabidopsis* seedlings adapted in the dark and exposed to blue or red light or kept in darkness. Myc-CRY2 was pulled down by AGB1 from *Myc-CRY2-ovx* seedlings exposed to blue light, but not from those kept in darkness or exposed to red light (Supplemental Figure 3E). Taken together, these results indicate that CRY2 physically interacts with AGB1 in a blue light-dependent manner.

## AGB1 Acts in Parallel with COP1 to Regulate Hypocotyl Elongation and Anthocyanin Accumulation

COP1 is a master repressor in the light signal transduction pathway and acts downstream of CRYs to inhibit photomorphogenesis under blue light ([Wang et al., 2001; Yang et al., 2001\)](#page-15-9). The partial de-etiolation in darkness phenotype observed for both *agb1* and weak *cop1* mutants ([Schwechheimer and Deng,](#page-15-10) [2000; Ullah et al., 2003](#page-15-10)) prompted us to examine the possible functional relationship between AGB1 and COP1. We generated the *cop1 agb1* double mutant by genetic crossing and analyzed hypocotyl elongation and anthocyanin accumulation in the dark and under blue, red, and far-red light. We found that the *cop1 agb1* double mutant had significantly shorter hypocotyls and accumulated significantly more anthocyanin than the *agb1* and *cop1* single mutants in the dark and under blue, red, and far-red light ([Figure 2A](#page-4-0)–2H and Supplemental Figure 4A–4E), indicating an additive role for AGB1 and COP1 in regulating these processes.

Since COP1 directly interacts with HY5 and promotes its ubiquitination and degradation to repress photomorphogenesis [\(Ang](#page-13-11) [et al., 1998; Hardtke et al., 2000; Osterlund et al., 2000\)](#page-13-11), we examined whether AGB1 regulates the stability of HY5 protein by performing western blot analysis of the HY5 protein in etiolated *agb1* mutant seedlings kept in the dark and those exposed to blue light using an antibody against HY5 [\(Chen](#page-13-12) [et al., 2013](#page-13-12)). As shown in Supplemental Figure 5A, high levels of HY5 protein were detected in the *cop1* mutant grown in the dark and in the mutant exposed to blue light for 2 h. However, like WT, the *agb1* mutant did not accumulate HY5 protein in the dark and had a much lower level of HY5 than the *cop1* mutant upon blue light exposure. We further tested whether AGB1 interacts with COP1 by performing a yeast two-hybrid assay and found that they did not interact (Supplemental Figure 5B). These data suggest that AGB1 functions in parallel with COP1 to repress photomorphogenesis, and that the underlying mechanism does not involve AGB1 regulation of HY5 protein stability.

To further confirm the interaction of AGB1 with HY5 in plant cells, we performed BiFC assays and found that AGB1 interacted with HY5 in the nucleus (Figure 4E). Since HY5 protein is degraded in the dark (Osterlund et al., 2000), we then performed Co-IP assays with light-grown transgenic *Arabidopsis* overexpressing FLAG-AGB1 in the *agb1* and *hy5* mutant backgrounds (*FLAG-AGB1-ovx/agb1* and *FLAG-AGB1-ovx/hy5*, respectively). These results indicate that AGB1 interacts with HY5 *in planta*. We also found that AGB1 interacted with HYH in both yeast two-hybrid and BiFC assays (Supplemental Figure 6).

Next, we explored whether the interaction of AGB1 with HY5 affects the DNA-binding activity of HY5. Given that HY5 binds to the G-box motif (Hardtke et al., 2000; Lee et al., 2007), we performed electrophoretic mobility-shift assays (EMSAs) using the promoter sequence of a classic G-box motif-containing light-responsive gene encoding chalcone synthase, CHS, as the probe. Consistent with a previous study (Hardtke et al., 2000), HY5 protein bound specifically to the G-box motif-containing region of the CHS promoter, as demonstrated in a competition experiment using cold competitor probes (Figure 5A). Interestingly, increasing amounts of His-AGB1 clearly reduced the binding of HY5 to the probe, whereas binding was not affected by high concentrations of the control protein, His-tagged trigger factor (His-TF) (Figure 5B). These results suggest that the interaction of AGB1 with HY5 inhibits the ability of HY5 to bind to its target DNA sequence in vitro.

To further investigate the effects of AGB1 on the DNA-binding activity of HY5 in vivo, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR) assays with light-grown WT, agb1, and hy5 mutant seedlings. However, the enrichment of the negative control sequence, the At4g26900 promoter (Lee et al., 2007), was very low and indistinguishable between WT and the agb1 mutant. Taken together, these results indicate that the interaction of AGB1 with HY5 leads to inhibition of the DNA-binding activity of HY5 in planta.

## CRYs, AGB1, and HY5 Coregulate a Large Number of Genes Associated with Photomorphogenesis under Blue Light

To provide more evidence that CRYs, AGB1, and HY5 function in the same pathway to regulate photomorphogenesis, we performed transcriptomic analyses of blue light-grown WT, *cry1 cry2*, *agb1*, and *hy5* mutant seedlings, and identified 1174 AGB1-regulated genes (WT versus the *agb1* mutant), 5595 CRY-regulated genes (WT versus the *cry1 cry2* mutant), and 3285 HY5-regulated genes (WT versus the *hy5* mutant) ([Figure 6](#page-8-0)A and Supplemental Table 1). There were 470 genes coregulated by AGB1 and HY5 ([Figure 6](#page-8-0)A and Supplemental Table 2), and a heatmap revealed that, of these genes, 327 (70%) were regulated by AGB1 and HY5 in the opposite direction [\(Figure 6B](#page-8-0) and Supplemental Table 2). Similarly, among the 624 genes coregulated by CRYs and AGB1, 488 (78%) were oppositely regulated [\(Figure 6](#page-8-0)C and Supplemental Table 3). We also compared the genes regulated by CRYs, AGB1, and HY5, and identified 327 coregulated genes ([Figure 6](#page-8-0)A). Among these genes, 293 (90%) genes were regulated by CRYs and HY5 in the same direction ([Figure 6D](#page-8-0) and Supplemental Table 4). Furthermore, among these 293 genes, 231 (79%) were oppositely regulated by AGB1; 167 genes were AGB1-activated and CRY/HY5 repressed, and 64 genes were AGB1-repressed and CRY/ HY5-activated ([Figure 6D](#page-8-0) and Supplemental Table 4). These data indicate that CRYs/HY5 act antagonistically with AGB1 to regulate gene expression in blue light.

Next, we performed gene ontology (GO) analysis and found that the genes coregulated by CRYs, AGB1, and HY5 were preferentially associated with the regulation of cell wall remodeling and anthocyanin biosynthesis and the response to blue light and plant hormones ([Figure 6](#page-8-0)E). Further analysis revealed that the AGB1-activated genes were enriched in the cell wall and hormone signaling-related genes, which are closely related to hypocotyl elongation ([Figure 6F](#page-8-0)), while the AGB1-repressed genes were enriched in anthocyanin biosynthesis genes [\(Figure 6G](#page-8-0)), which is consistent with the role of AGB1 in the repression of photomorphogenesis ([Figure 2A](#page-4-0)– 2H and Supplemental Figure 4). Further qRT–PCR analyses were carried out to evaluate the expression of some of the genes coregulated by CRYs, AGB1, and HY5, including the hypocotyl elongation-promoting genes, *EXP*s, *SAUR*s, *PRE1*, and *XTR4*, as well as the anthocyanin biosynthesispromoting genes, *CHS* and *FLS1*, in the WT, *agb1*, *cry1 cry2*, and *hy5* mutants. The results indicated that the genes involved in promoting hypocotyl elongation were activated by AGB1 but repressed by CRYs and HY5, whereas those involved in promoting anthocyanin biosynthesis were repressed by AGB1 but activated by CRYs and HY5 (Supplemental Figure 7). These results, together with the genetic and biochemical data ([Figures 1](#page-2-0), [2](#page-4-0), [3](#page-5-0), and [4](#page-6-0); Supplemental Figures 3, [5](#page-7-0), and [6\)](#page-8-0), demonstrate that CRYs negatively regulate AGB1 to promote photomorphogenesis, whereas AGB1 negatively regulates HY5 to repress photomorphogenesis.

## DISCUSSION

It has been demonstrated that the *Arabidopsis* CRY signaling mechanism involves the regulation of HY5 protein stability through physical interactions with COP1 and SPAs [\(Wang et al.,](#page-15-9) [2001; Yang et al., 2001; Lian et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2011\)](#page-15-9). In this report, we demonstrate that the *Arabidopsis* G-protein b subunit AGB1 inhibits photomorphogenesis by directly interacting with HY5 and repressing its DNA-binding activity. Furthermore, CRY1 directly interacts with AGB1 in a blue lightdependent manner to inhibit the association of AGB1 with HY5, indicating that the *Arabidopsis* CRY signaling mechanism involves the regulation of HY5 DNA-binding activity. We therefore propose that CRY photoreceptor signaling and G-protein b subunit-mediated signaling are integrated through direct interactions between CRY1 and AGB1 and between AGB1 and HY5, and that the CRY1-AGB1-HY5 regulatory module may allow plants to coordinate light and G-protein signaling to optimize photomorphogenesis.

## AGB1 Physically Interacts with HY5 to Inhibit Its DNA-Binding Activity and Photomorphogenesis

It has been shown that AGB1 represses photomorphogenesis in both red and far-red light ([Jones et al., 2003; Ullah et al., 2003](#page-14-3)). In this study, we further demonstrate that AGB1 also inhibits this process in blue light, strengthening the notion that AGB1 is an important repressor of photomorphogenesis. Given the previous demonstrations that COP1 is the master negative regulator of photomorphogenesis and that HY5 is a major positive regulator of photomorphogenesis, we defined the genetic interactions of *AGB1* with *COP1* and *HY5*. Our results suggest that AGB1 and COP1 act additively to inhibit photomorphogenesis, and that AGB1 acts partially through HY5 and HYH to repress photomorphogenesis. COP1 directly interacts with HY5 and promotes HY5 ubiquitination and degradation to repress photomorphogenesis [\(Ang et al., 1998;](#page-13-11) [Hardtke et al., 2000](#page-13-11)). However, we found no evidence that AGB1 regulates the stability of HY5 protein.

The Gb subunits of animal G proteins localize in the nucleus, cytoplasm, and membrane fractions. Furthermore, the animal G-protein b subunit interacts directly with nuclear transcription factors, such as AEBP1 (adipocyte enhancer-binding protein 1), glucocorticoid receptor, and activator protein 1, to attenuate their transcriptional repression activity [\(Park et al.,](#page-14-4) [1999; Kino et al., 2005; Robitaille et al., 2010](#page-14-4)). These studies suggest that G-protein b subunits not only transduce extracellular signals to their downstream effector molecules, but also transduce extracellular stimuli alone or by physically interacting with transcription factors inside the nucleus to regulate the expression of target genes in mammals. Likewise, the *Arabidopsis* and rice G-protein b subunits, AGB1 and RGB1, have been shown to localize to the plasma membrane, cytosol, and nucleus ([Anderson and Botella, 2007; Yadav et al.,](#page-13-14) [2014](#page-13-14)). It has been demonstrated recently that AGB1 promotes hypocotyl elongation by interacting with BBX21, a B-box-like zinc-finger transcriptional factor, and inhibiting its transcriptional activation activity rather than its DNA-binding activity [\(Xu et al.,](#page-15-11) [2017](#page-15-11)). AGB1 also promotes hypocotyl elongation by interacting with BES1, a master transcription factor mediating BR signaling, and promoting its transcriptional activity [\(Zhang](#page-15-12) [et al., 2018\)](#page-15-12). In this study, we have demonstrated by performing protein–protein and protein–DNA interaction assays that AGB1 interacts physically with HY5 in the nucleus [\(Figure 4](#page-6-0)B–4F) and that this leads to a significant decrease in the ability of HY5 to bind DNA ([Figure 5](#page-7-0)B and 5C). Consistent with this, genomewide gene expression studies revealed that AGB1 and HY5 coregulate a large number of genes but in the opposite direction ([Figure 6](#page-8-0)B–6G). qRT–PCR assays further confirmed that AGB1 and HY5 antagonistically regulate the expression of classical HY5 target genes including the HY5-activated gene *CHS* and the HY5-repressed gene, *EXP2* (Supplemental Figure 7). Given that HY5 is a pivotal positive regulator of photomorphogenesis and acts directly downstream of COP1 to mediate light signaling ([Ang et al., 1998; Hardtke et al., 2000; Osterlund et al.,](#page-13-11) [2000](#page-13-11)), our results reveal an important mechanism by which AGB1 represses photomorphogenesis–through the inhibition of HY5 DNA-binding activity, which is mediated by the direct interaction of AGB1 with HY5.

## The Cryptochrome Signaling Mechanism Involves Blue Light-Dependent Interaction with AGB1

CRY1 is the primary blue light photoreceptor mediating blue light inhibition of hypocotyl elongation. It is known that in *Arabidopsis* CRY1 signaling is mediated through the direct interaction of its CCT1 with COP1/SPA1, which relieves HY5 from COP1/SPA1 mediated ubiquitination and degradation and eventually leads to HY5 stabilization. CNT1 does not interact with either COP1 or SPA1 and is not thought to be directly involved in mediating CRY1 signaling [\(Yang et al., 2001; Lian et al., 2011](#page-15-13)). A recent study demonstrated that CNT1 is able to mediate blue lightspecific inhibition of hypocotyl elongation independent of CCT1 ([He et al., 2015\)](#page-13-10). Here we report that the G-protein b subunit AGB1 is a new CRY1-interacting protein. We also demonstrated by performing yeast three-hybrid assays and Co-IP assays in *Arabidopsis* that blue light-triggered interaction of CRY1 with AGB1 interferes with the association of AGB1 with HY5 ([Figure 5E](#page-7-0) and 5F). Given that AGB1 interacts with and inhibits the DNA-binding activity of HY5 ([Figures 4](#page-6-0)B–4F, [5B](#page-7-0), and 5C), these results suggest that the blue light-triggered interaction of CRY1 with AGB1 interferes with the association of AGB1 with HY5, which might relieve the inhibitory effects of AGB1 on the DNA-binding activity of HY5. Our genome-wide transcriptomic analyses and gene expression studies support the proposal that AGB1 negatively regulates HY5, whereas CRY1 negatively regulates AGB1 ([Figure 6A](#page-8-0)–6D and Supplemental Figure 7). Taken together, our findings suggest that the CRY1 signaling mechanism involves the positive regulation of the DNA-binding activity of HY5.

Based on previous reports and our findings, we propose a molecular regulatory framework for coordination of photomorphogenesis by CRY1 and AGB1. In darkness, CRY1 is inactive and unable to repress the activity of COP1/SPAs, and as a result, COP1/SPAs actively mediate the ubiquitination and degradation of HY5. The HY5-repressed hypocotyl elongation-promoting genes are actively expressed, leading to enhanced hypocotyl elongation ([Figure 7](#page-10-0)A). Upon blue light radiation, CRY1 potentially undergoes a redox reaction-induced conformational change and is activated ([Cashmore et al., 1999; Kondoh et al.,](#page-13-1) [2011; Ahmad, 2016\)](#page-13-1). CRY1 may then repress COP1 activity through its C terminus-mediated interactions with COP1 and SPA1 ([Yang et al., 2001; Lian et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2011;](#page-15-13) [Holtkotte et al., 2017](#page-15-13)), leading to the accumulation of HY5 protein [\(Figure 7B](#page-10-0)). The DNA-binding activity of HY5 is negatively regulated by AGB1. At the same time AGB1 can be sequestered by photoactivated CRY1, which interacts with AGB1 through its N terminus ([Figure 7B](#page-10-0)). Given that AGB1 interacts with and represses the transcriptional activity of BBX21 [\(Xu et al., 2017\)](#page-15-11), it is possible that the CRY1–AGB1 interaction affects the association of AGB1 with BBX21, which may result in an increase in the transcriptional activity of BBX21. A recent study showed that BBX21 directly activates the transcription of HY5 and is targeted by COP1 through direct interaction for 26S proteasome-mediated degradation [\(Xu et al., 2016\)](#page-15-14). Based on previous and current studies, we propose that AGB1 represses photomorphogenesis at least partially by inhibiting the DNAbinding or transcriptional activity of several transcription factors such as HY5, HYH, and BBX21, which are the substrates of the COP1 E3 ligase. This proposition explains how COP1 and AGB1 act additively to repress photomorphogenesis. Given that HY5 is involved in both CRY and phytochrome signaling ([Osterlund et al., 2000; Wang et al., 2001; Yang et al., 2001;](#page-14-5) [Jang et al., 2010; Lian et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2011; Lu et al.,](#page-14-5) [2015; Sheerin et al., 2015\)](#page-14-5), and that CRY and phytochrome share the same downstream components including the COP1, SPA, PIF, and Aux/IAA proteins [\(Lian et al., 2011; Liu et al.,](#page-14-2) [2011; Lu et al., 2015; Sheerin et al., 2015; Ma et al., 2016;](#page-14-2) [Pedmale et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2017\)](#page-14-2), it is possible that phytochromes may regulate photomorphogenesis by interacting with AGB1 and regulating the DNA-binding activity of HY5.

## Plant Materials

All *A. thaliana* plants used in this study are of the Columbia ecotype. The *agb1*(*agb1-2*), *cop1*(*cop1-4*), *cry1 cry2* (*cry1-104 cry2-1*), and *hy5* mutants, the transgenic lines overexpressing Myc-tagged full-length CRY1 (*Myc-CRY1-ovx*, in the *cry1* mutant background), and CRY2 (*Myc-CRY2-ovx*, in the *cry2* mutant background) were described previously [\(McNellis et al., 1994; Lin et al., 1998; Ullah et al., 2003; Mao et al.,](#page-14-7) [2005; Sang et al., 2005; Lian et al., 2011\)](#page-14-7).

The *MCS* (multiple cloning site)-33*FLAG* fragment from pBS-33FLAG [\(Liu et al., 2008\)](#page-14-8) was cloned into the HindIII and BamHI sites of the pHB vector (Mao et al., 2005) to generate pHB-3×FLAG. The full-length cDNA of *AGB1* was amplified with the primers AGB1-BamHI-F and AGB1-Spel-R (all PCR primers are listed in Supplemental Table 5) by PCR, and ligated into the <u>BamHI</u> and <u>Spel</u> sites of pHB-3×FLAG to generate pHB-FLAG-AGB1. This construct was transferred into *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* strain GV3101. Transgenic lines overexpressing FLAG-AGB1 in the *Myc-CRY1-ovx, agb1*, and *hy5* mutant backgrounds (*FLAG-AGB1-ovx/Myc-CRY1-ovx, FLAG-AGB1-ovx/agb1*, and *FLAG-AGB1-ovx/hy5*, respectively) were screened on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) nutrient medium containing 50 mg/ml hygromycin, and confirmed by western blot analysis with antibodies against Myc (Millipore) and FLAG (Sigma).

Imbibed seeds were stored at 4°C for 3 days and grown on MS nutrient medium plus 1% sucrose with 0.8% agar at 22°C under white light (100  $\mu$ mol/m<sup>2</sup>/s). Experiments involving blue, red, and far-red light illumination were described previously (Jia et al., 2014). Light spectra and intensity were measured with a HandHeld spectroradiometer (ASD) and an Li250 quantum photometer (Li-Cor).

#### **Construction of Double and Triple Mutants**

The following double and triple mutants were generated for this study: cop1 agb1, cry1 cry2 agb1, agb1 hy5, hy5 hyh, and agb1 hy5 hyh. The double mutants cop1 agb1 and agb1 hy5 were generated by crossing agb1 with cop1 and hy5, respectively. hyh (CS849765) was obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (Ohio State University, USA). The hy5 hyh mutant was obtained by selecting F2 seedlings from a hy5 × hyh cross that had longer hypocotyls than the hy5 single mutant under blue, red, and far-red light. The triple mutants cry1 cry2 agb1 and agb1 hy5 hyh were obtained by crossing agb1 with cry1 cry2 and hy5 hyh, respectively. All genotypes were confirmed by phenotypic analyses and PCR.

#### Hypocotyl Length Measurements

For mutant phenotype analysis, seeds were sown on MS medium with 1% sucrose, cold-treated for 3 days at 4°C, and transferred to different light conditions as indicated. Seedlings were photographed with a digital camera (Olympus) after growth for 5 days. The hypocotyl length was measured using NIH ImageJ software (http://rsbweb.nih.gov/ij/).

#### **Anthocyanin Measurements**

Anthocyanin measurements were conducted as previously described with minor modifications (Chory et al., 1991). Twenty seedlings were soaked in 600  $\mu$ l of 1% HCl (v/v) diluted with methanol, acidified, and incubated for 12 h at 4°C. After addition of 400  $\mu$ l of ddH<sub>2</sub>O and 1 ml of chloroform, the absorbance of the aqueous phase at 530 nm and 657 nm was measured, and anthocyanin content was calculated using the following equation ( $A_{530} - A_{657}$ )/Fw, where Fw represents the fresh weight of 20 seedlings. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

For the LexA yeast two-hybrid assay, the cDNA sequences encoding full-length AGB1, AGB1 lacking the coiled-coil region, and AGB1 lacking the WD40 domain were amplified with the primers AGB1-EcoRI-F/AGB1-XhoI-R,  $\Delta$ CC-EcoRI-F/AGB1-XhoI-R, and AGB1-EcoRI-F/ $\Delta$ WD40-XhoI-R, and ligated into the EcoRI and XhoI sites of pLexA (Clontech Laboratories) to generate pLexA-AGB1, -AGB1 $\Delta$ CC, and -AGB1 $\Delta$ WD40, respectively. The cDNA sequences encoding HY5, HYH, and the HY5 deletion fragments HN1 (residues 1–77), HC1

(residues 78–168), HN2 (residues 1–115), H(N2-N1) (residues 78–115), and HC2 (residues 116–168) were amplified with the primers HY5-EcoRI-F/ HY5-XhoI-R, HYH-EcoRI-F/HYH-XhoI-R, HY5-EcoRI-F/HN1-XhoI-R, HC1-EcoRI-F/HY5-XhoI-R, HY5-EcoRI-F/HN2-XhoI-R, HC1-EcoRI-F/ H(N2-N1)-XhoI-R, and HC2-EcoRI-F/HY5-XhoI-R, and cloned into the EcoRI and XhoI sites of pB42AD to generate pB42AD-HY5, -HYH, -HN1, -HC1, -HN2, -H(N2-N1), and -HC2, respectively. pB42AD-COP1 was made previously (Yang et al., 2001).

## Yeast Three-Hybrid Assay

The LexA system yeast three-hybrid assay was used to test the influence of CRY1 on the AGB1-HY5 interaction. pGADT7-CRY1 was made previously (Lian et al., 2011). The pLexA-AGB1, pB42AD-HY5, and p8op-LacZ vectors, together with pGADT7-CRY1 or pGADT7 (negative control) were cotransformed into EGY48 yeast cells using the lithium acetate transformation procedure (Clontech Laboratories). Transformed colonies were selected on SD-Trp-Leu-His-Ura (SD-T-L-H-U) medium. Four independent clones with three replicates were used in each experiment. Cell cultures in liquid SD-T-L-H-U medium with 20 g/l D-(+)-galactose and 10 g/I D-(+)-raffinose pentahydrate were cultured with shaking in darkness for at least 9 h before being treated with blue light at the indicated light intensities or further incubated in the dark. All of the cell cultures were incubated until the OD<sub>600</sub>  $\approx$  0.8, then the relative  $\beta$ -galactosidase activities were tested as described previously (Yang et al., 2001). Three independent experiments were performed and one representative is shown.

## Pull-Down Assays with E. coli-Expressed Proteins

The full-length cDNA of *AGB1* was amplified with the primers AGB1-EcoRI-F and AGB1-XhoI-R and ligated into the EcoRI and Sall sites of pCOLD-TF (TaKaRa) to generate the vectors expressing His (histidine)tagged AGB1 in *E. coli*. The cDNA sequences encoding CNT1 and CCT1 were amplified with the primers CRY1-EcoRI-F/CNT1-XhoI-R and CCT1-EcoRI-F/CRY1-XhoI-R, respectively, and cloned into the EcoRI and Sall sites of pMAL-c2X (New England Biolabs) to generate the vectors expressing MBP-tagged CNT1 and CCT1 in *E. coli*. The MBP-tagged proteins were purified according to the manufacturer's protocol (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Detection of the interactions of AGB1 with CNT1 and CCT1 was performed as described previously (Wang et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2018). The prey proteins were detected using the anti-MBP antibody (NewEast, Wu-han, China), and the bait proteins were visualized by Coomassie brilliant blue staining.

## Pull-Down Assays with Arabidopsis Protein Extracts

For investigation of the blue light-dependent CRY1–AGB1 and CRY2-AGB1 interactions, His-AGB1 bait protein was first incubated with 10  $\mu$ l of nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) agarose beads for 1 h and washed three times with lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 0.2% Triton X-100). The protein extracts from *Myc-CRY1-ovx* and MG132-treated *Myc-CRY2-ovx* seedlings adapted in the dark and exposed to different fluence rates of blue light for 1 h (*Myc-CRY1-ovx*) or blue light (20  $\mu$ mol/m<sup>2</sup>/s) and red light (20  $\mu$ mol/m<sup>2</sup>/s) for 1 h (*Myc-CRY2-ovx*) served as the prey. All seedlings were homogenized with lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 0.2% Trition -X-100)

containing 1 mM Pefabloc and cocktail, and 50  $\mu$ M MG132. After centrifugation the supernatant was incubated with His-AGB1 proteinbound Ni-NTA agarose beads for 30 min and washed 3–4 times with 1 ml of lysis buffer each time, then the precipitates were eluted into 20  $\mu$ l of SDS 1× loading buffer and subjected to western blot analysis with anti-Myc antibody.

#### **Protein Colocalization and BiFC Assays**

The constructs used to produce the vectors for protein colocalization assays were pHB-CFP and -YFP, which carry the 35S::CFP and 35S::YFP cassettes, respectively (Lian et al., 2011; Luo et al., 2014). The fulllength cDNA of AGB1 was amplified with the primers AGB1-BamHI-F and AGB1-Spel-R and ligated into the BamHI and Spel sites of pHB-CFP to obtain pHB-CFP-AGB1. pHB-YFP-NLS-CNT1 was made previously (He et al., 2015). The vectors used to produce constructs for the BiFC assays were pXY104 and pXY106, which carry fragments encoding the C- and N-terminal halves of YFP (cYFP and nYFP), respectively (Luo et al., 2014; Lu et al., 2015). The full-length cDNAs of AGB1 and HY5 were amplified with the primers AGB1-BamHI-F/AGB1-Spel-R and HY5-BamHI-F/HY5-Spel-R, respectively, and ligated into the BamHI and Spel sites of the pXY106 vector (Yu et al., 2008; Liu and Howell, 2010) to generate pXY106-AGB1 and -HY5. The cDNA sequences encoding AGB1, CRY1, CNT1, CRY2, and HYH were amplified with the primers AGB1-Spel-F/AGB1-Sall-R, CRY1-Spel-F/ CRY1-Sall-R, CNT1-Spel-F/CNT1-Sall-R, CRY2-Spel-F/CRY2-Sall-R, and HYH-Spel-F/HYH-Sall-R and ligated into the Spel and Sall sites of the pXY104 vector to generate pXY104-AGB1, -CRY1, -CNT1, -CRY2, and -HYH, respectively. These constructs were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. GV3101 cultures harboring constructs expressing nYFP fusion proteins and those expressing cYFP fusion proteins were mixed at a ratio of 1:1, infiltrated into tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) leaf epidermal cells at the combinations indicated in Figures 1F and 4F. and Supplemental Figures 1F, 1G, 3D, and 6D, and BiFC signals were detected using a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP5II) after 36-40 h.

### **Co-IP Assays**

For the Co-IP assays testing the interaction of CNT1 with AGB1 in vivo, FLAG-AGB1 was coexpressed with YFP-NLS-CNT1 (He et al., 2015) or YFP-NLS-GUS-CCT1 (Wang et al., 2018) in tobacco leaf cells. The tobacco leaf samples transiently coexpressing FLAG-AGB1 and YFP-GUS-CCT1 or FLAG-AGB1 and YFP-CNT1 were first adapted in darkness for 24 h and exposed to blue light (30 µmol/m<sup>2</sup>/s) or red light (30 µmol/m<sup>2</sup>/s) for 12 h. The samples adapted in the dark for a total of 36 h served as the dark control. The samples were homogenized in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 0.2% Trition -X-100) containing 1 mM Pefabloc and cocktail, and 50 µM MG132. After centrifugation, the supernatant was incubated for 2 h at  $4^{\circ}$ C with 20  $\mu$ l of protein G magnetic beads (bed volume, GE Healthcare), which had previously been incubated at 4°C for 1 h with 5 µl of anti-GFP antibody (GeneScript). The immunoprecipitates were washed two times with lysis buffer and eluted by boiling with 2× SDS loading buffer for 5 min. The eluates were subjected to western blot analysis with anti-FLAG (Sigma) and anti-GFP (Abmart) antibodies.

For the Co-IP assay testing the interaction of CRY1 with AGB1, 4-day-old etiolated seedlings of double transgenic plants *FLAG-AGB1-ovx/Myc-CRY1-ovx* were exposed to the indicated light conditions for 10 h. Total proteins were extracted with lysis buffer and incubated with 10  $\mu$ l of FLAG beads (Sigma) for 1 h at 4°C. For the Co-IP assay testing the interaction of AGB1 with HY5, *FLAG-AGB1-ovx/agb1* and *FLAG-AGB1-ovx/hy5* transgenic seedlings were grown in white light (100  $\mu$ mol/m<sup>2</sup>/s) for 4 days. Total proteins were extracted with lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 0.2% Trition -X-100) containing 1 mM Pefabloc and cocktail, and 50  $\mu$ M MG132. After centrifugation, the supernatant was incubated at 4°C for 3 h with 10  $\mu$ l of protein G magnetic beads (GE Healthcare), which had previously been incubated with 5  $\mu$ l of anti-

HY5 antiserum (Jing et al., 2013) at 4°C overnight. The beads were washed three times with lysis buffer, and the precipitated proteins were eluted by boiling with 2× SDS sample buffer for 5 min and then subjected to immunoblot analysis. Anti-FLAG (Sigma), anti-Myc (Millipore), and anti-HY5 (Jing et al., 2013) antibodies were used to detect FLAG-AGB1, Myc-CRY1, and HY5, respectively.

#### Electrophoretic Mobility-Shift Assay

EMSA assays were performed using biotin-labeled probes and the Light Shift Chemiluminescent EMSA kit (Thermo). The *CHS* promoter sequences containing a double G-box (43-bp fragment of the *CHS* promoter from -150 to -192) were used to generate the biotin-labeled and unlabeled probes. A total of 300 ng of MBP-HY5 protein alone or MBP-HY5 combined with increasing amounts of His-AGB1 (100–500 ng) protein or with 500 ng of His-TF protein was incubated with 20 nM biotin-labeled probe in a 20 µl of reaction mixture (10 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 50 ng/ml poly dl-dC, 2.5% glycerol, 0.05% NP-40, 100 µM ZnCl<sub>2</sub>, and 0.5 µg/ml BSA) at room temperature for 20 min. For the cold competitor (lane 4 in Figure 5A), 4 µM unlabeled probe was added into the reaction mixture. The reaction mixtures were separated on 6% native polyacrylamide gels. The labeled probes were detected according to the instructions provided with the EMSA kit.

#### Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Followed by Quantitative PCR

ChIP assays were performed as described by Zhang et al. (2014) with minor modifications. WT, *agb1*, and *hy5* mutants were grown in white light (100  $\mu$ mol/m<sup>2</sup>/s) for 4–5 days. All seedlings were harvested under the same light conditions and fixed in fixation buffer (0.4 M sucrose, 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 1% formaldehyde, 5mM β-mercaptoethanol) under vacuum at 4°C. Glycine was added to a final concentration of 0.125 M under vacuum to stop the fixation reaction. The sonicated protein–DNA complexes were immunoprecipitated with anti-HY5 antibody (Jing et al., 2013). The *hy5* mutant processed using the same immunoprecipitation conditions was used as a negative control. After ChIP, the enrichment for specific DNA fragments was examined by qPCR. IP/input (%) was calculated by comparing the Ct values of the immunoprecipitate and input from each genotype. The primers used for qPCR are listed in Supplemental Table 5.

#### **RNA-Seq and qRT-PCR**

Seeds were germinated on half-strength MS plates supplemented with 1% sucrose and placed at 4°C for 3 days, then transferred to white light for 12 h before being placed in blue light (6 μmol/m<sup>2</sup>/s) for another 4 days. Total RNA was extracted from samples including WT, agb1, cry1 cry2, and hy5 with an RNAprep plant kit (Tiangen), followed by DNase I (Takara) treatment. Quality control was performed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Two replicates for each genotype/treatment were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeg2500. The library construction and sequencing were performed by Hanyu BioTech in Shanghai (Pu Dong, Shanghai, China). Illumina Casava1.7 software was used for base calling. Sequenced reads were trimmed to remove adaptor sequence, masked to remove low-complexity or low-quality sequence, then mapped to a primary transcript dataset consisting of the primary transcripts of the A. thaliana TAIR10 gene set using Bowtie 2 version 2.2.2. When identifying genes differentially expressed between two samples, we considered both fold change and adjusted p value (q value), which was revised for multiple testing with the Benjamina–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR < 0.01) (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) Genes with a fold change >1.5 and q < 0.01 were defined as differentially expressed genes. A Venn diagram was generated in Venny (http://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/ venny/index.html). Heatmap generation and hierarchical clustering analysis were performed using MeV 4.7 software. GO analysis was performed in agriGO (http://bioinfo.cau.edu.cn/agriGO/), with TAIR10 as the reference, using a hypergeometric statistical test method and complete GO type.

The detailed qRT–PCR method was described previously ([Zhang et al.,](#page-15-17) [2014; He et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2016a, 2016b](#page-15-17)). *ACTIN2* was used as an internal control. The primers used are listed in Supplemental Table 6.
