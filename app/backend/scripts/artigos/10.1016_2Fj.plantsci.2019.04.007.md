#### Highlights

• EMSY-Like histone readers EML1/EML3 are required for fertilization-dependent seed development

- EML1/EML3 are involved in preventing seed development in the absence of fertilization
- Auxin responses are elevated in seeds containing mutations in EML1/EML3

#### Abstract

Seed development is a complex regulatory process that includes a transition from gametophytic to sporophytic program. The synchronized development of different seed compartments (seed coat, endosperm and embryo) depends on a balance in parental genome contributions. In the most severe cases, the disruption of the balance leads to seed abortion. This represents one of the main obstacles for the engineering of asexual reproduction through seeds (apomixis), and for generating new interspecies hybrids. The repression of auxin synthesis by the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) is a major mechanism contributing to sensing genome balance. However, current efforts focusing on decreasing PRC2 or elevating auxin levels have not yet resulted in the production of apomictic seed. Here, we show that EMSY-Like Tudor/Agenet H3K36me3 histone readers EML1 and EML3 are necessary for early stages of seed development to proceed at a normal rate in Arabidopsis. We further demonstrate that both EML1 and EML3 are required to prevent the initiation of seed development in the absence of fertilization. Based on the whole genome expression analysis, we identify auxin transport and signaling genes as the most enriched downstream targets of EML1 and EML3. We hypothesize that EML1 and EML3 function to repress and soften paternal gene expression by fine-tuning auxin responses. Discovery of this pathway may contribute to the engineering of apomixis and interspecies hybrids.

#### Introduction

The transcriptional output of gene expression is governed by the interaction of the DNA with transcription factors (TFs) and by epigenetic mechanisms. These mechanisms act at the chromatin level and depend on the positioning and occupancy of the nucleosomes, as well as posttranslational modifications (PTMs) of certain amino acid residues on histone proteins [1]. The PTMs of the conserved N-terminal histone tails are deposited by histone writer proteins, and can be removed by eraser proteins in a reversible process. Histone PTMs (marks) can affect chromatin interactions and structure directly and/or indirectly through binding of specific effector proteins histone readers. Histone tail modifications have been proposed to serve as a complex histone code, in which common modifications including acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation and ubiquitination are interpreted by reader proteins to orchestrate specific chromatin alterations [2, 3]. Lysine methylation in plants can be associated with transcriptional activation or repression. Examples of repressive marks include dimethylated histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9me2) and H3K27me3, while H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 are linked to active genes [4]. Histone readers recognize and bind marks on histone tails by specific domains, including plant homeodomains (PHD) and "Royal Family" domains (chromodomain, Tudor/Agenet, PWWP and MBT) [5]. Some

readers contain a single domain, whereas others contain multiple domains and/or interact with other factors in macromolecular complexes. A number of plant readers have been identified in the past decade. These include LHP1, whose single chromodomain recognizes H3K27me3, SHH1 (SAWADEE HOMEODOMAIN HOMOLOGUE 1), containing a tandem Tudor-like fold known as the SAWADEE domain specific for H3K9me2, as well as MRG1 (MORF RELATED PROTEINS GENE 1) and MRG2, each with a chromodomain showing dual binding preferences for H3K36me3 and H3K4me3 marks [6-8]. Thus far, characterization of plant readers has been mostly limited to *in vitro* histone-binding studies and structural analysis, while relatively little is known about their function *in planta*.

A large group of histone readers, represented by the Tudor/Agenet domain-containing proteins, shows specificity for methylated lysine or arginine residues [9]. A small subgroup of plant Tudor/Agenet proteins also harbors an EMSY N-terminal (ENT) domain, characteristic of the BRCA2-interacting human EMSY protein [10]. The founding member of this subgroup, maize R-Interacting Factor 1 (RIF1), was identified as a regulator of anthocyanin gene expression [11]. The ENT-Tudor/Agenet domain subgroup is represented in Arabidopsis (*Arabidopsis thaliana*) by EMSY-Like proteins EML1 - EML4 [12], with a structure closely related to animal Tudor domain proteins [5]. The Tudor/Agenet domain of EML1 was recently characterized as a dual H3K4me3/H3K36me3 histone reader [5], while the full-length EML1 protein shows preference for H3K36me3 and is involved in the defense against geminiviruses [13]. Still, the functional relevance of EML histone readers during normal plant development remains poorly understood.

Double fertilization in flowering plants results in a diploid embryo and a triploid endosperm, enclosed by maternal diploid seed coat. The 2:1 maternal to paternal genome ratio in

the endosperm is required in many species to sustain successful seed development. However, it also presents a barrier against asexual seed development (apomixis), as well as interspecies and interploidy hybridization [14-16]. Multiple mechanisms exist in Arabidopsis to keep parental contributions in balance, including the regulation of genes and transposable elements by the DNA methylation and Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2)-dependent histone methylation [17], by small RNAs [18-20] and by paternally expressed auxin [21-23]. Of these mechanisms, the role of PRC2 in this regulation has been studied in most detail. The FIS-PRC2 is a four subunit (MEA, FIS2, FIE and MSI1) complex, specific to the female gametophyte and endosperm, which acts to repress endosperm and seed coat development in the absence of fertilization. Two other PRC2 complexes, VRN-PRC2 and EMF-PRC2, function in sporophytic tissues and are responsible for the repression of seed coat development before fertilization. Auxin synthesis appears to be necessary for endosperm and seed coat development in Arabidopsis [21, 22], and auxin transport is required for embryogenesis in Arabidopsis and *Brassica napus* [24]. Auxin overproduction in fertilized seeds results in phenotypes resembling paternal excess. Conversely, seeds derived from paternal excess crosses are characterized by the upregulation of genes involved in the biosynthesis, transport, and response to auxin [23]. Recent studies have established that FIS-PRC2 serves as a repressor of auxin synthesis in the central cell before fertilization [21]. After fertilization, paternally-supplied auxin triggers endosperm development and is transported to the integuments, where it represses the function of the sporophytic EMF/VRN-PRC2 complex, resulting in the initiation of seed coat formation [22]. Most importantly, either a lack of PRC2 function or maternal overproduction of auxin can result in the development of endosperm and seed coat in the absence of fertilization [22]. However, neither the PRC2 complex mutants, nor the ectopic application of auxin is sufficient to completely induce apomictic seed development, suggesting that there are

additional control mechanisms that need to be leveraged as part of efforts to engineer apomixis and to successfully bypass interspecies hybridization barriers.

Here, we used a reverse genetic approach to characterize the function of the EML histone readers EML1 and EML3 in Arabidopsis plants. By observing fertilization products in loss-of-function mutants, we demonstrate that these two proteins act synergistically to ensure that the ovules are receptive to fertilization, enabling the success of this process and subsequent seed production. Consistent with the published role of auxin in controlling seed development, we show that transcripts encoding components of auxin signaling and transport pathways are elevated in the *eml1-2 eml3-4* double mutant after fertilization. In addition, we demonstrate the ability of emasculated *eml1-2 eml3-4* to produce seeds containing embryos in the absence of fertilization. Finally, we propose that one mechanism by which EML1 and EML3 histone readers act to prevent asexual seed development and coordinate post-fertilization seed production is accomplished by the regulation of auxin responses during these processes.

#### 1. Materials and Methods

#### **1.1. Plant material**

*Arabidopsis thaliana* accessions Col-0 (CS70000), Ler-0 (CS20), and Col-3 (CS908), as well as a T-DNA mutant line *eml4-1* (CS860536) were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resources Center (ABRC). The transposon/T-DNA homozygous mutant alleles *eml1-2*, *eml2-1* and *eml3-4* were identified from ABRC stocks CS101744, SALK\_116222 and SALK\_106147, respectively, by PCR genotyping using primers listed in Table S1. The *eml3-4* and *eml1-2* single mutants were crossed and their resultant F2 population was screened for *eml1-2 eml3-4* double mutant line. Its corresponding wild-type line was identified as a sibling of the double mutant line

*eml1-2 eml3-4* from the F2 progeny of the same cross. Plants were grown in a growth chamber under a long day photoperiod (16/8 h day/night) at 22°C.

#### **1.2.** Genetic crosses and emasculation

Crosses were performed by emasculating designated female partners and hand-pollinating the pistils. Siliques were harvested at various times after pollination, as indicated in figure legends and collected in RNA*later*<sup>TM</sup> Stabilization solution (Ambion) until further use. Autonomous seed development was microscopically observed 10 days after emasculation. RNA was isolated 24 hours after emasculation (ovules) or 5 days after pollination (5 DAP seeds). Cross-pollination was prevented by wrapping the emasculated pistils in saran wrap until collection.

#### **1.3.** DNA extraction and genotyping

For genotyping, genomic DNA was extracted from leaf samples as previously described [25]. Primers used for genotyping are listed in Table S1.

#### **1.4.** Plasmid Constructs and Generation of Transgenic Plants

The *pEML1::GFP-EML1* construct was generated by combining the *EML1* promoter with a GFP-tagged *EML1* coding sequence using Multisite Gateway Technology (Invitrogen). The *EML1* promoter region (1,349 bp upstream from the ATG start codon) was amplified from Ler genomic DNA. *EML1* CDS was amplified from Ler cDNA and sGFP sequence was amplified from pGWB6 vector [26]. All PCR reactions were performed with high-fidelity Pfx DNA polymerase (Invitrogen) and primers with suitable recombination site (*attB*) adapters (see Table S1). PCR fragments were cloned into pDONR vectors (Invitrogen; pDONR221 for GFP tag, pDONRP4P1R for the *EML1* promoter region, and pDONRP2RP3 for *EML1* CDS) via BP reaction using BP

Clonase II (Invitrogen). The three fragments were combined in an LR reaction into pH7m34GW (VIB-UGent Center for Plant Systems Biology), and were used to transform the Agrobacterium (*Agrobacterium tumefaciens*) strain GV3101. *eml1-2* mutants were transformed by floral dip and transgenic plants were selected on Hygromycin. The homozygous T3 lines were assayed for the complementation of embryo development. Reporter expression and transgene complementation were verified in three independent homozygous T3 lines.

The *pEML3::GFP-EML3* construct was generated as follows: full-length *EML3* coding sequence, amplified from Col cDNA, was cloned into the pENTR/D-TOPO vector (Invitrogen) to make *pENTR-EML3* and mobilized into the destination vector pGWB6 containing an N-terminal GFP tag using Gateway LR clonase (Invitrogen). The *CaMV35S* promoter in pGWB6 was replaced by the *EML3* promoter region (1,114 bp upstream from the ATG start codon amplified from the Col genomic DNA), using *Xba*I and *Sbf*I. The construct was transformed into Agrobacterium strain GV3101. *pEML3::GFP-EML3* construct was transformed into *eml3-4* mutant plants by floral dip. Transformants were selected on hygromycin and kanamycin. The homozygous T3 lines were assayed for the complementation of embryo development and protein localization. Reporter expression was verified in three independent homozygous T3 lines.

#### 1.5. Microscopy

For phenotypic analysis, individual seeds and embryos were observed using an Olympus light microscope with U-PMTVC microscope camera (Olympus). The vanillin staining was performed on ovules 10 days after emasculation and seeds 6 days after pollination in 1% (w/v) vanillin (4-hydroxy-3-methoxybenzaldehyde) in 6 N HCl. After incubating in the vanillin staining solution for 20-45 min, emasculated pistils and siliques were dissected to expose ovules and seeds,

which were then mounted on a microscope slide. Images were recorded using a U-PMTVC microscope camera (Olympus). To perform differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy, seeds were mounted in Hoyer's solution as described [27], and embryo development was examined using the Nikon TiE inverted microscope equipped with differential interference contrast (Andor Revolution WD spinning disk confocal and the Andor Neo camera for imaging). Confocal laser scanning microscopy was performed using a Nikon Confocal A1+ microscope (Nikon). The images were captured and processed by NIS-Elements software (Nikon).

#### 1.6. RT-qPCR

The results were obtained using at least three biological replicates. A bioreplicate represented a pool of tissue collected from multiple plants. Plant tissue was harvested and either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen or collected in RNA*later*<sup>TM</sup> Stabilization solution (Ambion) until further use. Total RNA was extracted from tissue ground in liquid nitrogen using PureLink<sup>®</sup> RNA Reagent (Life Technologies), according to the manufacturer's instructions followed by DNase I treatment (Life Technologies). cDNA was synthesized using Thermoscript II Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Life Technologies) with oligo-dT<sub>12-18</sub> primer. A negative control without reverse transcriptase was used for each RT-PCR experiment. IQ<sup>TM</sup> SYBR<sup>®</sup> Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) was used to perform the qPCR in a BioRad CFX96 System (Bio-Rad). *MEA* primers used for the RT-qPCR are listed in Table S1. *PPA2* (At1G13320) was used as a reference gene. Relative quantification of gene expression was performed as described [28].

#### 1.7. mRNA-Seq library construction

The preparation of mRNA-Seq libraries was performed according to instructions from the Illumina mRNA-Seq sample preparation kit (Illumina). Total RNA samples extracted from three

replicates of each wild-type and eml1-2 eml3-4 siliques (1-5 DAP) were treated with DNase I (Life Technologies) twice to ensure no genomic DNA contamination. One microgram of total RNA was subjected to two rounds of hybridization to oligo(dT) beads (Illumina). The poly(A)-enriched mRNA was fragmented by heating at 94°C for 3 min in fragmentation buffer supplied by Illumina. The fragmented mRNA was added to the first strand cDNA synthesis reaction including the First Strand cDNA Synthesis Buffer (Invitrogen), 0.5 mM deoxynucleotide triphosphate (dNTP) mix, 10 mM DTT, RNaseOUT, and random primers mix. After adding Superscript II (Invitrogen), first strand cDNA synthesis was performed at 25°C for 10 min, 42°C for 50 min, and 70°C for 15 min. The second-strand cDNA synthesis was performed in the Second Strand Buffer (Invitrogen), 0.3 mM dNTP mix, with RNase H and DNA Pol I for 2.5 h at 16°C and purified using 1.8X AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter). After end repair and dA-tailing, adaptor ligation was performed using the NEBNext ChIP-Seq Library Prep Master Mix Set for Illumina (NEB) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Ligated fragments were subjected for size selection of fragments of ~300 bp in length, followed by the 12 cycles of PCR amplification. PCR products were purified with the AMPure XP Beads. The quality and size distribution of obtained libraries were tested using an Agilent Bioanalyzer before being subjected to Illumina sequencing.

#### 1.8. Computational analysis of RNA-Seq data

Paired-end FASTQ data was adapter and quality trimmed using *Trim Galore* tool and aligned to Araport11 genome using STAR version 2.5.2a [29] with maximum intron size of 10 kb. Duplicates were removed from alignments using *SAMtools* [30]. Deduplicated alignments were converted to counts per genomic feature with *featureCounts* from the subread package [31]. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the R package DESeq2 [32]. The final list of

584 genes upregulated in the *eml1-2 eml3-4* double mutant was selected from the differential expression data by filtering for genes with a  $\log_2$  fold change > 0.6 (approximately 1.5x fold change) and a multiple-testing corrected *P*-value < 0.05. The data was analyzed using Ohio Supercomputer Center resources [33].

#### **1.9.** Gene list analysis

*eml1-2 eml3-4* upregulated genes were compared with genes up- and down- regulated in paternal-excess crosses 2n x 4n [34] and WT x *osd1* [35], and genes upregulated in *mea* mutant crosses at 3 DAP and 6 DAP [36]. The significance was determined using the hypergeometric test.

#### **1.10. Statistical analysis**

The ANOVA, Fisher LSD post-hoc tests and Mann-Whitney U test were carried out at  $\alpha < 0.05$  using Minitab.

#### 1.11. Phylogenetic and expression profiles analyses

Ensembl Plants [37] was used to obtain the homologs of maize RIF1 in all available plant species. Representative clade members of RIF1 homologs were aligned using the Clustal Omega algorithm of MegAlign Pro (DNASTAR) to generate a phylogenetic tree. Tissue-specific expression analysis of EML genes was performed using Genevestigator [38].

#### **1.12.** Accession numbers

The mutant lines *eml1-2* (CS69823), *eml2-1* (CS69824), *eml3-4* (CS69825), *eml1-2 eml3-4* (CS69826), its corresponding wild type (CS69827), as well as transgenic lines *GFP-EML1* (CS69828) and *GFP-EML3* (CS69997) have been donated and are available from ABRC. The

RNA-Seq data have been submitted and are available through the NCBI GEO database [http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/] under the accession number GSE113847.

2. Results

#### 2.1. Mutations in EML genes lead to a delay in embryo development

To determine EML evolutionary conservation we examined ENT-Tudor/Agenet domain proteins in sequenced plant genomes and found that this domain structure has been conserved throughout the plant lineage, with representative proteins present in basal clades such as green algae, mosses and lycophytes (Figure S1). To functionally characterize Arabidopsis EML proteins, we identified homozygous mutants *eml1-2*, *eml2-1*, and *eml3-4* by genotyping the transposon/T-DNA insertion lines obtained from the ABRC collection, as described in the section 2.1. The *eml4-1* homozygous insertion mutant stock was obtained as a homozygous line from the ABRC. All insertions were confirmed by sequencing and their positions are represented in the Figure S2a. Using RT-qPCR and primers that flank (*eml1-2* and *eml4-1*) or are downstream (*eml2-1* and *eml3-4*) of the transposon/T-DNA insertions, we showed that the transcripts corresponding to these genes were either undetectable or significantly decreased in the mutants (Figure S2b). Vegetative growth of the homozygous mutants was indistinguishable from their corresponding wild-types, consistent with all four genes being primarily expressed in seeds and other reproductive structures (Figure S2c).

To assess reproductive development in *eml*, the mutant plants were self-pollinated and the development of the resulting seeds was compared to their respective wild-types at six days after pollination (6 DAP). Seeds obtained by reciprocal crosses between mutant and corresponding wild-type plants were also analyzed. To distinguish seeds from unfertilized ovules, we utilized

vanillin, which reacts with proanthocyanidins produced in the endothelium of fertilized seeds resulting in the dark red staining of this seed coat layer [22, 39]. The vanillin staining suggested that, while all types of crosses resulted in some level of ovule abortion (2-20%; Figure S3), the difference was statistically significant only when self-pollinated *eml3-4* (14% aborted ovules) was compared to crosses including the *eml3-4* mutant as one parent, either female (5% aborted ovules) or male (3% aborted ovules) (Figure S3, Table S2). Following vanillin staining, the embryos were dissected from the seeds and surveyed to determine their developmental stage. The dissection revealed that embryo development of each of the self-pollinated mutants was statistically significantly delayed (49-86% delayed) in comparison with the wild-type (10-20% delayed) (Figure S3, Table S2). The delay in embryo development was evident up until 7 DAP, after which these mutant seeds progressed through development at a normal rate. This phenotype was also observed in the reciprocal crosses of *eml1-2*, *eml2-1*, and *eml3-4* with their respective wild-types (Figure S3, Table S2). To investigate the inheritance of the delay in embryo development, we crossed *eml1-2* and *eml3-4* heterozygotes to their respective wild-type parents, Ler-0 and Col-0. For both mutants, we observed a delay in embryo development in ~50% of the progeny (Table S3), consistent with either gametophytic or dominant inheritance of the mutant phenotype, suggesting that both EML1 and EML3 are haploinsufficient.

#### 2.2. EML1 and EML3 localize to reproductive structures before and after fertilization

To assess the combinatorial regulation of seed development by different EML proteins and better understand the phenotypic effects of combining multiple EML mutations, we performed a detailed localization study, selecting *EML1* and *EML3* as representative genes with relatively mild single mutant phenotypes. EML1 localization was determined through the use of an *EML1* 

minigene consisting of  $\sim 1.35$  kb upstream of the start codon, followed by GFP translationally fused to the *EML1* protein coding sequence (*pEML1::GFP-EML1*). This construct was transformed into *eml1-2* homozygous plants, and transgene presence in transgenic plants was verified by PCR (as described later, this construct complemented the *eml1* mutant phenotypes). Confocal fluorescence microscopy showed that, before fertilization, *EML1* was expressed in the male gametophyte and in the ovule integuments of eml1-2/eml1-2 pEML1::GFP-EML1/pEML1::GFP-EML1 plants (Figure 1a). After fertilization, EML1 expression was observed in the endosperm up to 3 DAP (Figure 1c) and in the embryo and seed coat throughout seed development (Figure 1d). Similarly, an *EML3* minigene construct, consisting of 1.1 kb of the *EML3* promoter driving GFP-EML3 expression (*pEML3::GFP-EML3*), was transformed into the *eml3-4* homozygous mutant background. The resulting eml3-4/eml3-4 pEML3::GFP-EML3/pEML3::GFP-EML3 transgenic plants were verified by PCR and observed by confocal microscopy (*pEML3::GFP-EML3* also complements the mutant phenotype, see below). Much like EML1, EML3 showed localization in the ovule integuments. EML3 was also present in pollen, albeit at a lower level than EML1 (Figure 1b). While we could not detect EML3 expression in the endosperm after fertilization, EML3 was detected in both the seed coat and the embryo (Figure 1e). Both EML1 and EML3 were found in the suspensor during early stages of embryo development (Figure 1d, transition stage embryo and Figure 1e, globular stage embryo). At later developmental stages, EML3 localization was confined to the root apical meristem region (Figure 1e, heart to bent cotyledon embryos); which was in contrast to EML1, which marked the entire embryo (Figure 1d). The reciprocal crosses of eml1*pEML1::GFP-EML1/pEML1::GFP-EML1* eml3-4/eml3-4 pEML3::GFP-2/eml1-2 and *EML3/pEML3::GFP-EML3* to their respective wild-types demonstrated that embryo localization for both proteins was independent of the parental origin of the transgenic construct (Figure S4a,

b). Together, localization studies demonstrated accumulation of EML1 and EML3 in pollen and ovules, suggesting an important role for these histone readers prior to fertilization. The presence of both proteins in different seed compartments indicates their continued regulatory function after fertilization, with shared roles in the embryo and seed coat, and perhaps a more prominent role of EML1 in the endosperm.

The reciprocal crosses of *eml1-2* and *eml3-4* to their respective wild-types showed a significant delay in embryo development, compared to self-pollinated wild-type (Figure S3). The mutant phenotype was observed regardless of whether a mutant allele was provided by the male or female gametophyte (Figure S3a, c). To investigate the ability of *pEML1::GFP-EML1* and *pEML3::GFP-EML3* to complement their respective mutants, we observed embryo development of *eml1-2/eml1-2 pEML1::GFP-EML1/pEML1::GFP-EML1* and *eml3-4/eml3-4 pEML3::GFP-EML3* reciprocally crossed to their respective wild-types. Demonstrating that the mutations in *EML1* and *EML3* were responsible for the respective mutant phenotypes, the introduction of the corresponding transgene, regardless of parent of origin, restored wild-type embryo development (Figure S3a, c).

#### 2.3. EML1 and EML3 regulate sexual seed development

To further investigate the function of EML1 and EML3, we analyzed the development of the homozygous *eml1-2 eml3-4* double mutant. In contrast to vegetative growth, which was not affected, the double mutant displayed phenotypic differences in reproductive development. While about 90% of the wild-type seeds contained embryos at the linear cotyledon stage at 6 DAP (representing normal embryo development), seeds of the self-pollinated *eml1-2 eml3-4* plants showed a range of developmental defects. The mutant ovule/seed developmental stages,

determined based on vanillin staining, included aborted ovules, aborted seeds, and seeds with delayed embryo development (Figure 2a). Aborted ovules, characterized by small size and the absence of any vanillin staining, represented 32% of the total ovule/seed count in self-pollinated *eml1-2 eml3-4* (Figure 2b). The remaining 68% of these structures showed presence of a vanillin-stained seed coat and were therefore classified as seeds. About 40% of these seeds (27% of the total) were small and displayed minimal vanillin staining, suggesting that they aborted shortly after fertilization (Figure 2b). The remaining *eml1-2 eml3-4* homozygous mutant seeds displayed normal size and vanillin staining, but embryo development was delayed (Figure 2a, *eml1-2 eml3-4*, delayed embryo development). Similar to the single mutants, the seeds in this category continued developing to produce mature seeds. These mature seeds appeared lighter in color and plumper than the wild-type (Figure 2c), but had normal viability with germination rates of about 90%.

In addition to analyzing self-pollinated *eml1-2 eml3-4* homozygous plants, we examined the seeds produced by the reciprocal crosses of *eml1-2 eml3-4* to wild-type plants. In cases when the *eml1-2 eml3-4* mutant was used as a female parent, 40% of the ovules were aborted (Figure 2b), similar to the self-pollinated mutant progeny and significantly different from the cross where the wild-type was used as a female parent (Figure 2b, Table S2). This result indicates that coordinated action of EML1 and EML3 is required to prevent ovules from aborting, consistent with a maternal function of these proteins. Unlike in the homozygous mutant, no significant seed abortion was observed in any of the reciprocal crosses (Figure 2b, Table S2), indicating that EML1 and EML3 also act after fertilization to prevent seed abortion, and that this function requires only one copy of each protein to be present. Each reciprocal cross had about 60% of seeds in which

embryo development was delayed (Figure 2b), which was statistically significantly higher than 42% observed in the self-pollinated *eml1-2 eml3-4* (Table S2). While none of the phenotypes was completely penetrant, this result suggests that the delay in embryo development is a milder version of the seed abortion phenotype, because the former requires *EML1* and *EML3* alleles to be mutated in just one parent, while seed abortion requires both alleles to be mutated in both parents. Mutant *eml1-2 eml3-4* pollen did not show any decrease in viability or *in vivo* germination compared to wild-type, suggesting that pollen defects take effect after fertilization (Figure S5). Together, our findings point towards two distinct roles for EML1 and EML3: a pre-fertilization maternal regulatory function to maintain fertilization potential in ovules and ensure successful fertilization, and a dosage-dependent regulation of sexual seed development after fertilization.

#### 2.4. EML1 and EML3 regulate auxin transport and signaling

To identify the molecular basis for the phenotypic changes in seed development, we analyzed genome-wide gene expression changes in the self-pollinated *eml1-2 eml3-4* mutant compared to wild-type. RNA-Seq was performed on mRNAs isolated from *eml1-2 eml3-4* and wild-type siliques during early stages of seed development (1-5 DAP). The mRNA accumulation levels were compared and the genes with > 1.5-fold change and a corrected *P*-value < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed between the two genotypes (DE, Table S4). DE analysis identified 584 upregulated and 174 downregulated genes, which were expressed at statistically significantly higher and lower levels in the mutant, respectively.

GO enrichment analysis of the genes upregulated in *eml1-2 eml3-4* showed that auxin transport and signaling genes were among the most significantly enriched/over-represented categories (Table 1), while the downregulated genes showed no enrichment for any category. Since

auxin responses are upregulated under paternal excess conditions [23], we wanted to determine whether upregulated auxin responses in *eml1-2 eml3-4* could be correlated to paternal excess at the molecular level. To do this, we compared DE genes of this mutant with genes known to be upregulated under increased paternal contribution conditions. These were represented by two published datasets: one containing genes upregulated in 2n x 4n interploidy crosses [34], and another comprising genes upregulated in a cross between wild-type and the *omission of second division (osd1)* mutant that generates unreduced diploid pollen [35]. The overlap between the genes upregulated in *eml1-2 eml3-4* and 2n x 4n dataset was statistically significant (P = 0.008, hypergeometric test), unlike the intersection of *eml1-2 eml3-4* and 2n x 4n downregulated genes (Figure 3a, Table S5). Similarly, there was a statistically significant overlap of genes upregulated in *eml1-2 eml3-4* and WT x *osd1* cross ( $P = 9 \times 10^{-24}$ , hypergeometric test) (Figure 3a, Table S5). These findings indicate that fertilized *eml1-2 eml3-4* has some characteristics of paternal excess, suggesting that the phenotypes observed in fertilized *eml1-2 eml3-4* are a result of a disrupted balance in parental contributions.

Published data suggest that a lack of function mutation in the *MEA* gene is sufficient to lead to a phenocopy of paternal excess at both the macroscopic and molecular level [40, 41]. To test whether *eml1-2 eml3-4* resembles *mea* at the molecular level, we compared the genes upregulated in *eml1-2 eml3-4* to the genes upregulated in *mea*. This analysis showed that the *eml1-2 eml3-4* upregulated gene dataset has a statistically significant overlap with each of the datasets of genes previously shown to be upregulated in the *mea* mutant at 3 DAP and at 6 DAP ( $P = 7 \times 10^{-7}$  and  $P = 3 \times 10^{-12}$ , respectively, hypergeometric test) [36] (Figure 3b, Table S5).

MEA has been shown to repress its own expression both before and after fertilization, alone and as part of the FIS-PRC2 complex, respectively [42, 43]. We anticipated that some of the effects seen in the *eml1-2 eml3-4* mutant could be due to a disruption in the delicate balance of MEA autoregulation. Thus, we tested *MEA* expression in *eml1-2 eml3-4* before and after fertilization, by performing RT-qPCR in *eml1-2 eml3-4* ovules and seeds and comparing *MEA* expression to wildtype. We found that *MEA* expression in the mutant was significantly increased in both tissues (Figure 3c), with a fold-change similar to the one obtained after RNA-Seq. This result suggests that EML1 and EML3 are involved in the mechanisms repressing *MEA* expression both before and after fertilization, thus resembling some FIS-PRC2 complex (*fis*) mutants. The observation that MEA is upregulated in *eml1-2 eml3-4* while it shows molecular phenotypes resembling *mea* and other *fis* mutants, can be reconciled by a model in which EML1/EML3 share some of the same targets with FIS-PRC2 complex, which is consistent with the RNA-Seq results.

#### 2.5. EML1 and EML3 prevent fertilization-independent seed development

*fis* mutants are also characterized by their ability to undergo fertilization-independent seed production [44-47]. However, while *fie* and *msi1* promote autonomous development of both endosperm and seed coat, *fis2* and *mea* are unable to initiate seed coat production. Likewise, *msi1* is the only *fis* mutant capable of developing embryos in the absence of fertilization. *vrn2*, a mutant in one of the sporophytic PRC2 complexes is able to initiate fertilization-independent seed coat development, but the seeds remain small and abort due to the absence of endosperm development [48]. Given the phenotypic similarities between the *fis* mutants and *eml1-2 eml3-4*, we tested whether *eml1-2 eml3-4* could also develop seeds in the absence of fertilization. For this, we

prevent any possibility of self- or cross-pollination. Remarkably, while the wild-type pistils contained just unfertilized ovules 6 days after emasculation (DAE), the eml1-2 eml3-4 pistils continued developing into elongated siliques, containing structures resembling seeds by their shape and size (Figure S6). To characterize these seed-like structures in more detail, we dissected siliques of both genotypes at 10 DAE and stained them with vanillin. The absence of staining demonstrated that wild-type pistils contained only unfertilized ovules. In contrast, apart from unfertilized ovules (indicated with "-", Figure 4a) that made up 30% of all reproductive structures, seeds of different sizes were clearly present in the siliques of the emasculated eml1-2 eml3-4 mutant based on positive staining with vanillin (Figure 4a). The mutant contained about 60% small (+), 10% medium-size (++) and 2% full-size (+++) seeds, with the size showing a correlation with the intensity of vanillin staining (Figure 4a). Additional DIC microscopy observations revealed that the small and medium-size seeds were characterized by the presence of incompletely differentiated seed coats and no embryos (Figure S7). On the other hand, the 2% of emasculated mutant seeds that developed to full-size showed the presence of a completely differentiated fivelayer seed coat and a structure resembling a globular stage embryo (Figure 4b). Regardless of size or morphology, all seeds which developed after emasculation of *eml1-2 eml3-4* failed to progress beyond the globular embryo stage and eventually aborted. Therefore, some of the fertilizationindependent eml1-2 eml3-4 seeds showed characteristics of fie and msi1, based on their large size, strong proanthocyanidin accumulation and the occasional presence of an embryo. However, the majority of *eml1-2 eml3-4* seeds resembled *vrn2*, based on their small size and weak vanillin staining. To check whether eml1-2 eml3-4 also phenocopies vrn2 by the absence of endosperm development without fertilization, we introduced the YUC10::GFP marker into the mutant and wild-type background. This marker is expressed exclusively after fertilization in the sexual

endosperm and its presence in seeds produced in the absence of fertilization marks a developing endosperm [21, 49]. Confocal microscopy revealed a *YUC10::*GFP signal in endosperm nuclei of *eml1-2 eml3-4* autonomous seeds analyzed at 5 DAE, while the signal was never observed in wildtype (Figure 4c). The *YUC10::GFP* nuclear signal was observed in 12 out of 145 analyzed ovules, consistent with expected segregation ratio of the reporter in the F1 flowers produced after crossing the *YUC10::GFP* into the *eml1-2 eml3-4* mutant background. The size of the seeds most commonly observed by confocal microscopy corresponded to the "+" category (small seeds), previously identified by vanillin staining (Figure 4a). This result indicates that endosperm development is initiated in autonomously developing *eml1-2 eml3-4* seeds, but fails to develop any further than this stage. Together, our data indicate that EML1 and EML3 function synergistically to prevent asexual and enforce sexual seed development.

#### 3. Discussion

Seed development in the absence of fertilization has been described for several Arabidopsis mutant classes, revealing a few distinct mechanisms that control this process. *fis* mutants display autonomous endosperm development with (*fie*, *msi1*) or without seed coat production (*mea*, *fis2*). The *vrn2* mutants of the VRN-PRC2 complex are able to initiate seed coat development in the absence of endosperm development [48]. Autonomous endosperm and seed coat are also produced as a result of the exogenous application of auxin or its ectopic expression [21, 22]. It was proposed that FIS-PRC2 represses maternal auxin synthesis in the central cell and sporophytic EMF/VRN-PRC2 repress the development of the integuments into a seed coat before fertilization [21]. Fertilization triggers paternal auxin production that leads to endosperm development and gets transported to the integuments, where it initiates seed coat development by lifting sporophytic

PRC2 repression [50]. Therefore, PRC2-regulated repression combined with maternally repressed auxin responses play an essential role in preventing fertilization-independent seed development and the aberrations in these processes lead to phenotypes mimicking paternal excess. Other mechanisms leading to fertilization-independent seed development include the release of pollen tube content, which has been shown to be sufficient to trigger the enlargement of ovules and their development into seed-like structures containing a seed coat and expanded endosperm with 1-2 nuclei [51, 52].

In this study, we identified *eml1-2 eml3-4* as a representative of another category of mutants capable of seed development in the absence of fertilization. The majority of *eml1-2 eml3-4* fertilization-independent seeds are small and characterized by the initiation of endosperm and seed coat development (Figure 4a and 4c). By their small size and seed coat presence, these seeds phenocopy *vrn2*. However, seeds from *eml1-2 eml3-4* contain endosperms and seed coats, unlike those from *vrn2*, which only contain seed coats. In contrast, medium or full-size fertilization-independent *eml1-2 eml3-4* seeds with completely differentiated seed coats resemble *msi1* or *fie*. While similar to PRC2 mutants, *eml1-2 eml3-4* seeds developed in the absence of fertilization show a range of seed phenotypes, mostly resembling the effects of ectopic auxin production or auxin application on autonomous seed development. Based on this finding, we propose a model in which the function of EML1 and EML3 to prevent apomictic seed development is linked to auxin responses, especially auxin signaling and transport.

RNA-Seq results, performed on fertilized seeds of self-pollinated *eml1-2 eml3-4*, provides additional molecular evidence of a link between EML1/EML3 and auxin in regulating seed development. We found that auxin responses, especially signaling and transport, are strongly

upregulated in *eml1-2 eml3-4* (Table 1). Examples of upregulated genes include an auxin receptor AFB1, auxin influx transporters LAX2 and LAX3, auxin efflux transporters ABCB4 (PGP4) and ABCB19 (PGP19), and a vacuolar auxin transporter WAT1. Some of these proteins, such as AFB1 and LAX2, regulate embryo development and co-localize with EML1/EML3 during early embryogenesis [24, 53]. PGP4 and PGP19 are involved in the efflux of auxin [54, 55] and their close homolog PGP10 has been implicated in the transport of auxin from the endosperm to the integuments [22]. Based on their localization in the integuments (Figure 1) and on the mutant phenotype (Figure 4), we propose that EML1 and EML3 function to repress auxin responses in the integuments before fertilization (Figure 5a). Based on the expression of YUC10::GFP in emasculated *eml1-2 eml3-4*, we further hypothesize that, in addition to FIS-PRC2, EML proteins act as repressors of auxin responses in the central cell. Unlike *fis* mutants in which endosperm develops completely in the absence of fertilization, *eml1-2 eml3-4* is only capable of initiating endosperm production, most likely because the FIS-PRC2 repression mechanism is still functional. The initiation of endosperm development leads to the enlargement of ovules and seed coat development, resembling mutants in which pollen tube content is released without fertilization [51, 52]. Auxin produced in the endosperm is transported to the integuments, where auxin responses are already elevated as a consequence of the lack of EML function within this compartment, which leads to seed coat development. Finally, the transport of the auxin signal to the egg cell results in the initiation of embryo development. This hypothesis is supported by the recent finding that auxin levels in the integuments increase after pollination and this maternally supplied auxin is required for proper embryogenesis [56]. Our model is consistent with the phenotypes we observed after emasculation of *eml1-2 eml3-4* and explains the mechanism by which the lack of *EML1/3* function leads to apomictic seed development.

We propose that EML1 and EML3 continue to serve as repressors of auxin responses after fertilization and that this function is provided by both male and female gametophytes (Figure 5b). Consistent with a range of defects observed in the seeds of fertilized eml1-2 eml3-4 (ovule abortion, seed abortion or embryo development delay), EML1 and EML3 may be involved in finetuning the level of auxin and its flow between seed compartments, which is required for normal seed development. According to this model, the absence of EML-mediated auxin repression in the endosperm would lead to elevated auxin levels, resulting in an increased rate of endosperm proliferation, a hallmark of paternal excess, and subsequent seed abortion with about 27% penetrance (Figure 2b). Two lines of evidence support the idea that the seeds of self-pollinated eml1-2 eml3-4 have characteristics of paternal excess. First, we found that the changes in gene expression detected in self-pollinated eml1-2 eml3-4 show correlation with the changes detected in hybridization crosses resulting from paternal excess (Figure 3). Remarkably, the group of genes identified in the overlap of genes upregulated in eml1-2 eml3-4 and the WT x osd1 paternal excess cross is highly enriched for proteins with extracellular localization, especially cell wall metabolic enzymes and small peptides, including EC1.4, an egg-cell specific peptide essential for a fusion with sperm cell [57]. Second, mature eml1-2 eml3-4 seeds are larger and plumper than wild-type, as shown for viable seeds with increased paternal contribution [15, 34], which additionally demonstrates that *eml1-2 eml3-4* phenocopies paternal excess phenotypes. We further speculate that, due to the remaining maternal FIS-PRC2 repression, the defects in endosperm development in eml1-2 eml3-4 are similar but not as dramatic as observed in the fis mutants and paternal excess crosses [15, 58]. According to the model, auxin flow from the endosperm to the integuments and along to the embryo continues to increase due to the lack of EML-based repression in the seed coat and within the embryo itself (Figure 5b). This hypothesis is supported by the co-localization of

EML1 and EML3 in the seed coat and in the suspensor during early stages of embryo development (Figure 1). It has been demonstrated that the timing of the developmental transition from endosperm proliferation to cellularization is essential for embryo development and seed survival [59, 60]. Thus, we speculate that EML-mediated chromatin-based machinery is harnessed to synchronize embryo and endosperm development by modulating the rate of auxin flow. Milder defects in this process would lead to minor asynchrony in the development of these two fertilization products and result in delayed embryo development, while more dramatic changes in auxin flow might result in greater asynchrony or even complete temporal decoupling of these processes, leading to seed abortion. Consistent with this, we found that self-pollinated *eml1-2 eml3-4* seeds have phenotypes of different severity (Figure 2). This incompletely penetrant phenotype also suggests that the function of EML1 and EML3 depends on their dosage.

The dependence on dosage is not uncommon for epigenetic regulators such as histone readers [61] and for members of multiprotein complexes with synergistic function [61, 62]. Two lines of evidence support the idea that EML1 and EML3 display dosage-sensitivity. First, they are haploinsufficient as single alleles (Table S3). Second, there are multiple members of the EML family expressed in similar, yet distinct patterns (Figure S2), which could serve as additional components of EML1- and EML3-containing complexes and could influence the penetrance of seed phenotypes. We continue testing this hypothesis by analyzing the phenotypes of other higher order mutant combinations. The synergy between multiple EML members might be sufficient to account for the dramatic changes in fertilization capacity we observed in the *eml1-2 eml3-4* double mutant compared to single *eml* mutants and might explain the proposed role of EML proteins in

balancing auxin flow to measure parental contributions and synchronize the development of fertilization products.

A recently demonstrated role of EML1 as a dual H3K4me3/H3K36me3 reader and of EML3 as an H3K36me3 histone reader [5, 13], presents additional questions about the mechanism by which they accomplish their seed-related functions. In metazoans and yeast, H3K36me3 is primarily associated with body methylation of active genes and is implicated in a variety of processes, including suppression of spurious intragenic transcription, control of alternative splicing, mismatch repair, and transcriptional regulation [63-67]. In Arabidopsis, H3K36me3 and H3K27me3 have an antagonistic role in regulating the expression of the flowering locus FLC and of an imprinted gene MOP9.5 [68, 69]. Thus, while our finding that H3K36me3 readers EML1 and EML3 have a repressive function similar to the H3K27me3 writer PRC2 might appear puzzling, it is not without a precedent. For example, the Tudor domains of human H3K36me3 readers PHF1 and PHF19 interact with PRC2, leading to spreading of repressive H3K27me3 into active chromatin [70]. It is possible that EML binding to active marks also promotes the generation and spreading of a repressed chromatin environment. In support of the hypothesis that the EML proteins contribute to PRC2-mediated repression, we identified a statistically significant overlap of 165 genes in the intersection of the genes upregulated in eml1-2 eml3-4 and recently identified H3K27me3-enriched dataset [71] ( $P = 1.4 \times 10^{-9}$ , hypergeometric test, Table S6). Our data suggest that while a portion of the EML repressive function could be in conjunction with the PRC2 complex, there is also a component of PRC2-independent repression (see Figure 5). The proposed reader function of EML proteins in modulating the chromatin environment might be particularly important for the developmental transition from gametophytic to sporophytic development.

Taken together, our results show that Arabidopsis uses a robust combination of repressive mechanisms to ensure that post-fertilization genes are not expressed in the female gametophyte. We propose that, in addition to being part of the repressive machinery that prevents premature auxin responses and seed development prior to fertilization, EML proteins act immediately after fertilization to ensure that the parental contributions are balanced, by modulating auxin signaling and transport. Future efforts directed towards unraveling the mechanism by which these histone readers participate in fine-tuning auxin responses and identifying their molecular partners in this process will contribute to our understanding of seed development, and how to manipulate it for the development of apomictic crops and new interspecies hybrids.
