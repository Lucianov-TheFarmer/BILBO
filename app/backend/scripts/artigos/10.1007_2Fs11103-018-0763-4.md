# **Global transcriptome and weighted gene co-expression network analyses reveal hybrid-specific modules and candidate genes related to plant height development in maize**

**Hengsheng Wang<sup>1</sup> · Longjiang Gu1 · Xingen Zhang1 · Mingli Liu1 · Haiyang Jiang1 · Ronghao Cai1 · Yang Zhao1 · Beijiu Cheng<sup>1</sup>**

Received: 16 April 2018 / Accepted: 3 August 2018 © Springer Nature B.V. 2018

#### **Abstract**

#### *Key message* **Weighted gene co-expression network analysis was explored to find key hub genes involved in plant height regulation.**

**Abstract** Plant height, an important trait for maize breeding because of its close relatedness to lodging resistance and yield, has been reported to be determined by multiple qualitative and quantitative genes. However, few genes related to plant height have been characterized in maize. Herein, three different maize hybrids, with extremely distinct plant height, which were further classified into low (L), middle (M) and high (H) group, were selected for RNA sequencing at three key developmental stages, namely, jointing stage (I), big flare period (II) and tasseling stage (III). Intriguingly, transcriptome profiles for hybrids ranging from low to high group exhibited significantly similarity in both jointing stage and big flare period. However, remarkably larger differentially expressed genes could be detected between hybrid from low to either middle or high group in tasseling stage. These results were repeatedly observed in both phenotyping and gene ontology enrichment analysis, indicating that transition from big flare period to tasseling stage plays a critical role in determination of plant height. Furthermore, weighted gene co-expression network analysis was explored to find key hub genes involved in plant height regulation. Hundreds of candidate genes, encoding various transcription factors, and regulators involved in internode cell regulation and cell wall synthesis were identified in our network. More importantly, great majority of candidates were correlated to either metabolism or signaling pathway of several plant phytohormones. Particularly, numerous functionally characterized genes in gibberellic acid as well as brassinosteroids signaling transduction pathways were also discovered, suggesting their critical roles in plant height regulation. The present study could provide a modestly comprehensive insight into networks for regulation of plant height in maize.

#### **Introduction**

Maize is both an ideal model species for genetic research and an economically important crop for fuel, food and feed (Godfray et al. [2010\)](#page-14-0). During recent decades, one of the most important strategies to increase the per unit area yield of maize has been to increase planting density rather than improving yield potential per plant (Duvick et al. [2004](#page-14-1)). However, high-density planting often makes plants susceptible to lodging, which can reduce the maize yield (Tang et al. [2007](#page-15-0)). To avoid lodging, one breeding strategy has been to moderately decrease the plant height of maize. Plant height is one of the most heritable and easily measured traits. It is also an important grain yield-associated trait in maize and has been frequently selected during modern breeding programs and crop domestication. There is a very close correlation between plant height and grain yield in maize (*r<sup>2</sup>*>0.73) (Abdel-Ghani et al. [2016\)](#page-14-2). Hence, plant height is a vital trait for maize breeding (Yuan et al. [2008;](#page-15-1) Peiffer et al. [2014](#page-15-2)).

Biologically, two main components define plant height in maize: internode number and average internode length. Stem elongation is a decisive factor that determines plant height. In monocots, internode elongation is mainly attributed to the development of intercalary meristems at the base of the growing internode (Van der Knapp et al. [2000\)](#page-15-3). Thus, investigating the theory of accelerated internode elongation in hybrids will provide a comprehensive understanding of the molecular basis of plant height. As plant height is one of the most heritable traits, it is possible to manipulate the trait genetically (Ku et al. [2015\)](#page-14-3). Niklas and Enquist's research indicated that plant height is a complex trait affected by phytohormones and controlled by many qualitative genes (Niklas and Enquist [2001](#page-15-4)). At present, numerous QTLs related to plant height in maize have been identified, but little more than 40 plant height-related genes have been cloned and characterized at present (Peiffer et al. [2014](#page-15-2)), including genes involved in signaling, transport and phytohormones synthesis (Wang and Li [2008](#page-15-5)). Some typical plant height-related genes of maize include *na1*, a gene in the BR biosynthetic pathway that affects brassinosteroid synthesis and can lead to reduced stem internode length (Hartwig et al. [2011\)](#page-14-4); *D3*, a maize gene cloned by transposon tagging, which encodes a protein that catalyzes an early step in the GA biosynthesis pathway in maize and regulates gibberellin synthesis (Winkler and Helentjaris [1995](#page-15-6)); *D8* and *D9*, which mediate gibberellin signal transduction pathways through DELLA proteins, as DELLA proteins are keystones of the gibberellin signal transduction cascade that act as negative regulators of the gibberellin response (Lawit et al. [2010](#page-14-5)); the *Br2* gene, which is expressed preferentially in elongating stalk internodes, resulting in the shortening of lower stalk internodes (the height reduction in these dwarf mutant results from the loss of a P-glycoprotein that regulates polar auxin transport in the maize stalk) (Multani et al. [2003\)](#page-15-7); and *ZmGA3ox2*, an ortholog of *OsGA3ox2* that was positionally cloned through association mapping, which encodes a GA3 β-hydroxylase and is significantly associated with plant height (Teng et al. [2013](#page-15-8)). However, research on these genes has not provided enough evidence to identify the molecular genetic mechanisms of plant height.

In contrast, the plant height determination mechanisms in *Arabidopsis thaliana* and *Oryza sativa* have been well clarified and numerous genes related to plant height have been identified. The genetic regulatory networks of plant height regulation in *O. sativa* and *A. thaliana* are clearly understood (Wang and Li [2008\)](#page-15-5), while the network in maize is still unclear and the comprehensive molecular mechanisms of plant height remain poorly defined, which has limited the genetic selection efficiency and genetic control of plant height. Therefore, it is imperative that we dig deeper into the plant height-related genes at the transcriptome or genome level to elucidate the mechanism of plant height interactions in maize.

Recently, transcriptome deep sequencing technologies have been widely used to uncover potential functional genes and gene regulatory mechanisms (Bo et al. [2015](#page-14-6)). For instance, Fu et al. utilized RNA-Seq technologies to identify some key genes in maize kernel development and built co-expression networks for kernel developmental pathways (Fu et al. [2013](#page-14-7)). Qi et al. systematically profiled the transcriptome of *Gossypium arboreum* to identify functional modules associated with development and stress (You et al. [2016](#page-15-9)). Here, to better understand the genetic regulation of plant height in maize, three different hybrid combinations of maize were selected, which were defined as low (L), middle (M) and high (H). Plant height was measured and RNA samples were collected at the jointing stage, big flare period and tasseling stage, because these three stages are crucial for maize development (Li et al. [2012\)](#page-14-8). Then, RNA-Seq technology was used to identify and characterize the expression of a large number of genes, and develop a genome-wide co-expression network providing a gene expression overview for biological process analysis by combining algorithms (You et al. [2016\)](#page-15-9). This report describes a large-scale transcriptomic analysis of maize genotypes that allowed the identification of possible gene networks involved in plant height regulation.

#### <span id="page-1-0"></span>**Experimental procedures**

#### **Four‑year field experiments and trait measurements**

A 4-year experiment was conducted in 2012–2015 (April 10–September 10) at the National Key Laboratory of Crop Genetic Improvement in Suzhou City, Anhui, China. Seeds were directly sown into soil and single-row plots of 12 plants were grown for each hybrid combination, with a distance of 40 cm between rows and 30 cm between individuals. Field management followed standard agricultural practice. From 2012 to 2014, more than 200 maize hybrids were used in the experiment, each with 10 replicates. Finally, three hybrids were selected, which were defined as low (L, Dan 598 × FAPW), middle (M, Dan 598 × CML444) and high (H, Dan 598×ES40) group. Each hybrid showed relatively stable plant height over 3 years (Student's *t*-test, *P*-value>0.05). In 2015, the three hybrid groups were replicated 24 times and plant height was investigated in each hybrid at three time points: the jointing stage (L1, M1, H1), big flare period (L2, M2, H2) and tasseling stage (L3, M3, H3). Samples were collected from the stems at these three developmental stages for each hybrid, and three biological replicates for each genotype were taken (one replicate for qPCR validation and two replicates for RNA-seq analysis).

#### **RNA isolation and sequencing**

Eighteen samples from the low, middle and high hybrid at three development stages were used for RNA sequencing. RNA was isolated and RNA sequencing (RNA seq) was performed on Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China). Before library construction, an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to confirm the quantity and quality of the RNA with the criteria that the RNA integrity number was >7 and the rRNA ratio (28S/18S) was >1.5. In total, 0.1–0.4 µg total mRNA was purified and fragmented using PCR plates with a magnetic plate stand. Superscript II and random primers (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) were used to reverse transcribe the fragmented mRNA to cDNA. Blunt-ended cDNA was generated by end repair and then ligated to yield 3′ adenine base overhangs. Thymine overhangs with oligonucleotide adapters were ligated to the cDNA and added to the adapter index for each library.

#### **Trimming and mapping of reads**

The raw sequencing reads from all the runs were first trimmed for low-quality regions and adapter sequences. Reads containing N (refers to the uncertain base information) greater than 10% were removed, and low quality reads were also removed. Clean reads (~ 90% of the total read count) were mapped to the maize genome (B73\_RefGen\_ v4 annotation build, [http://plants.ensembl.org/Zea\\_mays/](http://plants.ensembl.org/Zea_mays/Info/Index) [Info/Index](http://plants.ensembl.org/Zea_mays/Info/Index)) and a recent internal maize transcriptome using Tophat 2 with default parameters (Pertea [2010](#page-15-10)). The DESeq Bioconductor package (Version: 1.28.0) was used to normalize the mapped reads. Data quality analysis was evaluated by visualizing the results of principal component analysis (PCA) (Anders and Huber [2010;](#page-14-9) Cox et al. [2010\)](#page-14-10).

#### **Identification and functional annotation of DEGs**

The mapped sequence reads for all of the identified genes were used for differential expression analysis by the DESeq method (Anders and Huber [2010](#page-14-9)). Using Cufflinks 2.2.1 and Cuffdiff, gene expression levels were calculated as reads per kilobase of transcript sequence per million base pairs sequenced (FPKM) (Trapnell et al. [2016](#page-15-11)). Based on statistical analyses, genes with a *P*-value < 0.05 and an absolute value |log2 ratio ≥ 1| were considered to be significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs). GO enrichment analysis was conducted using Blast2GO [\(https](https://www.blast2go.com/) [://www.blast2go.com/](https://www.blast2go.com/)) with FDR< 0.05. Based on their expression patterns, the DEGs were clustered by the Genesis K-means method (Li et al. [2016\)](#page-14-11). In the test group, GO terms were analyzed for enrichment compared with a reference group using Fisher's Exact Test with FDR, and GO terms with a corrected *P*-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched among the DEGs. All of the test gene modules were subjected to KOBAS 2.0 analysis and significantly enriched pathways were selected with a *P*-value<0.05 [\(http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/home.do\)](http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/home.do) (Wu et al. [2006\)](#page-15-12).

#### **Gene network construction**

The weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) R package (Version:1.51) was used to construct a gene co-expression network to further explore the possible functions of the genes in determining plant height (Langfelder and Horvath [2008](#page-14-12); Zhang and Horvath [2005\)](#page-15-13). The genes used for the network were based on the above RNA-Seq data from nine samples of different stem developmental stages (jointing stage, big flare period and tasseling stage) in the low, middle and high maize hybrids, using each biological and technical replicate as an individual dataset (totaling 18 samples). To make the network show an approximate scale-free topology, a power of nine was chosen (model fitting index *R<sup>2</sup>*=0.8). To ensure that the network was biologically relevant, the mean connectivity of the network and the scale-free topology model fit were evaluated over a range of the soft threshold power β. The topological overlap-based dissimilarity measure was used to cluster all the coding sequences hierarchically (Zhang and Horvath [2005\)](#page-15-13). Finally, the resulting gene dendrogram was used for module detection using the dynamic tree cut method (minModuleSize = 100 and mergeCutHeight = 0.25). In the weighted gene coexpression network, if any two genes were connected, the topology overlap measure provided in WGCNA was used to determine edge weight. The weights ranged from 0 to 1, and reflected the strength of communication between the two genes. Only weights above 0.2 between any two genes were considered to represent a strong co-expression relationship in our network. Connectivity was defined as the sum of the weights across all the edges of a node. Genes that shared a consistent expression pattern were clustered into different 'modules' based on their correlation, the co-expression network was built using a basic correlation function in the Cyni Toolbox [\(http://www.prote](http://www.proteomics.fr/Sysbio/CyniProject) [omics.fr/Sysbio/CyniProject](http://www.proteomics.fr/Sysbio/CyniProject)) of the Cytoscape platform, the Cytoscape\_v 2.8.3 software was used to display the co-expression network.

#### **Validation of DEGs by quantitative real‑time PCR**

Thirty-six genes were randomly selected for expression validation using quantitative real-time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR). The first strands of the cDNA fragments were synthesized from total RNA using cDNA Synthesis and TransScript One-Step gDNA Removal SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). The Primer Premier 6.0 software was used to design gene-specific primers, and the qRT-PCR was performed on an ABI7500 real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Each 20 µl PCR contained 10 µl 2×SYBR premix Ex Taq (Takara, Japan), 6.8 µl ddH2O, 1.6 µl cDNA, 0.8 µl forward primer and 0.8 µl reverse primer. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: an initial denaturation step of 10 min at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C for denaturation, 25 s at 60 °C for annealing and 30 s at 72 °C for extension. Then, melting curve analysis was performed. The relative expression levels of the candidate genes were measured using the 2<sup>−</sup>△△CT method (Shinozaki et al. [2003](#page-15-14)).

#### **Results**

#### **Identification of maize hybrids with extremely distinct plant heights**

Three hybrids that exhibited different plant heights (Student's *t*-test, *P*-value < 0.01) were selected and defined as low (L), middle (M) and high (H). Plant height was measured at the jointing stage, big flare period and tasseling stage, respectively. The low, middle and high group showed no visible differences in plant architecture or leaf number while exhibited a discrepancy in plant height at the jointing stage and big flare period. Especially at the tasseling stage, these three groups showed great differences in plant height, the low and middle was only 51% and 78% of high group in plant height, respectively (Figs. [1a](#page-3-0), S1). Indicating that transition from big flare period to tasseling stage may contribute greatly to determining plant height. These data suggested that the phenotypic differences in plant height among the L, M and H groups were reliable and distinctly different. Hence, these three hybrids were ideal for researching the transcriptional regulation of plant height in maize.

#### **Transcriptome profiles of maize hybrids at three developmental stages**

To assess the global transcriptome profiles of the three hybrids, we performed deep RNA-Seq sequencing of the L, M and H groups at the jointing stage, big flare period and tasseling stage. At each time point, two biological replicates were included. In all, 18 libraries were constructed (RNA samples from three hybrids at three stages with two biological replicates, 3×3×2). More than 1 billion high-quality reads were acquired from the 18 libraries with 39–44 million reads per library. After removing low quality reads, adaptors, ambiguous reads and duplications, an average of 88.19% clean reads at the Q30 level (ratio of error rate≤0.1%) and 29.94 million reads were obtained for each maize sample

#### **Identification and functional annotation of plant height‑related modules**

It has been proposed that genes having similar expression profiles would have similar biological functions (Shin and Kim [2009\)](#page-15-17). Thus, in order to explore if there were modules involving in the transcriptional regulation of plant height in maize, all of 32,732 expressed genes were used for construction of co-expression network (Supplementary Fig. S8). Collectively, 55 gene modules, with the module size ranging from 26 to 4913 (Supplementary Fig. S9, Table [2](#page-8-0)), was identified. ### **Co‑expression network of plant height‑specific TFs and plant height‑related genes**

Transcription factors (TFs) are serving as critical molecular switches, which could regulate the mRNA expression of vast majority of associated genes (Rohit et al. [2016](#page-15-21)). Hence, plant height-specific TFs which might be intensively correlated with transcriptional regulation of plant height in maize were initially identified in the present study (see "[Experimental procedures](#page-1-0)" part). Among the 1046 DEGs, there were 29 plant height-specific TFs (Supplementary Table S8). Subsequently, these 29 TFs along with 43 functionally characterized plant height related genes, were used to build a co-expression network to further infer the potential roles for these TFs and identify candidate genes related to plant height in maize (Shi et al. [2017](#page-15-22)). Afterwards, pairwise Pearson correlations were calculated based on log-transformed transcript abundance data for the selected genes. Theoretically, a threshold of 0.2 for the absolute value of positive or negative correlation coefficients (*r*) was used as a cutoff to remove low correlations. A global co-expression network was assembled by Cytoscape (Fig. [6](#page-10-0)). The final co-expression network contains hundreds of hub genes, among which included 20 plant height-specific TFs and 30 reported genes. Surprisingly, the expression of most TFs was positively or negatively correlated with the expression of reported genes or candidate genes (Fig. [7\)](#page-11-0). Our constructed network contained some candidate genes related to plant height, which were mainly divided into three types: transcription factors, cell wall-related genes and phytohormone signaling pathway genes. #### **Discussion**

### **Generation of a comprehensive transcriptome from three maize hybrids at three developmental stages**

Maize is an economically important crop throughout the world and the height of maize plants is important for yield performance, lodging resistance and plant architecture. Although some typical plant height-related genes of maize have been identified, the molecular mechanisms determining plant height in maize are still unclear. Peiffer et al. found that maize height has a multi-genic architecture and is under intense genetic regulation (Peiffer et al. [2014\)](#page-15-2). In their report, however, no specific potential plant height-related genes were authenticated. In our research, we selected three maize hybrids that exhibited inherently different plant heights to provide a powerful foundation for identifying genes related to plant height. In addition, the three hybrids showed distinct time-dependent responses at three developmental stages and we observed high correlation in the RNA-Seq data between biological replicates, indicating that our system for analyzing differences in plant height was reliable and robust, and may provide valuable data for researching plant height. Our differential expression analyses revealed that more DEGs were present at the tasseling stage than the jointing stage or big flare period, indicating that the number of DEGs gradually increased with maize development. Between any two hybrids, more downregulated genes were identified than upregulated genes in the same developmental stage, which was consistent with previous studies of plant height in *Gossypium hirsutum* using RNA-Seq analysis (An et al. [2015\)](#page-14-15).

### **Internode cell regulation and cell wall‑related DEGs in plant height control**

Internode length and number are two major factors affecting plant height. In maize, internode elongation is caused by the elongation and cell division of intercalary meristems. During stem development, rind region parenchyma tissues and the sclerenchyma produce thick and lignified cell walls (Wilson [1995\)](#page-15-25). Genes involved in cell wallrelated processes might also function in cell wall remodeling and cell growth during stem development (Maurice et al. [2011](#page-15-26)). Furthermore, the cell wall of maize stalks represents a large energy investment, as the majority of the solar energy accumulated in plants is photosynthetically converted into chemical energy and then locked into the cell wall polymers lignin, hemicellulose and cellulose, and is an important factor that may directly affect cell elongation and plant height (Maurice et al. [2011\)](#page-15-26). Our research showed that the DEGs in the tasseling stage significantly altered cell and cell wall component synthesis, meristem growth and development, eventually leading to plant height discrepancies. For instance, *bv1* (Zm00001d016486), which was upregulated in the tasseling stage; *bv1* mutants have short internode cells, shortened internodes and are deficient in auxin transport, and the genes misregulated in *bv1* mutants include genes important for cytoskeletal function, transmembrane transport, and cell wall synthesis (Avila et al. [2016](#page-14-16)). *ZmBK2* (Zm00001d047276) was also upregulated in the tasseling stage; it is involved in cell wall biogenesis, affects the mechanical strength of maize tissues, and is upregulated in longer internodes (Ching et al. [2006](#page-14-17); Bosch et al. [2011](#page-14-18)). The phenylpropanoid pathway is involved in lignin precursor production, and previous research has indicated that DEGs related to phenylpropanoid biological processes are upregulated in non-elongating internodes (long stem) compared with elongating internodes (short stem), which is consistent with our results (Supplementary Table S9). Zm00001d047276, which encodes a cell wall biogenesis gene, was upregulated in taller plants at the tasseling stage (Bosch et al. [2011\)](#page-14-18). Zm00001d036293 was upregulated in the tasseling stage and is involved in lignin biosynthesis; previous research has indicated that lignin levels are reduced in Zm00001d036293 mutant maize plants, accompanied by a decrease in plant height (Fornalé et al. [2016](#page-14-19)). The results of WGCNA analysis also indicated that plant height-related genes exhibited co-expression patterns with cell wall-related genes (Figs. [6](#page-10-0), S13).

#### **Possible roles of different network modules in regulating plant height**

WGCNA is widely applied for inferring co-expression network modules. Here, eigengene-trait correlation analysis was performed at three developmental stages and in each hybrid by using WGCNA, and we identified several modules highly associated with plant height that showed significant positive or negative correlation with plant height in specific developmental stages or hybrids. For instance, the blue module exhibited positive correlation with plant height in the big flare period but showed negative correlation at the jointing stage. The yellowgreen, red and darkturquoise modules were significantly positively correlated with plant height at the jointing stage but not in the big flare period or tasseling stage. The midnightblue, steelblue and floralwhite modules exhibited negative correlation with plant height at the jointing stage, big flare period and tasseling stage, respectively. The eigengene-trait correlation analysis in each hybrid also showed similar results. From the results of KEGG pathway analysis, we found that the genes in these modules were enriched in phytohormone signaling pathways related to plant height, and several functionally characterized plant height related genes were included in these modules (Supplementary Table S7). We can infer that the genes in specific modules may regulate plant height in specific developmental stages or specific hybrids.

#### **TFs regulating plant height**

TFs act as master regulators of plant-specific genes and/ or a variety of plant-specific signals. Numerous TFs have been reported to function in plant height development. For instance, bZIP TFs are involved in various developmental processes like flower development and plant height determination, including *RF2a* and *RF2b*, which are highly expressed in the stem and control the activity of gibberellins and ultimately affect plant height (Ali et al. [2016](#page-14-20); Jakoby et al. [2002\)](#page-14-21). Many TB1-type TCP genes act as axillary bud-specific regulators; for example, overexpression of *TEOSINTE BRANCHED 1* (*TB1*) of maize, a TCP-family gene, results in a dwarf phenotype (Shi et al. [2016](#page-15-27); Studer et al. [2011](#page-15-28)). *Dlf1*, a WRKY TF, is involved in regulating flowering time and plant height in rice (Cai et al. [2014](#page-14-22)). In our results, multiple TFs were identified among the DEGs and in the co-expression network, including *ZmMADS3*, with 230 nodes corresponding to this gene (Supplementary Table S8). Previous research has indicated that transgenic maize plants ectopically expressing *ZmMADS3* have reduced height due to a reduced number of nodes. Among vegetative tissues, *ZmMADS3* is expressed in stem nodes and displays a gradient with the highest expression in the uppermost node (Heuer et al. [2001](#page-14-23)). Similar to kn1 loss-of-function mutants, *blh12 blh14* (*blh12*/*14*) double mutants fail to maintain axillary meristems, and exhibit precocious internode differentiation and abnormal tassel branching that results in dwarfism and reduced veins in stems (Tsuda et al. [2017\)](#page-15-29).

#### **Possible roles of phytohormone signaling pathways in regulating plant height**

It is well known that internode length and number are two decisive factors influencing maize plant height. Internode elongation is ascribed to intercalary meristems through the adjustment of cell elongation and division (Zheng et al. [2010;](#page-15-30) Knoeller et al. [2010](#page-14-24)). Meristem expansion and cell elongation contribute greatly to the extension and growth of maize internodes, which is often accompanied by the expansion of volume when they differentiate. The extent of elongation depends on the cell type and is usually affected by endogenous hormones. In our network, some candidate genes were annotated and related to phytohormone signaling pathways including GA, Br, ABA, ET, IAA, SL and JA, which may play an important role in plant height regulation (Depuydt and Hardtke [2011](#page-14-25); Wang et al. [2017](#page-15-18)). For instance, *BZR1* (Zm00001d039439), a homolog of *OsBZR1*, suppression of *OsBZR1* results in erect leaves, dwarfism, impaired BR feedback regulation and reduced BR sensitivity (Bai et al. [2007](#page-14-26); Tong et al. [2009\)](#page-15-31). *BAK1* (Zm00001d038534) was identified by its homology to *OsBAK1*, and overexpression of *OsBAK1* resulted in a dwarf phenotype, similar to rice BR-insensitive mutant plants (Li et al. [2009\)](#page-14-27). Previous research on dwarf mutants has shown that GA plays critical roles in plant height control, and plants defective in GA biosynthesis exhibit typical GA-deficient phenotypes, such as dark-green leaves, small size, dwarfism, defective flowering and inhibited cell growth. *GID2* (Zm00001d048185), which is a putative homolog of *OsGID2*, was identified in our GA-related network; *gid2* mutants show dwarf phenotypes because of decreased GA signaling, and DELLA mutations that break the interactions with *GID1* or *GID2* proteins cause dominant GA-insensitive dwarf phenotypes in all (Ueguchitanaka et al. [2008\)](#page-15-32) species (Hu et al. [2017](#page-14-28); Ueguchi-Tanaka et al. [2005](#page-15-33)). GA receptors play a key role in the GA signal transduction pathway; in our network, eight GA receptor genes were identified and named *GID1L2* (Zm00001d037288, Zm00001d020736, Zm00001d016895, Zm00001d022326, Zm00001d020737, Zm00001d037180, Zm00001d031804, Zm00001d020741). In the SL-related network module, *CCD7* and *CCD8* were identified as putative homologs of *OsCCD7* (*D17*) and *OsCCD8* (*D10*) (Albabili and Bouwmeester [2015\)](#page-14-29). In the IAA-related network module, 15 candidate genes were identified, including *AUX1* (Zm00001d000288), *AUX3* (Zm00001d039624), *ARF2* (Zm00001d041056) and *PIN1b* (Zm00001d018024). In the ABA-related network module, nine candidate genes were identified. Genes participating in ET signaling pathways also contribute to plant height control to an important extent. Members of the APETALA2 (AP2)/ethylene responsive factor (ERF) family in rice (*OsEATB*) can mediate the GA biosynthetic gene OsCPS2 to suppress ET-induced GA responses, which leads to the inhibition of internode elongation and reduced plant height. In our network, several ERF family members were identified including *ERF2* (Zm00001d017462), *ERF4* (Zm00001d049364), *ERF7* (Zm00001d023662), *ERF27* (Zm00001d029679) and *ERF056* (Zm00001d052229). Finally, there were four candidate genes in the JA-related module, including *MYC2* (Zm00001d047017), an important activator of JA responses.

Our co-expression analysis revealed putative regulators of plant height and identified hundreds of candidate genes expected to be key regulators. These genes were mainly divided into three types: TFs, cell wall-related genes and phytohormone signaling pathway genes. Functional characterization of candidate genes is a time-consuming and complicated task, making it necessary to accurately identify targets. This is especially important for plant height regulation in maize. In conclusion, this study not only provides a comprehensive overview of the plant height regulatory network, but also provides new insights for the improvement and enhancement of germplasm banks specifically tailored to dwarf variety breeding.
