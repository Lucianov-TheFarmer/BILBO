## SUMMARY

The formation of a zygote by the fusion of egg and sperm involves the two gametic transcriptomes. In flowering plants, the embryo sac embedded within the ovule contains the egg cell, whereas the pollen grain contains two sperm cells inside a supporting vegetative cell. The difficulties of collecting isolated gametes and consequent low recovery of RNA have restricted in-depth analysis of gametic transcriptomes in flowering plants. We isolated living egg cells, sperm cells and pollen vegetative cells from Oryza sativa (rice), and identified transcripts for approximately 36 000 genes by deep sequencing. The three transcriptomes are highly divergent, with about three-quarters of those genes differentially expressed in the different cell types. Distinctive expression profiles were observed for genes involved in chromatin conformation, including an unexpected expression in the sperm cell of genes associated with active chromatin. Furthermore, both the sperm cell and the pollen vegetative cell were deficient in expression of key RNAi components. Differences in gene expression were also observed for genes for hormonal signaling and cell cycle regulation. The egg cell and sperm cell transcriptomes reveal major differences in gene expression to be resolved in the zygote, including pathways affecting chromatin configuration, hormones and cell cycle. The sex-specific differences in the expression of RNAi components suggest that epigenetic silencing in the zygote might act predominantly through female-dependent pathways. More generally, this study provides a detailed gene expression landscape for flowering plant gametes, enabling the identification of specific gametic functions, and their contributions to zygote and seed development.

Keywords: plant sperm cell, plant egg cell, pollen, rice gametes, germ line epigenetic states, Oryza sativa.

## INTRODUCTION

The fusion of haploid gametes to form a diploid zygote is a critical step for successful reproduction in multicellular organisms. During flowering plant development, the formation of gametes occurs during the haploid, gametophyte stage of the life cycle (Walbot and Evans, 2003; Chang *et al.*, 2011). The angiosperm female gametophyte typically contains four cell types: a single egg cell, two synergid cells, a diploid central cell and three or more antipodal cells. The male gametophyte, pollen at anthesis, contains only two cell types, including two sperm cells and one much larger vegetative cell. The vegetative cell generates the pollen tube to facilitate the transport of the sperm cells through the pistil to the female gametophyte. Whereas the importance of the gametes in contributing

genetic information to the next generation is evident (Palanivelu and Johnson, 2010), the vegetative cell has an essential role in supporting the sperm cells, which are usually lacking both mitochondria and plastids. In addition, it has been shown in Arabidopsis that the vegetative cell plays a major role in the epigenetic regulation of transposable elements in the sperm cell (Slotkin et al., 2009). Thus the transcriptome of this cell type is also pertinent to gamete development and function.

Deep-sequencing studies of gamete transcriptomes have been conducted in animals (Tang et al., 2009; Laiho et al., 2013). The common ancestor for plants and animals was most likely unicellular, so many of the similarities in reproduction, such as genomic imprinting, are the result of convergent evolution rather than common ancestry (Feil and Berger, 2007). Although a few microarray studies have been carried out (Borges et al., 2008; Wuest et al., 2010; Ohnishi et al., 2011; Russell et al., 2012; Abiko et al., 2013), deep coverage transcriptomic studies of gametes are still unavailable in plants. This results, in part, from the difficulty of collecting sufficient material for these experiments, which requires isolation of single cell types of high purity. In particular, the female gametophyte is embedded within maternal tissues, making the isolation of egg cells extremely difficult and time intensive. With the introduction of high-sensitivity deep RNA sequencing, analysis of lowly expressed genes in very small quantities of materials is now feasible. Here, we present RNAseqderived transcriptomes of egg cells (EC), sperm cells (Sp) and vegetative cells (Ve) from Oryza sativa ssp. japonica, a model monocot species. Our data reveal insights into gametic processes at the pre-fertilization stage, including chromatin organization, epigenetic regulation, auxin signaling and cell cycle synchrony, as well as providing a framework for future studies on gametic functions and their roles in fertilization and early embryogenesis.

## RESULTS

## Transcriptomes of egg, sperm and pollen vegetative cells derived from RNA-seq

Images of the isolated cell types studied are shown in Figure 1. Illumina sequencing by the RNA-seq protocol was performed on RNA samples from three biological replicates for each of the three cell types (see Experimental procedures). Illumina RNA-seq reads were mapped to the Oryza sativa (rice) genome, and those reads occurring uniquely were assigned to exon regions of gene models from the RGAP 7.0 version of the rice genome hosted at Michigan State University (MSU) (Kawahara et al., 2013). The RGAP data set contains a total of 55 986 annotated gene loci with 96 and 89 belonging to ChrUn and ChrSy, respectively, and with the remaining 55 801 belonging to the LOC\_ type loci. A total of 36 029 of these annotated LOC\_ genes had exon reads in at least one of the nine libraries (Appendix S4). Analysis revealed that approximately 40–60% of uniquely mapping reads from each library overlapped with exon regions (Table S1). The vast majority, over 99% of repeatmapping reads (Appendix S2) for ECs or Ve, and over 97% of repeat-mapping reads for Sp, corresponded to ribosomal sequences (Figures S1A, B). #### The RNA-seq profiles of cell types are highly divergent

After conversion of read counts to length-normalized values, i.e. transcripts per million, or tpm (Wagner et al., 2012), it can be seen that the shape of the gene expression distribution is markedly different between the three cell types (Figures S1C, D). This bias in distribution is also seen in the top 50 statistically ranked genes within each cell type. The top 50 EC genes range from approximately 100 000 tpm down to 1000 tpm, the top-50 Sp genes range from approximately 180 000 tpm down to 1000 tpm and the top-50 Ve genes range from 25 000 tpm down to 3200 tpm (Figure S2). So, although the Ve has a relatively higher density of genes expressed in the upper range, the maximum expression level seen for a gene in the vegetative sample is less than that observed for the other cell types. We noted some overlaps in the identities of the top 50 genes for the three different cell types (Figure S2D), despite the overall high proportion of differentially expressed genes within the three transcriptomes (see below).

#### Three-quarters of the genes are differentially expressed between cell types

Differential gene expression analysis was performed on all three cell types using the raw read counts in a single generalized linear (GLM) model based on a negative binomial distribution (see Experimental procedures). The nine RNAseq samples clustered into three groups of three replicate samples according to cell type (Figure S1E). Using six possible contrasts on the fitted data, approximately threequarters of all the genes assessed among the three cells were considered as differentially expressed (DE), with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 5% (Appendix S4; Figure S1F). For the pairwise comparisons between the three cells, the null hypothesis of the absence of differential expression could not be rejected for 14 236, 20 650 and 16 627 genes, corresponding to EC versus Sp, Sp versus Ve, and EC versus Ve, respectively. The higher number of genes in common for the Sp and Ve comparison is perhaps not surprising, as Sp and VEs constitute the male gametophytes, and, importantly, are also physically associated with one another (Mogensen, 1992). From the results of the six contrasts, the genes were grouped into six differential expression sets (see Experimental procedures) and are presented in a Venn diagram (Figure 2a).

#### Sets of differentially expressed genes between EC, Sp and Ve cells can be assigned distinct gene ontology terms

The enrichment of gene ontology (GO) terms was assessed using the agriGO web tool for the six possible differential expression groups, based on overexpression: EC, not-EC, Sp, not-Sp, Ve and not-Ve. Many significant GO terms were found to be in common for more than one of the six DE gene sets, and have been summarized in Venn diagrams (Figure S3), but of more interest are the GO terms that were only found within one of these six categories (Figure 2b). It is reassuring that the assignment of many of these GO terms is consistent with the known differences in the functional, structural and molecular features of the three different cells. For instance, the gene set corresponding to not-Sp (i.e. intersection of Ve and ECs, but not Sp) is enriched for GO terms associated with cytoplasmic structures, including organelles (e.g. endomembrane system, organelle envelope), which is consistent with the known paucity of cytoplasm and organelles in the Sp. Interestingly, the term pollen–pistil interaction is enriched in the Sp gene set, suggesting a possible role for the Sp in signal transduction during the pollen germination process.

#### Genes with known cell type-specific expression show expected expression patterns

We used genes previously known to be highly or differentially expressed in gametes to provide the initial validation of the data sets. ECA1, which has been studied extensively as an EC-specific gene (Ohnishi et al., 2011), was confirmed in our data to be specific to the egg (Figure S4). To validate Sp expression, we examined the rice orthologs of GEX1 and GEX2. GEX2 is among the 50 most highly expressed genes in the Sp, and both GEX1 and GEX2 show high expression in the sperm with very low expression in the egg (Figures S2B, S4). We also investigated the potential expression in Sp of the rice genes that could be orthologous to SSP (SHORT SUSPENSOR), an unusual sperm-specific gene required for Arabidopsis embryo development (Bayer et al., 2009). We could not identify any definitive candidate rice ortholog for SSP (Figure S5), and none of the most closely related rice genes to SSP exhibited Sp-specific expression (Figure S4). Therefore, it is likely that in rice, unlike Arabidopsis, zygotic development does not depend upon sperm-transmitted SSP transcripts, although other transcripts from the Sp might be required in the zygote.

We further investigated the expression of genes involved in specific cellular processes, to ascertain how these might be differentially regulated in the male and female gametes. ### Histone variants expressed in the gametes

In general, as shown in Figure 3, we found that the histone variants expressed in the EC were distinct from those expressed in the Sp and Ve, with the latter histone variants showing some similarities. The chromatin of plant Sp is highly condensed and contains a male gamete-specific histone H3 variant called MGH3/HTR10 (Okada et al., 2005). Rice has one putative MGH3 ortholog according to sequence similarity (Figures S6B, C), and we found that this putative MGH3 ortholog was preferentially expressed in the Sp, consistent with its being a male gamete-specific histone (Figure 3). Interestingly, the histone H3 variant with the highest expression in mature Sp was not MGH3 but a variant histone H3.3, a replication-independent histone typically associated with euchromatin (Loyola and Almouzni, 2007).

The histone H2A variant, H2A.Z, is associated with nucleosomes at transcription start sites, especially those containing H3.3 (Deal and Henikoff, 2011), and is correlated with promoters that are inactive but poised for transcription (Li et al., 2007). Rice has three putative H2A.Z orthologs (Figure S6A). Two of these genes have EC enrichment, consistent with post-fertilization transcription of the maternal genome in the zygote (Figure 3). Interestingly, the third H2A.Z gene (Os10 g28230.1) showed Sp enrichment, suggesting that at least a subset of paternal genes are poised for expression in the zygote (see Discussion).

This complex contains four protein subunits: MSI1, FIE1 or FIE2, EZ1 or CLF, and EMF2A or EMF2B. In our transcriptome data, we found that the genes encoding all four components of PRC2 (Luo et al., 2009; Gleason and Kramer, 2012) are expressed in both gametes at maturity. The EC shows expression of all possible subunits of the PRC2 except for FIE1 (Figure 4a). The sperm PRC2 appeared to be more specialized, with a putative PRC2 consisting of MSI1, FIE2, CLF and EMF2B (Figure 4a). Thus, it is likely that both gametes are capable of PRC2-mediated gene silencing, although using different PRC2 components.

In opposition to histone methylation is epigenetic regulation by the Jumonji (JMJ) family of histone demethylases. There are 20 JMJ genes in rice with a range of putative targets on histone H3 (Chen et al., 2011). JMJ proteins seem to be widely expressed in both gametes, with slightly lower expression for some families in the Ve (Figure 4b). The gametes express different members of specific target classes. Interestingly, we found that the Sp contained very high levels of ZOS1-20 (Agarwal et al., 2007), the putative rice ortholog of H3K27 demethylase REF6 in Arabidopsis (Lu et al., 2011).

Another important class of histone demethylases is the human lysine-specific demethylases, called LDLs in plants, which reduce the levels of H3K4 trimethylation, an activation mark in the genome (Jiang et al., 2007). We found that all four rice LDL genes are expressed in the egg, whereas the sperm had moderate expression of LDL3 (Figure 4c). Therefore, LDL demethylases may play a role in the removal of active chromatin marks in both gametes.

## DNA methylation genes

We next looked at genes that are involved in DNA methylation, an epigenetic mark that can be transmitted through the germ line. In plants, cytosine DNA methylation occurs in CG, CHG and CHH sequence contexts. There are ten DNA methylase genes in rice that are sequence orthologs of Arabidopsis methylases (Sharma et al., 2009). In Arabidopsis, the primary maintenance methylase is the CG methylase MET1, whereas CHG methylation is provided by CMT3. There have been contradictory results indicating both expression (Schmidt et al., 2013) and repression (Jullien et al., 2008) of the maintenance methylase MET1 in the Arabidopsis female gametophyte. The Sp has expression of MET1 but not the de novo methylase DRM2, whereas the converse is true for the Ve, consistent with the observations of low CHH methylation in Sp and low CG methylation in Ve. We observe low MET1 expression in the EC and Sp, with nearly undetectable expression in the Ve, whereas OsDRM2 is expressed in both male cells, although low in the Ve (Fig-

The SWI2/SNF2-like gene DDM1 is required for methylation of repeat sequences in Arabidopsis, and was shown by Slotkin et al. (2009) to be absent from the Ve in Arabidopsis, resulting in the expression of transposable elements in the Ve. For the two copies of DDM1 in rice (Higo et al., 2012), we find that the expression of DDM1 is lower in the Ve than in the Sp, a result that can be considered to be consistent with the observations in Arabidopsis. In general, our data show that both gametes express genes for the different known DNA methylases.

The action of the DME/ROS1/DML3 family of DNA glycosylases can reverse DNA methylation. Rice contains two DML3 orthologs and four ROS1 orthologs, with no DME orthologs (Zemach et al., 2010). One of these ROS1 orthologs, ROS1a, appears to be required for the proper development of both gametes, as a loss-of-function allele of this gene is not transmissible to offspring (Ono et al., 2012). Supporting the previous genetic observations, we found ROS1a to be the most highly expressed ROS1 gene in all three cell types, although other ROS1 genes were also expressed in the two gametes.

#### RNA interference pathways

Silencing by small RNAs has been proposed to be an important mechanism for the repression of transposon activity in the gametes (Slotkin et al., 2009), so we examined the gamete transcriptomes for the expression of components of the micro RNA (miRNA) and siRNA pathways. The core proteins involved in RNA-mediated gene silencing include members of the Dicer-like (DCL), Argonaute (AGO) and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RDR) families, with other proteins acting to facilitate the function of these core enzymes (Simon and Meyers, 2011). There are six DCL genes in rice, compared with four in Arabidopsis (Song et al., 2012a), and rice contains 19 AGO genes (Kapoor et al., 2008). RDR6 is necessary for the

biogenesis of 21-nt trans-acting siRNAs (tasiRNAs) and phased small RNAs in rice, and requires the activity of SGS3 (Du et al., 2011; Song et al., 2012b), whereas RDR2 is required for the biogenesis of most 24-nt siRNAs in Arabidopsis (Lu et al., 2006; Jia et al., 2009). In addition to these pathway-specific genes, HEN1 in Arabidopsis acts in all pathways to stabilize small RNAs and prevent uridylation by methylation of their 3′ ends (Li et al., 2005), and a knock-out of this gene in rice leads to pleiotropic phenotypes resulting from the loss of small RNA stability (Abe et al., 2010).

We investigated the expression of these genes in our data to try to infer which pathways are likely to be active in each cell type at maturity. DCL1 and AGO1, the miRNAspecific genes, seem to be expressed in all cell types at maturity (Figure 5a); however, HEN1 is expressed at a moderate level in the EC, at a very low level in the Sp and is nearly undetectable in the Ve. These data suggest that the miRNA pathway is potentially only active in the EC. The 21-nt post-transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS) pathway requires RDR6, DCL4 and AGO1 or AGO7. In our data, the EC shows expression of all of these genes, whereas the Sp and Ve lack RDR6, and show little to no expression of DCL4 and AGO7 (Figure 5b). However, AGO1 in the male cells may still serve the targeting function in the absence of AGO7.

#### Auxin

Auxin is an important plant hormone with diverse roles, from cell expansion and division to cell specification. Auxin has been proposed to play a major role in cell identity acquisition in the female gametophyte (Pagnussat et al., 2009), and is also important for early embryogenesis (Friml et al., 2003). We therefore examined the expression of auxin response genes and auxin biosynthesis genes in the gametes. As shown in Figure 6a, seven genes encoding auxin response factors (ARFs) (Wang et al., 2007) are highly expressed in the egg. By contrast, only two ARFs were found to have moderate to high expression in the Sp, and in the Ve four ARFs had the highest expression. The AUX/IAA genes are negative regulators of auxin signaling, and several of these showed low to moderate expression in both gametes. In contrast to the ARF gene expression, which appeared to be primarily in the female gamete, expression of YUCCA auxin biosynthetic genes (Yamamoto et al., 2007; Abu-Zaitoon et al., 2012; Figure S9) was essentially absent in the EC, but was detectable in the Sp and Ve (Figure 6b). These observations raise the interesting possibility that the zygote might be able to activate the auxin response pathway by the combined contributions of synthesis and response factors from the male and female gametes, respectively. It is also possible that import of auxin from the surrounding maternal tissues could activate auxin response pathways within the unfertilized EC.

#### Cell cycle

We examined the expression profiles of genes controlling the cell cycle using the previously described classifications of cyclins and other core cell-cycle regulators (La et al., 2006; Guo et al., 2007; Cho et al., 2008). Most of the major cell-cycle regulators were expressed at relatively low levels in the Ve, as compared with both gametes (Figure S8). This low expression is consistent with the fate of the Ve, which disintegrates after fertilization, and therefore might have exited the cell cycle (Twell, 2011). Both the EC and Sp express many of the same classes of major cell-cycle regulators, although there are clear differences between individual genes within protein families, especially the cyclins (Figure S8). Because of the high degeneracy of plant cyclins, multiple cyclins from each class perform similar functions, and therefore such expression differences between the gametes might not be functionally significant. On the other hand, any difference in expression of a single copy gene encoding a key cell-cycle regulator is more likely to have functional importance. The rice ortholog of the WEE1 gene showed differential expression between the gametes. Phosphorylation of CDK1 by WEE1 kinase is an important step in the cell cycle, resulting in inactivation of CDK1 during the G2 phase of the cell cycle. M-phase entry is promoted by phosphatases that reverse the inactivation by WEE1. We observed that rice WEE1 is preferentially transcribed in the EC (Figure S8), which leads us to surmise that although the Sp is likely to be in G1 at anthesis, the EC might have reached S phase at maturity (see Discussion).

#### DISCUSSION

We have used whole-genome expression profiling by RNAseq to provide deep coverage transcriptomes of Sp and Ve isolated from pollen, and EC isolated from the embryo sacs of rice plants. We detected expression of up to approximately 27 000 genes in the EC, 25 000 genes in the Sp, and 29 000 genes in the pollen Ve. Reads were found in all three replicates for 21 172 genes in the EC, 16 985 genes in the Sp, and 18 611 in the Ve. Previously, the numbers of genes with detectable expression were about 7200 in the Arabidopsis EC (Wuest et al., 2010), 5800 in the Arabidopsis Sp (Borges et al., 2008) and 10 700 in the rice Sp (Russell et al., 2012). In addition, the absence of background using RNA-seq allows the detection of lowly expressed genes, and meaningful comparisons can be made over the entire transcriptome. The degree of coverage that we have achieved has allowed us to explore in depth the expression of genes for individual biological processes within these three cell types. In view of the highly distinct chromatin configurations of these cell types, particularly the Sp, we have focused our analysis on the expression of genes that might be important for the inheritance or the maintenance of epigenetic states prior to fertilization.

#### Histones and chromatin modification: elevated expression of genes for active chromatin in the male gamete

In both animals and plants, Sp have highly condensed chromatin. In animal Sp, most of the histones in the nucleosomes are replaced by protamine, which is presumed to promote the dense packaging of sperm chromatin. Plants contain a histone H3 variant called MGH3 expressed in Sp, which might serve a similar function as protamine in animals. As expected, we observed preferential expression of the putative rice ortholog of MGH3 in the Sp. Surprisingly, we also observed very elevated expression of the euchromatin-associated histone H3.3 in Sp, sufficient to be represented in the top-50 genes (Figures 3, S2B). As we do not observe a relatively greater number of actively transcribed genes in Sp compared with the other cells, it is difficult to account for this excessive level of H3.3 based on Sp requirements alone. A possible function for these transcripts might be in post-fertilization events. Ingouff et al. (2007)have observed that after fertilization, there is a replication-independent removal of H3.3 from both parental genomes, followed by the deposition of new H3.3 before the first zygotic division. Thus, the H3.3 transcripts in the sperm could contribute to the synthesis of the new H3.3 that is deposited in the zygote. Alternatively, or perhaps additionally, sperm H3.3 might be involved together with H2A.Z in preparing the male genome for transcription in the zygote.

Expression of the variant histone H2A.Z genes was also significantly higher in gametes compared with in Ve (Figure 3). The preferential expression of H2A.Z in the Sp relative to the Ve might mark a subset of paternal genes that will be expressed after fertilization. Thus far, few genes have been identified that unambiguously display paternal genome expression during the one-cell zygotic stage, although there appears to be widespread expression of the paternal genome by the two- to four-cell stage in Arabidopsis (Autran et al., 2011; Xiang et al., 2011; Nodine and Bartel, 2012). The expression levels of H3.3 and H2A.Z in the sperm support a model in which a subset of the paternal genome has been prepared for transcriptional activation in the one-cell zygote, in contrast with animals, where the paternal genome is usually not expressed at this stage.

The transcriptional state of chromatin is further defined by modifications of the N-terminal tail of histone H3. For example, H3K9 and H3K27 trimethylation are silencing marks that lead to the condensation of chromatin into inaccessible heterochromatin, whereas H3K4 trimethylation is a mark found at active promoter sites (Berr et al., 2011). Based on the transcriptome data, both gametes appear to be competent to carry out these modifications, as both have expression of all histone methylases, although the levels are relatively higher in EC compared with Sp (Figure 4a). We found unexpectedly high expression levels of ZOS1-20, the putative rice ortholog of Arabidopsis REF6, a H3K27 demethylase, in the Sp (Figure 4b). In mammals, the removal of the H3K27me3 repressive mark in the primordial germ cells during embryogenesis by the Jumonji domain protein Utx re-establishes pluripotency (Mansour et al., 2012). Thus, in plants, there could similarly be a mechanism to clear H3K27-silencing marks for the next generation, but one that is active in the gametes prior to fertilization, instead of the germ line of the early embryo, as in animals. However, it is unclear why this mechanism might be more active in the male germ line, because the EC expression of the ZOS1-20 gene is low. It is possible that the male germ lineage requires more extensive reprogramming of somatic repression marks, or that similar functions are carried out in the EC by genes yet to be identified. Alternatively, it is also possible that the Sp transcripts result in zygotic proteins that carry out this function on both sets of parental chromosomes.

#### Evidence for RNAi activity in the female, but not in the male, germ line

In Arabidopsis, transposable elements play an important role in male gametophyte development. In the model proposed by Slotkin et al. (2009) for the male gametophyte, DDM1 is downregulated in the vegetative nucleus of the pollen, allowing the widespread demethylation of transposable elements. These transposon sequences are then transcribed and processed into 21-nt siRNAs, which travel to the Sp to direct silencing of these elements in the germline. It has been suggested that this model could be extended to silencing of transposons in the EC by 24nt siR-NAs generated in the central cell (Martinez and Slotkin, 2012). However, we found that the siRNA pathways examined in the sperm and Ve of mature rice pollen do not have any siRNA pathway components with higher expression in the Ve than in the Sp. Moreover, the very low expression of essential genes such as HEN1, RDR2 and RDR6 in either sperm or Ve types suggests that neither 21nt nor 24nt siR-NA pathways are likely to be very active in the pollen. Therefore the findings of RNAi-mediated silencing of transposons in the Sp of Arabidopsis cannot at present be extended to rice. It is possible, however, that these processes are active in rice, but at an earlier stage of pollen development, such as the bicellular stage. The comparably high rates of reads mapping to transposon associated repeats in both the Sp and the Ve (Figure S1B), suggests that such silencing is not extensive. Moreover, persistence of any previously silenced state would need to be maintained through DNA and chromatin modifications, and such modifications are typically associated with 24nt siR-NAs, rather than the 21nt siRNAs found in the male germ line of Arabidopsis. In contrast, all the critical components of the pathways for miRNA, 21-nt siRNA and 24-nt siRNA processing show expression in the EC. Thus, the egg appears to be competent for gene silencing through the major RNAi pathways. It is conceivable then that siRNAs inherited from the EC can additionally mediate zygotic silencing of transposons in rice. Because rice primarily reproduces by selfing, female-derived siRNAs should be capable of silencing transposons of the male genome as well. It would be worthwhile to investigate whether Zea mays (maize), a cereal that is primarily an out-crosser, employs strategically different regulation of its RNAi pathways in the two gametes to ensure the silencing of different parental sets of transposons – an especially interesting question given the importance of hybridization in maize seed production.

#### Synchrony of gametic cell cycles

It is important that the two gametes arrive at the same phase of the cell cycle during fertilization, to ensure conformity of cell-cycle checkpoints and contribution of the same nuclear DNA content to the zygote. In Arabidopsis, the Sp undergoes the S phase while it travels down the pollen tube (Friedman, 1999), implying that gamete fusion must occur at the G2 phase. In cereals, the Sp of mature pollen appears to be at G1 (Mogensen and Holm, 1995; Mogensen et al., 1995). Expression of the gene for the WEE1 kinase is specific to the S phase in synchronized Arabidopsis tissue culture cells (Menges et al., 2005), consistent with its function to repress CDK activity at the onset of the G2 phase. We observed preferential expression of WEE1 in the EC of rice, suggesting that the mature egg might be in the S phase of the cell cycle prior to fertilization. The low expression of WEE1 in the rice Sp is consistent with the G1 phase during anthesis, as is the case for other cereals. Higher expression of replication-dependent histone H3.1 in the egg with low expression in sperm supports this scenario. The Sp could enter the S phase during pollen tube growth or during gamete fusion, but is unlikely to reach G2 because the time between pollination and fertilization in rice is just 30– 60 min. If gamete fusion occurs in the S phase, there must exist mechanisms to prevent the under- or over-replication of DNA from either parent in the fertilized egg. This might be achieved through 'licensing factors' belonging to the MCM2-7 protein family (Blow and Dutta, 2005; Tuteja et al., 2011), which can be loaded separately on to replication origins of the parental chromosomes when they are in the early G1 phase. Studies on the expression of cell cycle-regulated genes in the zygote should help resolve these questions of cell cycle synchrony following fertilization.

#### Sperm cell and vegetative cell isolation

Sperm cells and Ve were isolated from near anthesis anthers of vigorous, visibly disease-free, field grown rice at Dale Bumpers National Rice Research Center in Stuttgart, AR, USA. A differential centrifugation isolation method was used to separate Sp and Ve from whole anthers (Gou et al., 1999; Russell et al., 2012). Briefly, the collected anthers were gently ground in cold 45% (w/v) sucrose solution to release mature pollen grains, filtered through 100-lm nylon mesh, resuspended in a 109 volume of 15% (w/v) sucrose solution for 20 min to release the Sp, filtered through a 30-lm nylon mesh and layered on a Percoll density gradient, centrifuged and recovered as described previously (Russell et al., 2012). The precipitate consisting of Ve materials was treated as a sperm-depleted sample and collected in concert with each Sp isolation. Samples collected were immediately transferred and stored in 80°C until use.

## Library preparation

RNA from the Sp and Ve was extracted using the QIAGEN RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen,<http://www.qiagen.com>) following the manufacturer's instructions, including the optional on-column DNAse treatment. For the EC samples, RNA was extracted using the RNAqueous-Micro Kit (Applied Biosystems, [http://www.lifetechnol](http://www.lifetechnologies.com)[ogies.com](http://www.lifetechnologies.com)). The protocol was modified to add an oncolumn DNAse treatment (Qiagen) after the first wash step. RNA quality for all samples was assessed and concentrations were measured using the Agilent Bioanalyzer (Agilent, [http://www.agi](http://www.agilent.com)[lent.com\)](http://www.agilent.com), and samples had an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ranging from 7.7 to 9.0. cDNA synthesis was performed using the NuGEN RNA-seq system V2 (NuGEN, [http://www.nugeninc.com\)](http://www.nugeninc.com) with an input of 1.5–10.0 ng of RNA. cDNA was quantified on the ND 1000 Nanodrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., [http://www.thermofisher.com\)](http://www.thermofisher.com), and then sheared on the Covaris S220 system (Covaris, [http://covarisinc.com\)](http://covarisinc.com) using the manufacturer's recommended settings for 200-bp fragments. A 170-ng portion of sheared cDNA was purified using the MinElute Reaction Cleanup Kit (Qiagen), and libraries were prepared using the NuGEN Ovation Ultralow DR Multiplex Systems 1-8. Samples were multiplexed and run with six samples per lane on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 DNA sequencer (Illumina,<http://www.illumina.com>).

## RNA-seq data preprocessing

The barcoded FASTQ files of the reads were mapped to rice RGAP 7.0 genomic reference sequences using TOPHAT 2.0.5 with default settings, except that the 'microexon' search option was switched on, and minimum and maximum intron sizes were set at 20 and 15 000 bases, respectively. The mapped reads in the resulting accepted\_ hits.bam files were assigned to gene exons of the different isoforms of the RGAP 7.0 gene models GFF3 file, using an in-house PERL script. Only uniquely mapped read counts that were assigned to gene exons were used for further analysis. Additionally, where more than one isoform is present, the one with the highest number of reads across the three cell types assessed was analysed further.

## Differential expression analysis

For the purpose of ranking gene expression levels and for visualization, the gene counts were normalized using transcripts per million (tpm), as defined in Wagner et al. (2012). The heat maps shown were colored with steps based on log-transformed tpm values. The estimation of the top 50 expressed genes in each cell type, EC, Sp and Ve, was achieved by using rank aggregation via the <sup>R</sup> package RANKAGGREG using the Cross Entropy Monte Carlo algorithm, with rho set at 0.01, n set at 100 000 and convIn set at 5. These settings enabled the same top 50 after repeating the run.

To determine differentially expressed genes between the three cell types, the BIOCONDUCTOR R package EDGER (Robinson et al., 2010) was used; however, the default normalization method, TMM, assumes that most genes are not differentially expressed between conditions, which was clearly not the case with the three cell types, and so the upper quartile method (75th percentile) was used. A GLM was fitted to the data with tag-wise dispersion. The following contrasts were created: EC versus Sp, Sp versus Ve, Ve versus EC, EC versus [(Sp + Ve)/2], Sp versus [(Ve + EC)/2] and Ve versus [(EC + Sp)/2]. Likelihood ratio tests were performed to determine differential expression for each contrast. From these contrast results a DE Venn diagram was produced (Figure 2a) that describes the differential expression between three cells. This Venn diagram is not the same as those from the LIMMA package, which instead indicate the likely presence or absence of gene expression. The membership to each Venn subset was determined using Table 1.

The membership to overexpression (relative to any comparison) for any of the three cell types was considered to be true if at least one test indicated this. For example, relative overexpression will be considered for Sp if any of the following is true: Sp versus not-Sp = 1; Sp versus Ve = 1; or EC versus Sp = 1. Over-expression in two cell types means the gene is to be placed in the intersect of the Venn sets of two cells, but excluding the center and remaining cell type.
