#### **Abstract**

RNA binding proteins (RBPs) play an important role in posttranscriptional gene regulation. However, the functions of RBPs in plants remain poorly understood. Maize kernel mutant *dek42* has small defective kernels and lethal seedlings. *Dek42* was cloned by *Mutator* tag isolation and further confirmed by an independent mutant allele and CRISPR-Cas9 materials. *Dek42* encodes an RRM\_RBM48 type RNA binding protein that localizes to the nucleus. *Dek42* is constitutively expressed in various maize tissues. The *dek42* mutation caused a significant reduction in the accumulation of DEK42 protein in mutant kernels. RNA-seq analysis showed that the *dek42* mutation significantly disturbed the expression of thousands of genes during maize kernel development. Sequence analysis also showed that the *dek42* mutation significantly changed alternative splicing in expressed genes, which were especially enriched for the U12-type intron retained type. Yeast two-hybrid screening identified SF3a1 as a DEK42-interacting protein. DEK42 also interacts with the spliceosome component U1-70K. These results suggested that DEK42 participates in the regulation of pre-mRNA splicing through its interaction with other spliceosome components. This study showed the function of a newly identified RBP and provided insights into alternative splicing regulation during maize kernel development.

#### **INTRODUCTION**

Alternative splicing (AS) of precursor mRNAs (pre-mRNAs) is an essential mechanism in posttranscriptional regulation that removes introns and ligates various exons to produce multiple mature mRNA isoforms from a single gene (Reddy et al. 2013). AS has greatly enriched the transcriptome and proteome. More than 50% of the genes in animals contain at least two splicing forms (Letunic et al. 2002), while in plants, 40%-70% of the multiexonic genes undergo AS, and some of them are conserved among different species (Chamala et al. 2015). AS is generally classified into five different types as follows: exon skipping (SE), alternative 5'/3' splicing site (A5SS, A3SS), alternative first/end exon (AltStart/AltEnd), mutually exclusive exons (MXE), and intron retained (IR) (Matlin et al. 2005; Pan et al. 2008). Among them, IR is the most common AS pattern in plants, accounting for 56.1% in *Arabidopsis* and 53.5% in rice (Wang and Brendel 2006). In addition, AS plays a key role in the process of plant growth and development, including the seedling condition, circadian rhythm, flowering regulation, and abiotic stress (Staiger and Brown 2013).

The splicing process is intricate and elaborate in multicellular eukaryotes (Romero et al. 2006). The splicing of pre-mRNA occurs in a large RNA-protein complex known as a spliceosome (Romero et al. 2006). Many multicellular eukaryotes have two types of spliceosomes, the major U2-type and the minor U12-type (Sharp and Burge 1997). The U2-type spliceosome is composed of five small nuclear ribonucleoprotein particles (U1, U2, U4/U6, and U5 snRNPs) responsible for the vast majority of introns (U2-type introns) (Burge et al. 1998; Russell et al. 2006). The U12-type spliceosome is composed of U11, U12, U4atac/U6atac, and U5 snRNPs and is responsible for a small fraction (less than 0.5%) of introns (U12-type introns) (Will et al. 1999). In the U1 snRNP, U1-70K is an important component that interacts with SRZ21, SRZ22, SCL33, and SC35 proteins and assists U1 snRNP binding to the accurate site of pre-mRNA (Golovkin and Reddy 1998, 1999). In the U2 snRNP, SF3a is a multisubunit splicing factor composed of three subunits, named Prp9/11/21 in yeast and SF3a60/66/120 in humans (Wells and Ares 1994). SF3a is required for U2 snRNP activation and binds to the branch site (Yan and Ares 1996; Will et al. 2002).

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) play essential roles in mRNA metabolism, such as biogenesis, stability, splicing, transport, degradation and cellular localization (Antic and Keene 1997; Glisovic et al. 2008). The defining feature of RBPs is the presence of putative RNA binding domains, such as the RNA recognition motif (RRM) domain, K homology domain, DEAD/DEAH boxes domain, etc. (Clery et al. 2008). Based on these domains, bioinformatic analyses have shown that eukaryotic genomes encode a large number of RBPs. The *Arabidopsis thaliana* genome encodes more than 600 putative RBPs (Lorkovi and Barta 2002), and the maize genome contains 336 nonredundant RBP genes (Zhang et al. 2014). Among the RBPs, RRM is the most abundant RNA binding domain (Lunde et al. 2007). Usually, one to four RRM copies are contained in different RRM-containing RBPs (Burd and Dreyfuss 1994). In plants, some RRM-containing RBPs also contain modular auxiliary domains that are rich in glycine, arginine, and serine, such as glycine-rich RNA binding proteins (GRPs) and serine- or arginine-rich (SR or RS) proteins (Zahler et al. 1992; Mousavi and Hotta 2005). However, the detailed functions of the vast majority of RBPs remain unclear in plants.

Maize is a key crop and a research model for plants, partly because of its classical genetics and numerous mutants. Among them, *defective kernel* (*dek*) mutants affect the development of both embryos and endosperms and offer opportunities to investigate different biological processes during kernel development (Walbot and Evans 2003). In this study, we characterized the maize kernel mutant *dek42*, which has a small kernel and lethal seedlings. *Dek42* was cloned and found to encode an RBP with one RRM\_RBM48 domain that targets the nucleus. RNA-seq analysis showed significant levels of aberrant AS and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in *dek42*. In addition, DEK42 directly interacts with the spliceosome component proteins U1-70K and SF3a1.

#### **RESULTS**

#### *Dek42* **is required for normal kernel development**

The *dek42* mutant was originally obtained from the Maize Genetics Cooperation Stock Center. It was crossed with the W22 inbred line and then self-crossed to produce an F2 population. The F2 ears showed a 3:1 segregation of wild type (WT) (+/+ and *dek42*/+) and *dek42* (*dek42*/*dek42*) phenotypes (339:109, χ <sup>2</sup>=0.1; Figure 1A). The segregation ratio suggested that *dek42* was caused by a single recessive gene mutation.

The mutant phenotype was clearly identified as early as 15 days after pollination (DAP) with a whiter and softer texture compared with the WT kernels (Figure S1A). Mature *dek42* kernels were smaller than WT kernels (Figure 1B, C). The 100-kernel weight of *dek42-ref* was only 40% of the WT weight (Figure 1D). The *dek42* kernels germinated and grew certain principal roots but failed to develop normal shoots and died at earlier seedling stages (Figure S1B).

Biochemical analysis of WT and *dek42* mature kernels showed that the total protein content in *dek42* kernels was significantly decreased (Figure 1E). Compared with the WT kernels, the content of zein protein in *dek42* kernels, especially 27 kD zein, was significantly decreased, while the non-zein protein in *dek42* kernels was increased (Figure S1C). The content of total starch and the amylose in *dek42* kernels were both decreased (Figure 1F). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis showed that the starch granules were spherical

and smooth in the WT kernels at the immature stage, while it had a rough surface and many pores in the *dek42* kernels. In addition, at the mature stage, the starch granules had a high degree of crystallinity in WT kernels, while it had numerous pores on the rough surface in *dek42* kernels (Figure 1H). The total fatty acid in *dek42* kernels did not show noticeable differences compared with WT kernels (Figure 1G). These results suggested that *dek42* strongly affected the accumulation of protein and starch in mutant kernels.

To investigate the cytological changes during kernel development in *dek42*, immature kernels collected at 12, 15 and 18 DAP (days after pollination) were observed microscopically. The *dek42* kernels were less developed than the WT kernels at the same stage. The embryos of *dek42* kernels were smaller and developmentally delayed compared with the WT kernels at the same stage. The endosperm of *dek42* kernels was smaller and less filled compared with the WT kernels (Figure 1I). The BETL cells in *dek42* endosperm were thinner and less developed compared with WT endosperm (Figure 1J). These results suggested that *dek42* greatly affected kernel development in the mutant.

#### *Dek42* **was cloned and confirmed by allelism test**

The F2 kernels of the *dek42* mutant ear exhibiting 3:1 segregation were used to make two genomic DNA pools, one for WT kernels (+/+ or *dek42/+*) and the other for mutant kernels (*dek42/dek42*). Each pool contained the DNA extracted from 40 individual kernels. These two DNA pools were analyzed with a maizeSNP3072 genotyping array (Tian et al*.* 2015). The *dek42* mutant locus was mapped to a 3.27 Mb interval on maize chromosome 4 (from 242,696,065 bp to 245,973,596 bp, maize B73 RefGen\_v4). An expanded population with 203 mutant kernels from the F2 population was used to confirm and narrow down the mapped interval. *Dek42* was then mapped to an interval of 777 kb between the molecular markers InDel-2 and InDel-3 (Figure 2A). Because *dek42-ref* was initially isolated from an active *Mutator (Mu)* line, *Mu* tags were isolated and sequenced using the method described by Williams-Carrier (Tian et al. 2015). Only one unique *Mu* insertion, at the 25 bp upstream of the start codon of *Zm00001d054077*, was found within the *dek42*-mapped interval. Therefore, *Zm00001d054077* was considered the candidate gene.

A *UniformMu* insertion line (*UFMu-07152*), carrying a *Mu* insertion at the 318 bp downstream of the start codon of *Zm00001d054077*, was obtained from the Maize Genetic Cooperation Stock Center. The F2 ears of the *UFMu-07152* mutant had a defective kernel phenotype similar to the *dek42-ref* mutant*,* and the *Mu* molecular marker was used to confirm the *Mu* insertions in the +318 location (Figure 2B). The heterozygous *UFMu-07152* mutant exhibited a 3:1 segregation of *dek* kernels (Figure S2H), and the genotype was consistent with the phenotype in 24 randomly selected kernels (Figure S2I). The mutant kernels were unable

to germinate (Figure S2L). Allelism test was conducted between *UFMu-07152* and *dek42*. The test crosses between heterozygous *dek42-* (*dek42-ref/+)* and *UFMu-07152* generated ears exhibiting 3:1 segregation of *dek* kernels (273:94, χ <sup>2</sup>=0.07, Figure 2D). *Mu* molecular markers (Figure S2J) were used to identify the *Mu* insertions in *UFMu-07152* and *dek42-ref*. The inserted *Mu* was identified by M-F and O-R, with 199 bp in *dek42-ref* and 534 bp in *dek42-mu*. The WT was identified by O-F and O-R with 771 bp. Twelve WT and 12 mutant kernels were sorted from F2 ears (Figure 2E). All mutant kernels carried the *Mu* insertions with the genotype *dek42-ref/dek42-mu* from both *dek42-ref* and *UFMu-07152*, while WT kernels contained *dek42-ref* (*dek42-ref/+*) or *UFMu-07152* (*dek42-mu/+*) *Mu* insertions or none of them (+/+) (Figure 2F). The result indicated that the *Mu* insertion mutation in *UFMu-07152* was allelic to *dek42-ref*. *Zm00001d054077* was *Dek42*. *UFMu-07152* was then named *dek42-mu*.

Additional CRISPR-Cas9-generated mutation alleles of *Zm00001d054077* were obtained according to a single target system using maize transgenic technology (Zhu et al*.* 2016). The gRNA spacer sequence in the third exon was selected as the target site for Cas9 cleavage (Figure 2B). Two independent transgenic lines carrying CRISPR-Cas9 edits at the gRNA target site were obtained. One line contained a 1 bp deletion, and the other line contained a 1 bp insertion at the gRNA targeted region in *Zm00001d054077*, both causing a frame-shift of the coding protein. They were named *dek42-cas9-1* and *dek42-cas9-2*, respectively (Figure 2G). The ear of the *dek42-cas9-1* population showed a similar phenotype as *dek42-ref* (Figure S2D, E and F). And the *dek42-cas9-1* mutant kernels failed to germinate (Figure S2G). Test crosses between *dek42-ref* (*dek42-ref/+)* and the CRISPR-Cas9-edited line (*dek42-cas9-1/+*) generated ears exhibiting 3:1 segregation of mutant kernels (213:78, χ<sup>2</sup>=0.51; Figure 2H). The mutant kernels (*dek42-ref/dek42-cas9-1*) were thinner and smaller, similar to homozygous kernels (*dek42-ref/dek42-ref*) *from Dek42-ref* self-crossed ears (Figure 1A). Twelve WT and 12 mutant kernels were randomly selected from F2 ears and subjected to genotyping analysis (Figure 2J). C-F and C-R identified the CRISPR-Cas9 target sequence, and the edited sites were sequenced (Figure S3). All mutant kernels carried both the *Mu* insertions of *dek42-ref* and the CRISPR-Cas9-edited sites (*dek42-ref/dek42-cas9*) (Figures 2J, S3). However, the WT kernels carried either the *Mu* insertion (*dek42-ref/+*) or the CRISPR-Cas9-edited sites (*dek42-cas9/+*) or none of them (*+/+*). Test crosses between *dek42-mu* (*dek42-mu/+)* and the CRISPR-Cas9-edited line (*dek42-cas9-1/+*) generated hybrid ears, and the mutant kernels were smaller than the WT kernels (Figure S2A-C). Therefore, the mutations in *Zm00001d054077* caused by CRISPR-Cas9 editing did not complement *dek42-ref*. This result further confirmed that *Zm00001d054077* was *Dek42*.

#### *Dek42* **encodes an RNA-binding protein (RBP)**

The genomic DNA sequence of *Dek42* (*Zm00001d054077*) contained 7 introns and 8 exons. It produced a 1,169 bp transcript that encoded a protein of 219 amino acids with a predicted size of 24.5 kDa. To further examine the expression of the DEK42 protein, immunoblot analysis was conducted with a DEK42 polyclonal antibody. Total proteins isolated from the immature kernels of WT, *dek42-ref, dek42-mu* and *dek42-cas9-1* at 15 DAP were analyzed by immunoblot. Actin was used as a sample loading control. The results showed that the DEK42 protein was not detected in *dek42-cas9-1,* but significantly decreased in *dek42-ref* and *dek42-mu* (Figure 2C). Therefore, we attributed the molecular basis of the mutant phenotype to the dramatically reduced DEK42 protein expression that caused a defect in DEK42 function. A conserved domain analysis indicated that DEK42 had one RRM domain belonging to RBM48. DEK42 homologous proteins were searched by BLASTP, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed based on DEK42 homologous proteins in other species, with *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* as an outgroup (Figure 3A). A detailed sequence alignment with rice, *Arabidopsis*, *Physcomitrella patens* and *Homo sapiens* indicated that DEK42 shared a highly conserved RBM48 domain (Figure S4A). These analyses indicated that DEK42 shared a highly conserved RRM, especially in monocotyledons.

#### *Dek42* **is constitutively expressed in multiple tissues**

A real-time quantitative PCR analysis was used to examine *Dek42* gene expression in different tissues, including the root, stem, leaves, tassel, ear, silk, husk and immature kernels. Ubiquitin was used as an internal control. The results indicated that *Dek42* was expressed in all tested tissues and had a relatively higher expression in kernels (Figure 3B). In developing kernels, the expression of *Dek42* was clearly detected at 6 DAP and gradually increased and peaked at approximately 24 DAP (Figure 3C). The results showed that *Dek42* was ubiquitously expressed during plant development.

#### **DEK42 is a nucleus-localized protein**

To investigate the subcellular localization of DEK42, transient expression assays using GFP-fused DEK42 were conducted in onion and tobacco leaf epidermal cells. DAPI indicated the nucleus. The GFP signals were also localized in the nucleus, as observed by confocal laser microscopy (Figure 3D). The results indicated that DEK42 was localized in the nucleus.

#### **The** *dek42* **mutation affects gene expression during kernel development**

The *Dek42* transcript was analyzed by quantitative RT-PCR in developing kernels of WT and

*dek42-ref* at different stages (12, 15, and 18 DAP). Ubiquitin was used as an internal control. Compared with WT kernels, the expression level of *Dek42* was dramatically decreased at the three tested developmental stages in *dek42* kernels (Figure 4A). This explained the cause of the loss of function of *dek42*.

To explore the effects of loss of function of *dek42*, RNA-seq analysis was conducted for WT and *dek42-ref* developing kernels at 15 DAP. Sequencing data analysis indicated that among the 44,917 genes detected by RNA-seq, a total of 2,695 were identified as statistically significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between WT and *dek42* kernels (fold change >2.0, q<0.05). Among these DEGs, approximately 1,433 DEGs were upregulated and 1,263 DEGs were downregulated (Table S1). Twenty genes were randomly selected for analysis by real-time RT-PCR with independent biological replicates (Figure 4C). The results indicated that the DEGs detected by RNA-seq were highly reliable.

Among the DEGs, 1,002 genes could be functionally annotated (annotations were found using BLASTN and BLASTX analyses against the GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database). The DEGs were subjected to GO analysis using the GO database (http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/agriGOv2/). According to this analysis, the results indicated that DEGs were significantly enriched in 5 terms (Table S2; Figure 4B). For the nutrient reservoir activity term, 18 DEGs related to storage proteins were also downregulated in *dek42* kernels, mainly in α-zein (α-zein3, *Zm00001d048809*; α-zein4, *Zm00001d048812*; α-zein5, *Zm00001d048806*; floury2*, Zm00001d049243*; and floury4, *Zm00001d048851*). For the starch metabolic process term, 8 DEGs related to starch synthase were almost downregulated. GBSS1 (granule-bound starch synthase I, *Zm00001d033937*) and PTST (protein targeting to starch, *Zm00001d015200*) that were dramatically downregulated were also consistent with the decreased starch and amylose content in *dek42* kernels. The plastid stroma were also affected according to 18 DEGs, including *Fdx3* (Ferredoxin3, *Zm00001d034760*) and *Ptac2* (plastid transcriptionally active chromosome 2, *Zm00001d022599*). The terms of nucleus (GO: 0005634, p-value=2.10E-7) were mostly related to the upregulated DEGs. These results suggest that the loss of function of DEK42 significantly affects starch synthesis and nutrient accumulation in maize kernels.

#### **The** *dek42* **mutation affects alternative splicing of expressed genes in kernel**

The RNA-seq data were also analyzed for alternative splicing events by *CASH* (Wu et al*.* 2017). CASH reported 68,688 AS events within 16,621 genes. A significant level of alternative splicing events was detected by CASH analysis. Among 68,688 splicing events, 241 alternative splicing events (from 222 genes) were identified as significantly changed (p<0.05) in *dek42* kernels compared with WT kernels (Table S3). The 222 statistically significant splicing genes were subjected to GO analysis, and 23 of them were significantly

enriched in the RNA binding term (Table S4). Six IR events were randomly selected for reanalysis by semiquantitative RT-PCR for both *dek42-ref* and *dek42-mu* kernels (Figure 5C-I). These results confirmed that the alternative splicing events identified by RNA-seq were reliable.

Among the 241 alternative splicing events, 142 belong to the IR pattern and 99 belong to the remaining AS types (Figure 5A). In the IR splicing pattern, there were 119 enhanced intron-retained and 23 reduced intron-retained events in *dek42* kernels compared with WT kernels. Among the 142 IR events, 94 were classified into the U2-type and 48 into the U12-type, based on the [classfication](file:///D:/us/WinRAR/Youdao/Dict/8.1.2.0/resultui/html/index.html#/javascript:;) of U2- and U12-type introns in ERISdb (http://lemur.amu.edu.pl/share/ERISdb/) (Table S4; Figure 5B). The 48 U12-type events included 47 over-retained events in *dek42* kernels, and the ratio of U12 type was 34% in IR. These results suggest that DEK42 plays an important role in U12-type intron splicing.

#### **DEK42 interacts with the spliceosome components SF3a1 and U1-70K**

To detect the interacting proteins of DEK42, Y2H screening was performed using full-length *Dek42* as bait against a yeast two hybrid (Y2H) library constructed from developing maize kernels (Zhang et al. 2012). The truncated splicing factor 3a (SF3a1) was identified as the high-frequency positive clone. The coding sequence (CDS) of SF3a1 was verified to interact with DEK42 by the Y2H assay. Because DEK42 interacted with SF3a1, which is a subunit of the U2 snRNP complex, other splicing-related proteins from the U2 snRNP complex (U2AF35 and U2AF65), U1 snRNP complex (U1-70K) and SR proteins (SCL28, SC35, and RSZ22) were selected to test for the protein interaction with DEK42 using the Y2H assay. Only U1-70K showed a positive protein-protein interaction with DEK42 in yeast cells (Figure 6A).

In the in vitro pull-down assays, GST-fused SF3a1 and U1-70K strongly interacted with His-tagged DEK42 proteins (Figure 6B). These positive interactions identified in yeast were further confirmed using the luciferase complementation image (LCI) assay in tobacco (*Nicotiana benthamiana*) leaf epidermal cells. The results showed that the cotransfection of DEK42*-*NLUC with SF3a1-CLUC and U1-70K-CLUC showed strong signals in tobacco cells compared to other negative controls (Figure 6C), indicating that DEK42 can interact with spliceosome components in tobacco cells. These results confirmed the interaction of DEK42 with SF3a1 and U1-70K in tobacco cells.

## **DISCUSSION**

*Dek42* **is a newly identified RRM\_RBM48 RBP that participated in alternative splicing** In this study, *Dek42* was cloned by *Mu* tag isolation and confirmed by the allele *dek42-mu* (Figure 2). *Dek42* encodes an RBP with one copy of the RRM\_RBM48 domain at the N terminus (Figure S4). The RRM domain is the most common and widespread eukaryotic RNA binding domain (Lunde et al. 2007), composed of approximately 90 amino acids containing two highly conserved motifs, RNP1 and RNP2 (Figure S4). The RRM family is located in the nucleus, although some of them shuttle between the nucleus and the cytoplasm (Jeong 2017). The subfamily of RRM\_RBM48 is a highly conserved single copy in higher eukaryotes, which is supported by the phylogenetic analysis (Figure 3). Many RRM proteins have been reported, especially SR proteins. The SR proteins play redundant roles in *Arabidopsis*, such as SR (SR34, SR34a, SR34b and SR30) and RS (RS31, RS31a, RS40 and RS41) (Yan et al. 2017). However, there are no proteins similar to DEK42 in maize. The results suggest that the function of DEK42 is nonredundant and critical for kernel development.

In this study, 68,688 AS events (16,620 genes) were detected according to RNA-seq. Similarly, 53,692 AS events were identified within 14,321 genes in B73 (Mei et al. 2017). Alternatively, nearly 60 % of expressed multiexon genes in B73 were subjected to AS, and one gene contained multiple AS events. However, 241 significantly aberrant AS events (222 genes) (p<0.05) were screened out in *dek42* (Figure 5). These results suggest that DEK42 plays a key role in AS. To date, several genes associated with AS have been reported. UBL1, a U6 biogenesis-like-1 gene, also disturbs 1154 IR events by affecting the 3' end process of U6 snRNA in maize (Li et al. 2017). RGH3 encodes a U2AF35-related protein that is mainly responsible for U12-type AS (Fouquet et al. 2012; Gault et al. 2017). *Zmsmu2* is a putative RNA splicing factor involved in rRNA procession and SR-type gene (TRA2 and RSp31B) splicing (Chung et al. 2007; Chung et al. 2009). These proteins all play important roles in AS.

In plants, IR is the predominant form of AS types (Marquez et al. 2012; Marquez et al. 2015). In *dek42*, the over-retained IR events are five-fold greater than the less-retained IR events in the 142 IR splicing events. Further evidence on the 48 U12-type events has suggested that, almost all of them (47/48) are over-retained in *dek42*. Interestingly, in our results, the ratio of the U12 type accounted for 34% of IR, which was much higher than the normal level (0.5%) (Zhu and Brendel 2003; Alioto 2007). The results suggest that DEK42 functions as a splicing activator. Among the 222 genes, 23 genes were enriched in the RNA binding term (GO: 0003723, p-value=4.30E-06), including SR splicing factors, such as CBP20 (*Zm00001d051065*), SC35 (*Zm00001d022189*) and SRp34a (*Zm00001d047498*). CBP20 is a conserved gene containing the U12 intron gene, which is essential for the CBP20 protein in *Arabidopsis* (Pieczynski et al. 2018). SC35 is a typical SR protein associated with pre-mRNA alternative splicing (Klein et al. 2016). VAL3 (*Zm00001d006597*, Figure 5C) is a plant-specific B3 domain containing the transcription factor VAL3 that is essential for seedling development repression of embryogenesis (Jia et al. 2014). YTH (*Zm00001d011602,* Figure 5D) is a newly identified RNA binding protein associated with splicing site selection (Zhang et al. 2010). Surprisingly, among the 2,695 DEGs, 34 are with significantly aberrant AS events (Table S4), suggesting that the relationship between DEGs and alternative splicing genes is not closely coupled and that the regulation network is intricate. *Sh1* (*Zm00001d045042*) and *Sh2* (*Zm00001d044129*), which are responsible for sugar accumulation, containing over-retained introns are downregulated in *dek42*. This case may lead to a wider range disturbance in pre-mRNA splicing. Future studies could investigate which aberrant AS events are the primary inducers in *dek42*.

#### **DEK42 interacts with spliceosome components to participate in alternative splicing**

The interaction of spliceosome and splicing factors has been widely reported (Lopato et al. 2002), especially between the components of U1/U2 subunits and the SR protein (Lopato et al. 2002). U1-70K, an important component of U1 snRNP, is evolutionarily conserved between different plant species, such as *Arabidopsis* and maize (Gupta et al. 2006). It has been reported to interact with at least five SR proteins, including SC35 (Barta et al. 2008; Yan et al. 2017), SCL33 (Golovkin and Reddy 1999; Yan et al. 2017), SR45 (Day et al. 2012), SRZ21 and SRZ22 (Golovkin and Reddy 1998), for binding to the 5'SS at the earliest stages in plants (Golovkin and Reddy 1999). In this study, we also observed the direct interaction of DEK42 with U1-70K in yeast and tobacco cells, which was similar to the feature of other SR proteins. In vivo, the splicing process was highly dynamic, involving the collective activity of hundreds of proteins. However, loss of one splicing-related protein is accompanied by coordinated loss or compensatory gain in the interaction of other proteins at the affected exons (Pandit et al. 2013). Therefore, proper interactions between DEK42 and U1-70K might be essential for its function.

The mature U2 snRNP is activated by the SF3a complex and recruited to the branch site through interactions with SR proteins (Tronchere et al. 1997; Tanackovic and Krämer 2005). SF3a was assembled by SF3a60, SF3a66 and SF3a120, with the former two elements interacting with SF3a120 in humans (Hodges and Beggs 1994). In our study, SF3a1 was similar to SF3a120 in structure, in which both proteins have a ubiquitin domain (Nesic and Kramer 2001) and interact with DEK42 in yeast and tobacco cells (Figure 6). Thus, DEK42 may play an important role in activating the U2 snRNP. The results suggest that DEK42 is directly associated with core spliceosome assembly at an early stage and splicing regulation, especially considering that loss of function of DEK42 leads to a reduction in splicing efficiency.

## **The** *dek42* **mutation affects kernel development and seedling growth in maize**

The defective kernel (*dek*) mutants were characterized by a drastic reduction in embryo and

endosperm filling and improper development (Neuffer and Sheridan 1980). *Dek42* plays an essential role in plant development, pre-mRNA splicing and general gene expression. Loss of function of *dek42* was shown to confer severe defects in various aspects of development, including delayed development, small kernels, delayed root development and lethal seedlings (Figures 1, S1). To date, several kernel mutants related to pre-mRNA splicing have been reported, such as RGH3 (Fouquet et al. 2012; Gault et al. 2017), UBL1 (Li et al. 2017) and *Zmsmu2* (Chung et al. 2007; Chung et al. 2009). RGH3, as a rough endosperm mutant, is responsible for endosperm differences and protein accumulation during kernel development. UBL1 and *Zmsmu2* mutants have smaller embryos and arrested seedlings. In conclusion, these mutants are caused by various mechanisms and diverse pathways.

The AS functional defect in *dek42* also causes changes in the expression level of other important developmental biological process-associated genes. Nutrient reservoir activity in the endosperm, the main storage tissue, largely determines the nutritional value of maize. The most abundant storage protein is zein, which accounts for 50%-70% of the total protein, and α-zein is the largest class of the zein protein (Holding and Larkins 2006). The genes encoding zein proteins were shown to be widely downregulated in *dek42* mutants, which might be the cause for the dramatically decreased zein protein content (Figure 1). In the endosperm, the nonphotosynthetic cells use specialized plastids, named amyloplasts, for long-term storage of starch, accounting for 70%-75 % of kernel weight (Howitt and Pogson 2006; Brust et al. 2013). The starch biosynthetic process (GO: 0019252) was shown to be enriched in downregulated DEGs. In the formation process of starch granules, *Wx1*, *Ss1*, *Sh2*, *Su2* and ADP subunits are important elements in different steps, with high activity for the conversion of sucrose to starch (Bhave et al. 1990; Schultz and Juvik 2004; Adams et al. 2012). RNA-seq also showed that α-amylase was double expressed in the *dek42* mutant compared with the WT, which may accelerate the degradation of formed starch granules (Buléon et al. 1998; Shrestha et al. 2012). The RNA-seq results explained the abnormal phenotype of the low starch content and damaged starch granules in the *dek42* mutant (Figure 1). The nucleus (GO: 0005634) is essential for kernel development (Qi et al. 2016). The interchromatin are storage/reassembly sites for the pre-mRNA splicing factor, and perichromatin fibrils are sites of actively transcribing genes and cotranscriptional splicing (Qi et al. 2016). The *dek42* mutation caused aberrant AS, which might result in feedback upregulation of nucleus- and chromosome-related genes for delayed kernel development (Figure 1). Consequently, the collective effect of disrupted expression of developmental regulators at the molecular level might explain the pleiotropic phenotypes observed in *dek42*.

## **MATERIAL AND METHODS**

**Plant materials**

The *dek42-ref* mutant stock (accession number 5604L) and UFMu-07152 (*dek42-mu*) were obtained from the Maize Genetics Cooperation Stock Center. The *dek42-ref* was crossed into the W22 inbred line and then self-pollinated to produce F2 populations. Kernels of the F2 ears exhibited a 3:1 segregation of WT kernels (*dek42*/+ or +/+), and homozygous mutant kernels (*dek42*/*dek42*) were used for cytological and biochemical analyses. The F2 population was used for genetic mapping of *dek42*. Root, stem, leaf, tassel, silk, husk and ear tissues were collected from W22 plants at the V12 stage for tissue expression pattern analysis. Immature kernels of the W22 inbred line were harvested at 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 27 and 30 DAP for expression pattern analysis. Maize plants were cultivated at the Shangzhuang Experimental Station of China Agricultural University, Beijing, China. In addition, *Nicotiana benthamiana* were grown in a greenhouse (25°C /22°C, 16 h light/8 h dark).

#### **Measurement of starch, proteins, and fatty acids**

For the starch measurements, twenty mature kernels without pericarp of the WT and *dek42* from the same ears were ground in liquid N2 and dried to a constant weight. Total starch was measured using an amyloglucosidase/α-amylase starch assay kit (Megazyme) according to the manufacturer's protocol and adapted from Feng (Feng et al. 2018). Each assay was conducted with three independent biological replicates.

For the protein measurements, the endosperm of *dek42* and WT mature kernels was separated from the embryo and pericarp by dissection after soaking the kernels in water. The samples were dried to constant weight and pulverized with a mortar and pestle in liquid N2. Then, fat was removed by diethyl ether, total protein was extracted by sodium borate buffer (12.5 mM Na2B4O7 •10H2O, 1% SDS, and 2% β-mercaptoethanol, pH 10.0), and zein protein was separated with 75% ethyl alcohol. The detailed process is described in a previously reported protocol (Wang et al. 2011). All of the measurements were replicated at least three times.

For the fatty acid measurements, the pretreatment of kernels was the same as that for the measurement of starch. The fine powder was extracted by mixing (anhydrous methanol: acetyl chloride-benzene=10:1), heating for 2 h in a water bath at 80℃, neutralizing in 7% K2CO3, and then analyzing by gas-liquid chromatography; the detailed process is described in a previously reported protocol (Yang et al. 2010). Each measurement was conducted with three independent biological replicates.

## **SNP genotyping array**

Genomic DNA of mature F2 kernels was extracted, and two equivalent genomic DNA pools were prepared, with one from the WT (+/+ or *dek42*/+) and the other from the mutant (*dek42*/*dek42*). Crude genomic DNA was extracted again with a QIAGEN DNeasy Plant Mini

Kit and quantified by NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometers, and working solutions were prepared at a concentration of 100 ng/μL. Based on a segregation ratio of 3:1, 750 ng of WT genomic DNA was mixed with 250 ng of mutant genomic DNA to prepare the total 1 µg F1 sample. Another 1 µg of mutant genomic DNA was severed as the mutant sample. The two DNA samples were hybridized and sequenced using the maizeSNP3072 chip (Tian et al. 2015). The genotype data from each sample were analyzed by the Illumina Genome Studio Genotyping Module according to the manufacturer's instructions (Chen et al. 2017).

#### *Mu* **tag isolation**

*Mu* tag isolation was performed according to a previously described protocol (Chen et al. 2017) and adapted as follows. Genomic DNA was extracted with the same protocol as the SNP genotyping array. A sample was sheared into fragments of 200-500 bp by sonication and adapter ligation at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. *Mu*-containing DNA fragments were enriched by hybridization to a biotinylated oligonucleotide corresponding to the end of the *Mu* terminal inverted repeat. Subsequently, low-cycle PCR (15 cycles) was used to amplify the recovered DNA. An additional hybrid enrichment step was performed to ensure that the vast majority of the sequenced DNA fragments harbored *Mu* sequences. Another low-cycle PCR (18 cycles) was performed, and the products were cloned into the pMD18-T vector to test the enrichment efficiency. Typically, samples with enrichment efficiencies greater than 30% were selected for further *Mu* Illumina sequencing at Berry Genomics (Beijing, China).

#### **Allelism test**

Crossing of heterozygous *dek42-ref* (*dek42-ref/+*) with heterozygous *dek42-mu* (*dek42-mu/+*) was performed. F2 ears with segregated WT and *dek* kernels were subjected to genotyping analysis. Twelve WT and 12 mutant kernels were randomly selected from the F2 ears. Genomic DNA was isolated from mature kernels according to a modified CTAB DNA extraction protocol (Porebski et al. 1997). The *Mu* insertion in the *dek42-ref* or *dek42-mu* allele was identified by PCR with the *Mu* molecular marker (primers are shown in Table S5).

### **Generation of CRISPR-Cas9-edited mutant alleles**

The CRISPR-Cas9 vector of *Zm00001d054077* was constructed using a simplex editing strategy as described by Zhu (Zhu et al. 2016). The 20 bp target sequence for editing is located in the third exon of *Dek42* (the target sequence is GACGAAGAAGACGTCAGTGTAGG). Agrobacterium-mediated maize transformation using F2 immature zygotic embryos (1.5-2.0 mm) of the Hi-Ⅱ hybrid (pApB) was conducted according to Frame (Frame et al*.* 2011). Transgenic lines (T0) were hybridized to the W22 inbred line. For the molecular identification

of F1 transgenic plants, a marker was designed to identify the editing effect of *Zm00001d054077*, and the BAR gene was used to identify the stability of editing (primers are shown in Table S5). The PCR products of the target were cloned into the pMD18-T vector for sequencing analysis. The kernels containing stably edited sites were used for allelism test by crossing heterozygous *dek42* (*dek42-ref/+* and *dek42-mu/+*) with heterozygous *dek42-cas9*  (*dek42-cas9/+*). The F2 ears were analyzed as described above.

#### **Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)**

For SEM analysis, WT and *dek42* kernels were harvested from the same self-pollinated ear at 21 DAP and at the mature stage, respectively. The immature samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution, substituted with osmic acid, dehydrated with ethanol, and then treated as mature samples. The mature samples were directly dried and sprayed with gold. The gold-coated samples were fixed on the electric objective table and then observed with scanning electron microscopy (Hitachi S-3400N). The detailed process is described in a previously reported method (Wang et al. 2014).

#### **RNA extraction, quantitative RT-PCR and semiquantitative RT-PCR**

Total RNA was extracted from pooled WT or *dek42* mutant kernels obtained from the same F2 ear at 12, 15, and 18 DAP (20 kernels for each sample) with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to a previous study (Feng et al. 2009). The crude RNA was treated with RNase-free DNase (Promega) and cleaned with the RNeasy miniElute cleanup kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA with Oligo dT<sup>18</sup> primers (Thermo Scientific).

Quantitative RT-PCR of *dek42* was performed on three independent RNA sample sets with ubiquitin as the reference gene. Twelve genes were randomly selected from RNA-seq for quantitative RT-PCR, and primers were designed as previously described (primers in Table S5) (Arvidsson et al. 2008). The assay was performed with SYBR Premix Ex Taq Ⅱ (Takara) using a 7500 Real-Time PCR system (ABI) according to the standard protocol. The obtained data were analyzed as previously described (Livak and Schmittgen 2001).

Semiquantitative RT-PCR was performed to identify the retained introns with actin as the reference gene in WT and mutant. The concentration of cDNA was adjusted uniformly by analyzing the actin expression with a dilution series. The primers were designed on both sides of the retained introns (the primers are shown in Table S5). Semiquantitative RT-PCR was performed with PCR Master Mix (TIANGEN) using T100 (Bio-Rad) according to the standard protocol. Electrophoretic results of PCR products were analyzed by Gel Doc XR+ (Bio-Rad).

#### **Polyclonal antibody**

For anti-DEK42 antibody production, the CDS of *Dek42* was inserted into pGEX-4t-1 (Amersham) at the EcoRⅠ- and BamHⅠ-digested sites. The GST-tagged DEK42 fusion protein was purified, and antibodies were produced in rabbits according to the standard protocols of Shanghai ImmunoGen Biological Technology.

#### **Immunoblot analysis**

Proteins extracted from developing WT, *dek42-ref* and *dek42-mu* kernels were separated by SDS-PAGE. Separated protein samples were then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (0.45 μm; Millipore). The membrane with the attached protein sample was incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. Using the Super Signal West Pico chemiluminescent substrate kit (Pierce), the signal was visualized according to the manufacturer's instructions. Polyclonal antibodies against DEK42 were used at a dilution of 1:1000. The antibody against actin (CST) was used at a dilution of 1:5000.

#### **Phylogenetic analysis**

Homologous sequences were identified in the NCBI nr (nonredundant protein sequences) database by performing a BLASTP search with DEK42 protein sequences. Amino acid sequences were aligned with MUSCLE in the MEGA7.0 software package using the default parameters. Evolutionary distances were calculated using Poisson correction analysis. The bootstrap method with 1000 replicates for phylogeny testing was used.

#### **Subcellular localization of DEK42**

The CDS sequence of *Dek42* was cloned into a CWG plant expression vector. The expression vector was introduced into onion epidermal cells through transient transformation using a Bio-Rad PDS-1000/HeTM biolistic particle delivery system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). The expression vector was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. The agro-infiltration procedure was performed as previously described (Sun et al. 2016). Fluorescence signals were detected using a LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss).

#### **RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis**

Total RNA was extracted from developing WT and *dek42* kernels at 15 DAP. cDNA libraries were constructed following the Illumina standard protocols. High-quality fastq data were then mapped to B73 RefGen\_v4.37 using TopHat with default parameters (version: 2.1.0) (Kim et al. 2013) (Kim et al*.* 2013). The data were normalized as reads per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped, because the sensitivity of RNA-seq depends on the transcript

length. Cufflinks (version: 2.2.1) was employed to reconstruct the transcripts and to estimate gene expression levels (Trapnell et al. 2010). Statistically significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified as those with a q-value<0.05 and further filtered with a fold change>2. BAM data were used for the detection of AS events by CASH (Comprehensive Alternative Splicing Hunting, https://sourceforge.net/projects/cash-program/files/2.2.0/) (Zhou et al. 2014). The events with a p-value<0.05 were considered statistically significant changes.

#### **Yeast two-hybrid screening and analysis of positive clones**

Two-hybrid screening was performed using the Matchmaker GAL4 Two-Hybrid System3 (Clontech) as previously described (Qiao et al. 2016). The CDS of *Dek42* was fused to the GAL4 BD domain in pGBKT7 at the EcoRI and Bam HI sites to form pGBKT7-DEK42 (primers are shown in Table S5). The bait vector was transformed into the yeast cell line AH109 and analyzed for autonomous activation. For library screening, the library constructed from harvested kernels on different days was screened by AH109-containing bait pGBKT7-DEK42 with the LiAc method (Zhang et al. 2012). The transformants were spread on QDO (SD/-Ade/-His/-Leu/-Trp) plates and incubated at 30°C for 5 days to allow for the growth of positive clones. The transformants were diluted in series and spread on DDO (SD/-Leu/-Trp) plates for transformation efficiency analysis. Plasmids from all the positive clones were extracted and amplified in *E. coli*. All the clones were sequenced and analyzed by GenBank BLASTx. The sequences that aligned with the same gene were grouped together.

#### **Yeast two-hybrid analysis of the interaction between DEK42 and different prey**

For the pGADT7-prey, the coding regions of the top 5 screened genes and splicing-related genes were amplified with cDNA and ligased to the pGADT7 vector. The yeast strain AH109 was cotransferred with pGBKT7-DEK42 and pGADT7-prey. The interaction between T-antigen and human P53 was used as a positive control, and the interaction between pGBKT7-empty and pGADT7-empty was used as a negative control. The putative positive clones were further spotted with a dilution series onto SD/-Leu/-Trp medium and SD/-Ade/-His/-Leu/-Trp medium (Yao et al. 2016).

## *In vitro* **pull-down assay**

The CDS sequence of *Dek42* was cloned and constructed into pET-32a using restriction enzymes. The CDS sequences of SF3a1 and U1-70K were cloned and constructed into the pGET-4t-1 vector. The vectors were transformed into Rosetta (DE3) cells for protein expression. The cells were grown in LB medium until OD600 reached 0.8, followed by induction expression at 25°C for 4 h in the presence of 2.5 mM isopropyl β–D-1-thiogalactopyranoside. Soluble His-DEK42 and GST-empty/SF3a1/U1-70K proteins were purified using His beads (Beaver) and GST beads (Beaver) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Approximately 200 ng of each GST protein sample was then rebound to 40 μL of GST beads, after which the beads were incubated with 1 μg purified His-DEK42 alone in 200 μL of binding buffer (50 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 1× C-complete proteinase inhibitor) for 4 h at 4°C. The beads were then washed four times with 1 mL binding buffer, and the proteins were released by binding buffer with 10 mM glutathione. The released proteins were then detected by protein blotting using an anti-His-tagged antibody (Sigma-Aldrich).

#### **LCI assay**

The CDS sequences of SF3a1 and U1-70K were cloned into both JW771 (NLUC) and JW772 (CLUC) (Feng et al. 2018) using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme) (primers are shown in Table S5). The constructs were transformed into *A. tumefaciens* strain GV3101. The Agrobacterium (strain GV3101) cells were grown to OD600 = 1.0, pelleted and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM methylester sulfonate, 10 mM MgCl2, and 150 µM acetosyringone, pH 5.7) and infiltrated into 4-week-old *Nicotiana benthamiana* leaves using a needleless syringe in the different combinations. After 48 h under 16 h of light and 8 h of dark, the leaves were injected with 1 mM luciferin (Promega). The resulting luciferase signals were analyzed using the Tanon-5200 imaging system. The LCI assay was performed three times independently.
