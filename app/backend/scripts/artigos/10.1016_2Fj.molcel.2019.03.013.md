## In Brief

How nucleotide-binding site leucine-rich repeat (NLR) receptors trigger immune transcriptional activation remains obscure. Zhai et al. discover an RRM class of transcription factors, PIBP1s, that directly interacts with the rice broadspectrum blast resistance NLRs, resulting in their nuclear accumulation and direct activation of defense genes, including OsWAK14 and OsPAL1.

#### SUMMARY

Nucleotide-binding site leucine-rich repeat (NLR) receptors perceive pathogen effectors and trigger plant immunity. However, the mechanisms underlying NLR-triggered defense responses remain obscure. The recently discovered Pigm locus in rice encodes a cluster of NLRs, including PigmR, which confers broad-spectrum resistance to blast fungus. Here, we identify PIBP1 (PigmR-INTERACTING and BLAST RESISTANCE PROTEIN 1), an RRM (RNArecognition motif) protein that specifically interacts with PigmR and other similar NLRs to trigger blast resistance. PigmR-promoted nuclear accumulation of PIBP1 ensures full blast resistance. We find that PIBP1 and a homolog, Os06 g02240, bind DNA and function as unconventional transcription factors at the promoters of the defense genes OsWAK14 and OsPAL1, activating their expression. Knockout of PIBP1 and Os06 g02240 greatly attenuated blast resistance. Collectively, our study discovers previously unappreciated RRM transcription factors that directly interact with NLRs to activate plant defense, establishing a direct link between transcriptional activation of immune responses with NLR-mediated pathogen perception.

#### INTRODUCTION

Plants have evolved a two-tiered immune system to prevent pathogen invasion: pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) governed by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI) conferred by highly polymorphic resistance (R) proteins [\(Jones and Dangl, 2006; Spoel and Dong, 2012](#page-13-0)). The robust and selective activation of o defense genes ensures effective ETI, which has been widely deployed in crop breeding [\(Dangl et al., 2013](#page-13-1)). The majority of R proteins belong to the nucleotide-binding site and leucine-rich repeat (NLR) protein family [\(Boller and He, 2009; DeYoung and Innes, 2006\)](#page-13-2). The N-terminal coiled-coil (CC) or Toll-like/interleukin-1 (IL-1) receptor (TIR) domains of NLRs function as signaling modules, forming homo- and/or hetero-NLR complexes that engage other signaling components ([Jones et al., 2016; Maekawa et al.,](#page-13-3) [2011\)](#page-13-3). The coordinated action of immune transcription factors (TFs) and general transcriptional machinery contribute to the selectivity of defense gene activation during ETI ([Birkenbihl](#page-13-4) [et al., 2017; Tsuda and Somssich, 2015](#page-13-4)). Recent studies have shed light on the transcriptional landscape and transcription factors that regulate defense gene expression [\(Buscaill and Rivas,](#page-13-5) [2014; Li et al., 2016, 2017; Tsuda and Somssich, 2015](#page-13-5)). The discoveries of direct interactions between specific NLRs and transcription factors revealed ''shortcut'' regulatory mechanisms underlying ETI. For instance, SPL6, a member of the SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN-LIKE transcription factor family, interacts with NLRs to promote immunity in plants [\(Padma](#page-14-0)[nabhan et al., 2013](#page-14-0)). Another transcription factor, bHLH84, interacts with NLRs to mediate transcriptional reprogramming upon infection ([Xu et al., 2014](#page-14-1)). Several WRKY transcription factors function in the regulation of defense genes via direct interaction with NLRs in diverse plants [\(Birkenbihl et al., 2017; Chang](#page-13-4) [et al., 2013; Inoue et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2016; Shen et al.,](#page-13-4) [2007\)](#page-13-4). Interestingly, the *Arabidopsis* NLR RRS1 carries a WRKY domain that is targeted by pathogen effectors ([Le Roux](#page-13-6) [et al., 2015; Sarris et al., 2015](#page-13-6)). Although these studies demonstrate that some NLRs can directly interact with and regulate transcription factors, a comprehensive understanding of the transcriptional machinery operating in ETI is still largely lacking [\(Dodds and Rathjen, 2010\)](#page-13-7).

Rice blast caused by *Magnaporthe oryzae* (*Pyricularia oryzae*) is the most devastating rice disease ([Dean et al., 2012; Talbot,](#page-13-8) [2003; Zhang et al., 2016](#page-13-8)). We previously identified the *Pigm* locus that confers broad-spectrum and durable blast resistance, which has been widely used in rice breeding ([Deng et al.,](#page-13-9) [2017](#page-13-9)). The *Pigm* locus encodes a cluster of NLRs, including the antagonistic receptor pair PigmR and PigmS. PigmR confers resistance to a broad-spectrum of blast races, whereas PigmS competitively attenuates PigmR homodimerization to suppress resistance. PigmR and PigmS control the balance between high disease resistance and yield, revealing a unique immune mechanism through the interaction of an antagonistic NLR pair in plants ([Deng et al., 2017; Wang and Valent, 2017\)](#page-13-9). However, the molecular mechanisms underlying PigmR-mediated broadspectrum resistance remained elusive.

In this study, we uncover a previously unappreciated protein binding partner of PigmR, PIBP1, an RNA recognition motif (RRM)-containing protein. We show that PIBP1 and its homolog, Os06 g02240, are specifically required to mediate resistance by PigmR and other broad-spectrum NLRs, but not NLRs with limited race-specific resistance. We provide evidence that PIBP1 and Os06 g02240 are unconventional transcription factors that directly activate the expression of the defense genes *OsWAK14* and *OsPAL1*. Thus, our work identifies previously unappreciated regulators of ETI and RRM-containing transcription factors that regulate immunity in plants.

#### RESULTS

#### PigmR Interacts with PIBP1 through the CC Domain

To elucidate the mechanism underlying PigmR-mediated blast resistance ([Deng et al., 2017\)](#page-13-9), we performed a yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screen to isolate interacting proteins. We used the PigmR CC domain as bait to screen a cDNA library derived from rice challenged with *M. oryzae*. PigmR-INTERACTING and BLAST RESISTANCE PROTEIN 1 (PIBP1/Os03 g50560) strongly interacted with the PigmR CC domain and fulllength PigmR [\(Figure 1A](#page-3-0)). We validated the interaction between full-length PigmR or PigmR-CC and PIBP1 *in planta* using a split luciferase complementation (SLC) assay in *Nicotiana benthamiana* (*N. benthamiana*) ([Figure 1](#page-3-0)B). Further, a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay showed that PigmR-CC and PIBP1 interact predominantly in the nucleus ([Figure 1](#page-3-0)C). We also generated transgenic rice plants constitutively expressing GFP-tagged PIBP1 (PIBP1-GFP) and Myctagged PigmR (PigmR-7Myc-6His) in the Nipponbare background, which lacks the *Pigm* locus. PigmR-7Myc-6His confers blast resistance equal to the near-isogenic line NIL-*Pigm* [\(Fig](#page-13-10)[ures S1](#page-13-10)A and S1B). A coimmunoprecipitation (coIP) assay confirmed that PigmR interacts with PIBP1 *in planta* ([Figure 1](#page-3-0)D). Finally, we detected a direct physical interaction between glutathione S-transferase (GST)-PigmR-CC and His-PIBP1 in an *in vitro* pull-down assay [\(Figure 1](#page-3-0)E). Together, these data demonstrate that PigmR interacts with PIBP1 through the CC domain in the nucleus.

PIBP1 is a predicted RNA-binding protein containing an RRM domain (amino acids 5–74) ([Figure S1C](#page-13-10)), with homologs in other sequenced plant genomes [\(Figures S1C](#page-13-10) and S1D). To determine the amino acids of PIBP1 required for interacting with PigmR, we designed a series of PIBP1 truncations and performed the Y2H assay. We found that the RRM domain was not required for binding to PigmR, but deletion of PIBP1 residues 270–279 abolished the interaction [\(Figures S1E](#page-13-10) and S1F). We generated site-specific mutations and found that one amino acid (S275) is essential for the interaction with PigmR [\(Figures S1E](#page-13-10)–S1G).

#### PIBP1 Contributes to PigmR-Mediated Blast Resistance

To determine whether PIBP1 functions in PigmR-mediated blast resistance, we generated transgenic rice plants in the *Pigm* background that either overexpress a PIBP1-GFP fusion (*PIBP1*-OE) [\(Figures S2](#page-13-10)A and S2B) or downregulate *PIBP1* expression by RNAi (*PIBP1*-RNAi) [\(Figure S2](#page-13-10)A). Neither PIBP1-GFP overexpression nor *PIBP1* knockdown affected the expression of *PigmR*[\(Fig](#page-13-10)[ure S2](#page-13-10)C). However, the *PIBP1*-RNAi lines displayed significantly decreased blast resistance compared with both the NIL-*Pigm* and *PIBP1*-OE plants, which were similarly resistant ([Figure 2](#page-4-0)A). Additionally, *PIBP1* CRISPR/Cas9 knockout (*PIBP1*-KO) lines showed a similar decrease in PigmR-mediated blast resistance [\(Figures S2D](#page-13-10)–S2F). Thus, PIBP1 contributes to full PigmR-mediated blast resistance. Wild-type Nipponbare contains a functionally different NLR, Pish, which confers race-specific resistance against isolate YN2 ([Deng et al., 2017\)](#page-13-9). Interestingly, PIBP1 does not interact with Pish [\(Figures 2](#page-4-0)B, [S2](#page-13-10)G, and S2H), and resistance to YN2 was unaffected by RNAi-mediated depletion of *PIBP1* in Nipponbare [\(Figures S2](#page-13-10)I and S2J).

Based on the above results, we hypothesized that PIBP1 functions in blast resistance by interacting with specific NLRs. To test this, we evaluated other known blast-resistance NLRs, including Pi9 and Pizt, which have a resistance spectrum similar to that of Pigm [\(Deng et al., 2006; Deng et al., 2017; Qu](#page-13-11) [et al., 2006](#page-13-11)), and Pid3, which confers limited race resistance [\(Shang et al., 2009\)](#page-14-2). A Y2H assay revealed that PIBP1 interacts with the CC domains of Pi9 and Pizt, but not Pid3 [\(Figures 2B](#page-4-0), [S2](#page-13-10)K, and S2L). SLC assays confirmed the interactions between PIBP1 and the CC domains of Pi9 and Pizt [\(Figure 2C](#page-4-0)). In contrast to Pid3 and Pish, the CC domains of PigmR, Pi9 and Pizt are highly conserved ([Figure S2](#page-13-10)M), suggesting that PIBP1 interacts with specific NLRs via conserved CC sequences to mediate blast resistance. We generated *PIBP1* CRISPR/Cas9 KO lines in the *Pizt* background (variety ZH11) [\(Figure S2N](#page-13-10)). *PIBP1*-KO in *Pizt* decreased its resistance to the *M. oryzae* avirulent strain CH97 ([Figure 2](#page-4-0)D). Together, our findings indicate that PIBP1 is required for complete blast resistance by directly interacting with conserved CC domains of NLRs that confer resistance to a broad spectrum of blast races.

#### PIBP1 Functions as a Transcription Factor

*PIBP1* encodes an RRM-containing protein. To assess whether PIBP1 binds nucleic acids, we performed *in vitro* DNA/RNA binding assays using recombinant His-PIBP1 fusion proteins purified from *E. coli* ([Figures S5](#page-13-10)A and S5B). We used MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry analysis to analyze purified His-PIBP1 proteins, which revealed full-length as well as partially degraded His-PIBP1 [\(Figure S5C](#page-13-10); [Table S1\)](#page-13-10), verifying the sample purity. We found that PIBP1 bound RNA/Poly(U), single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) and DNA *in vitro* ([Figure S5](#page-13-10)A). A mutant of PIBP1 lacking the RRM domain, PIBP1<sup>D</sup>5–74, showed a reduced or abolished interaction with RNA and ssDNA relative to wild-type PIBP1. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) further confirmed that PIBP1 directly bound to rice RNAs, which required the RRM ([Figure S5D](#page-13-10)). Therefore, PIBP1 has RNA-binding capacity through its RRM domain. However, the double-stranded DNAbinding activity of PIBP1 is independent of the RRM domain [\(Figure S5](#page-13-10)A).

We were intrigued by PIBP1's dsDNA binding activity, given that its nuclear localization is critical for its function in blast resistance. To evaluate whether PIBP1 binds genomic DNA *in planta*, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) in the transgenic NIL-*Pigm* line (#8) overexpressing PIBP1-GFP. We utilized wild-type NIL-*Pigm* as a negative control. We aligned 10 million reads to the rice reference genome, resulting in 434 PIBP1-enriched genomic regions ([Table S2\)](#page-13-10), distributed throughout genic regions ([Table S2](#page-13-10)). We analyzed the top-scoring ChIP peaks and discovered four overrepresented DNA motifs (M1–M4) that were bound by His-PIBP1 [\(Fig](#page-8-0)[ure 5A](#page-8-0)). The affinity assay indicated that PIBP1 likely bound preferentially to motifs 2 and 4 ([Figure 5B](#page-8-0)). EMSA analysis revealed that purified His-PIBP1 bound 3 out of 4 tested promoter sequences that contain the putative motifs identified by ChIP-seq [\(Figure S5](#page-13-10)E).

Notably, His-PIBP1 binding with the motifs 1 and 3 was reduced in the presence of the corresponding ssDNA [\(Figure 5](#page-8-0)A). In contrast, ssDNA did not substantially reduce the His-PIBP1 binding with motifs 2 and 4, further indicating that PIBP1 bound preferentially to these A/T-rich DNA motifs in a dose-dependent manner ([Figures 5A](#page-8-0) and [S5F](#page-13-10)). Indeed, mutations that reduced the A/T content of motifs 2 and 4 also reduced their binding affinity to PIBP1, whereas those with increased the A/T content improved binding ([Figure 5](#page-8-0)C). To determine the influence of the RRM domain on the interaction between PIBP1 and DNA, we performed EMSA with His-RRM and His-PIBP1D5-74 [\(Fig](#page-13-10)[ure S5](#page-13-10)G). As expected, His-PIBP1DRRM retained binding to DNA, whereas His-PIBP1-RRM did not show binding activity [\(Figure 5](#page-8-0)D). Taken together, our data show that PIBP1 binds DNA independently of the RRM domain at A/T-rich motifs.

Based on the results above, we hypothesized that nuclear PIBP1 functions as an unconventional transcription factor. Consistent with this hypothesis, PIBP1 displayed transactivation activity in yeast, and the RRM domain was necessary and sufficient for transactivation ([Figure 5E](#page-8-0)). In addition, we found that PIBP1 self-associated in Y2H and SLC assays [\(Figures S5](#page-13-10)H and S5I), consistent with the observation that transcription factors often dimerize or oligomerize [\(Pireyre and Burow, 2015\)](#page-14-3). BiFC analysis in rice protoplasts showed that the PIBP1-self interaction mainly occurred in the nucleus and cell periphery [\(Figure S5J](#page-13-10)). Taken together, our data suggest that nuclear PIBP1 functions as a previously unappreciated transcription factor with an RRM transactivation domain.

#### PIBP1 Directly Regulates Defense Response Genes

To uncover genes potentially regulated by PIBP1, we performed RNA-seq analysis of PIBP1-RNAi and wild-type NIL-Pigm plants challenged with *M. oryzae*. We detected 1,919 differentially expressed genes in PIBP1-RNAi plants compared to wild-type NIL-Pigm [\(Table S2](#page-13-10)), suggesting that PIBP1 influences gene expression. We integrated our RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data with our ChIP-seq data, which yielded seven candidate direct targets of PIBP1 that were downregulated in infected PIBP1-RNAi plants [\(Table S2\)](#page-13-10). Among them, OsWAK14 (wallassociated kinase 14) drew our attention, because it positively regulates rice blast resistance ([Delteil et al., 2016\)](#page-13-12), and many other WAKs also confer plant disease resistance [\(Hu et al.,](#page-13-13) [2017; Nelson et al., 2018; Saintenac et al., 2018; Zuo et al.,](#page-13-13) [2015\)](#page-13-13). We found that the predicted promoter regions P1 and P2 of OsWAK14 contain A/T -rich motifs [\(Figure 6](#page-9-0)A; [Table S3\)](#page-13-10), and His-PIBP1 bound specifically to P1 and P2, but not P3, which lacks a A/T rich motif, in EMSA ([Figures 6](#page-9-0)A and [S6](#page-13-10)A). Moreover, a ChIP-qPCR assay revealed that PIBP1-GFP was enriched at the OsWAK14 promoter regions in NIL-Pigm [\(Figure 6B](#page-9-0)). To determine whether the binding of PIBP1 to the promoter of OsWAK14 affects its expression, we performed dual-luciferase assays by co-expressing a ProOsWAK14::Luc reporter with PIBP1 or NLS-PIBP1 in *N. benthamiana* [\(Figures](#page-13-10) [S6](#page-13-10)B and S6C). Compared with the vector control, the luciferase-to-Renilla (LUC/REN) ratio was slightly elevated by PIBP1 but significantly increased by nuclear-localized NLS-PIBP1 ([Figure 6C](#page-9-0)). These data suggest that PIBP1 positively regulates the expression of OsWAK14, dependent on its nuclear localization.

Further, we examined the expression of OsWAK14 during blast infection. OsWAK14 transcription was induced by *M. oryzae*, and its expression level was much higher in NIL-Pigm than in Nipponbare during pathogen challenge ([Figures 6](#page-9-0)D and [S6](#page-13-10)D). In contrast, pathogen-induction of OsWAK14 was significantly diminished in PIBP1-RNAi/NIL-Pigm plants compared with in NIL-Pigm and PIBP1-OE/NIL-Pigm plants [\(Figure 6E](#page-9-0)). These data together suggest that PIBP1 directly regulates OsWAK14 expression *in planta*. To verify the role of OsWAK14 in PigmR-mediated blast resistance, we generated two independent OsWAK14 CRISPR/Cas9 KO lines (OsWAK14- KO #1 and #9) in NIL-Pigm [\(Figure S6](#page-13-10)E). Compared with NIL-Pigm, the OsWAK14-KO plants showed decreased resistance to *M. oryzae* [\(Figure 6](#page-9-0)F), strengthening the conclusion that OsWAK14 promotes PigmR-mediated resistance downstream of PIBP1. Collectively, our results suggest that PIBP1 functions as a transcription factor, and directly binds the A/T-rich *cis* elements of the OsWAK14 promoter to activate its expression.

Rice phenylalanine ammonia lyases (OsPALs) are involved in rice blast resistance ([Tonnessen et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2018\)](#page-14-4).

#### The PIBP1 Homolog Os06 g02240 Is Also a Transcription Factor Functioning in Blast Resistance

Phylogenetic analysis of *PIBP1* homologs revealed that PIBP1 shares similarity with rice Os11 g34680, Os06 g02240, and Os04 g53330 ([Figure S7](#page-13-10)A). To investigate the function of these homologs, we engineered and transiently expressed their YFP fusion proteins in NIL-*Pigm* protoplasts. We found that these YFP fusion proteins exhibited nuclear localization, similar to

<span id="page-9-0"></span>*(legend on next page)*

PIBP1-YFP ([Figure S7](#page-13-10)B). Moreover, we found that Os06 g02240 and Os11 g34680 bound DNA motifs 2 and 4 [\(Figures 7](#page-11-0)A and [S7C](#page-13-10)). Further, Os06 g02240 exhibited transactivation activity in yeast ([Figure 7B](#page-11-0)) and exhibited self-association in the Y2H and SLC assays [\(Figures S7](#page-13-10)D and S7E), suggesting that Os06 g02240 may also serve as a transcription factor.

Interestingly, Os06 g02240, but not the other two homologs of PIBP1, also interacted with PigmR depending on its C-terminal region in a Y2H assay, in spite of lacking the similar C-terminal of PIBP1 ([Figures 7C](#page-11-0), [S7](#page-13-10)A, S7F, and S7G), and the interaction was confirmed by SLC assay in *N. benthamiana* [\(Figure 7](#page-11-0)D). Similar to PIBP1, Os06 g02240 also interacted with Pi9 and Pizt, but not with Pish and Pid3 ([Figures S7](#page-13-10)H and S7I). Further, Os06 g02240 also bound the promoter regions of *OsWAK14* and *OsPAL1* by EMSA [\(Figure 7](#page-11-0)E) and activated their expression in *N. benthamiana* ([Figure 7F](#page-11-0)), suggesting that Os06 g02240 also functions in blast resistance mediated by PigmR and the other NLRs. To test this hypothesis, we used CRISPR/Cas9 to generate KO lines of *Os06 g02240* (*Os06 g02240*-KO #9 and #10) [\(Figure S7](#page-13-10)J). Loss of *Os06 g02240* resulted in decreased PigmR-mediated resistance [\(Figure 7G](#page-11-0)), revealing that it functions in NLR-mediated immunity. Furthermore, KO lines of both *PIBP1* and *Os06 g02240* exhibited enhanced susceptibility compared with single-KO plants, indicating their functional redundancy in the NLR-mediated resistance. Therefore, our results collectively suggest that PIBP1 and its homologs represent a previously unrealized plant transcription factor family and contribute to blast resistance via direct interaction with NLRs to promote their nuclear accumulation and activate the expression of defense genes in rice.

#### DISCUSSION

NLR-mediated disease resistance plays a central role in warding off invading pathogens in plants ([Boller and He, 2009; Dangl](#page-13-2) [et al., 2013; Jones et al., 2016; Spoel and Dong, 2012; Williams](#page-13-2) [et al., 2014](#page-13-2)). Transcriptional reprogramming upon NLR activation orchestrates key immune signaling pathways in plants. However, only a few transcription factors have been identified that directly interact with NLRs ([Birkenbihl et al., 2017; Le Roux](#page-13-4) [et al., 2015; Sarris et al., 2015; Shen et al., 2007](#page-13-4)). Thus, the molecular mechanisms underlying NLR-triggered immunity (ETI) are only understood for few NLRs, and the components of ETI signaling remain mostly elusive [\(Dodds and Rathjen,](#page-13-7) [2010; Kourelis and van der Hoorn, 2018](#page-13-7)). The recently discovered rice NLR locus *Pigm* confers broad-spectrum and durable blast resistance ([Deng et al., 2017; Wang and Valent, 2017\)](#page-13-9). In this study, we identified an RRM-domain protein, PIBP1, and its homolog that interact with PigmR and other close NLRs. Our findings suggest that PIBP1 and its homolog function as previously unrecognized transcription factors, which directly interact with NLRs to regulate immune responses in rice.

Plant genomes contain a large number of RRM-containing proteins, but only a few RRM proteins have been elucidated for their roles in plants, including immunity in plants, likely through RNA processing [\(Fedoroff, 2002; Lee et al., 2012;](#page-13-14) [Lorkovic, 2009; Woloshen et al., 2011](#page-13-14) ). The well-known RNAbinding protein FCA, which harbors two RRMs, controls flowering time through posttranscriptional regulation ([Macknight et al.,](#page-14-5) [1997\)](#page-14-5) or RNA-mediated DNA methylation and/or chromatin silencing ([Baurle et al., 2007](#page-13-15) € ). [Figure S7](#page-13-10)K shows a model in which the PIBP1s act as unconventional transcription factors in disease resistance. PIBP1 accumulates in the nucleus in an NLRdependent manner, directly binds DNA at A/T-rich *cis* elements of target genes, such as *OsWAK14* and *OsPAL1*, and directly activates the defense genes. Similar criteria were used to define another plant transcription factor class, including BES1 ([Yin](#page-14-6) [et al., 2005](#page-14-6)). To our knowledge, direct gene activation via the RRM domain within a transcription factor has not previously been reported.

A proposed model for ETI signaling suggests that NLRs relocate to the nucleus on activation and interact with nuclear factors to trigger defense gene expression [\(Dodds and Rathjen, 2010](#page-13-7)). Nuclear transport of NLRs and key regulators is essential to initiate plant immunity; however, nuclear dynamics and immune signaling are only understood for a few of NLRs and regulators [\(Dodds and Rathjen, 2010; Gu et al., 2016; Kourelis and van](#page-13-7) [der Hoorn, 2018; Shen et al., 2007](#page-13-7)). In this study, we strongly suggest that NLR-mediated nuclear accumulation of PIBP1 and Os06 g02240 is required for blast resistance. Understanding the dynamic interaction and nuclear localization of NLRs and transcription factors, and other immune regulators, remains an important challenge. Intriguingly, the RRM transcription factors display NLR-dependent nuclear localization, which is not affected by pathogen challenge, likely ensuring the unique features of the transcription factor family in plant immunity. This is in contrast to the constitutive nuclear accumulation of WRKY45, a transcription factor that interacts with another rice blast resistance NLR (Pb1) in the nucleus ([Inoue et al., 2013](#page-13-16)), and is also contrast to the nuclear interaction of the barley NLR MLA10 with the transcription factors, WRKYs and MYB6 ([Chang](#page-13-17) [et al., 2013; Shen et al., 2007\)](#page-13-17). A similar but mechanistically distinct NLR-dependent nuclear translocation occurs in the interaction between *Arabidopsis* NLR, RSP4, and EDS1, a lipase-like defense signaling regulator, both distributed between the cytoplasm and nucleus [\(Wirthmueller et al., 2007](#page-14-7)). The complex of RPS4 and EDS1 mainly localizes in the cytoplasm and is observed in the nucleus in steady state or upon AvrRps4 recognition ([Bhattacharjee et al., 2011; Heidrich et al., 2011](#page-13-18)). Further, RPS4 associates with EDS1 in the cytoplasm in the absence of another NLR, RRS1, whereas the RPS4-EDS1 association is observed in the nucleus in the presence of RRS1, suggesting a nuclear-localized complex consisting of RPS4, RRS1, and EDS1 in both pre- and post-activation states. The RPS4-EDS1 association in the nucleus is likely not altered by pathogen effectors such as AvrRps4 in the presence of RRS1 ([Huh et al., 2017](#page-13-19)).

Our data suggest that the nuclear accumulation of PIBP1 by the pre-activation receptor complex in the absence of pathogen or effector is a prerequisite to activate defense genes upon pathogen perception by NLRs. We propose that the NLR-dependent nuclear accumulation of RRM transcription factors might poise defense genes, including *OsWAK14* and *OsPAL1*, for strong induction upon pathogen recognition. However, their induction should require additional host components that are likely activated by pathogen challenge. These proposed host components might be activated and function in the nucleus upon pathogen perception, either directly modifying and/or activating the RRM transcription factors or derepressing the activity of RRM transcription factors in the nucleus. Our results also suggest that NLR CC domains mediate the nuclear accumulation or translocation of the transcription factors. However, similar to many other NLRs, the CC domains lack an NLS. Therefore, it will be crucial to determine the intricate mechanism underlying the nuclear accumulation of PIBP1 by PigmR and other NLRs. It is possible that a yet-unknown component helps the NLRs or CC domains recruit PIBP1 into the nucleus. Further genetic and biochemical investigations could uncover such a component.

This RRM transcription factor family we identified likely comprises limited members, although we have not tested all predicted RRM proteins for their roles in transcription activation in rice and other plants. Our findings therefore provide insights into the framework of transcriptional reprogramming directly guided by NLRs. Moreover, given that PIBP1 functions in NLRmediated blast resistance, our study may also provide a potential molecular approach to breed crops with broad-spectrum disease resistance. For example, instead of trying to deploy NLR genes, whose products are usually exploited and inactivated by pathogens, the RRM transcription factor family could be manipulated technologically to achieve more lasting outcomes.

#### <span id="page-18-1"></span>EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS

#### Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

All transgenic lines and near-isogenic *Pigm* line (NIL-*Pigm*) used in this study are in Nipponbare background (*Oryza sativa* ssp. *Geng*/ *Japonica*). Nipponbare, NIL-*Pigm*, *Pizt* (ZH11), *Pi9* (Ky*-Pi9*) and CO39 were generated or collected in our previous study ([Deng et al.,](#page-13-9) [2017](#page-13-9)). Transgenic lines developed in this study include *PIBP1*-RNAi/Nipponbare, *PIBP1*-RNAi/NIL-*Pigm*, *PIBP1* CRISPR/Cas9 KO mutants (*PIBP1*-KO/NIL-*Pigm*), *pUBI::PIBP1*-*GFP*(*PIBP1*-OE)/Nipponbare, *pUBI::NLS-PIBP1-GFP*/Nipponbare, *pUBI::PigmR-7Myc-6His*/Nipponbare, *pUBI::PIBP1-GFP*(*PIBP1*-OE)/NIL-*Pigm*, *OsWAK14*-KO/NIL-*Pigm*, *PIBP1*-KO/*Pizt*, *Os06 g02240*-KO/NIL-*Pigm* and *PIBP1*/*Os06 g02240*-double KO/NIL-*Pigm*. Plants were grown at the experimental stations in Shanghai for the summer season, or Lingshui for the winter season (Hainan Island, southern China) under natural conditions for tillering plant inoculation (punch injection) and seed production. For seedling spraying inoculation, seedlings were grown at greenhouse at 26C, 14-h day/10-h night. *Nicotiana benthamiana* (*N. benthamiana*) for transient expression was grown at 22C under long-day conditions (16-h day/8-h night).

#### <span id="page-18-2"></span>METHOD DETAILS

#### Plasmid Construction and Rice Transformation

To generate the *PIBP1* RNAi construct, a unique 160-bp CDS and 372-bp 3<sup>0</sup> UTR fragments of *PIBP1* was amplified by RT-PCR using specific primers and inserted as inverted repeats into the conventional RNAi vector PTCK303 to generate hairpin RNAi constructs. To generate constructs for *PIBP1* fusion overexpression, the *PIBP1* coding sequence was inserted into PUN1301-*pUbi-GFP* vector ([You](#page-14-9) [et al., 2016](#page-14-9)). The *PIBP1* RNAi and overexpression plasmids were introduced into NIL-*Pigm* and Nipponbare, respectively, via *Agrobacterium* (EHA105)-mediated transformation to generate more than 30 independent transgenic lines for each background, further selected by RT-PCR or western blot. *PIBP1* was fused with the nuclear location sequence (*NLS*) ([Kalderon et al., 1984\)](#page-13-21) to generate an NLS-PIBP1 fusion construct. PUN1301-*NLS-PIBP1-GFP* expression plasmid was introduced into Nipponbare via *Agrobacterium*-mediated transformation to generate more than 30 independent lines for each transgene, which were identified by western blot using anti-GFP antibody.

For CRISPR (Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats)/Cas9 constructs, target sequences of *PIBP1*, *Os06 g02240* and *OsWAK14* were selected. The designed target sequences were synthesized and CRISPR/Cas9 constructs were generated following the reported protocol ([Ma et al., 2015\)](#page-14-8). The CRISPR/Cas9 plasmids were introduced into *Agrobacterium* strain EHA105 and transferred to different genetic backgrounds. More than 30 independent lines were developed for each transformation. PCR-based sequencing was performed to identify target gene mutations. All primer sequences used for cloning can be found in [Table S3](#page-13-10).

#### Pathogen Inoculation and Disease Resistance Assay

*M. oryzae* isolates, TH12, CH97 and YN2, were used for inoculation. Spores were produced by growing on complete agar medium for 10 days at 25C in a 12 h-light/12-h dark condition before used. For inoculation, spores were collected in sterile water, and the spore concentration was adjusted to approximately 1 3 10<sup>5</sup> spores/ml. For gene induction, two-week-old rice seedlings were spray-inoculated with spore suspensions in a dew growth chamber as previously described ([Deng et al., 2017\)](#page-13-9). Leaves of tillering plants grown in the field were punch-injection inoculated or punch inoculated with pierced leaves as previously described ([Deng et al., 2006; Wang](#page-13-11) [et al., 2008](#page-13-11)). Disease symptom was evaluated at 7 dpi with calculating lesion areas/lengths of fifteen infected leaves using the software ImageJ or ruler, and measuring relative fungal growth by DNA-based quantitative PCR as previously reported ([You et al., 2016](#page-14-9)). Relative fungal growth was calculated using the threshold cycle value (CT) of *M. oryzae* 28S *rDNA* against the CT of rice genomic *ACTIN1* DNA. All inoculation experiments were repeated at least three times independently. Sequences of the primers are listed in Table S3.

#### Yeast Two-Hybrid Screen and Interaction Assay

The Y2H screening procedures were used for identification of PigmR-interaction proteins (PIPs) as previously described [\(You et al.,](#page-14-9) [2016](#page-14-9)). Briefly, a rice cDNA library in pDEST22 (Invitrogen) was generated with mRNAs isolated from leaves of two-week-old GM4 (*Pigm*) seedlings after blast infection. The CC domain (1-187 aa) of PigmR were fused to the GAL4 DNA binding domain in pDEST32 as the bait. Yeast (strain AH109) was transformed and positive clones were screened and sequenced.

For Y2H assay, the target coding sequences were isolated by PCR using the gene-specific primers [\(Table S3](#page-13-10)) and inserted into the vectors pDEST22/32 (Invitrogen) or PGADT7/PGBKT7 (Clontech). To assay protein-protein interactions, Different construct combinations were co-transformed into yeast strain AH109, clones were grown on dropout medium (lacking either Trp and Leu or Trp, Leu and His) containing proper 3-aminotriazole. All Y2H experiments were repeated three times independently.

#### Split Luciferase Complementation and Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation Assay

To generate constructs for split luciferase complementation (SLC) assays, the tested coding sequences were PCR-amplified and inserted into pCAMBIA-35S-nLuc and pCAMBIA-35S-cLuc, respectively. The assay was performed as previously described [\(You](#page-14-9) [et al., 2016\)](#page-14-9). In brief, the resulting plasmids were transformed into *Agrobacterium* (strain GV3101), which were cultured overnight in LB media, and were collected and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10mM MgCl2, 10mM MES, 150 mM acetosyringone, pH 5.6), mixed with GV3101 expressing p19, and incubated for 2-3 hours at 30C before infiltration. The suspensions were then infiltrated into leaves of three-week-old *N. benthamiana*. After 2 days of growth, the luciferase activity was measured with Luciferase Assay Systems (Promega).

For bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay of PIBP1/PigmR-CC and PIBP1-self interaction in rice protoplasts, the PIBP1 and PigmR-CC coding sequences were inserted into PSAT1-nVenus and PSAT1-cCFP vectors, respectively. Rice protoplast isolation from seedling sheaths and PEG-mediated transformation were performed as described previously ([You et al.,](#page-14-9) [2016\)](#page-14-9). After incubation in the dark for 16-20 h at 26C, transformed protoplasts were imaged under a confocal microscope (Olympus Fluoview FV1000). All PCR primers used are listed in [Table S3.](#page-13-10)

#### Subcellular Localization

For subcellular localization of proteins in rice protoplasts (Nipponbare, NIL-*Pigm*, *Pizt* or *Pi9*), the coding sequences of the targeted proteins (PIBP1, PIBP1S275A, PigmR, PigmR-CC, Os04 g53330, Os06 g02240 and Os11 g34680) were inserted into the PA7-35S-YFP. The coding sequences of PigmR-CC, Pizt-CC and Pi9-CC were cloned into PA7-35S-CFP for co-expression with PIBP1- YFP. The NLS sequence was fused in-frame with RFP into PA7-35S-RFP as a nuclear marker. Rice protoplast isolation from seedling sheaths and PEG-mediated transformation were performed as described previously [\(You et al., 2016\)](#page-14-9). After incubation in the dark for 16-20 h at 26C, transformed protoplasts were imaged under a confocal microscope (Olympus Fluoview FV1000) or collected for protein analysis.

For subcellular localization analysis of proteins transiently expressed in *N. benthamiana*, the coding sequences of PIBP1 and PigmR-CC were cloned into the pCAMBIA-35S-YFP and pCAMBIA-35S-mCherry, respectively. HTR4 (*At4g40030*) fused with YFP or mCherry was used as a nucleus marker. These constructs were then transformed into *Agrobacterium* (GV3101) and co-expressed in the *N. benthamiana* using *Agrobacterium* infiltration method. The fluorescence signal was detected using a confocal microscope (Olympus Fluoview FV1000) 36 h after infiltration. Plasmolysis was induced on root cells by incubating the samples in 30% (w/v) sucrose solution for 10-20 min.

#### Protein Extraction and Co-Immunoprecipitation

Total proteins were extracted from 0.2 g of fresh leaves, which were ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in the protein extraction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 4 M urea and 1mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride). Extracts were vortexed and then centrifuged at 16,000 g for 30 min at 4C to remove debris. Supernatants were collected for protein gel blot analysis. For the nuclear protein extraction, tissues without cross-linking were harvested and the nuclear pellets were collected as previously described ([Zhu](#page-14-10) [et al., 2012\)](#page-14-10). The procedures used for nuclei-enriched protein extraction from rice protoplasts followed the manufacture's protocol (Invent). For total protein extraction form yeast, yeast cells are harvested, resuspended in lysis buffer, and heated for 10 min at 90C. Then the pH of the lysate was adjusted to neutral by adding acetic acid, and heated again. The loading buffer was then added to the lysate. After centrifuging at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4C, supernatants were collected for immunoblotting.

Co-immunoprecipitation (coIP) experiment was performed as previously described [\(You et al., 2016\)](#page-14-9). Briefly, for enrichment of PIBP1-GFP, tissues without cross-linking were harvested and the nuclear pellets were collected as previously described ([Zhu](#page-14-10) [et al., 2012](#page-14-10)). Different combinations of supernatants were incubated with anti-GFP beads for 2 h at 4C and then washed four times with extraction buffer. The precipitates were released by boiling for 5 min in SDS loading buffer and immunoprecipitated proteins were analyzed by western blot. Antibodies were used for coIP and western blot below: Myc (1:2000), GST (1:1000), His (1:1000), GFP (1:2000), Histone H3 (1:2000), PEPC (1:2000), HA (1:2000), GAL4-AD (1:1000), GAL4-BD (1:1000). Actin was immunodetected (1:2000) as loading control. CoIP experiments were repeated two times independently.

#### Recombinant Protein Preparation

To construct the plasmids for recombinant protein production, the coding sequences of target proteins were PCR-amplified from cDNAs and inserted into pCOLD (*His-PIBP1*, *His-PIBP1*D*5-74*, *His-PIBP1-RRM*, *His*-*Os04 g53330*, *His*-*Os06 g02240*, *His*-*Os11 g34680*) or pGEX-6p-3 (*GST-PigmR-CC*) vector to create fusions with His and GST tags, respectively. The recombinant proteins were expressed in *E. coli* strain Rosetta (DE3) and induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl b-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) for 20 h at 18C. Bacterial cells were collected and affinity-purified with Ni-NTA agrose (QIAGEN), Glutathione Sepharose 4B (GE) and Amylose Resin (New England Biolabs), respectively, following manufacturer's instructions. Protein concentrations were determined using Coomassie brilliant blue R-250 staining with quantified BSA as a standard. To identify the recombinant protein purity, His-PIBP1 and His-PIBP1D5-74 were confirmed by western blot using anti-His antibody. Further, recombinant protein bands were excised from the CBB-stained gels, and matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight/time of flight (MALDI-TOF/TOF) mass spectrometry (MS) (Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co. Ltd) was performed to identify the recombinant proteins. All PCR primers are listed in [Table S3](#page-13-10).

#### Protein Pull-Down Assay

For *in vitro* pull-down assays, 30 mg *E. coli* produced fusion proteins were captured with Glutathione Sepharose 4B (GE) for GSTtagged PigmR-CC, GST served as a negative control. The PigmR-CC-GST beads were washed five times and incubated with 10 mg His-tagged PIBP1 at 4C for 2 h in binding buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 10% [v/v] glycerol). The beads were then washed five times with the washing buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 10% [v/v] glycerol, 0.1% [v/v] Triton X-100) to remove non-specifically bound proteins, the precipitates were released by boiling in SDS sample buffer at 100C for 5 min and detected by immunoblotting using corresponding antibodies. The protein pull-down assays were repeated two times independently.

#### ChIP-seq and ChIP-qPCR Analysis

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) followed by high-throughput sequencing (ChIP-seq) was performed following a previously reported protocol ([Zhu et al., 2012](#page-14-10)). Briefly, 3 g of two-week-old seedling leaves of *PIBP1*-GFP/NIL-*Pigm* and NIL-*Pigm* (negative control) were harvested at 36 h after infection with *M. oryzae* (TH12) and cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde. After quenching the reaction by adding glycine solution, the tissue was ground into fine powder, and chromatin was isolated and sheared into about 200-bp fragments by sonication. An anti-GFP antibody (Abcam) was added to the sonicated chromatin solution and incubated overnight at 4C. Immune precipitants were robustly washed to remove non-specifically bound DNA. Precipitated DNA fragments were then extracted with the Dr.GenTLE precipitation carrier (Takara) according to the manufacturer's procedures. Pooling DNA from three biological replicates of *PIBP1*-GFP/NIL-*Pigm* or NIL-*Pigm* (negative control) were collected, quality tested. Robust wash yielded insufficient DNA of negative control for sequence. ChIP-seq was then performed with Hiseq2500 by Beijing Genome Institute (BGI). After removing duplicate reads and reads mapping to multiple genomic locations, 10 million reads were successfully aligned to the rice reference genome. Putative binding sites were identified based on relative enrichment of different chromosomal regions as previously reported [\(Ji et al., 2008; Robertson et al., 2007\)](#page-13-22).

ChIP-qPCR was performed with three biological replicates and results were calculated as percentage of input DNA. Independent ChIP-qPCR experiments were performed twice with similar results. Primers used for ChIP-qPCR are listed in [Table S3.](#page-13-10)

#### Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay

The His-tagged recombinant proteins (His-PIBP1, His-PIBP1<sup>D</sup>5-74, His-PIBP1-RRM, His-Os04 g53330, His-Os06 g02240, His-Os11 g34680) were developed in *E. coli* Rosetta (DE3) and purified as described above. DNA fragments were end-labeled with Cy5. The fluorescence-labeled DNA (2 nM) was incubated with different amount (200 ng-800 ng) of purified protein in 20 mL binding system (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6, 25 mM KCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 0.6 mM BSA, 0.05 mg/ml poly [dI-dC] and 10% glycerol) at 25C for 30 min. For competition assays, 100-fold non-labeled competitor DNA was also added in the reaction. The reaction mixture was electrophoresed at 4C on a 6% native polyacrylamide gel in 0.5 3 Tris-borate-EDTA for 2 h at 100 V in the dark. Fluorescence-labeled DNA on the gel was then directly detected with FLA 2000 (Fuji). To identify the relative binding affinity of PIBP1 to the four DNA motifs, EMSA was normalized with quantitative protein and probes, and calculated the fraction of different labeled probes bound by PIBP1 with ImageJ.

For RNA EMSA, total RNAs extracted from water or *M. oryzae* (TH12)-inoculated NIL-*Pigm* plants were labeled with biotin using the RNA 3<sup>0</sup> End Biotinylation Kit following the manufacturer's instructions (Thermo Fisher). The labeled RNA was incubated with Histagged PIBP1 or His alone as a negative control. For competition assay, excess unlabeled RNA (100-fold) was added. The RNA EMSA was carried out with the Light Shift Chemiluminescent RNA EMSA Kit following the manufacturer's instructions (Thermo Fisher). Binding reaction was analyzed by gel electrophoresis on a native 6% polyacrylamide gel and detected by Nucleic Acid Detection Module Kit (Thermo Fisher). The EMSA experiments were repeated three times independently.

#### Transactivation Assay

For transactivation activity assay in yeast, the corresponding coding sequences of target genes were cloned into pDEST32 (Invitrogen) to fuse to the GAL4 binding domain. The pDEST32 empty vector was served as a negative control. All constructs were transformed into yeast strain AH109 and screened on selective medium (lacking either Leu or both Leu and His).

For transactivation assay in *N. benthamiana* leaves, we used a dual-Luc reporter system in which RENILLA (REN) was used as an internal control ([Hellens et al., 2005](#page-13-20)). The 3-kb promoter sequences of *OsWAK14* and *OsPAL1* were cloned into vector pGreenII 0800-Luc to generate *ProOsWAK14::Luc* and *ProOsPAL1::Luc* reporter constructs. *PIBP1*, *NLS-PIBP1* and *NLS-Os06 g02240* were cloned into vector pGreenII 62-SK, respectively, to produce different effectors. All plasmids were transformed into *Agrobacterium* strain GV3101, respectively, and transiently expressed in *N. benthamiana* as described above. The firefly luciferase and REN activity were measured following the manufacturer's instructions (Promega). The luciferase activity was calculated by normalizing the REN expression level. The transactivation assays were repeated three times independently.

#### RNA Preparation and Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) Analysis

Total RNAs were extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) from different rice tissues or leaves infected at different time points with *M. oryzae* isolate TH12 or YN2. RNAs were used for RNA-EMSA assays and for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover kit according to the manufacturer's procedures (TOYOBO). The qRT-PCR reactions

were performed with SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara) following the manufacturer's instructions using a CFX96 Real-time PCR Instrument (Bio-Rad). The rice *ACTIN1* was used as a control to normalize expression levels. The 2-DDCT method was used to calculate the relative expression levels with three technical or biological repeats. All qPCR primers are listed in [Table S3.](#page-13-10)

#### RNA Sequencing

Total RNAs were isolated from leaves of two-week-old seedlings (NIL-*Pigm* and *PIBP1*-RNAi/NIL-*Pigm*) after blast isolate TH12 incubation for 36 h using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA contents were measured by NanoDrop 2000c UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the quality of RNA samples was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent technologies). RNA sequencing was performed by Beijing Genome Institute (BGI).
