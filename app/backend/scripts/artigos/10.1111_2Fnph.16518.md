# Summary:

- Lamin proteins in animals are implicated in important nuclear functions, including chromatin organization, signaling transduction, gene regulation, and cell differentiation. Nuclear Matrix Constituent Proteins (NMCPs) are lamin analogues in plants, but their regulatory functions remain largely unknown.
  - We report that OsNMCP1 is localized at the nuclear periphery and induced by drought stress. Os*NMCP1* overexpression resulted in a deeper and thicker root system and enhanced drought resistance compared to the wild-type control. An assay for transposase accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq) analysis revealed that OsNMCP1-overexpression altered chromatin accessibility in hundreds of genes related to drought resistance and root growth, including *OsNAC10*, *OsERF48*, *OsSGL*, *SNAC1*, and *OsbZIP23*.
  - OsNMCP1 can interact with SWITCH/SUCROSE NONFERMENTING (SWI/SNF)

chromatin remodeling complex OsSWI3C. The reported drought resistance or root growth related genes that were positively regulated by OsNMCP1 were negatively regulated by OsSWI3C under drought stress conditions, and *OsSWI3C* overexpression led to decreased drought resistance.

We propose that the interaction between OsNMCP1 and OsSWI3C under drought stress conditions may lead to the release of OsSWI3C from the SWI/SNF gene silencing complex, thus changing chromatin accessibility in the genes related to root growth and drought resistance.

Keywords: Oryza sativa, OsNMCP1 protein, drought resistance, SWI/SNF, root architecture

# Introduction

Lamin proteins interact with integral proteins of the inner nuclear membrane, chromatin and DNA, provide a platform for the binding of proteins and chromatin and confer mechanical stability in chromatin organization and transcription, cell development and differentiation, and nuclear migration in metazoan cells (Stuurman *et al.*, 1998; Dittmer & Misteli, 2011). The lamina network is composed of lamin proteins which are grouped as A/C- and B-types on the basis of their biochemical characteristics (Gruenbaum *et al.*, 2003). A-type lamins are expressed in a tissue specific manner, and B-type lamins are essential for cell viability (Gruenbaum *et al.*, 2005). Lamin A/C acts as a modulator which interacts with regulatory elements to modulate gene expression in primary adipose stem cells (Lund *et al.*, 2013), and plays an important role in chromatin remodeling at the nuclear envelope during osteoblast differentiation (Markiewicz *et al.*, 2005; Frock *et al.*, 2006).

Nuclear Matrix Constituent Proteins (NMCPs) are the lamin analogues and the fundamental constituents of plant nuclei. The first NMCP protein identified in Daucus carota was predicted to have a typical lamin tripartite structure and was considered as the best candidate to fulfill the role of lamins in plants (Masuda et al., 1993; Ciska et al., 2013). NMCPs are coiled-coil proteins that form a nuclear peripheral framework, and widely exist in plants (Masuda et al., 1997; Inoue, 1997). In Allium cepa, AcNMCP1 is abundant in the root proliferating meristem and involved in the organization of multimeric complexes in the plant peripheral nucleoskeleton (Ciska et al., 2013). LITTLE NUCLEI (LINC) and CROWDED NUCLEI (CRWN) are the NMCP homologues. which are important architectural components of plant nuclear structure and play different roles in controlling nuclear size and morphology in Arabidopsis (Dittmer et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2013). CRWN3 co-localizes with ABA-INSENSITIVE5 (ABI5) in nuclear bodies and regulates seed germination by modulating ABI5 degradation (Zhao et al., 2016). CRWN genes play vital roles in plant immunity; since a crwn1 crwn2 double mutant is more resistant to virulent pathogens than wild-type (WT) plants (Guo et al., 2017). CRWN1 also interacts with chromatin protein to regulate gene expression (Mikulski et al., 2019) and plays critical role in modulating chromatin positioning patterns as a key component of the plant lamina-chromatin network (Hu *et al.*, 2019).

SWITCH/SUCROSE NONFERMENTING (SWI/SNF) ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes (CRCs) are conserved in eukaryotes including yeast, Drosophila, mammals, and plants (Abrams et al., 1986; Sarnowska et al., 2016). SWI/SNF CRCs move or restructure nucleosomes for DNA-histone contacts and play critical roles in the regulation of signal transduction, growth, and differentiation (Sarnowska et al., 2013; Zhu et al., 2013; Choi et al., 2015). The SWI/SNF CRCs promote chromatin condensation and mediate Arabidopsis transcriptional gene silencing (Jerzmanowski, 2007; Jing et al., 2016) and have been implicated in plants water stress responses (Han & Wagner, 2014). Chromatin remodeler (CHR) protein AtCHR12 mediates temporary growth arrest and has been suggested as a negative regulator in drought response in Arabidopsis (Mlynarova et al., 2007). AtBRM interacts with AtSWI3C and acts as a negative regulator in the abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathway. In addition, AtBRM directly represses the transcription of AtABI5, and brm mutant plants display enhanced drought tolerance (Han *et al.*, 2012). The *swi3c* mutant plants are sensitive to ABA, which is similar to the brm mutant plants (Sarnowska et al., 2013). Also, AtBRM directly targets the chromatin of PIN-FORMED (PIN) 1, PIN2, PIN3, PIN4, and PIN7 by altering PINs expression, with effects on both the cell division activity of root meristems and on cell elongation (Yang et al., 2015).

The plant root system is plastic and dynamic, and plays critical roles in nutrient and water uptake, storage, and transport (Gowda *et al.*, 2011). The root system architecture (RSA) consists of root traits including root volume, root length, root penetration ability, and these traits are controlled by numerous genes involved in diverse developmental processes and stress-responsive pathways (Qu *et al.*, 2008). A deep and thick RSA, may help plants to avoid drought stress by extracting water from deep soil layers (Hu & Xiong, 2014; Li *et al.*, 2017). For example, the rice gene *DEEPER ROOTING 1* (*DRO1*) is involved in the regulation of deep rooting by affecting the root growth angle, and it controls root cell elongation through a vertically deeper RSA in the rice cultivars with high adaptability to drought stress conditions (Uga *et al.*, 2013). Ethylene-responsive factor OsERF48 can directly bind to the promoter of the calmodulin (CAM)-like (CML) gene *OsCML16* thereby activating its transcription, causing longer primary roots and greater lateral root density, which contribute to increased drought resistance (Jung *et al.*, 2017).

2017). Root-specific overexpression of NAC (NAM, ATAF, and CUC2 family) transcription factor genes *OsNAC5*, *OsNAC9*, and *OsNAC10* in rice resulted in greater root diameter and RSA, and increased drought resistance and grain yield in field conditions (Jeong *et al.*, 2010; Redillas *et al.*, 2012; Jeong *et al.*, 2013).

In this study, we report that OsNMCP1, a lamin-like nuclear periphery protein in rice, confers drought resistance and modulates root growth. Further analysis showed that OsNMCP1 positively regulated the expression of genes related to root-growth and drought resistance. Interestingly, we found that OsNMCP1 can change chromatin state and physically interact with OsSWI3C, a subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeler complex that functions in gene silencing. We propose a previously unreported regulation mechanism in which the lamin-like protein OsNMCP1 can alter chromatin accessibility by interacting with the OsSWI3C-containing chromatin remodeler complex, to thus reduce the suppression effect of the complex.

# **Materials and Methods**

# Generation of transgenic rice plants

Rice (*Oryza sativa*) varieties Zhonghual1 (ZH11), Dongjing (DJ), and Huayoung (HY) were used as WT for the genetic materials. For *OsNMCP1*-OE construct, the full-length *OsNMCP1* coding region was amplified and inserted into the pCAMIA1301-flag (pCAMIA1301 (CAMBIA)) overexpression vector, which resulted in a fusion with FLAG tag. *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* (strain EHA105)-mediated transformation of ZH11 rice plants was conducted according to a published protocol (Lin & Zhang, 2005). Two T-DNA insertion lines, *osnmcp1-1* (PFG\_3A-60922, DJ background) and *osnmcp1-2* (PFG\_1C-01360.R, HY background), were obtained from the Postech rice mutant database (http://signal.salk.edu/cgi-bin/RiceGE). The *OsSWI3C*-OE lines (ZH11 background) were produced by using the same method as for generating the *OsNMCP1*-OE lines. The *OsSWI3C* RNA interference transgenic plants (ZH11 background) were produced by inserting the 500 bp C-terminal coding sequence of *OsSWI3C* into the ds1301 vector. The *Ubi:OsNMCP1:GFP* transgenic plants (ZH11 background) were produced by inserting the *OsNMCP1* coding region into the pH7WGF2.0 vector with a green fluorescent protein fused. The pH7WGF2.0 vector was transformed into rice as control. The full-length

OsNMCP1 driven by its native promoter was tagged with GFP at the C terminal end and introduced into the *osnmcp1-1* mutant callus to produce the *osnmcp1-1*- complemented plant.

#### **Plant growth and treatments**

For drought testing at the seedling stage, rice seeds were geminated on half-strength MS medium ( $\frac{1}{2}$ MS). Five days after germination, the seedlings were transplanted into pots filled with water-saturated soil. Each pot contained two genotypes (i.e, mutant line and WT) with an equal number (n = 12 plants per genotype) of plants for phenotypic observation, and at least three repeats were performed with two independent transgenic families. Plants at the four-leaf stage were stressed with drought by stopping watering for 7-10 days, and re-watered until all leaves rolled. After recovery, plants with green leaves and regenerating shoots were considered as survived plants and the survival rate (percentage of survived plants) was recorded. For drought stress testing at the reproductive stage, plants were planted in polyvinyl chloride (PVC) pipes, and the details of drought treatment and trait measurement were the same as described previously (Yue *et al.*, 2006; Xiao *et al.*, 2007).

For phytohormone treatments, seeds were germinated in  $\frac{1}{2}MS$  at 14 h: 10 h, light: dark cycles in glasshouse. Two days after germination, seeds with similar vigorous growth were transferred to normal  $\frac{1}{2}MS$  or  $\frac{1}{2}MS$  containing 0.02  $\mu$ M indole-3-acetic acid (IAA). After growth for seven days, primary root length was measured.

Seedling (25-day-old) roots from hydroponic culture were harvested for genomic DNA or total RNA extraction. 15-day-old seedlings were treated with 100  $\mu$ M ABA for three hours for checking gene expression levels. The seedlings were deprived from water supply for three hours as drought samples.

### **Protein interaction assay**

The yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay was performed using the ProQuest Two-Hybrid System (Invitrogen). The *OsSWI3C* coding region was cloned into the pDEST32 vector (BD) and *OsNMCP1* was cloned into the pDEST22 (AD) vector using Gateway recombination technology (Invitrogen). The AD and BD constructs were co-transformed into the Mav203 yeast strain and grown on the synthetic dropout medium minus Leu and Trp (SD-LT). To confirm the interaction,

the positive yeast clones were selected from the SD-LT medium and then grown on the 2×YPDA medium for three days, and then subjected to an X-gal assay according to the manufacturer's manual (Invitrogen).

Co-Immunoprecipitation assay (Co-IP) was performed in a rice protoplast system. The *OsSWI3C* coding regions was constructed in fusion with an N-terminal FLAG tag in pUGW11, and *OsNMCP1* was constructed in fusion with a C-terminal GFP tag in pUGW6. The procedure for rice protoplast isolation and transformation was the same as described previously (Xie *et al.*, 2015; Shen *et al.*, 2017). The total protein was extracted from the rice protoplasts for Co-IP assay as described previously (Tang *et al.*, 2016). The Co-IP samples were detected with anti-GFP (1:10000 dilution, ab290, Abcam) and anti-FLAG (1:10000 dilution, F3165, Sigma) antibodies by Western blot.

For bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay, the rice full-length *OsNMCP1* coding sequence was cloned into the pVYCE vector and *OsSWI3C* was cloned into the pVYNE vector according to (Waadt *et al.*, 2008). Rice shoot protoplasts were prepared as mentioned in Co-IP assays and 5 µg of each two fusion vectors were mixed and co-transformed into the rice protoplasts, together with Ghd7-CFP as a marker for nuclear localization. The fluorescence signal in the transformed protoplast was imaged by confocal laser scanning microscope (OLYMPUS, FV1200).

#### **Protein Extraction and Western Blot**

Total protein extractions of leaf and root were dissolved in lysis buffer as described previously (Zong *et al.*, 2016). For western blot, the samples were resolved by a 12% SDS-PAGE gel, and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 2% non-fat milk and probed with an anti-OsNMCP1 antibody (1:1000 dilution, Abmart, Shanghai) and anti-H3 (1:10000 dilution, ab1791, abcam). A Coomassie blue–stained gel image was shown as a loading control in the leaf tissue and anti-H3 was used in the root tissue.

### Quantification of endogenous IAA concentration

For the endogenous hormone level analysis, the roots of 25-day-old seedlings were ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen and 50 mg weighed into a 1.5-mL tube by extracting twice

with 700  $\mu$ L of extraction buffer [methanol:H2O:acetonitrile=90:9:1(v/v)]. Quantification was performed in an ABI 4000Q-TRAR LC-MS system (Applied Biosystems, USA) with stable-isotope-labelled IAA as an internal standard (OlChemIm, Czech Specials) by following the method as described previously (Liu *et al.*, 2012).

# RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and quantification of gene expression

Total RNA was extracted by TRIzol<sup>®</sup> reagent (AmbionTM, Lot No. 15596018) /chloroform extraction according to the manufacturer's instructions. Library construction and sequencing were performed by Novogene Inc (Tianjin, China) with HiSeq-PE150 (Illumina, Inc) using the paired-end sequencing strategy (150 bp for each end).

The total RNA (3 µg) were reverse-transcribed using the SuperScript reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Real time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Premix ExTaq (TaKaRa). Rice *ubiquitin* gene (LOC\_Os03g13170) was used for normalization. Three biological replicates were performed for each qRT-PCR analysis. Primers are listed in Supporting Data Set 1.

# Assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (ATAC-seq)

The roots of 25-day-old seedlings were splintered into pieces and nuclei were isolated using ice-cold buffer (15 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 20 mM NaCl, 80 mM KCl, 0.5 mM spermine, 3 mM DTT, 0.2% TritonX-100) then filtered with a 100  $\mu$ m filter. Nuclei were stained with DAPI and loaded into a flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson FACSCalibur). Nuclei were sorted and collected at 200,000 g in a 1.5-ml centrifuge tube containing lysis buffer. Nuclei were resuspended in 1× TD buffer (Illumina) and 4  $\mu$ L Tn5 transpose (Illumina) was added. The transposition reaction was performed in a thermocycler for 30 min at 37°C, and DNA was directly purified with a TaKaRa MiniBEST DNA Fragment Purification Kit (Potter *et al.*, 2018). DNA libraries were amplified for a total of eleven cycles as described (Buenrostro *et al.*, 2013), and the product purified with an AMPure® XP Beads Kit. The DNA samples were sequenced by Novogene Inc (Beijing, China) with Illumina HiseqXten-PE150 and a paired-end sequencing strategy.

# Sequencing data analysis and visualization

For RNA-seq analysis, the raw reads were filtered by fastp with the parameter '-q 30' (Chen

al., 2018) FastQC and checked by v0.11.8 et (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc) to ensure the exclusion of adaptor and low-quality sequences. The resulting reads were mapped to the rice RGAP 7 reference genome (Kawahara et al., 2013) using STAR v1.5.2 (Dobin et al., 2013) with default parameters. The alignments were further filtered (-b -f 2 -F 524 -q 5), sorted, and indexed by SAM tools v1.9 (Li et al., 2009) before the transcripts were assembled and counted by StringTie v1.3.6 (Pertea et al., 2015) according to the reference genome. Differential gene expression was determined by DESeq2 with the gene count matrix generated by StringTie at the threshold of |log<sub>2</sub>(Fold Change) $\geq 1$  and the adjusted P value <0.05. We downloaded two public RNA-seq datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database: GSE93081 for OsERF48 (Jung et al., 2017), GSE102921 for OsNAC10 (Jeong et al., 2010).

ATAC-seq analysis was conducted according to the recommended pipeline by ENCODE (https://www.encodeproject.org/atac-seq/). In detail, the filtered reads were mapped to the reference genome using Bowtie2 v 2.2.6 (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012) with parameters '--no-ulna --threads 8 --sensitive -k 1 -q'. Uniquely mapped and properly paired reads were deduplicated using Picard v2.60 (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Peaks in each biological replicate were called using MACS2 (version 2.1.0) (Zhang *et al.*, 2008) with parameters '--no model --shift -100 --Ext size 200'. For differential peak analysis, reads density of the three replicates within the region of peaks between different experiments was quantified and compared using DiffBind based on the DESeq2 method (Stark & Brown, 2011). The differential peaks were filtered out by the threshold of log<sub>2</sub>(Fold Change)  $\geq$ 0.5, FDR<0.05, P value <0.05, and the genomic features assigned to the peaks were annotated with ChIPSeeker (Yu *et al.*, 2015), with the definition of a promoter as the -3500 to +100 bp region in relation to the TSS.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was analyzed using the Virtual Plant database (http://virtualplant.bio.nyu.edu/) with the P value  $\leq 0.05$ . The enrichment result was visualized using R package GO plot (Walter *et al.*, 2015). To illustrate the sequence abundance of the transcripts and chromatin accessible region, wiggle tracks were generated using deep Tools (Zhou *et al.*, 2011), using the aligned reads and visualized using IGV (Robinson *et al.*, 2011). All

networks were plotted using Hiver (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/HiveR/index.html), and the gene symbols were extracted from the Rice Encode Project (Yao *et al.*, 2017).

The ATAC-Seq and RNA-seq data were deposited to the GEO database under accession number GSE136373.

## 5-Ethynyl-2'- deoxy uridine (EdU) staining and immunofluorescence staining

EdU staining was performed as described (Li *et al.*, 2015) by using an EdU kit (C10337; Apollo 488) from Life Technologies. Primary roots of 7-day-old *OsNMCP1*-OE, *osnmcp1* mutant, and corresponding WT seedlings were immersed in 10  $\mu$ M EdU solution for 10 h. The Edu florescence was captured with a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus FV1200).

For immunofluorescence staining, the root tips of OsNMCP1-GFP and GFP transgenic plants of 15-day-old seedlings were fixed for 30 min in 4% paraformaldehyde and then embedded in 5% agarose with a longitudinal vibration section with a thickness of 50 µm. The samples were blocked and subsequently incubated with the anti-GFP (ab290, Abcam)/anti-OsNMCP1/anti-FLAG antibody (1:1000 dilution), then with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated second anti-rabbit antibody (1:500 dilution, AS053, Aclinal) or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated second anti-mouse antibody (1:500 dilution, AS054, Aclinal). The cells were counterstained with Propidium Iodide (PI) and imaged by laser scanning confocal microscopy (Olympus FV1200).

# Results

### OsNMCP1 is a lamin-like nuclear periphery protein induced by drought stress

In our previous rice microarray analysis, a gene with high similarity to *NMCP1*, a candidate lamin analogue in plants (Ciska *et al.*, 2013), was induced by drought stress (Ning *et al.*, 2010). This prompted us to investigate the function of the gene (named *OsNMCP1*) in stress resistance, since lamin-like proteins in plants have seldom been reported in stress responses. First, we determined the OsNMCP1 subcellular location by transforming OsNMCP1 fused to GFP (*Ubi*:OsNMCP1:GFP) into rice. Immunofluorescence staining was performed in the roots of *Ubi*:OsNMCP1:GFP transgenic plants, the fluorescence was observed only in the nuclei (Fig. 1a-c). Close observation of single cells under high magnification showed that OsNMCP1 was

predominantly localized at the nuclear periphery (Fig. 1d-f). In the control GFP transgenic plants, the fluorescence was observed both in cytoplasm and nucleus (Fig. 1g-l). The results suggest that the lamin-like protein OsNMCP1 is localized at the nuclear periphery.

To further assess the *OsNMCP1* expression levels under different abiotic stresses, we performed qRT-PCR analyses, and the results showed that *OsNMCP1* transcript level was increased both in leaf and root tissues after ABA and drought stress treatments (Fig. 1m and 1n). Furthermore, Western blot analysis by an OsNMCP1-specific antibody (Fig. S1) showed that the OsNMCP1 protein level was also increased in leaf and root tissues under drought stress (Fig. 1o). Immunofluorescence staining was performed in ZH11 roots under ABA treatment, and the fluorescence was stronger compared to normal conditions with anti-OsNMCP1 antibody (Fig. S2). These results suggest that OsNMCP1 is responsive to drought stress and ABA in rice.

### **OsNMCP1** positively regulates drought resistance

To investigate whether *OsNMCP1* actually has a role in regulating drought resistance in rice, we generated *OsNMCP1* overexpression (OE) transgenic rice (ZH11 background) and confirmed the expression by qRT-PCR analysis, and two independent lines (*OsNMCP1*-OE-5 and *OsNMCP1*-OE-8) were selected for further analysis (Fig. 2a). Then we collected two allelic T-DNA insertion lines, *osnmcp1-1* (PFG\_3A-60922, DJ background) and *osnmcp1-2* (PFG\_1C-01360.R, HY background), from the Postech rice mutant database. The isolated flanking sequence confirmed the T-DNA insertions in the 5' untranslated region and the last exon, respectively (Fig. 2b). Next, we examined the performance of the *OsNMCP1*-OE and *osnmcp1* mutant plants under drought stress conditions at the seedling stage. The *OsNMCP1*-OE lines exhibited greater survival rates than the ZH11 after recovery from drought stress treatment (Fig. 2c) and 2e). On the contrary, the *osnmcp1* mutant lines showed increased sensitivity to drought stress treatment (Fig. 2d), and the survival rates of the *osnmcp1* mutant lines were lower than that of the DJ/HY after recovery(Fig. 2f). We repeated the testing three times and obtained the same results. These results suggest that *OsNMCP1* has a positive effect on drought resistance.

To further confirm the function of OsNMCP1 in drought resistance, drought stress treatment

was also applied at the reproductive stage, using the method described previously (Yue *et al.*, 2006). The *OsNMCP1*-OE lines showed increased drought resistance (Fig. 3a) and the *osnmcp1* mutant lines were hypersensitive to drought stress treatment (Fig. 3b). We did not observe a significant difference in water loss rate in detached leaves of WT and the transgenic plants so that we checked the root traits at the seed maturation stage. We measured maximum root length and root volume of these lines under normal and drought stress conditions. The *OsNMCP1*-OE lines had longer root length and greater root volume than the ZH11 under normal growth conditions, and the difference was more significant under drought stress conditions (Fig. 3c, 3e and 3f). The *osnmcp1* mutant lines exhibited significantly shorter root length and smaller root volume compared to the DJ/HY under both normal and drought stress conditions (Fig. 3d, 3g and 3h). These results together suggest that *OsNMCP1* plays a positive role in regulating drought resistance, which may be partially due to the increased root growth.

### **OsNMCP1** modulates root growth

We further examined the root phenotypes at the seedling stage. The *OsNMCP1*-OE lines had longer primary root length (Fig. 4a) and the *osnmcp1* mutant lines showed reduced primary root length (Fig. 4b) compared with the corresponding WT. The *OsNMCP1* transcript level was analyzed by qRT-PCR in the meristem (1–2 mm from the root tip), elongation (2–6 mm), and mature ( $\geq$ 6 mm) zones, and root caps ( $\leq$ 1 mm) (Fig. 4c), we found that the highest *OsNMCP1* expression level was detected in the elongation zone followed by the meristematic zone, while it was decreased in the mature zone and the root cap (Fig. 4d). To determine whether the root phenotype was due to differences in root cell elongation or division, longitudinal root sections of the *OsNMCP1*-OE-5, *osnmcp1-1* mutant, and corresponding WT plants were visualized by toluidine blue staining. The primary roots of *OsNMCP1*-OE-5 plant showed a longer meristem zone while the *osnmcp1-1* mutant line showed a shorter meristem zone than the corresponding WT plants (Fig. S3a). In the meristem and elongation zone, the *osnmcp1* mutant had significantly fewer cells (Fig. S3b-e). These findings indicated that OsNMCP1 may have influenced on cell numbers and cell size.

To further confirm that the cell division rate was affected in the *OsNMCP1*-OE and *osnmcp1* mutant roots, EdU was used to staining the plants roots. The EdU signal was obviously increased in the *OsNMCP1*-OE, while decreased in the *osnmcp1* mutant, compared with the corresponding WT (Fig. 4e). These data suggested that *OsNMCP1* may modulate root growth by regulating cell division. Since auxin controls root meristem size and the extent of cell division (Dello Ioio *et al.*, 2008), we measured the free IAA concentration in *OsNMCP1*-OE-5 and *osnmcp1-1* roots. The data showed that IAA was increased in *OsNMCP1*-OE-5, but was reduced in the *osnmcp1-1* mutant compared to the corresponding WT (Fig. 4f). The shortened root phenotype of the *osnmcp1* mutant plants can be partially recovered by low concentration treatment with IAA (Fig. S4a-c). These results suggested that the role of *OsNMCP1* in root growth may be partly related to auxin. Furthermore, the full-length OsNMCP1 genomic sequence, driven by its native promoter and fused with GFP at the C terminal end was introduced into the *osnmcp1-1* mutant. The *OsNMCP1* expression level and the primary root length phenotype were restored in the complemented line (Fig. S4d and S4e).

# OsNMCP1 regulates numerous genes related to root growth and drought response by changing chromatin accessibility

To examine whether *OsNMCP1* participates in the regulation of gene expression associated with chromatin status, we conducted integrative analyses of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq using the root tissues of *OsNMCP1*-OE-5 and ZH11 plants under normal and drought conditions. We observed high quality datasets and a high correlation between three biological replicates (0.80–0.99) of the ATAC-seq (Fig. S5 and S6a) and significant enrichment of *OsNMCP1*-OE sequencing reads in transcription start sites (TSS) comparing to ZH11 under both conditions (Fig. 5a). With the threshold of average absolute log<sub>2</sub>(Fold Change)  $\geq$ 0.5 and FDR <0.05, a total of 9588 and 15253 differentially enriched ATAC-seq peaks, which represented the differentially accessible regions (DARs), were identified under normal and drought conditions, respectively (Supporting Data Set 2). We further investigated the distribution pattern of the DARs among the genomic features, referring to the nearest flanking genes. It is noticeable that, in the *OsNMCP1*-OE plants,

the distribution of intensity increased DARs mainly enriched around the transcription start site (TSS) of the adjacent genes, while the distribution of intensity decreased DARs had no obvious pattern (Fig. 5b), indicating that the OsNMCP1-OE-caused chromatin accessibility increasing events mainly occurred in the TSS region. Furthermore, the number of increased DARs were significantly greater than that of decreased DARs under both normal (8791 vs 1524) and drought (12742 vs 2511) conditions (Fig. 5c and Supporting Data Set 2). In addition, statistics of the feature proportion in terms of the intensity increased DARs revealed a clear promotor-enriching pattern, in which over 65% of the peaks were assigned to the promotor region (defined as the -3500 to +100 bp regions to TSS under both conditions, with the highest annotation priority if overlapped with other features). We further examined differentially accessible genes (DAGs) with at least one intensity-increased peak in the promoter region, and 8934 and 8641 DAGs were identified under the normal and drought stress conditions respectively (Supporting Data Set 2). These results together suggest that OsNMCP1 may mainly function as a positive regulator of chromatin accessibility, and regulate the expression of downstream genes by altering the accessibility of their promoter regions.

To confirm this hypothesis, we performed RNA-seq analysis with sample from the same plants used for ATAC-seq to assess whether these changes in chromatin were related to changes in adjacent gene expression. With the threshold of absolute  $\log_2(\text{Fold Change}) \ge 1$  and the adjusted P value <0.05, we discovered 728 up-regulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and 434 down-regulated DEGs under normal conditions, while 3042 and 2696 DEGs, up and down-regulated respectively, were identified under drought condition (Supporting Data Set 3). Then, we integrated DAGs and up-regulated DEGs and found 362 and 619 common genes, accounting for 49.93% and 20.35% of the up-regulated DEGs, under normal and drought conditions, respectively (Fig. 5d). We defined these common genes as the promoter-accessibility and expression increased genes (PEIGs) under normal and drought conditions (NPEIGs and DPEIGs) by OsNMCP1 (Fig.5d and Supporting Data Set 4). GO enrichment analysis on the PEIGs and the DEGs revealed that all the gene sets were functionally related to stress responses (Fig. 5e), which was in accordance with the role of OsNMCP1 in drought resistance. It is also

noticeable that both PEIG sets, but not DEG sets, were enriched with the term of 'regulation of transcription'. We further investigated the PEIGs composition and found that 17.4% NPEIGs and 7.9% of DPEIGs encode putative transcription factors (TFs), which was the largest category except for the unclarified proteins, and several drought resistance related TFs such as OsERF48, OsNAC10, SNAC1, and OsbZIP23 were included (Fig. 5d and 5f and Supporting Data Set 4) (Hu et al., 2006; Xiang et al., 2008; Jeong et al., 2010; Jung et al., 2017). NPEIGs were also enriched in 'developmental process'. In addition to drought responsive genes, such as OsSGL, OsERF48, and OsNAC10 that also contribute to the regulation of root growth and architecture, several auxin-response genes including OsSAUR2 and OsSAUR12 were also included in the PEIGs (Supporting Data Set 4) (Jain et al., 2006; Jeong et al., 2010; Jung et al., 2017). This may at least partially explain the improved root growth of OsNMCP1-OE (Fig. 3e). To our surprise, only 27 genes were common between NPEIGs and DPEIGs (Fig. 5g), which may suggest that other key players may also be involved in the OsNMCP1-mediated chromatin accessibility regulation mechanism under drought stress conditions. In addition, eight drought-responsive DEGs including OsCML15, OsCML25, OsMSR2, OsASR1, OsASR2, OsASR3, OsNAC133, and OsNAC22 were confirmed by qRT-PCR in the seedling roots of OsNMCP1-OE-5, osnmcp1-1, and the corresponding WT plants, which was in accordance with the sequencing data (Fig. S7). These results suggested that OsNMCP1 may regulate these genes related to drought response and root growth.

It is worth noting that, beside the PEIGs, the remaining DEGs may also have potential functions in root growth and drought response, and they may be regulated by OsNMCP1 through indirect manners. We assessed the potential links between PEIGs and the remaining DEGs, by analyzing the public RNA-seq data on the transcription factor genes *OsNAC10* and *OsERF48* (Jeong *et al.*, 2010; Jung *et al.*, 2017). The two transcription factors can induce the expression of 114 (31.1%) and 50 (13.7%) of the DEGs that were not among the NPEIG set in the *OsNMCP1-OE* plants (Fig. S8 and Supporting Data Set 5), suggesting that OsNMCP1 may indirectly regulate these DEGs through the modulation of key transcription factor genes in the PEIG sets. Collectively, these results indicate that OsNMCP1 regulates root growth and drought

response by changing the chromatin accessibility of relevant genes.

# OsNMCP1 interacts with OsSWI3C, a subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex

It has been reported that chromatin remodeler AtSWI3B, can contact the nuclear matrix (Sarnowski et al., 2002). Considering the effect of OsNMCP1 overexpression on the landscape of chromatin accessibility, we conducted Y2H assay to determine whether OsNMCP1 interacts with any homologous component of the chromatin remodeling complexes, including OsSWI3A, OsSWI3B, OsSWI3C, OsSWI3D and OsSWI3E in rice. The results indicated that OsNMCP1 only interacts with OsSWI3C (Fig. 6a). To further confirm the interaction between OsNMCP1 and OsSWI3C, we performed BiFC and Co-IP assays using rice protoplasts. In BiFC assays, OsNMCP1-pVYCE and OsSWI3C-pVYNE were co-transformed with a nuclear marker protein OsGhd7-CFP into rice protoplasts. A strong yellow fluorescence signal was observed in co-expressing OsNMCP1-cYFP and OsSWI3C-nYFP, however, no signal was observed when each construct was co-expressed with an empty vector (Fig. 6b). In the Co-IP assays, 35S:OsNMCP1-GFP and 35S:OsSWI3C-Flag were transiently expressed in rice protoplasts. The transformation of 35S:GFP and 35S:OsSWI3C-Flag were used as negative control. Anti-GFP beads could successfully precipitate OsSWI3C-Flag only when OsNMCP1 was present, suggesting that OsNMCP1 could immunoprecipitate OsSWI3C in vivo (Fig. 6c). These data validated that OsNMCP1 can directly interact with OsSWI3C in rice.

# **OsSWI3C** negatively regulates drought resistance

To assess whether *OsSWI3C* is also involved in drought resistance, we generated *OsSWI3C* overexpression (3C-OE) and RNA interference (*3C-RNAi*) transgenic lines. We tested the *OsSWI3C*-OE, *OsSWI3C*-RNAi along with ZH11 plants for drought resistance at seedling stage. After recovery from drought stress treatment, the *OsSWI3C*-OE and *OsSWI3C*-RNAi lines showed significantly lower and higher survival rates, respectively, than ZH11, indicating a negative role of *OsSWI3C* in drought resistance (Fig. 7a-c). Immunofluorescence staining showed that OsSWI3C

was distributed in the nucleus under normal and ABA treatment conditions, and speckle-like distributions were observed in nucleoplasm but no signal was observed in the nucleolus (Fig. S9). These results suggest that OsSWI3C is a nuclear protein and has a negative effect on drought resistance.

Chromatin remodeling complexes were reported to participate in transcription regulation by altering chromatin accessibility (Clapier & Cairns, 2009). We further examined if suppression of OsSWI3C affected the chromatin accessibility of the genes in the OsNMCP1 overexpression line since OsSWI3C-RNAi plants also showed increased drought resistance. The ATAC-seq reads mapping profiles of the OsSWI3C-RNAi and OsNMCP1-OE on the genomic regions for the genes up-regulated in OsNMCP1-OE showed high similarity under drought conditions, with strong reads enrichment in the TSS region (Fig. 7b). However, under normal conditions, the reads in the OsSWI3C-RNAi showed an opposite distribution pattern to those in the OsNMCP1-OE, which was significantly less enriched in the TSS region (Fig. 7b). Of the 17925 DARs identified in the OsSWI3C-RNAi under drought condition, 15199 showed increased intensity, which was similar to the result in OsNMCP1-OE, indicating a negative role of OsSWI3C in chromatin accessibility under drought conditions (Supporting Data Set 6). Annotation of the intensity-increased DARs in the OsSWI3C-RNAi line revealed 10294 DAGs with at least 1 DAR assigned to promoter regions. Comparison of the DAGs in the OsSWI3C-RNAi with DPEIGs in the OsNMCP1-OE revealed 544 common genes (CoPEIGs), accounting for 87.88% of DPEIGs (Fig. 7e), indicating a co-regulatory relationship of OsNMCP1 and OsSWI3C. GO enrichment analysis of CoPEIGs resulted in no significantly enriched terms except for 'transcription regulation' since 45 TFs were included in this GO term (Supporting Data Set 7). To illustrate the potential link between the putative TF genes in CoPEIGs and the remaining DEGs in OsNMCP1-OE, strong co-expression relationships based on RNA-seq data with the Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.7 were extracted from Rice Expression Database (Xia et al., 2017). As shown in Fig. 7f, 884 and 726 co-expression relations were found between the TF genes and DEGs, involving 459 and 546 DEGs up and down-regulated, respectively. These results suggest that OsNMCP1 may act together with OsSWI3C in regulating the expression of downstream genes with diverse functions (Fig. 5e) under drought conditions.

To confirm the involvement of OsSWI3C in regulating gene expression, we examined the transcript levels of several drought-responsive or root growth related genes, which were up-regulated in the *OsNMCP1*-OE lines, in the *OsSWI3C*-OE and OsSWI3C-RNAi lines along with WT plants by qRT-PCR analysis. The results showed that the gene expression levels were not significantly altered in the *OsSWI3C*-OE plants under normal conditions compared to the ZH11 plants. However, the CoPEIGs like *OsERF48* and *OsbZIP23* showed higher fold-change in expression levels in the *OsSWI3C*-RNAi lines compared to ZH11 under drought condition than that under normal conditions (Fig. S10). These results together indicate that OsSWI3C plays a negative role in drought resistance by suppressing the expression of drought resistance or root growth -related genes.

#### Discussion

#### **OsNMCP1** is localized at the nuclear periphery and modulates chromatin accessibility

In our study, we found that OsNMCP1 was located in the nuclear periphery (Fig. 1), which agrees with the localization as CRWN and lamins (Dittmer & Misteli, 2011), and overexpression of *OsNMCP1* in rice increased chromatin accessibility (Fig. 5). However, the peripheral structure has proven to be generally gene-poor and is regarded as a repressive environment for chromatin in animals (Dittmer & Misteli, 2011). Similarly, in *Arabidopsis*, heterochromatic domains are enriched at the nuclear periphery and CRWN1 directly interacts with repressed chromatin domains that play critical roles in modulating patterns of chromatin positioning at the nuclear periphery (Bi *et al.*, 2017; Hu *et al.*, 2019). Thus, our results imply that the nuclear periphery-localized OsNMCP1 may have different functions from CRWNs and lamin proteins in up-regulating gene expression. Nuclear periphery has been proven to be an important layer involved in the regulation of genome topology. In the previous studies, Rabl, Rossette, and Bouquet structures were identified as three major chromosome configurations involved in the genomic topology of plant, and Rabl conformation tends to localize in the nuclear periphery (Rodriguez-Granados *et al.*, 2016). However, until now, no obvious Rabl configuration has been identified in rice (Zhou *et al.*, 2019), indicating potential diversity in the genomic topology pattern in different species. Recent

studies revealed that rice chromatin packing patterns are different from *Arabidopsis* both at the chromosomal and local levels, with the chromosomal differences associated with the distinct epigenomic features in rice, which implies different molecular mechanisms underpinning gene regulation and genome stability (Liu *et al.*, 2017; Dong *et al.*, 2018). Furthermore, the active and inactive compartments form larger units in discrete locations and arranged in the nucleus, and the heterochromatic domains are mosaicked in the active and inactive regions in rice (Zhou *et al.*, 2019). Rice displays its own three-dimensional (3D) genome patterns of chromosomal compartment folding and spatial distribution distinguished from mammalian 3D genomes (Zhou *et al.*, 2019; Zhao *et al.*, 2019). Therefore, the nuclear periphery and heterochromatic domains may have no direct relationships in rice, and it is reasonable that nuclear periphery protein OsNMCP1 modulates chromatin accessibility to up-regulate gene expression.

# OsNMCP1 promotes root growth and enhances drought resistance as a regulator in genes expression related to chromatin accessibility

Plants have evolved a wide range of intricate mechanisms for coping with drought stress (Lafitte *et al.*, 2007). Our result showed that hundreds of genes involved in processes related to stimulus responses and transcriptional regulation pathways were enriched among up-regulated genes in the *OsNMCP1*-OE line under both normal and drought conditions (Fig. 5 and supporting Data Set 5). A significant proportion of genes related to root growth and development were up-regulated genes in the *OsNMCP1*-OE line, suggesting the potential contribution of OsNMCP1 to root growth, which was also supported by the phenotype of *OsNMCP1*-OE (Fig. 3 and Fig. 4). Improved root growth may be one of the causes for the increased drought resistance of the *OsNMCP1*-OE plants, since it is a common strategy for plants to grow larger roots for better water uptake in the coming water shortage environment.

As revealed by ATAC-seq and RNA-seq analyses, about half of the up-regulated genes in the *OsNMCP1* overexpression line showed increased chromatin accessibility, mainly in their promoters. Among the PEIGs, the proportion of TF genes was obviously higher than other categories, so we speculate that OsNMCP1 regulates downstream gene related to root growth and

drought stress responses by mainly changing the chromatin accessibility and the expression levels of key TF genes. For example, it has been reported that OsERF48 and OsNAC10, two TFs in the NPEIG set, regulate both root growth and drought resistance. By integrating the available RNA-seq data for these merely two TFs, we found that almost one third of the regulatory relationships between NPEIGs and DEGs can be explained (Supporting Data Set 5). Beside these TFs, there were three genes encoding COBRAs that function in root cell expansion in *Arabidopsis* (Schindelman *et al.*, 2001; Roudier *et al.*, 2002) and two SAUR-encoding genes that are possibly related to auxin regulated root growth (Fig. S6c). The OsSAURs play major roles in plant growth and development, and are involved in auxin-mediated growth (Jain *et al.*, 2006; Ren & Gray, 2015). In addition, *OsSGL* also functions in the auxin response pathway and regulates root development and hormone response genes, and overexpressing *OsSGL* improved drought tolerance significantly (Jeong *et al.*, 2010; Cui *et al.*, 2016).

### OsSWI3C plays an important role in OsNMCP1-mediated gene expression regulation

The SWI/SNF chromatin remodeler subunit OsSWI3C physically interacts with OsNMCP1 (Fig. 6). Previous studies verified that the SWI/SNF-type complexes are involved in transcriptional control of key developmental processes, growth, and stress responses in plants (Archacki *et al.*, 2017). SWI/SNF remodeler complexes are targeted to particular nucleosomes and change the positioning, occupancy, density, and composition of nucleosomes by modulating the developmental phase transitions and stress responses (Kasten *et al.*, 2011; Han *et al.*, 2015). Our study showed that OsSWI3C had opposite effect on drought response compared to OsNMCP1, since OsSWI3C overexpression decreased drought resistance while suppression of *OsSWI3C* increased drought resistance (Fig. 7). Interestingly, increases in TSS accessibility of the OsNMCP1-up-regulated DEGs in the *OsSWI3C*-RNAi mainly occurred under drought stress conditions, but not under normal conditions, suggesting that OsSWI3C may be involved in the OsNMCP1-mediated gene regulation mainly under drought conditions (Fig. 7d). In fact, there were only 27 overlapped genes between NPEIGs and DPEIGs, but 88% of DPEIGs were overlapped with the promoter accessibility increased DAGs in the *OsSWI3C*-RNAi line. It is

possible that OsNMCP1 may have other unknown regulation mechanism under normal conditions.

In the previous studies, the SWI/SNF remodeler AtBRM and AtCHR12 have been suggested as negative regulators in drought stress response pathway (Mlynarova *et al.*, 2007; Archacki *et al.*, 2009; Han *et al.*, 2012). BRM causes high occupancy of the nucleosome and acts as a barrier for transcription (Yen *et al.*, 2012). In particular, the SWI/SNF CHR subunit BAF60 has been reported to have dual regulatory functions on epigenome modification and chromatin architecture. BAF60 exhibits histone deacetylase activity and can regulate the deposition of H3K27me3 and H3K9Ac histone marks, and it can directly bind and transcriptionally suppress downstream genes (Jegu *et al.*, 2014; Jegu *et al.*, 2015; Jegu *et al.*, 2017). This knowledge together with our results suggested that OsSWI3C may be a conserved negative regulator of chromatin structure and gene expression.

Based on the fact that SWI/SNF chromatin-remodeling complexes remodel chromatin condensation (Zhu et al., 2013) and our results in this study, we propose a model to describe the OsNMCP1-OsSWI3C interaction mechanism in root growth and drought resistance regulation (Fig. 8). Drought stress can induce OsNMCP1 expression, and the increased OsNMCP1 protein level may lead to more non-periphery OsNMCP1 to 'meet' with OsSWI3C. The interaction of OsNMCP1 with OsSWI3C might remove OsSWI3C from the SWI/SNF complex, partially relaxing the condensed chromatin, and thus modulate the expression of functionally important genes including transcription factors. Some of these transcription factors may further regulate the expression of downstream genes related to root growth and drought response. It should be noted that there was a proportion of OsNMCP1-OE DAGs that did not overlap with the OsSWI3C-RNAi DAGs. This may be partially explained by the 'Cat's cradling' model which propose that the initial transcription events may sever as targets for subsequent binding of lamins or other nuclear organizing proteins to these sites, leading to nuclear architecture reorganization and activation of gene expression on other loci (Mele & Rinn, 2016). Thus, if similar mechanism exists in rice, it is possible that the interaction between OsNMCP1 and OsSWI3C may activate certain CoPEIGs to serve as initial transcriptional events for the triggering of nuclear architecture reorganization. And as a result of the nuclear architecture alteration, the accessibility of thousands of DAGs may be

changed in the *OsNMCP1*-OE plants. Future studies focusing on the nuclear architecture regulatory role of OsNMCP1 may shed light on its complete mechanism.

# Conclusions

In conclusion, our results reveal that *OsNMCP1* has an important function in rice root growth and drought resistance, which involves an unreported gene regulation mechanism that the lamin-like protein interacts with SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complexes to reduce the effect of the complex on gene silencing under drought stress conditions. The new function of a plant lamin-like gene regulating drought resistance may provide a new mechanism for engineering and breeding drought-resistant crops.
