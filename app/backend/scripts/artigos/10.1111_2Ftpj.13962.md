Cytokinin plays diverse roles in plant growth and development, generally acting by modulating gene transcription in target tissues. The type-B ARR transcription factors have emerged as primary targets of cytokinin signaling and are required for essentially all cytokinin-mediated changes in gene expression. The diversity of cytokinin function is likely imparted by the activity of various transcription factors working with the type-B ARRs to alter specific sets of target genes. One potential set of co-regulators modulating the cytokinin response are the BARLEY B-RECOMBINANT/BASIC PENTACYSTEINE (BBR/BPC) family of plant-specific transcription factors. Here, we show that disruption of multiple *BPCs* results in reduced sensitivity to cytokinin. Further, the BPCs are necessary for the induction of a subset of genes in response to cytokinin. We identified direct in vivo targets of BPC6 using ChIP-Seq and found an enrichment of promoters of genes differentially expressed in response to cytokinin. Further, a significant number of BPC6 regulated-genes are also direct targets of the type-B ARRs. Potential *cis*-binding elements for a number of other transcription factors linked to cytokinin action are enriched in the BPC binding fragments, including those for the Cytokinin Response Factors (CRFs). In addition, several BPCs interact with a subset of type-A ARRs. Consistent with these results, a significant number of genes whose expression is altered in *bpc* mutant roots are also mis-expressed in crf1,3,5,6 and type-A arr3,4,5,6,7,8,9,15 mutant roots. These results suggest that the BPCs are part of a complex network of transcription factors that are involved in the response to cytokinin.

# Introduction

Cytokinin is a plant hormone that regulates diverse processes, including plant growth and stem cell maintenance, source sink relationships, vascular development, and biotic and abiotic interactions (Argueso *et al.* 2009, Kieber and Schaller 2014, Mok and Mok 2001, Werner and Schmülling 2009). Cytokinin acts primarily by altering gene expression, presumably by targeting distinct sets of genes in different cell types. The type-B ARRs mediate the primary transcriptional response to cytokinin, acting in concert with other transcription factors to generate distinct outcomes in different cellular contexts. Other transcription factors that have been implicated in the cytokinin-

regulated transcriptional network include TGA3, WUS, TCPs, CRFs, and DELLAs (reviewed in: (Argueso *et al.* 2010, Kieber and Schaller 2018)). The BARLEY B-RECOMBINANT/BASIC PENTACYSTEINE (BBR/BPC) family of transcription factors have also been linked to cytokinin (Simonini and Kater 2014). These plant-specific transcription factors bind to GA-dinucleotide repeat DNA sequences (Meister *et al.* 2004, Monfared *et al.* 2011, Sangwan and O'Brian 2002, Santi *et al.* 2003). In Arabidopsis, the BPC family consists of six genes and one pseudogene (*BPC5*) that fall into three classes based upon phylogeny and sequence similarity at their C-terminus: Class I (*BPC1*, *BPC2*, and *BPC3*); Class II (*BPC4*, *BPC5*, and *BPC6*); Class III (*BPC7*) (Meister *et al.* 2004, Monfared *et al.* 2011). Although the N-termini differ, all BPC members share the highly conserved BASIC PENTACYSTEINE (BPC) domain at their C-terminus, which is required for DNA-binding (Santi *et al.* 2003, Wanke *et al.* 2011). The BPCs interact with Polycomb Repressive Complex 1 (PRC1) proteins, which form protein complexes that remodel chromatin by modifying histone tails to repress transcription of genes (Mu *et al.* 2017; Xiao et al., 2017). Furthermore, BPC6, a class II BPC, interacts with a member of the PRC1 complex, LIKE HETEROCHROMATIN 1 (LHP1) and recruits LHP1 to GAGA binding motifs and the PRC2 member Vernalization 2 (VRN2) was shown to associate with BPC6 via LHP1 in vivo (Hecker et al. 2015). The BPCs likely also regulate transcription via PRC1/2 complex independent mechanisms (Hecker *et al.* 2015, Simonini *et al.* 2012). Furthermore, *in vitro* experiments suggest that binding of BPC1 results in the bending of DNA in *STK* promoter, which is likely facilitated by the ability of BPC1 to oligomerize and bind to multiple GA stretches in the *STK* promoter (Kooiker *et al.* 2005, Simonini *et al.* 2012).

The BPC proteins act redundantly to regulate plant development, as the higher order *bpc* mutants display a variety of reproductive and vegetative defects, while single and double mutants display no or less severe phenotypes (Berger and Dubreucq 2012, Kooiker *et al.* 2005, Monfared *et al.* 2011, Santi *et al.* 2003). It has been suggested that the BPCs function at least in part through regulation of phytohormone levels and/or signaling (Monfared *et al.* 2011). For example, the *bpc1,2,4,6* mutant has an altered response to ethylene, though it is unaffected in the response to auxin and gibberellic acid (Monfared *et al.* 2011). BPCs also regulate abscisic acid signaling by regulating

transcription of *ABI4* (Mu *et al.* 2017). Although BPCs are linked to ethylene and abscisic acid (ABA) signaling, not all of the phenotypes observed in the *bpc1,2,4,6* mutants can be attributed to alterations in the function of these hormones and thus, the BPCs may regulate multiple hormonal pathways (Monfared *et al.* 2011).

Previous studies have linked BPC transcription factors to cytokinin signaling in the meristem (Simonini and Kater 2014). The *bpc1,2,3, bpc1,2,3,4,6*, and *bpc1,2,3,4,6,7* mutants exhibit a hyperproliferative meristem, reminiscent of cytokinin oxidase mutants, which have increased cytokinin levels (Bartrina *et al.* 2011, Monfared *et al.* 2011). Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays with an antibody specific to Class 1 BPCs found that in inflorescence tissue, the BPCs bind to the promoters of some cytokinin-regulated genes, including *ARR7*, *WUSCHEL (WUS)*, and *SHOOTMERISTEMLESS (STM)* (Leibfried *et al.* 2005, Rupp *et al.* 1999, Simonini and Kater 2014). Furthermore, the expression of *IPT7*, which encodes an enzyme involved in cytokinin biosynthesis, is elevated in the inflorescence of *bpc1,2,3* mutants (Simonini and Kater 2014).

The cytokinin signaling cascade is similar to two-component signaling systems found in prokaryotes (Kieber and Schaller 2014, Stock *et al.* 2000). Cytokinin binds to the Arabidopsis histidine kinase (AHK) receptors and induces autophosphorylation on a conserved histidine residue. This phosphate is then transferred to an aspartic acid residue in the receiver domain of the AHK, then to a histidine residue of the Arabidopsis histidine-containing phosphotransfer proteins (AHPs) (Hutchison *et al.* 2006), and finally to an aspartic acid residue in the receiver domain of the type-B Arabidopsis response regulators (ARRs) (Ishida *et al.* 2008, Mason *et al.* 2005, Yokoyama *et al.* 2007). This phosphorylation results in the binding of the type-B ARRs to DNA, where they regulate the expression of cytokinin primary-response genes (Zubo *et al.* 2017), including the type-A *ARRs*, which are single domain response regulators lacking a DNA binding domain.

Here, we further explore the links between the BPC proteins and cytokinin signaling. We demonstrate that disruption of multiple BPCs results in altered cytokinin responsiveness and that the BPCs regulate a sub-set of cytokinin response genes. These results suggest that the BPCs are part of the complex combinatorial gene regulatory network that mediates changes in transcription in response to cytokinin.

### Results

# bpc mutants exhibit reduced sensitivity to cytokinin

To determine if the BPCs function in cytokinin signaling, we performed cytokinin response assays on *bpc* mutants. Exogenous cytokinin inhibits primary root growth (To *et al.* 2004). The *bpc1,2,3,4,6* and *bpc1,2,3,4,6,7* multiple mutants exhibit reduced sensitivity to cytokinin in this assay, with the largest difference from the wild-type occurring at the lower doses of cytokinin (25 nM and 50 nM benzyladenine (BA); Figure 1; Figure S1). Reduction in cytokinin sensitivity of the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* and *bpc1,2,3,4,6,7* mutants was not as pronounced as that observed with the *ahk2,4* cytokinin double receptor mutant. In contrast, the lower order *bpc1,2, bpc1,2,3, bpc4,6* and *bpc1,2,4,6* mutants did not show reduced cytokinin sensitivity at any tested dose (Figure 1b; Figure S1a). These results suggest that the BPCs have a redundant, positive effect on cytokinin sensitivity in Arabidopsis roots.

# A sub-set of cytokinin response genes are mis-regulated in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant

We employed RNA-Seq to determine genes that are differentially expressed in a *bpc* multiple mutant. We chose the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant because it had the most severely altered cytokinin response in root elongation assays (Figure 1; Figure S1). Since we observed cytokinin response phenotypes in the root, we used ten-day old wild-type or mutant roots for RNA-Seq. We identified 920 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant compared to wild type in the absence of exogenous cytokinin (false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, FC>1.5 or FC<0.66; Table S1). The *bpc2* and *bpc6* alleles are T-DNA insertions and appear to be null alleles as both display only background levels of sequencing counts from the RNA-Seq, (Figure S2a, b). The T-DNA for *bpc1-1* is inserted in the 5' UTR of the gene and previous transcript analysis of *bpc1-1* suggested that this allele is not a null as RT-PCR indicated 10% residual transcription (Monfared *et al.* 2011, Simonini and Kater 2014). However, we find that in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant roots, *BPC1* sequencing counts are only at the level of background, comparable to the level of the *BPC2* and *BPC6* in this line (Figure S2a, b), suggesting that *bpc1-1* is a transcript null allele, at least in *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant roots. BPC6, together with other BPCs, may positively regulate *BPC1* expression as it binds in the *BPC1* promoter region (Figure S2a) and therefore, the disruption of BPC6 in *bpc1,2,3,4,6* could lead to a further reduction in

*BPC1* expression in this mutant. Alternatively, the allele may have a more substantial effect on *BPC1* expression in the root. The *bpc4* allele results from a T-DNA insertion that occurs just prior to the DNA-binding domain of BPC4. We find that there is little or no *BPC4* transcript 3' to this insertion, but wild-type levels of *BPC4* transcript upstream of this insertion (Figure S2a, b); therefore, it is possible that the N-terminal protein-protein interaction domain of BPC4 is still expressed. The *bpc3-1* allele used is derived from a TILLING population (McCallum *et al.* 2000) that results in an early stop codon in the DNA binding domain. *BPC3* expression is two-fold higher in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant, which is consistent with the previous findings that *BPC3* expression is elevated in a *bpc1,2,4,6* mutant (Monfared *et al.* 2011).

To determine if the BPCs play a role in the transcriptional response to cytokinin, we conducted an RNA-Seq experiment with wild type and bpc1,2,3,4,6 mutant roots treated with exogenous cytokinin. There were 1,812 DEGs in wild-type roots in response to cytokinin (FDR <0.05; FC $\geq$ 1.5 or FC $\leq$ 0.66) and 2,004 DEGs in *bpc1*,2,3,4,6 roots in response to cytokinin (FDR < 0.05; FC $\geq$ 1.5 or FC $\leq$ 0.66). There were 1,312 genes that overlap between those differentially expressed in response to cytokinin in wild-type and *bpc1.2,3,4,6* mutant roots, which indicates that the response of many genes to cytokinin is not eliminated by disruption of these BPCs (Figure 2a; Table S2). The relative fold-change of these overlapping 1,312 genes is comparable in wild-type and *bpc* mutant roots (Figure 2b), suggesting that disruption of these BPCs does not dampen the relative change in the expression of these genes in response to cytokinin. However, there were 500 cytokinin response genes that are no longer differentially expressed in response to cytokinin in the bpc1,2,3,4,6 mutant (Figure 2a; Table S1), and also 692 DEGs in response to cytokinin in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant that were not significantly differentially expressed in wild-type roots (Figure 2a; Table S1). These latter genes are likely ones negatively regulated by BPCs in wild-type-roots that are now able to be induced upon disruption of the BPCs. Of the 500 genes no longer differentially expressed in response to cytokinin in the bpc1,2,3,4,6 mutant, 58 are differentially expressed in mutant the absence of cytokinin as compared to the wild type. Altogether, there are 1,986 genes that are differentially

expressed in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* in the presence and absence of cytokinin treatment as compared to wild-type roots (Table S1).

To validate the RNA-Seq results, we used qRT-PCR on a group of genes that showed an altered response to cytokinin in the bpc1,2,3,4,6 mutant. To identify candidates for re-testing, we used a fold-change cut off for genes induced by cytokinin in the wild type that was greater than 1.5. Of the four genes that were re-tested, three were confirmed as being induced by cytokinin in wild-type roots, but to be unaffected by cytokinin in the bpc1,2,3,4,6 mutant (Figure 2c). *ARR12* (a type-B ARR) and *ATL78* showed no response to cytokinin in the mutant; in the case of *ACS7* (involved in ethylene biosynthesis), the expression was constitutively elevated but not further induced upon cytokinin treatment. The expression of the fourth gene (*MYC2*) was higher in the bpc1,2,3,4,6 mutant in the absence and presence of cytokinin in the RNA-Seq analysis; However, upon re-testing, *MYC2* expression is not different in the absence of cytokinin, but is more highly induced by cytokinin in the mutant.

# Identification of BPC6 target genes using ChIP-Seq

Directed ChIP assays showed that the BPCs bind to the promoters of a number of cytokinin-regulated genes (*ARR7*, *STM*, *WUS*, and *BP/KNAT1*) (Simonini and Kater 2014) and our analysis demonstrates that the BPCs are required for the regulation of a subset of genes by cytokinin. To determine the binding sites for BPC6 *in vivo* across the genome, we used chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-Seq). We chose BPC6 for this analysis because, while multiple transcriptional targets of Class I BPCs have been identified in the inflorescence meristem, fewer target loci have been determined for the Class II BPCs (Simonini and Kater 2014). We used transgenic Arabidopsis plants expressing *GFP:BPC6* (*pBPC6::GFP:BPC6*) in a *bpc6* mutant background for the ChIP-Seq analysis, with *GFP* (*pBPC6::GFP*) as a negative control, both under the control of the endogenous *BPC6* promoter and terminator. We analyzed the localization of the recombinant proteins in epidermal leaf tissue of the transgenic lines (Figure S3a) and detected GFP fluorescence from *GFP:BPC6* expressing plants in the nucleous and nucleolus. The localization in the nucleoplasma and the nucleolus is consistent with previous results (Hecker *et al.* 2015, Wanke *et* 

*al.* 2011) and suggests a potential role for BPCs in transcription of genes encoding ribosomal proteins; *GFP* expressing control plants displayed GFP fluorescence in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus, but not in the nucleolus (Figure S3a), consistent with prior results (e.g. Punwani *et al.* 2010).

We performed ChIP-Seq on 14-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings and identified 3,581 putative BPC6 *in vivo* target loci from the *GFP:BPC6* expressing plants that were not found from a GFP control line (Table S3). The list of 3,581 peaks was determined by comparing the overlap between the two biological replicates of *GFP:BPC6* expressing plants and *GFP* expressing control plants (Figure S3b). We analyzed the distance distribution of all reads to the nearest TSS and found the majority of peaks were found within 1,000 base pairs on either side of the transcription start sites (TSSs) (Figure S3c). Furthermore, to determine the location of the GFP:BPC6 binding sites relative to the annotated gene models, we analyzed the distribution of all peaks over the entire Arabidopsis genome (Figure S3d). The majority (over 80%) of binding sites were located within the promoters of genes, consistent with a role in transcriptional regulation.

The identified peaks in our ChIP-Seq experiment defined 3,312 direct putative target genes of GFP:BPC6 that were determined by the nearest gene location (Table S4). Consistent with the localization to the nucleolus, 18 genes encoding ribosomal proteins are putative BPC6 targets (Table S5) (Barakat *et al.* 2001, Chang *et al.* 2005, Sormani *et al.* 2011). Other targets include *BPC1*, *BPC2*, *BPC4*, and *BPC6* (but not *BPC3*, *BPC5*, or *BPC7*), indicating that BPC6, likely together with other BPCs, transcriptionally regulates a sub-set of *BPC* genes (Figure S2; Table S4). Homeobox genes identified as BPC1 binding targets from other studies, including *STK*, *STM*, and *KNAT1/BP* (Kooiker *et al.* 2005, Simonini and Kater 2014, Simonini *et al.* 2012), were also identified as direct targets of BPC6 by our ChIP-Seq analysis.

To verify the ChIP-Seq results, we examined binding of GFP-BPC6 to *BPC1*, *BPC2* and *BPC6* using chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR). We also verified the binding of BPC6 to the promoter of the PcG protein *EMBRYONIC FLOWER 1* (*EMF1*) gene based on previous studies that link BPCs with PcG function (Hecker *et al.* 2015, Santi *et al.* 2003). To verify binding of GFP:BPC6 to these loci, we generated one amplicon covering the putative binding site for each gene derived from our ChIP-Seq experiment and one amplicon covering a

second non-binding region within the same locus (Figure S4). We confirmed *in vivo* binding of GFP:BPC6 to the 5'UTRs of *BPC1* (Figure S4a), *BPC2* (Figure S4b) and *EMF1* (Figure S4c). We could not confirm the *in vivo* binding of GFP:BPC6 to its own promoter, likely due to highly repetitive sequences within the predicted binding site (Figure S4d). Furthermore, we compared the BPC6 target genes to BPC1 target genes identified using ChIP-Seq in germinating embryos (Xiao *et al.* 2017). Overall, there was a significant overlap between the target genes (Hypergeometric probability test P(X>=362) = 2.2e-14; Figure S5a), even though the ChIP-Seq experiments were performed on different tissues at different developmental stages with different BPC proteins, which is consistent with the functional redundancy of the BPCs. These overlapping genes included previously described BPC targets like *STM*, *KNAT1* and *STK*, as well as *EMF1* which was confirmed as a BPC6 target with ChIP-qPCR (Figure S4c).

To further investigate BPC function, we examined the genes identified as direct BPC6 targets using gene ontology (GO) analysis with agriGO GO terms with Biological Process (Table S6) (Du et al., 2010). The *bpc* mutants exhibit reproductive and vegetative phenotypes, which correspond to the enrichment of GO terms related to the development of these tissues (Table S6). The BPC6 target genes are also enriched for terms related to the response to abscisic acid, auxin, jasmonic acid, ethylene and cytokinin, further supporting their role as regulators of hormone signaling.

To further study the DNA-binding properties of BPC6, we analyzed all ChIP-Seq reads for enrichment of *cis*-elements using the DREME algorithm followed by Tomtom (Bailey 2011, Gupta *et al.* 2007). The most frequent motif identified was 'KAGAGAGA' (e-value = 7.3e-233) (Figure 3a, Figure S6), which resembles the previously identified *in vitro* BPC binding site (Brand *et al.* 2010, Fischer *et al.* 2016, Hecker *et al.* 2015, Kooiker *et al.* 2005, Meister *et al.* 2004, Simonini *et al.* 2012, Theune *et al.* 2017). We also found binding motifs for the TCP family of transcription factors enriched in the BPC ChIP-Seq reads ('GTGGKS' e-value = 2.0e-009; 'CCATTW' e-value = 7.0e-009; Figure S6). The TCPs are non-canonical bHLH transcription factors specific to plants that have also been implicated in the cytokinin response (Efroni *et al.* 2013, Koyama *et al.* 2010, Nicolas and Cubas 2016, Steiner *et al.* 2012, Steiner *et al.* 2016). Interestingly, we also found that there is enrichment of the cytokinin response factor (CRF) binding site 'GCCRY' (e-value = 2.3e-021; Figure

S6; (Raines *et al.* 2016)). The CRFs are a group of AP2/ERF transcription factors that are transcriptionally induced by cytokinin via type-B ARRs (Raines *et al.* 2016). The CRFs are involved in developmental processes such as female gametophyte development, embryo development, and senescence, and regulate a sub-set of cytokinin response genes (Raines *et al.* 2016). These results suggest that the CRFs and TCPs may act in concert with the BPCs to regulate some of the same target genes.

Since the CRF binding motif is enriched in the BPC6 ChIP-Seq (Figure S6), we also compared the 3,060 DEGs in the *crf1,3,5,6* mutant roots (Raines *et al.* 2016) to the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* DEGs and found significant overlap (Hypergeometric probability test P (X>=368) = 1.16e-44; Figure S7a), suggesting that these two transcription factor families share common targets. However, there was no consistent direction to the various changes in gene expression in these two mutants as genes whose expression increased in the *crf* mutant were equally likely to be elevated or decreased in the *bpc* mutant and vice versa (Figure 7b).

As our results suggest a link between BPCs and the cytokinin response, we examined if the BPC6 targets included cytokinin-response genes. First, we examined the overlap between the BPC6 ChIP-Seq target genes and a set of robustly regulated cytokinin response genes, termed the golden list ((Bhargava *et al.* 2013). We find 33 genes that overlapped, including multiple type-A ARRs (*ARR3*, *ARR7*, *ARR8*, and *ARR9*), and CRFs (*CRF2*, and *CRF6*). Additional genes involved in cytokinin signaling and metabolism identified as BPC6 targets not on the golden list include type-B *ARRs* (*ARR1*, *ARR12*, *ARR14*, and *ARR19*), *AHK3*, *AHP4*, and a cytokinin glucosyltransferase (*UGT85A1*). These results suggest that the BPCs regulate cytokinin function at multiple levels. Furthermore, 23 of the 33 genes found to overlap with the golden list, also overlap with type-B ARR10 target genes identified using ChIP-Seq (Zubo *et al.* 2017). Visualizing the data using the Integrated Genome Browser (IGB) (Nicol *et al.* 2009) reveals that some of the BPC6 and ARR10 binding peaks are coincident, while others appear independent (Figure 3b). This overlap suggests that the BPCs target cytokinin-response genes perhaps both in concert with type-B ARRs, but also independently of type-B ARR binding. Approximately 20% of the DEGs in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant overlap with those identified as BPC6 target genes from ChIP-Seq (*P*(X>=184) = 4.0e-9). This suggests that many of the

DEGs in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant might also be direct targets of the BPCs in roots. Although the 20% overlap is similar to the level of overlap in other ChIP-Seq/RNA-Seq studies in Arabidopsis (Fan *et al.* 2014, Yu *et al.* 2011), it likely an underestimation of the direct BPC targets as: 1) the RNA-Seq specifically examined root tissues and the ChIP-Seq employed whole seedlings; and 2) whereas the mutant includes multiple *BPC* genes, the ChIP-Seq specifically examined BPC6. We find multiple transcription factors that are both differentially expressed in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant and identified as targets in the BPC ChIP-Seq, including homeobox genes such as *BLH4, KNAT1/BP*, *PROTODERMAL FACTOR 2 (PDF2)*, and *MERISTEM LAYER 1 (ATML1)* (Tables S1 and S4), consistent with previous studies (Simonini and Kater 2014).

### BPCs directly regulate a sub-set of cytokinin response genes

We defined genes that were identified both in the BPC6 ChIP-Seq and as differentially expressed in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant in the presence or absence of cytokinin; these 348 genes represent genes that are likely to be authentic BPC6 transcriptional targets (Figure 4a, Table S7). Analysis of these 348 genes indicate an enrichment for GO terms such as "response to hormone stimulus" and "regulation of nitrogen metabolic process" (Table S8), which is a process linked to cytokinin signaling (Kiba and Krapp 2016, Schaller *et al.* 2015). Furthermore, we found that of these 348 genes, 41 are also targets of ARR10 in response to cytokinin signaling. (Figure 4a). We examined the expression and the position of BPC6 and ARR10 binding sites using ChIP-seq for a selected set of genes using the Integrated Genome Browser (IGB) (Nicol *et al.* 2009)(Figure 4b). These genes are *WRKY39*, which is differentially expressed in response to cytokinin in wild-type but not *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant roots, and *ANNAT3*, which is differentially expressed in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant relative to the wild type in the absence of cytokinin. Binding sites for both ARR10 and BPC6 are apparent in the upstream regulatory regions of these genes, in some cases overlapping and in others not, suggesting both coordinated and independent binding of these transcription factors.

There were 211 genes identified in both the BPC6 and ARR10 ChIP-Seq ( $P(X \ge 211) = 5.5e$ -28; Figure 4a), suggesting that ARR10 and BPC6 co-regulate genes in some instances in response to cytokinin. Interestingly, ARR10 binds to the upstream regulatory regions of *BPC1* and *BPC2*,

Consistent with this, *BPC1* expression was significantly elevated in response to cytokinin in wild-type roots in our RNA-Seq analysis (Figure S2b). Further, BPC6 binds to the promoters of multiple type-B ARRs. This regulation could represent a positive feedback loop in which the type-B ARRs and BPCs regulate expression of each other.

Interestingly, the expression of all ten type-A ARRs was not altered in the bpc1,2,3,4,6 mutant (Figure S8a), despite the fact that ARR7 was previously reported to be differentially expressed in the shoot apical meristem of the *bpc1-2,2,3* mutant (Simonini and Kater 2014) and even though multiple type-A ARRs gene were identified in our BPC6 ChIP-Seq. One explanation for this result is that the regulation of the type-A ARRs by the BPCs is cell-type-specific and by analyzing gene expression of whole roots, the cell-type specific changes were not observed. Given that cytokinin regulates meristem development, and previous studies link BPCs to floral meristem development, expression of the type-A ARRs was examined in response to cytokinin in shoot and root tips to enrich for cells in the shoot and root apical meristems. The fold-induction of ARR7 expression was slightly higher in the mutant shoot tips in response to cytokinin as compared to the wild-type, but was slightly reduced in the mutant root tips (Figure 5). In the absence of cytokinin, ARR7 expression was comparable in wildtype and mutant root and shoot tips. The expression of ARR12 remained un-regulated by cytokinin in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant in both root and shoot tips (Figure 5). Both *ARR7* and *ARR12* are BPC6 target genes, which coincides with their differential gene regulation in the bpc1,2,3,4,6 mutant. Type-A ARR7 was the only type-A ARR differentially expressed in response to cytokinin in these tissues (Figure S8b, c).

# BPC proteins interact with type-A ARRs

We identified BPC6 as interacting with ARR4 in a yeast two-hybrid screen (Fig. 6a). We investigated the interactions among other BPCs and type-A ARRs. BPC1, a class I BPC, interacted with ARR4 in a yeast two-hybrid assay (Figure 6a). Further, both BCP1 and BPC6 (a class II BPC) interacted with ARR5, although BPC1 only weakly interacts with ARR5. BPC3, another class 1 BPC, did not interact with any of the type-A ARRs tested (Figure S9a). The type-A ARR proteins ARR7, ARR9, and

ARR16 did not interact with any BPCs tested (Figure 6a; Figure S9a). The interaction between the type-A ARRs and AHP2 or AHP3 and the BPC dimerization were used as positive controls as these interactions have been described previously (Figure 6a; Figure S9a; (Simonini *et al.* 2012, Wanke *et al.* 2011)). Both BPC6 and BPC1 specifically interact with ARR4 and ARR5, showing that there is specificity among the interaction of the BPCs and type-A ARRs. However, BPCs from different classes also interact with the type-A ARRs, which suggests functional overlap among the BPCs family members with regard to their role in mediating type-A ARR function.

To further investigate this interaction *in planta* and to determine the role of aspartic acid phosphorylation of the receiver domain, we transiently expressed epitope-tagged myc-BPC6 and HA-ARR4 in *Nicotiana benthamiana* epidermal leaf cells and examined the interaction in the presence and absence of cytokinin using a co-immunoprecipitation assay. The predicted molecular weight of ARR4 is 28-kDa; however multiple bands are observed for ARR4, none of which are not present in control samples, that may correspond to distinct post-translational modifications and/or to proteolytic processing. Only a subset of these bands were identified in the BPC6 immunoprecipitation, suggesting that not all forms stably interact with BPC6. The addition of cytokinin, which presumably resulted in the phosphorylation of the conserved Asp residue on HA-ARR4, did not affect the interaction between BPC6 and ARR4 (Figure 6b). Further, the ARR4<sup>D95E</sup> phosphomimic and the ARR4<sup>D95A</sup> non-phosphorylatable mutant proteins also interacted with BPC6 in a co-immunoprecipitation assay, suggesting that aspartic acid phosphorylation of the receiver domain does not play a major role in regulating the interaction with BPC6 (Figure S9d).

As the BPC proteins interact with some type-A ARR proteins, we queried if the BPCs and type-A ARRs regulate overlapping gene sets. To this end, we examined the 3,611 genes whose expression was altered in ten-day old roots of the type-A *arr3*,4,5,6,7,8,9,15 mutant as compared to wild-type roots (Shanks *et al.* 2016). A significant number of the DEGs in the *bpc1*,2,3,4,6 roots overlap with the DEGs in the *arr3*,4,5,6,7,8,9,15 mutant (P (x >= 348) = 8.9e-72; Figure 7a). However, similar to the *crf* mutants, there is no consistent direction to the changes in the expression of

these overlapping genes as genes whose expression increased in the type-A *arr* mutant were equally likely to be elevated or decreased in the *bpc* mutant and vice versa (Figure 7b).

### Discussion

In eukaryotes, multiple transcription factors often act in combination to regulate gene expression (Lee et al. 2012, Ptashne and Gann 2002, Zinzen et al. 2009). Given that the BPCs regulate multiple developmental processes, it is likely that they rely on co-regulation with other transcription factors to confer specificity on target genes. There is significant overlap between the BPC6 target genes identified with ChIP-Seq and the type-B ARR10 targets genes enriched in response to cytokinin. Furthermore, the BPCs and CRFs both regulate a sub-set of the same genes as identified with RNA-Seq studies. Prior studies indicate that the CRFs likely play a role in the differential expression of a subset of cytokinin-responsive genes (Rashotte et al., 2006, Raines et al., 2016). Thus, BPCs likely act together with the type-B ARRs and/or the CRFs to regulate specific genes in response to cytokinin signaling. Positive feedback loops likely exist between the BPCs and type-B ARRs as ARR10 binds to the promoters of BPC1 and BPC2, and BPC1 is induced in response to cytokinin; BPC6 also binds to the promoters of multiple type-B ARR and CRF genes, and the BPC proteins are necessary for induction of ARR12, a type-B ARR, in response to cytokinin. In this model (Figure 8), the BPCs, type-B ARRs and CRFs co-regulate genes expression by either independently binding to different *cis*-acting elements to convergently regulate target gene expression or by binding to elements as part of the same transcriptional complex. The BPCs may also recruit other complexes to these genes such as the PRC chromatin modifiers to promote or repress transcription of target genes (Hecker et al. 2015, Mu et al. 2017, Xiao et al. 2017).

Multiple type-A *ARR* genes were identified in our ChIP-Seq analysis as direct BPC6 targets. However, for the majority of type-A *ARRs*, we do not find altered expression of these primary cytokinin response genes in *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant roots in the presence or absence of cytokinin. However, when examining a subset of tissues, we identify altered expression of type-A *ARR7* and *ARR12* in response to cytokinin in the shoot and root apices of the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant. This coincides with previous studies that found altered *ARR7* expression in the *bpc1-2,2,3* inflorescence meristem (Simonini and Kater 2014). Unlike the previous studies, we do not find a significant difference in ARR7 expression in the absence of cytokinin, but we do find that ARR7 expression is specifically altered in response to cytokinin in the *bpc1,2,3,4,6* mutant. This difference could be due to the fact that we used shoot apices rather than inflorescence meristems and thus did not specifically examine meristematic cells. We hypothesize that the BPCs may regulate the other type-A ARRs in a tissuespecific manner. Given that the BPCs regulate multiple genes important for growth and development, they likely function differentially in specific tissues and times during development. It is possible that we did not observe changes in expression of the other type-A ARR genes in the bpc mutant because they are regulated in different tissues at different times and/or we did not specifically isolate meristematic cells. Furthermore, the BPCs are part of a gene regulatory network that likely includes multiple positive and negative feedback loops and redundant elements acting in a complex manner to modulate gene expression. Thus, while the BPCs bind to type-A ARR genes, they may function redundantly in some tissues with other transcription factors regulating type-A ARR transcription and/or may act only transiently to modulate their expression. Finally, we note that we are not using a complete *bpc* null mutant and the residual BPC function may suffice to drive wild-type levels of type-A ARR expression.

Overall, we find that the BPCs regulate a sub-set of cytokinin-regulated genes. Multiple cytokinin response genes are putatively directly targeted by both BPCs and type-B ARRs, while others are targets of only one or the other. Furthermore, some genes regulated by cytokinin that are not differentially expressed in the *bpc* mutant have neither BPC nor type-B ARR binding sites. At least some of these genes are likely either secondary cytokinin response genes, or bound by other BPC or type-B ARR proteins not used in the ChIP-Seq assays. While our data strongly support the notion that the BPCs play a role in the response to cytokinin, it is clear that they have additional functions in growth and development outside of cytokinin signaling.

We demonstrate that specific type-A ARRs directly interact with members from both classes of BPC transcription factors, providing insight into the mechanism by which these single domain response regulators negatively regulate cytokinin signaling. Phylogenetic studies of the type-A ARRs also show that ARR4 and ARR5 are closely related, even though ARR4 has a longer C-terminal

extension, while ARR5 consists mainly of the receiver domain. This suggests that the interaction between the BPCs and type-A ARRs is likely due to sequence specificity that resides in the conserved receiver domain of these type-A ARRs. In addition to their interaction, the significant overlap between the genes differentially regulated in the arr3,4,5,6,7,8,9,15 mutant and the bpc1,2,3,4,6 mutant further supports a functional link between the type-A ARRs and BPCs. Our data suggest that perhaps the type-A ARRs act to modulate BPC activity, providing at least one target for type-A ARR input into cytokinin responsiveness. How does cytokinin acting through the type-A ARRs act to modulate BPC function? As the interaction of the BPCs with the type-A ARRs is not dependent on phosphorylation, we propose that BPC function may be modulated by cytokinin via an increase in type-A ARR protein levels, which are synergistically elevated by cytokinin through increased type-A ARR transcription and protein stability (D'Agostino et al. 2000, To et al. 2007). The elevated type-A ARR protein levels could lead to increased interaction with the BPCs, thus modulating their activity by either interfering with the BPC binding to DNA, with the BPC transactivation capacity or with the BPC binding to other partners (Figure 8). As there is no clear direction to effect of disruption of type-A ARRs on BPC-dependent gene expression, it is possible that the type-A ARRs can either inhibit or promote BPC activity depending upon cell type and/or developmental stage.

Our results shed light on the complex transcriptional network impacted by cytokinin. The regulation of the transcriptome by cytokinin involves the type-B ARRs acting at the head of a transcriptional cascade. Accumulating evidence indicates that the type-B ARRs act in concert with multiple other transcription factors, including the CRFs, TCPs, TGA3, DELLAs and as demonstrated here, the BPCs (Choi *et al.* 2010, Efroni *et al.* 2013, Marín-de la Rosa *et al.* 2015, Raines *et al.* 2016) to combinatorially control the expression of genes in response to cytokinin. This is supported by the enrichment of binding sites for many of these transcription factors likely act differentially in specific cell types, at specific times in development and in response to various exogenous inputs to mediate the pleiotropic effects of cytokinin. Deciphering the cell type-specific function of these various transcription factors acting in concert with the type-B ARRs is a major challenge for the

future. Further, while our results indicate a link between the type-A ARRs and the BPCs, further studies are necessary to elucidate the manner in which the type-A ARRs alter BPC function.

### **Experimental procedures**

### Plant materials and growth conditions

The *bpc1-1,2, bpc1-1,2,3, bpc4,6, bpc1,2,4,6, bpc1-1,2,3-1,4,6, bpc1-1,2,3-1,4,6,7*, and *ahk2,4* multiple mutant T-DNA insertional lines have been previously characterized (Monfared *et al.* 2011) (Higuchi et al., 2004). Seeds were sterilized with 50% bleach, 0.2% Triton-X-100, and plated on ½ MS plates (½ Murashige and Skoog basal medium, 1% sucrose, 0.6% phytogel, pH 5.8). Seeds were stratified in the dark at 4°C for 3-4 days and transferred to a 24-hour light, 22°C growth chamber. For ChIP-Seq, seeds were stratified at 4°C for 2 days in darkness. Seeds were sown on agar plates containing 2.15 g/l Murashige & Skoog media (Duchefa Biochemie), 0.5 g/l MES (pH 5.8), and 15 g/l phytoagar. Plants used for ChIP-Seq and X-ChIP were grown in 16h light/8h dark at 22°C growth chambers.

### Cloning *pBPC6:GFP:BPC6* and *pBPC6:GFP*

For PCR reactions, Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Fermentas) was used (see Table S9 for primers used). Genomic Col-0 DNA was used to amplify the promoter of *pBPC6* consisting of 2352 bp upstream of ATG sequence plus ATG and the following 3 codons by using the primers S-pBPC6-ATG-NheI and AS-pBPC6-ATG-9Nu-NotI that introduce a 5' *NheI* and 3' *NotI* site. *eGFP6* was amplified using primers S-GFP-ATG-NotI and AS-GFP-w/o Stop-SacII that introduce a 5' *Not*I and 3' *Sac*II site. The remaining coding sequence of 1017 bp of *BPC6* including TGA was amplified using cDNA of Col-0 flowers and the primers S-BPC6-13Nu-SacII and AS-BPC6-Stop-PvuI that introduce a 5' *Sac*II and *3'Pvu*I site. 388 bp 3' of the *BPC6* coding region was amplified from genomic Col-0 DNA using the S-BPC6-3'UTR-PvuI and AS-BPC6-3'UTR-NheI primers that introduce 5' *Pvu*I and 3' *Nhe*I site. The PCR products were gel purified using QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen) and cloned into pCRII-TOPO/TA vector (Invitrogen). The fragments were isolated after restriction digestion, mixed in an equimolar ratio and ligated using T4-DNA-Ligase (Fermentas). PCR was

performed to amplify the whole product of the ligation using primers S-BPC6-CACC-ATG-MluI and AS-BPC6-Stop-PvuI and the products cloned into pCR2.1-TOPO/TA (Invitrogen). The *pBPC6:GFP:BPC6* in the pCR2.1-TOPO/TA was used to generate the control *pBPC6:GFP*. To be able to remove the coding sequence of *BPC6*, successive site directed mutagenesis was done to introduce *Sma*I sites on either end of the coding regions using appropriate primers (S-GFP-BPC6-*Sma*I + AS-GFP-BPC6-*Sma*I and S-BPC6-3'BPC6-*Sma*I + AS-GFP-BPC6-*Sma*I and S-BPC6-3'BPC6-*Sma*I + AS-GFP-BPC6). After sequencing, the control construct *pBPC6:GFP* and *pBPC6:GFP:BPC6* was digested using *Eco*RI and gel purified. Both *Eco*RI digested DNAs (*pBPC6:GFP:BPC6* or *pBPC6:GFP* as control) and pGPTVII.BARe were ligated respectively in an equimolar ratio using T4-DNA Ligase (Fermentas) and transformed into *E. coli*. The vectors were tested using different control digests and partial sequencing with primer AS-pGPTVII.BAR-Seq. Complete vector sequence see Supplemental Text File S1. Both vectors were transformed into Arabidopsis Col using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation.

### **Root elongation assay**

Seedlings were surface sterilized and grown on vertical <sup>1</sup>/<sub>2</sub> MS plates for four days under constant light. At day 4, the seedlings were transferred to plates supplemented with 0.05% NaOH as a vehicle control or different concentrations of BA and the root tips were marked on the plates. Seedlings were grown for 6 more days, and the plates were scanned on day 10. Root length was measured using FIJI software (Schindelin *et al.* 2012). A one-way ANOVA and Tukey HSD tests were conducted in R Studio version 0.98.1062 (https://www.rstudio.com).

# ChIP-Seq and data analysis

For chromatin immunoprecipitation, stable transgenic pBPC6::GFP-BPC6 and pBPC6::GFP plants were used. Two independent pools per line of seedlings were sampled as biological replicates, crosslinked, immunoprecipitated and used for sequencing. 3 g of 14-days-old seedlings grown on ½ MS plates were harvested and immediately cross-linked using 1% (v/v) formaldehyde and vacuum infiltration for 1 h. After washing, the material was frozen using

liquid nitrogen and ground into fine powder. Subsequent nuclei enrichment followed by chromatin isolation was performed. The isolated chromatin was sheared into 200-500 bp fragments using a S220 focused-ultrasonicator (Covaris) and immunoprecipitated with 2.5 µl of anti-GFP antibody (Abcam, ab290). The precipitated DNA was recovered with the MiniElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen). 10 ng of the immunoprecipitated DNA to constructed two ChIP-Seq libraries (pBPC6::GFP-BPC6) and two control library (pBPC6::GFP) using the TruSeq® ChIP Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina). Indexed libraries were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq3000 instrument with 11-15 million reads. The raw data which was obtained in this study is available at the ENA (PRJEB22963). The reads were aligned to the TAIR10 genome using Bowtie (version 1.1.1) with the options -m 1 (Langmead et al. 2009) with about 50% of the GFP:BPC6 reads aligning to the genome and 20% of the GFP reads aligning to the genome. SAMtools version 0.1.18 (Li et al. 2009) was used to create the BAM files. Peaks were called using MACS2 (version 2.1.0) (Zhang et al., 2008) by comparing each GFP:BPC6 replicate with each GFP replicate from two replicates making a total of four out-put peak files with a genome size of 119,667,750 and keep-dup set to auto. Peaks with a negative logQ value of less than 10 were removed. ChIPpeakAnno (Zhu et al., 2010) was used to find overlapping peaks of at least one base pair between the four MACS2 out-put peak files and a consensus peak file of 3,581 peaks was made. The 3,581 peaks associated with 3,312 different genes as determined by the nearest gene using ChIPpeakAnno. The 3,312 genes were annotated with Virtual Plant 1.3 (Katari et al., 2010) and the gene ontology analysis for these genes was performed with agriGO biological process (FDR < 0.05) (Zhou et al., 2010). The bedgraphs for visualization in the Integrated Genome Browser (IGB) (Nicol et al. 2009) were made by merging the GFP:BPC6 replicates and the GFP replicates separately and then taking the fold enrichment of the merged GFP:BPC/GFP

using the MACS2 "bgcmp" function with the to show one representative bedgraph. The consensus peak bedfiles were also visualized with IGB.

### **ChIP-qPCR** analysis

For ChIP-qPCR, we used target genes that were enrichment for GFP:BPC6 binding within the ChIP-Seq data. Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed as described above, but after DNA recovery quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed for X-ChIP experiments. We used three technical replicates each probe for qPCR and the results were calculated as percentage of input DNA. Fold enrichment represents fold change in comparison to the pBPC6::GFP control. Primer sequences used for X-ChIP were listed in Table S9.

# **RNA-Seq expression analysis**

10-day-old wild-type and *bpc1-1,2,3,4,6* mutant seedlings were treated with BA as described above. ### Yeast two-hybrid analysis

The L40cc $\alpha$ U yeast strain (Dortay *et al.* 2006) was transformed with both bait and prey constructs using the lithium acetate transformation method (Gietz and Woods 2006). Transformants were selected on leu<sup>-</sup> trp<sup>-</sup> media plates. Successful plasmid transformation was confirmed with colony PCR of re-streaked yeast colonies. Cell suspensions with yeast containing both constructs were grown to an A<sub>600</sub> of 0.2 and 10 µl of each interaction was plated on leu<sup>-</sup> trp<sup>-</sup> minimal media plates as a control and minimal media selection plates leu<sup>-</sup> trp<sup>-</sup> his<sup>-</sup> ura<sup>-</sup> to test for interaction (Dortay *et al.* 2006). Each strain was co-transformed with either the empty prey or empty bait vector to test for auto-activation. To suppress auto-activation, yeast were plated on selection plates supplemented with 5 mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3AT). Yeast were incubated at 30° C for 4-5 days to test for interaction.
