# 28 ABSTRACT

29

There are two main types of root systems in flowering plants, namely taproot systems of 30 31 dicots and fibrous root systems found in monocots. Despite this fundamental split, our 32 current knowledge of cellular and molecular mechanism driving root development is mainly based on studies of the dicot model Arabidopsis. However, the world major crops 33 are monocots and little is known about the transcriptional programs underlying cell 34 type specification in this clade. Here, we report the transcriptomes of more than 20,000 35 36 single cells derived from root tips of two agronomically important rice cultivars. Using combined computational and experimental analyses we were able to robustly identify 37 38 most of the major cell types and define novel cell type specific marker genes for both 39 cultivars. Importantly, we found divergent cell types to be associated with specific 40 regulatory programs including phytohormone biosynthesis, signaling and response, which were well conserved between the two rice cultivars. In addition, we detected 41 42 substantial differences between the cell-type transcript profiles of *Arabidopsis* and rice. 43 These species-specific features emphasize the importance of analyzing tissues across 44 diverse model species including rice. Taken together, our study provides insight into the transcriptomic landscape of major cell types of the rice root tip at single-cell resolution 45 and opens new avenues to study cell type specification, function and evolution in plants. 46 47 48 Key words: single cell RNA-seq, transcriptional landscape, rice, root, plants 49 50 51 52 53 54 55

#### 56 INTRODUCTION

57

58 Roots are essential for vascular plant growth and development and serve nutrient and water 59 uptake as well as providing firm anchorage to the soil. Quantifying gene expression in single 60 cells or cell types is crucial for understanding the complex gene regulatory networks 61 controlling root development. In recent years, powerful single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) techniques have been developed (Macosko et al., 2015; Tang et al., 2011), and have 62 63 allowed to record robust transcriptional signatures of cell types in a wide spectrum of 64 eukaryotic organisms, including human, mouse and C. elegans (Cao et al., 2017; Trapnell et 65 al., 2014; Zeisel et al., 2015). The technical advances have also made it possible to analyze the transcriptome of plant roots at single cell resolution, focusing on the reference species 66 Arabidopsis (Efroni and Birnbaum, 2016). To date, scRNA-seq of single root cells has only 67 68 been reported in dicot Arabidopsis, demonstrating high rate of heterogeneity of roots and the expression signatures for the major cell types (Denver et al., 2019; Jean-Baptiste et al., 2019; 69 70 Rich-Griffin et al., 2020; Ryu et al., 2019; Shulse et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019b). The 71 anatomy and morphology of the monocot species root system (Rebouillat et al., 2009), differs 72 from that of dicot, such as the presence of a lysigenous cortex and additional cell layers of epidermis and cortex (Supplemental Figure 1). Although previous work in transcriptional 73 74 profiling has been carried out by using bulk RNA isolated from root tissues (Gutjahr et al., 75 2015; Takehisa et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2010), the molecular basis of monocot root cell 76 types and their evolution are largely unknown. 77 In this study, we sequenced over 20,000 single cells of root tip of rice, which is a widely used 78 reference monocot species, as well as one of the most important crops world-wide. 79 Combining computational analyses with in situ hybridization experiments, we identified

80 nearly all major cell types with novel cell type specific marker genes. Comparative analyses

### 84 **RESULTS AND DISCUSSION**

85

### 86 Single-Cell RNA-Seq and Identification of Cell-Type Clusters

87 We performed scRNA-seq to Japonica group cultivar Nipponbare (Nip) and Indica group cultivar 93-11, which are the two main cultivated varieties and parental lines used for 88 89 breeding in Asia (Qian et al., 2016). Protoplasts were isolated from 250 root tips (5 mm in 90 length of crown roots) of 3-day-old Nip and 93-11 seedlings, respectively. About 18,000 91 protoplasts per sample were initially mixed with 10x Genomics single-cell reaction regents 92 including cell barcodes. The libraries were constructed followed by high-throughput 93 sequencing (Figure 1A). Data were prefiltered at both cell and gene level. In total, we successfully profiled 10,968 cells with 2,592 genes/cell and 12,564 cells with 2,636 genes/cell 94 95 for Nip and 93-11, respectively (Supplemental Table 1). To examine the sequencing depth 96 and robustness of the scRNA-seq results, we divided the data into two random subsamples, 97 and found high correlation between them in both Nip and 93-11 (Supplemental Figure 2A and 98 2B). Further, we found a high correlation between the combined scRNA-seq data with bulk 99 RNA-seq of independently isolated and protoplasted root tips (Supplemental Figure 2C and 100 2D). In contrast, the correlation between the combined scRNA-seq data with bulk RNA-seq of 101 independently isolated, but not protoplasted root tips was comparatively lower (Supplemental 102 Figure 2E and 2F), which was expected due to the cellular response during the three hours of 103 enzymatic digestion for generating protoplasts. Gene ontology (GO) analysis suggested that 104 the protoplasting induced differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (p < 0.01, fold change > 2) 105 were involved in environmental responses and RNA processing, etc. (Supplemental Figure

2G). All our results can interactively be mined on the web using the Root Cell Atlas in Rice
(RCAR), which freely available at http://www.elabcaas.cn/rcar/index.html.

108

109 After linear dimensional reduction, the uniform manifold approximation and projection 110 (UMAP) algorithm and t-distributed stochastic neighborhood embedding (t-SNE) tools were 111 used to visualize and explore the datasets (Becht et al., 2019; van der Maaten and Hinton, 112 2008). Unsupervised analyses grouped cells into eight major clusters based on UMAP or t-113 SNE (Figure 1B and 1C; Supplemental Figure 3A). Similarly, eight major clusters were also 114 observed when we removed 5,914 DEGs in response to protoplasting in both Nip and 93-11 115 (Supplemental Figure 3B), suggesting that the effect of cell wall digestion was minor for cell 116 clustering, Moreover, application of RaceID, another independent clustering approach, also 117 supported a similar structure with eight clusters (Supplemental Figure 3C). 118 Since only very few marker genes for rice root cell types were known, we first 119 performed RNA in situ hybridizations using orthologs of Arabidopsis marker genes to 120 annotate theses clusters (Denyer et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019b). This strategy allowed us to 121 identify several genes specifically expressed in stem cells (Supplemental Figure 4A), however, 122 these genes were only represented in very few cells in the scRNA-seq data, suggesting rare 123 cell types were not efficiently captured in our experiment or these genes were not well 124 detected by scRNA-seq technology because of their low expression. Importantly, we found 125 that more than 20 orthologs of known marker genes for diverse cell types of Arabidopsis did 126 not show cell-type specific expression in rice (Supplemental Figure 4B). This finding 127 indicated that a significant number of marker genes may not be conserved between 128 Arabidopsis and rice, and hence not suitable for robust cell type identification across species. 129 To alleviate this problem, we mined our own data for potential marker genes that were 130 highly and specifically expressed in one or two clusters (Figure 1D; Supplemental Figure 5; Supplemental Table 2 and 3), and also took relevant references into account and selected 131

132 genes with a maximum of available information (Ishimaru et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2007). To validate these candidates *in vivo*, we performed in situ hybridization and were able to identify 133 134 bona fide cell-type marker genes for rice root cell types (Figure 1E and Supplemental Figure 4C). For example, LOC Os06g38960 was specifically expressed in cells of the epidermis, 135 136 LOC Os03g25280 in the epidermis near root hair, LOC Os04g46810 in cortex cells, 137 LOC Os01g73700 in the root cap, LOC Os08g03450 in the endodermis, LOC Os03g37490 in the stele and LOC Os01g73980 in the metaxylem, respectively in both Nip and 93-11 138 139 (Figure 1E; Supplemental Figure 4C-E and 5A-C). In addition, we selected seven of these 140 maker genes and created reporter lines using fluorescent proteins, and observed cell-type 141 specific signal in transgenic rice (Supplemental Figure 6), which was in agreement with our in 142 situ hybridization results. Together with previously known markers for root hair (Ding et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2007), our novel marker genes allowed us to annotate all clusters containing 143 144 the major cell types of the root tip in Nip and 93-11 (Figure 1B and 1C; Supplemental Figure 3A-C). Interestingly, we robustly identified a specific sub-cell type of epidermal cell located 145 146 near root hair, which was not reported in Arabidopsis, suggesting an evolutionary divergent between monocots and dicots. Of note, epidermis, cortex and stele were the major clusters 147 148 contain cells ranging from 2,328 to 4,833, while clusters of root hair, metaxylem, root cap and 149 endodermis had only small number of cells (17 - 435) (Supplemental Table 4), which was 150 comparable to the actual ratio of cell numbers in the rice root tip (Supplemental Figure 1). 151 Next, we tried to identify subclusters within the clusters containing many cells, and identified 152 LOC Os01g68589 from the cortex cluster, which turned out to be specifically expressed in the inner most layer of cortex (Supplemental Figure 4F). In summary, our scRNA-seq dataset 153 154 revealed eight distinct clusters, which corresponded to eight major cell types. These clusters 155 were validated by identifying a set of novel rice specific marker genes and using them for in 156 situ hybridization and reporter genes.

### 158 Highly Conserved Cell-Type Clusters between Rice Cultivars

159 The similarity in cell cluster separation and cell morphology between Nip and 93-11 160 promoted us to investigate the conservation of their single-cell transcriptomes. To this end, 161 the single-cell data from Nip and 93-11 were merged and subsequently clustered. We 162 observed that all eight cell type clusters were well-aligned and matched one-to-one between 163 the two cultivars (Figure 2A). Cell-type homologies were also supported by our cell-type marker genes and high Pearson's correlation coefficients of the expression profiles between 164 165 cell-type clusters of both cultivars (Figure 2B and Supplemental Figure 7A). Furthermore, we 166 compared conserved and divergently expressed genes for each of the homologous cell types (Supplemental Table 5). Venn diagram analyses showed that more than 80% of expressed 167 168 genes were shared between Nip and 93-11 in all big cell type clusters, including cortex, 169 endodermis, epidermis and stele. For the cultivar-specific genes, the highest percentage was 170 found in the cluster of root cap (Figure 2C), which fits well with the fact that the morphology 171 of the root cap is the most divergent while other cell types are nearly identical between Nip 172 and 93-11 (Supplemental Figure 1). Since auxin-related genes are important for root cap 173 formation (Wang et al. 2005), we found that 14 auxin-related genes in root cap were 174 differentially expressed between Nip and 93-11 (Supplemental Table 6), which may 175 contribute to the difference in their morphology. Next, we identified cultivar specific marker 176 genes for cell types (Supplemental Figure 8A and 8B), which may contribute to the 177 understanding of the cellular and physiological differences between rice cultivars (Zhang et 178 al., 2019a). To provide insights into the molecular functions in each cell type in both cultivars, 179 we performed GO analysis, and found matching functional categories for each cell type 180 (Figure 2D). For example, genes related to water and fluid transport were enriched in cells of 181 the cortex, genes related to cell wall were enriched in the epidermis, while genes related to 182 response to extracellular and external stimuli were enriched epidermis (near root hair) (Figure 2D). We also examined the function of the cultivar-specific genes for each cell type, and 183

found most of the resulting overrepresented GO terms were related to response to various
environmental stimuli (Supplemental Figure 8C), suggesting distinct mechanisms may act in
response to external environmental factors between Nip and 93-11.

187 Next, we analyzed spatio-temporal expression patterns for genes with known functions 188 in cell type specification, focusing on phytohormones, which are important for root 189 development and stress responses (Vanstraelen and Benkova, 2012). To this end, we plotted 190 the expression of genes related to major hormone biosynthesis and response pathways and 191 found that for several hormones, biosynthesis and response genes were overrepresented in the 192 same cell types (Figure 2E and Supplemental Figure 7B). For example, auxin biosynthesis 193 and response genes were overrepresented in the clusters of stele and epidermis suggesting an 194 autocrine function of auxin in these cells. In contrast, for other hormones, these two types of 195 genes were enriched in distinct clusters. For example, abscisic acid (ABA) responses genes 196 were enriched in the clusters of epidermis and cortex, whereas there was no clear pattern for 197 the ABA biosynthesis genes. Gibberellic acid (GA) biosynthesis genes were overrepresented 198 in the cluster of cortex cells, but GA response genes were overrepresented in the stele cluster. 199 Taken together, our datasets revealed complex transcriptional programs in all cell types and 200 allowed to predict potential molecular signaling between cell types.

### 202 Differentiation Trajectory of Cells of Epidermis and Root Hair

Since cells in both terminal and intermediate developmental states are captured simultaneously (Trapnell et al., 2014), scRNA-seq enables the exploration of the continuous differentiation trajectory of a developmental process. Epidermis and root hair are the outmost layers in the root, and root hair are differentiated from the epidermis. To examine the trajectory of these developmentally related cell types, pseudotime analysis was applied to the clusters representing these cell types using Monocle 2 (Trapnell et al., 2014). Individual cells were projected into two ends of the pseudotime backbone, representing two distinct final

210 states (Figure 3A). Cells from the two cultivars showed highly consistent pseudotime order, suggesting a conserved developmental trajectory (Figure 3A and 3B). By labelling the cells 211 212 according to their types, we found that the developmental trajectory started with cells from the subset of epidermis cluster, after a bifurcation point that marks developmental state transition, 213 214 the trajectory gradually ended with two differentiated cell types: the root hair cells (Branch 1) 215 and the differentiated epidermis cells (Branch 2), respectively (Figure 3A-C). In the root hair 216 branch, cells from epidermis, epidermis (near root hair) and root hair were ordered 217 continuously (Figure 3B), suggesting that the pseudotime order derived from single cell data 218 essentially recapitulates the root hair developmental process. We then identified 534 differentially expressed genes across the pseudotime order. These genes fell into three clusters 219 with distinct gene expression patterns reflecting transcriptional rewiring during root 220 development (Figure 3D). Gene expression patterns differ greatly across the branch point. 221 222 Genes in the first cluster were preferentially expressed in the prebranch, which consists of epidermal cells. These genes were enriched in GO terms related to response to various stimuli, 223 224 which is consistent with the function of the epidermis in environmental sensing and 225 communication (Figure 3D and Supplemental Figure 8C). Branch 1 included more mixed cell 226 cluster, genes related to epidermis development and root hair differentiation were found, 227 suggesting that they may regulate this cell state transition. For example, compared to root hair 228 specific markers, we found LOC Os07g43670 encoding a ribonuclease T2 family domain-229 containing protein in the third cluster genes expressed in both epidermis near root hair and 230 root hair cells. LOC Os07g43670 expression was validated by in situ hybridization in both Nip and 93-11 (Figure 3E), supporting the ordered developmental process from epidermis to 231 232 root hair. Collectively, pseudotime analysis provided insight into the dynamic process of root 233 cell type development and essential gene expression rewiring during the transition of cell 234 states.

### 236 Conserved and Divergent Cell-Type Expression between Rice and Arabidopsis

To determine the evolutionary conservation of root cell type development in monocots 237 238 and dicots, we compared datasets from rice and Arabidopsis. To this end, we used a recently 239 published single-cell dataset, also derived from protoplasts of 5 mm root tips using the 10x 240 genomics platform (Zhang et al., 2019b). First, we used the previously described Arabidopsis 241 cell type maker genes and obtained similar cell type clusters to the reported ones 242 (Supplemental Figure 9A) (Denver et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019b), suggesting our analysis 243 pipeline was robust. Next, we combined rice and *Arabidopsis* datasets and aligned them by 244 using orthologous genes (Supplemental Figure 9B), and then clustered these cells by dimension reduction. Consistent cell-type homologies were identified based on shared cluster 245 246 membership using Seurat (Butler et al., 2018) second alignment (Figure 4A-D; Supplemental Figure 9C-E). UMAP visualization and Pearson's correlation coefficient analysis revealed 247 248 that clusters of root hair cells were highly correlated and matched one to one, while clusters of 249 cortex cells were more moderately correlated between rice and Arabidopsis (Figure 4E). We 250 also analysed another single-cell dataset from Arabidopsis root tip (Ryu et al., 2019), and our 251 cross-species comparison revealed similar results (Supplemental Figure 10A-E). Next, we 252 extracted the single-cell data of root hair and cortex from rice and *Arabidopsis*, subsequent 253 alignment and clustering further suggest that these two cell types are correlated between rice 254 and Arabidopsis (Supplemental Figure 11A and 11B). Moreover, these homologies were also 255 supported by shared marker gene expression between species (Figure 4F; Supplemental 256 Figure 11C) and may help to predict properties and conservation of homologous cell types 257 (Supplemental Figure 11D), as well as the function of marker genes. For example, *Proline*-258 rich protein-like 1 (PRPL1) was demonstrated as key regulator for Arabidopsis root hair 259 development (Boron et al., 2014). We found the rice ortholog also specifically expressed in root hair cells (Supplemental Figure 11C), suggesting a conserved function for root hair 260 development for this gene, although further experiments would be needed to test this 261

262 prediction. Of note, cells of Arabidopsis cortex matched to a specific subset of rice cortex cells (Figure 1D and Supplemental Figure 9E, 10B and 10D), maybe reflecting a partially 263 264 conserved function of cortex in the two species. In contrast, clusters of epidermis, endodermis, 265 stele and root cap cells seemed to diverge more substantially between rice and Arabidopsis 266 (Figure 4D-G and Supplemental Figure 9D, 9E and 10A-E). For these cell types, cell-type 267 clusters were not spatially overlapped in UMAP, and generally more genes showed divergent expression (Supplemental Table 7), which may contribute to anatomical differences between 268 269 rice and Arabidopsis. Additionally, marker genes for these tissues were generally not shared 270 between rice and Arabidopsis (Figure 4F and Supplemental Figure 4B). Consistently, for 271 epidermis, epidermis (near root hair), endodermis, stele and root cap, we performed in situ 272 hybridization on the orthologous of rice marker genes in Arabidopsis, and found these genes 273 were not cell type specifically expressed in *Arabidopsis* (Supplemental Figure 12A). 274 We performed GO analysis to investigate the function of the divergent genes for each cell type, and found the top GO terms were related to catabolic process and response to 275 276 various environmental stimuli (Supplemental Figure 12B). Of note, several GO terms, for example "response to cadmium ion", were found in almost all cell types, suggesting that rice 277 278 and Arabidopsis may have differences in basic cell functions, even though they are 279 anatomical similar for some cell type. The most-divergent gene families included those 280 encoding membrane proteins and primary pumps (Figure 4H), whose differential activities 281 may contribute to the divergence of cell function and root morphology between rice and

282 Arabidopsis (Hoffmann et al., 2019).

Taken together, our single-cell transcriptomic analysis has identified cell-type specific regulatory programs for almost all cell types of the rice root tip and robust cell type markers for two cultivars, Nip and 93-11. Hence, our results represent a valuable resource to study the developmental and physiological function cell types at the molecular level and at single-cell resolution. The comparison of rice and *Arabidopsis* scRNA-seq data revealed evolutionary

conserved and divergent cell-type, and species-specific features, emphasizing the importanceof expanding scRNA-seq approaches to multiple species.

#### 294 Rice Growth Conditions and Protoplast Isolation

295 Rice seeds were soaked in water in the dark until germination, and then cultured in

Hoagland's Complete Nutrient Solution for 3 days (28°C with 10-h-light/14-h-dark cycles, the

light intensity is 300 lux). 250 root tips (5 mm) of crown roots were cut into small pieces of

about 1 mm and incubated in digest solution (2% cellulase RS, 0.75% macerozyme R10, 1%

Hemicellulase, 0.5% Pectolyase Y-23, 10 mM MES, 0.6 M mannitol, 1 mM CaCl<sub>2</sub>, 0.1%

BSA and 0.04% 2-mercapto-Ethanol) at vacuum pump (-60 kPa) at room temperature for 30

301 minutes, then the mixture was placed in a 28°C incubator with 60-70 rpm for 2.5 hours to

302 release root cell protoplasts. The protoplasts were filtered through 80-µm nylon mesh and

303 centrifuged at 130×g for 5 min, and washed three times with 8% mannitol at room

304 temperature. After discarded the supernatant, a small amount of 8% mannitol solution was

305 added to resuspend the protoplasts. Trypan blue staining was applied to confirm the quality

306 (<10% stained) of the protoplasts. The concentration of the protoplasts was adjusted to

307 1,000-1,200 cells/µl and then processed with 10x Genomics Single Cell Protocol (CG00052,

308 RevC).

### 319 Cell Clustering and Identification of Marker Genes

320 scRNA-seq sequencing data were aligned to the Nip and 93-11 reference genomes (Zhang et

321 al., 2018), respectively, and counted using the Cell Ranger pipelines (version 2.0, 10x

322 Genomics). The raw count matrix data was imported into R using Seurat (V3.1.0) package for

323 further data analysis (Butler et al., 2018). Cells with fewer than 200 detected genes and genes

detected less than 20 cells were removed. Each individual dataset was scaled and normalized

325 by the ScaleData and NormalizeData functions. Top 2,000 highly variable genes were used

326 for principal component analysis (PCA) dimensionality reduction. The first 20 PCs were

327 selected according to the PCA elbow plot and used for clustering with resolution parameter

328 0.3. The clusters were visualized and explored by *t*-SNE (van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008)

and UMAP (Becht et al., 2019). Marker genes (cluster-enriched genes) were identified using

330 function FindAllMarkers in the Seurat package. Differential expression analysis was

331 performed based on the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The cluster-enriched genes were detected

under the parameters of min.pct = 0.5 and min.diff.pct = 0.3. The final cluster annotation was

333 validated by in situ hybridization experiments.

334 RaceID was used as an independent clustering approach to analyze rice scRNA-seq data.

335 RaceID has some improvements in detection of cell types with small cell number, offers batch

336 effect removal utilities, and optional imputing of gene expression. RaceID was run with the

following parameters: mintotal = 200, minexpr = 5, minnumber = 5, samp=1000, clustnr = 30,

- 338 FUNcluster="hclust", perplexity=60.

#### 340 Bulk RNA Sequencing and Analysis

Bulk RNA-seq was applied to RNA extracted from 3-day-old root tips (5 mm) of crown roots 341 342 and protoplasts, which were independently isolated from 3-day-old root tips (5 mm) of crown 343 roots from Nip and 93-11, respectively. RNA-seq was performed as previously described 344 (Liang et al., 2018). Briefly, total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit 345 (Oiagen). The amount and quality of RNA were tested by Oubit RNA Assay Kit (Life Technologies), gel electrophoresis and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit 348 (Illumina) was used to construct the library, and the amplified library was sequenced on 349 HiSeq X Ten platform. Sequence reads were trimmed using SOAPnuke (version 1.5.6) (Chen 350 et al., 2018), and aligned to the Nip and 93-11 reference genomes, respectively with HISAT2 351 (version 2.0.4) (Kim et al., 2019). Gene expression values were calculated on uniquely 352 mapped reads using HTSeq (version 0.11.2) (Anders et al., 2015), and DEseq2 (version 1.4.5) was used to calculate differentially expression (Fold change > 2 and P < 0.01) (Love et al., 353 354 2014).

## 388 Integrative Analysis of the Nip and 93-11 scRNA-seq Datasets

389 Normalized scRNA-seq data from Nip and 93-11 were combined into one object. Variable

390 genes were selected using the SCtransform function with default parameters. The

FindIntegrationAnchors function command used default parameters (dims = 1:30) to discover
integration anchors across all samples. The IntegrateData function was run on the anchor set
with default additional arguments. ScaleData and RunPCA were then performed on the
integrated assay to compute 20 principal components (PC). UMAP dimensionality reduction
was carried out and Shared Nearest Neighbour (SNN) graph constructed using dimensions
1:20 as input features and default PCA reduction. Clustering analysis was performed on the
Integrated assay at a resolution of 0.5.

### 399 **Pseudo-Time Analysis**

The Psudo-time analysis of cell differentiation and the determination of cell fate was 400 401 performed by the Monocle2 (v.2.10.0) R package (Trapnell et al., 2014). To explore the 402 developmental trajectory of specific cell types, subset of raw data with target clusters was 403 analyzed. The detailed process was as follow: Firstly, the variance in each gene's expression 404 across cells were calculated by the dispersionTable function. Variable genes were chosen 405 based on average expression level to define a developmental trajectory. Secondly, we reduced 406 the dimensionality of the data to two (set max components = 2, method = 'DDRTree). With 407 the expression data in a lower dimensional space, the state transition of single cells was 408 described by orderCells. The trajectory was plotted by plot cell trajectory in Monocle 2. To 409 specify a priori beginning of the trajectory, run orderCells again to set the root state argument. 410 Genes dynamically expressed along the pseudotime were clustered and visualized using the 411 plot pseudotime heatmap function. To determine genes that contribute to the branching of 412 developmental trajectory, branch point was selected. The differentialGeneTest function was 413 used to analyze the pseudo-time-dependent or branch-dependent genes. The genes that were 414 significantly branch-dependent were visualized by the plot genes branched heatmap 415 function.

### 417 Comparative Analysis of Root scRNA-seq Data from Rice and Arabidopsis 418 Arabidopsis root scRNA-seq data was obtained from (Zhang et al., 2019b). Diamond was 419 used to calculate pairwise similarity scores between Arabidopsis and rice for constructing 420 orthology groups (Buchfink et al., 2015). And the homology relationships were assessed using 421 InParanoid (Sonnhammer and Ostlund, 2015). InParanoid uses a clustering method based on 422 genome-wide pairwise sequence similarity matches to identify putative orthologous proteins 423 between two species and predicts ortholog groups, where each group contains one (the highest 424 sequence similarity matches) or more (with high pairwise similarity matches relative to the 425 best pair) pairs, including those in-paralogs within a defined cutoff value. Finally, a total of 426 9, 919 one-to-one orthologous gene pairs were kept for next analysis. An intersection set of 427 top 1,000 highly variable genes from each dataset that are expressed in all samples was used by FindIntegrationAnchors function command with other default parameters (dims = 1:20) to 428 429 discover integration anchors across all samples. The IntegrateData function was run on the 430 anchor set with default additional arguments. ScaleData and RunPCA were then performed on 431 the integrated assay to compute 20 principal components (PCs). UMAP dimensionality 432 reduction was carried out and Shared Nearest Neighbour (SNN) graph constructed using 433 dimensions 1:20 as input features and default PCA reduction. Clustering was performed on 434 the Integrated assay at a resolution of 0.2. Average expression values of each cluster were 435 log2-transformed and scatter plots and Pearson's correlations were calculated to compare 436 Arabidopsis and rice. The other approach was performed by using the 'Reference-based' 437 method in Seurat package. In the 'Reference-based' method, a subset of the scRNA-seq dataset (or a single dataset) are listed as a 'reference'. This method provides both advantage in 438 439 efficiency and in finding anchors. The Arabidopsis scRNA-seq results from two independent 440 studies (Ryu et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019b) were used as references. The functions 441 FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData were used for integrating and combining the 442 datasets before scaling, PCA and clustering.
