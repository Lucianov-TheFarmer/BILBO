# **A transcription factor coordinating internode elongation and photoperiodic signals in rice**

**Jorge Gómez-Ariza1 , Vittoria Brambilla1,2, Giulio Vicentini1 , Martina Landini1 , Martina Cerise1 , Esther Carrera3 , Roshi Shrestha1 , Remo Chiozzotto2 , Francesca Galbiati1 , Elisabetta Caporali1 , Isabel López Díaz  <sup>3</sup> and Fabio Fornara <sup>1</sup> \***

**In several plant species, inflorescence formation is accompanied by stem elongation. Both processes are accelerated in rice upon perception of shortening days. Here, we show that**  *PREMATURE INTERNODE ELONGATION 1* **(***PINE1***), encoding a rice zinc-finger transcription factor, reduces the sensitivity of the stem to gibberellin (GA). The florigens reduce** *PINE1* **expression to increase stem responsiveness to GA and promote flowering. These data indicate the existence of a regulatory network coordinating flowering and GA-dependent growth.**

Exposure of rice leaves to day lengths shorter than a critical threshold activates the expression of *Hd3a* (encoding HEADING DATE 3A) and *RFT1* (encoding RICE FLOWERING LOCUS T 1) in the companion cells of the phloem. The cognate proteins move through the vascular system of the plant to reach the shoot apical meristem (SAM), where they commit it to reproductive development[1](#page-4-0) . In cells of the SAM, Hd3a and RFT1 interact with proteins belonging to the 14-3-3 family and with the basic leucine zipper transcription factor OsFD1 (refs. [2,](#page-4-1)[3](#page-4-2) ). The resulting heterohexameric complex binds to the promoter of Os*MADS15*, a MADS-box transcription factor, whose activity is required for proper inflorescence development redundantly with Os*MADS14*, Os*MADS34*/*PAP2* and Os*MADS18* (refs. [4,](#page-4-3)[5](#page-4-4) ).

Commitment to reproductive growth of the SAM needs to be coordinated with stem elongation. This ensures that the emergence of the panicle from the protecting leaves (a process called heading in cereals) occurs as its development is complete. However, it is currently unclear whether regulatory genes exist that integrate photoperiodic and growth-promoting signals.

Elongation depends on the production and perception of gibberellins (GAs), to the extent that the metabolism of this hormone has been genetically manipulated by breeders in several species to modify plant heigh[t6](#page-4-5) . This led to the Green Revolution during the 1960s and represented a major advancement in agricultur[e7–](#page-4-6)[9](#page-4-7) . The common precursor of all GAs is converted to bioactive GAs by GA20 oxidases (GA20ox) and GA3o[x10](#page-4-8). Elongating internodes of rice express Os*GA20ox2* and Os*GA3ox2* in the meristematic and elongation zones at the base of the internodes, whereas their expression is reduced in the mature zone. This pattern corresponds to the gradient of growth and differentiation of cells in the internode[11.](#page-4-9)

We searched for genes that respond to variations in day length and that could account for early regulatory functions during transition of the SAM to reproductive growth. Different from previous studies that addressed transcriptomic changes at the SAM during floral inductio[n4,](#page-4-3)[5,](#page-4-4)[12,](#page-4-10) we sampled shoot apices at early stages, before any morphological change became visible. We developed a morphology-independent system to determine the temporal window of SAM commitment and used this information to guide sampling of apices for transcriptional profiling. Pools of plants of the cultivar Nipponbare (NB) were grown for 6weeks under non-inductive long days (LD) and transiently exposed to short days (SD) before returning them to LD. Expression of *Hd3a* and *RFT1* transcripts increased in leaves only under inductive photoperiods, but dropped to undetectable levels as soon as plants were returned to LD (Fig. [1b](#page-1-0)). At least 12 SD were required to irreversibly promote flowering of NB, whereas plants treated with less than 12 SD did not flower before the end of the experiment and behaved as plants maintained under continuous LD (Fig. [1a\)](#page-1-0). The same experiment was performed using Taichung 65 (T65), a photoperiod-insensitive cultivar harbouring mutations in *Hd1* and *EARLY HEADING DATE 1* (*Ehd1*), which encode transcriptional activators of the florigen[s13.](#page-4-11) Flowering could not be observed in T65 even after transient exposure to 18 SD and the expression of the florigens remained very low (Supplementary Fig. 1a,b). No major morphological changes could be observed at the SAM of NB before 12 SD, but during this time, the apex was fully committed to panicle formation (Supplementary Fig. 1g). Thus, sequencing experiments were performed on RNA time courses from shoot apices (Supplementary Fig. 1f) exposed to 0, 4, 8 and 12 SD, and differentially expressed genes were identified (Supplementary Data set 1). During commitment of the apex and after filtering for a log fold change (logFC) of >1.5 or <−1.5 (false discovery rate (FDR)≤0.05), we identified 301 upregulated and 222 downregulated genes across all time points (Fig. [1c](#page-1-0)). Among the ten most upregulated genes at the end of commitment, we identified Os*MADS14*, Os*MADS15* and Os*MADS34*, indicating activation of the panicle development programme (Supplementary Fig. 2). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on shoot apices of T65 identified 122 and 129 genes upregulated and downregulated, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data set 1).

We reasoned that downregulation of genes required to maintain vegetative features under LD must be crucial to induce the reproductive switch. We then searched for regulatory factors whose expression decreased in response to SD exposure. We identified the gene encoding the C2H2 zinc finger ZOS12-10 (LOC\_ Os12g42250) as the most downregulated transcription factor of the data set (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data set 1) and renamed it *PREMATURE INTERNODE ELONGATION 1* (*PINE1*). Transcript quantification in additional varieties suggested that SD-dependent reduction of expression is a general feature of *PINE1* (Fig. [1e](#page-1-0)). Transcription of *PINE1* was high in the full shoot apex, the SAM proper and the region below the SAM, almost undetectable in mature leaf blades and very low in mature culms and developing flowers (Fig. [1g](#page-1-0)). Diurnal time courses confirmed these findings and further indicated a biphasic rhythm at the shoot apex that dampened under SD (Supplementary Fig. 3e,f). Transgenic plants expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) under the control of the *PINE1* promoter showed fluorescence in the SAM and in young leaf primordia, as well as in tissues below the SAM (Fig. [1h,i\)](#page-1-0). GFP expression was not detected in the upper nodes, but examination of the basal part of the stem showed strong fluorescence at node 4 from the apex and in basal dividing cells of the internode (Fig. [1h,k,l](#page-1-0)). Expression of *PINE1* was reduced at the SAM when plants were exposed to SD, indicating sensitivity of its transcription to day length (Fig. [1g,j](#page-1-0) and Supplementary Fig. 3b–d). Finally, the nuclear localization of PINE1 was confirmed using a GFP-tagged protein expressed from the *35S* promoter in epidermal cells of tobacco leaves, consistent with PINE1 being a transcription factor (Supplementary Fig. 3a). These data indicate that *PINE1* is transcribed at the SAM and surrounding meristematic tissues and in central nodes and dividing cells of the compressed stem. Furthermore, its transcription depends on the photoperiod being reduced by SD. We next measured the expression of *PINE1* in apices of transgenic plants harbouring dexamethasone (dex)-inducible clones of *Hd3a* or *RFT1*, to assess whether its transcription depends on the florigens or other photoperiod-dependent but florigen-independent signal[s14.](#page-4-12) Transgenic lines harbouring either construct were grown under LD and treated with dex or mock. Expression of *PINE1* at the apex was quickly downregulated in response to dex in inducible lines but not in control lines, indicating that its transcriptional repression was dependent on Hd3a or RFT1 (Fig. [1f\)](#page-1-0).

To separate the effects of *PINE1* transcriptional repression from SD-dependent florigenic signals, we generated clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) mutant[s15](#page-4-13) and analysed them under LD. Three independent alleles (hereafter referred to as type 1) harboured indels (insertions or deletions) that created frameshifts, whereas two alleles maintained the reading frame (type 2 mutants) (Fig. [2a](#page-2-0)). Primary transformants bearing type 1 mutations showed a prostrate habit (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c). Removal of outer leaf sheaths indicated that prostrate growth was caused by premature elongation of the internodes that caused lodging. Excessive growth was observed also in subsequent generations in seedlings grown under LD (Fig. [2b\)](#page-2-0). Elongation became visible as early as 2weeks after germination and increased the distance between the SAM and the base of the plant during growth. Mutant plants elongated the central internodes first, followed by the most apical ones (Fig. [2b](#page-2-0)), similarly to the pattern of elongation shown by wild-type (WT) plants during panicle emergenc[e16](#page-4-14). Thus, we conclude that *PINE1* is a repressor of stem elongation, preventing elongation occurring before the reproductive switch, and that the photoperiod promotes elongation by reducing its transcription through florigen signalling. As *PINE1* mRNA expression is highest at the base of the stem, around the internodes that first elongate in the mutant, we suggest a cell-autonomous function for PINE1 to repress stem elongation. We also hypothesize that non-cell-autonomous information encoded by the florigens or by a florigen-dependent signal moves from the apex or the leaves to repress *PINE1* expression.

Transcriptional markers of panicle development were induced at the meristem only after exposure to SD (Supplementary Fig. 4d,e). Consistent with these observations, heading dates of *pine1-1* mutants were like the WT plants (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Transgenic rice plants expressing *PINE1* under the control of the *ACTIN2* promoter (pACT) developed panicles and flowers with aberrant morphologies (Supplementary Fig. 6a,b). At least three independent lines developed compact panicles bearing flowers with altered symmetry and duplications of floral organs (Supplementary Fig. 6b–f). Scanning electron microscopy revealed that in outer floral organs, paleas were converted into lemmas (Supplementary Fig. 7). In WT flowers, *PINE1* is expressed at very low levels, and these phenotypes could be caused by strong ectopic expression in floral meristems. The overexpressors were sterile, preventing the possibility of quantifying developmental defects in subsequent generations (Supplementary Fig. 6g–j). Thus, we expressed *PINE1* under the control of the *ORYZA SATIVA HOMEOBOX 1* (pOSH1) promoter that drives gene expression in the meristem and underlying stem tissues, including nodes and internode[s17](#page-4-15)[–19.](#page-4-16) *OSH1* is also expressed in floral meristems, yet panicles and flowers of pOSH1:PINE1 developed normally and were fertile. However, the emergence of the panicle was not complete, being limited by reduced elongation of the internodes (Supplementary Fig. 8). Taken together, these data indicate that *PINE1* is not affecting the floral transition per se, but rather the associated internode elongation that ejects the panicle out of the culm during heading. We conclude that *PINE1* coordinates the growth of the internodes downstream of *Hd3a* and *RFT1*, and that internode elongation and flowering can be genetically uncoupled.

Constitutive internode elongation in *pine1* mutants was rescued by treatments with an inhibitor of GA biosynthesis, suggesting that the phenotype is caused by increased GA abundance or by increased sensitivity to the hormone (Fig. [2b](#page-2-0)). To test these possibilities, we first quantified bioactive GAs in the stems of *pine1-1* mutants grown under LD. The abundance of GA1 and GA4 was reduced in *pine1-1* mutants compared to the WT plants in samples collected at zeitgeber 0 (Supplementary Fig. 9d,e). Quantifications performed on a segregating progeny of a *pine1-1a*/*pine1-2a* heterozygous plant corroborated the same difference of GA accumulation between elongating type 1 versus non-elongating type 2 alleles. Samples of this experiment were collected at ZT12, ruling out the possibility that the time of day could have an effect on the observed differences (Supplementary Fig. 9f,g). Expression of GA biosynthetic enzymes showed a mild increase in *pine1* mutants compared to the WT plants (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c), except for Os*GA20ox1* in *pine1-1a* mutants, whose expression was similar to the WT plants. As GA biosynthesis is characterized by negative-feedback regulation, increased expression of biosynthetic enzymes is correlated with the observed reduction of GA abundanc[e20,](#page-4-17)[21.](#page-4-18) Thus, the abundance of GA does not correlate with increased elongation.

Next, we quantified the sensitivity of the stem to GA under LD. To this end, 9-day-old NB WT, *pine1-1a* and *pine1-1c* plants were treated in vitro with paclobutrazol (PAC), to block endogenous GA biosynthesis, and GA3 was added to the growth medium at increasing concentrations. Internodes of NB do not elongate under LD in response to exogenously applied GA, regardless of PAC presence[22](#page-4-19) (Fig. [2c,d](#page-2-0)). Conversely, GA3 induced strong elongation of *pine1-1* stems that was proportional to the concentration of GA3 (Fig. [2c,d](#page-2-0)). Measurements of total stem length indicated that GA-treated *pine1-1* mutants were as long or longer than the corresponding untreated controls (Fig. [2d](#page-2-0) and Supplementary Fig. 9h). We conclude that *PINE1* prevents the stem from responding to GA before the onset of the floral transition. Reduction of *PINE1* levels by *Hd3a* and *RFT1* allows elongation and coordinates stem growth with conversion of the meristem into a panicle (Supplementary Fig. 10).

#### **Methods**

**Plant material and growth conditions.** Two *temperate japonica* cultivars of rice (*Oryza sativa*) were used in this study: NB and T65. NB bears functional alleles of *Hd1* and *Ehd1*, whereas T65 bears non-functional alleles (*hd1* and *ehd1*)[13](#page-4-11). Plants were grown under controlled LD (16-h light/8-h dark) or SD (10-h light/14-h dark) conditions with day and night temperatures set at 28 °C and 24 °C, respectively. Diurnal LD time courses were performed at 14.5-h light/9.5-h dark conditions. Leaves, fowers and culms (including nodes and internodes) were sampled using scissors and surgical knives. Shoot apices and SAMs were sampled using a stereomicroscope using scalpels as described in ref. [5](#page-4-4) . In this work, the term shoot apex indicates samples as represented in Supplementary Fig. 1f, whereas the SAM indicates only the meristematic dome. Plant samples for downstream nucleic acid preparations were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Heading date measurements were performed using 8–12 plants per genotype.

**RNA isolation and analysis of gene expression.** RNA from SAMs was extracted using the NucleoSpin RNA Plant Kit (Macherey-Nagel), whereas RNA from shoot apices, leaves, flowers and culms was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). The cDNA was synthesized using a Superscript II Kit (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's instructions. Quantification of gene expression by real-time PCR was performed using the Maxima SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific) in twin.tec real-time PCR plates (Eppendorf), under the conditions described in ref. [23](#page-4-20). All quantifications were calculated based on technical triplicates. Biological samples collected independently gave similar results. At least one biological replicate was collected for each experiment. In situ hybridizations were performed according to the protocol described in ref. [24.](#page-4-21) Primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

**RNA-seq and bioinformatics.** Three biological replicates composed of at least 8 shoot apices of NB and T65 were collected at 0, 4, 8 and 12d after shift from LD to SD (two replicates for T65 at 12 d). Total RNA was extracted using the Pure Link RNA Mini Kit (Ambion). Sequencing was performed at GATC Biotech (Germany) using an Illumina HiSeq 2000. RNA-seq yielded 12–29millions of cleaned 51-bp and 101-bp single reads (Supplementary Data set 1). The quality of the raw data was checked with FastQC [\(http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/\)](http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). About 94% of the cleaned reads were mapped on the NB genome version 7.0 [\(http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/](http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/)) using bowtie2 and TopHat2 (refs. [25,](#page-4-22)[26](#page-4-23)), counted by HTSe[q27](#page-4-24) and then used for gene-level differential expression analysis using R (version 3.1.0) and the edgeR package (version 3.6.4) for statistical computin[g28](#page-4-25)[,29](#page-4-26). Only features with more than 1 read per million in at least 3 samples were retained, for a total of 20,626 genes expressed across all conditions. Differentially expressed genes were called using a 5% FDR control and filtered for logFC≤−1.5 or logFC≥1.5 in at least one time point of the time course compared to non-induced apices. Gene Ontology enrichment was calculated using PANTHE[R30](#page-4-27) from the GOC website ([http://geneontology.org\)](http://geneontology.org). Data have been submitted to the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) with the series record number [GSE90493](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE90493).

**Vector construction and plant transformation.** The *PINE1* coding sequence was amplified from cDNA using primers reported in Supplementary Table 1, purified and recombined into a pDONR201 vector to create a *PINE1* entry clone using BP recombinase (Gateway). The entry clone was recombined with an LR reaction into pGWB6 to create a GFP-PINE1 amino-terminal fusion protein, expressed under the control of the viral *35S* promoter in transient assays in tobacco. LR recombination was used to introduce the same coding sequence into the pOSH1:Gateway and pACTIN:Gateway destination vectors that have been previously describe[d14.](#page-4-12) Mutations in *PINE1* were introduced using CRISPR– CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) mutagenesis. The *PINE1* protospacer (5′-GCGTCTCCTTCCATTCCTCC-3′) was cloned into the pOs-sgRNA vector using primers PINE1-CRISPR-F and PINE1-CRISPR-R, and then recombined into the pH-Ubi-Cas9-7 vector using an LR reaction[15](#page-4-13). The *PINE1* promoter was amplified using the pPINE1-F and pPINE1-R primers, bearing HindIII and BamHI restriction sites, respectively, and cloned into the pGEM T-Easy vector (Promega). The restriction sites were used to introduce the promoter in pGWB6, substituting the *35S* promoter.

NB seeds were used to induce callus formation, and the EHA105 *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* strain was used to infiltrate calli. Transgenic calli were isolated from non-transgenic calli by growing them on selection media supplemented with hygromycin in 2 rounds of selection (50mg l −1 and 100mg l −1 hygromycin, respectively) as described in ref. [31.](#page-4-28) Plants regenerated from transgenic calli were grown in rooting medium for 2weeks before transferring to soil in the greenhouse.

**DNA extraction and genotyping.** DNA extraction and PCR analyses were performed as described in ref. [23](#page-4-20). Primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

**Scanning electron microscopy.** NB and *pACT2:PINE1* overexpressing lines were grown in the greenhouse under LD conditions and apical meristems were sampled after 16, 18, 20 and 22 d after shifting to SD. At least three replicates were collected for each sampling. Samples were fixed for 24 h at 4 °C in FAE solution (10% formaldehyde, 5% acetic acid and 50% ethanol). Fixed tissues were washed with water and post-fixed with aqueous 2% osmium tetroxide for 2 h at room temperature. Tissues were rinsed several times in deionized water and dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol for 15 min per rinse. This step was followed by critical point drying with liquid CO2 and sputter-coating with gold in a Nanotech sputter coater. Specimens were analysed using a LEO 1430 Scanning Electron Microscope.

**GA quantification and treatments.** Rice seeds of WT NB and *pine1*-mutant lines were sown on soil in a growth chamber, under LD conditions. Seedlings were treated with PAC after 5d from germination. Five ml per plant of 10μM PAC were supplied directly to the soil twice per week for 6weeks. Mock-treated plants were used as controls. For the quantification of *pine1* internode sensitivity, NB and *pine1-1*-mutant seeds were surface sterilized with bleach, rinsed and germinated on 1/2 MS medium in Eco2box (Duchefa). After 9d from plating on MS, boxes containing at least 10 plants each were treated with a 10ml solution containing 10μM PAC or PAC 10μM+GA3 10μM, 50μM or 100μM. Pictures were taken after 14d from the beginning of treatments, and internodes were measured using the ImageJ software. Quantification of GA1 and GA4 was performed on 20-day-old stems collected at the base of the culm, according to the protocol described in ref. [32](#page-4-29). GA quantifications were performed in triplicates of eight plants each.

**Imaging.** Confocal images were obtained using a Nikon A1 Confocal Laser Microscope with a Nikon A1 Plus Camera. Three independent pPINE1:GFP transgenic lines were imaged, giving similar patterns of expression. Pictures of plants and seedlings were taken using a Nikon D3200.
