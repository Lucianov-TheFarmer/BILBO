# 24 ABSTRACT

- 25 As multifaceted molecules, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are known to accumulate in response to various stresses. Ozone  $(O_3)$  is an air pollutant with detrimental effect on plants, and  $O_3$  can also be used as a tool 26 27 to study the role of ROS in signaling. Genetic variation of O<sub>3</sub> sensitivity in different Arabidopsis accessions 28 highlights the complex genetic architecture of plant responses to ROS. To investigate the genetic basis of O<sub>3</sub> 29 sensitivity, a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population between two Arabidopsis accessions with distinct  $O_3$ 30 sensitivity, C24 (O<sub>3</sub> tolerant) and Te (O<sub>3</sub> sensitive), was used for quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping. 31 Through analysis of QTL mapping combined with transcriptome changes in response to O<sub>3</sub>, we identified 32 three causal QTLs and several potential candidate genes regulating the response to O<sub>3</sub>. Based on gene 33 expression data, water loss and stomatal conductance measurement, we found that a combination of relatively low stomatal conductance and constitutive activation of salicylic acid (SA) mediated defense 34 signaling were responsible for the O<sub>3</sub> tolerance in C24. Application of exogenous SA prior to O<sub>3</sub> exposure 35 36 can mimic the constitutive SA signaling in C24, and could attenuate O<sub>3</sub> induced leaf damage in the sensitive 37 Arabidopsis accessions Te and Cvi-0.
- 38
- 39 Key-words: cell death, ozone, QTL mapping, salicylic acid, stomatal conductance, transcriptome analysis

# 42 INTRODUCTION

43 Being sessile, plants have evolved sophisticated signaling systems to cope with various biotic and abiotic 44 stresses in the ever-changing environment. Many stresses cause increased production of reactive oxygen 45 species (ROS) as well as increased concentrations of cytosolic free calcium and various hormones such as 46 abscisic acid (ABA), ethylene, jasmonic acid (JA) and salicylic acid (SA) that are used as signaling molecules 47 (Overmyer et al., 2003). ROS were long considered as harmful byproducts of physiological metabolism. 48 However, ROS are now regarded as essential integral signaling components for defense responses and 49 several developmental pathways (Van Breusegem and Dat, 2006; Suzuki et al., 2012). The air pollutant 50 ozone ( $O_3$  – the triatomic form of oxygen) has long been studied in the context of plant physiology due to 51 its negative impact on growth and ultimately yield of important crop species (Wilkinson et al., 2012). The 52 effects of  $O_3$  include leaf injury, stomatal closure and reduced photosynthesis (Wilkinson et al., 2012; Kangasjärvi et al., 2005). To understand the basis of  $O_3$  sensitivity, the comparison of sensitive versus 53 54 tolerant varieties of the same species has given valuable information, including the role of stomatal 55 conductance (Brosché et al., 2010), stress hormones such as ethylene (Vahala et al., 2003), antioxidants 56 (Puckette et al., 2007) and changes in gene expression (Puckette et al., 2008; Mahalingam et al., 2006;

57 Street et al., 2011).

In addition to the influence of  $O_3$  on plant productivity,  $O_3$  is an important tool to study plant responses that are regulated by ROS. Many experimental systems designed to study ROS signaling require extensive manipulation of the plant, for example infiltration of  $H_2O_2$  or the Xanthine/Xanthine oxidase enzymatic system (Vaahtera et al., 2014).  $O_3$  can be used non-invasively to trigger apoplastic ROS production and activation of defense signaling (Wohlgemuth et al., 2002). This makes  $O_3$  a valuable tool to study the complexity of defense-signaling and leaf injury formation in plants.

64 Mutant analysis in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana has revealed some of the mechanisms involved in regulation of the responses to O<sub>3</sub>. Mutants with decreased antioxidant capacity, defective JA biosynthesis 65 66 or signaling, or more open stomata show increased leaf injury after O<sub>3</sub> treatment (Conklin et al., 2000; 67 Kangasjärvi et al., 2005; Overmyer et al., 2008). The role of another stress hormone, SA, is more complex. 68 Decreasing the levels of SA by the expression of salicylate hydroxylase (NahG) in one of the most  $O_3$ 69 sensitive Arabidopsis accessions Cvi-0, significantly reduced O<sub>3</sub>-induced cell death (Rao et al, 2000). In 70 contrast, expression of NahG in Col-0 turns this normally tolerant accession  $O_3$  sensitive (Rao and Davis, 71 1999). Furthermore, expression of NahG in the  $O_3$  sensitive mutant rcd1 (in Col-0 background), makes this

72 mutant more tolerant to  $O_3$  (Overmyer et al., 2005).

73 Increased levels of ROS and SA are a hallmark of programmed cell death. A general model emerging from 74 mutant analyses and stress experiments highlights a positive feedback loop between ROS and SA which 75 amplifies signals leading to defense or cell death (Overmyer et al., 2003; Vlot et al., 2009). Exposure of 76 Arabidopsis to  $O_3$  or  $H_2O_2$  activates biosynthesis of SA and activates hypersensitive cell death, and 77 incubation of leaves with SA increases H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> levels in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Leon et al., 1995; 78 Rao et al., 1997; Overmyer et al., 2005). Extensive screens for SA binding proteins revealed that SA could 79 alter  $H_2O_2$  accumulation through binding and inhibiting ROS scavengers including the  $H_2O_2$  degrading 80 enzyme catalase (CAT) and cytosolic ascorbate peroxidase (APX) (Chen et al., 1993; Li et al., 2013; Kumar, 81 2014). However the interrelationship between SA signaling and ROS signaling is not limited to a positive 82 feedback relationship. Different subcellular sources of ROS production induce different responses 83 (Vaahtera et al., 2014); and in the case of apoplastic ROS production, plants are capable of prioritizing 84 responses between ROS signaling and SA signaling through antagonistic mechanisms (Xu and Brosché, 85 2014).

86 Many mutants with constitutively activated defense response and spontaneous cell death are collectively 87 named lesion mimic mutants. The cell death in these mutants is often associated with increased 88 accumulation of SA and ROS (Lorrain et al., 2003). In lesion-stimulating disease1 (lsd1), the elevated 89 superoxide and SA are necessary and sufficient to induce light-dependent spreading of cell death (Jabs et 90 al., 1996; Torres et al., 2005), which is probably regulated through the SA-dependent protein interaction 91 between LSD1 and catalases (CAT1, 2, 3) (Li et al., 2013). Another mutant, hypersensitive response-like 92 *lesions* 1 (*hrl1*) is also characterized by spontaneous cell death, accumulation of  $H_2O_2$  and superoxide, and 93 constitutive expression of SA/JA/ethylene defense genes (Devadas et al., 2002). Increased SA and  $H_2O_2$  in 94 null mutants of mitogen activated protein (MAP) kinase kinase kinase MEKK1 and the MAP kinase MPK4 95 reveal that signaling through MAP kinases could play a role either upstream of SA or as part of an SA-96 positive feedback loop (Nakagami et al., 2006; Pitzschke et al., 2009). At the transcriptome level, mutants 97 with reduced SA levels (*NahG*, *ald1*, *sid2*) have increased expression levels of several  $O_3$  responsive genes 98 (Blomster et al., 2011; Xu and Brosché, 2014). These observations indicated that the interaction between 99 ROS and SA could vary under different conditions and depending on which parameter is measured.

The leaf injury caused by O<sub>3</sub> is the result of activation of programmed cell death (Overmyer et al., 2005).
 Arabidopsis mutant analysis for altered O<sub>3</sub> responses has been largely restricted to the laboratory strain
 Col-0. Natural variation in O<sub>3</sub> sensitivity of Arabidopsis accessions provides a promising alternative to find
 additional knowledge on the molecular details of O<sub>3</sub> action (Brosché et al., 2010). Quantitative trait locus
 (QTL) mapping of F2 or recombinant inbred line (RIL) populations derived from crosses between two or
 more parental accessions, is still the most popular way to identify genomic regions that confer complex
 phenotypic traits. However, identification of the underlying genes in the identified QTL genomic regions can

- 107 be a long and laborious task. As a complement, quantification of RNA expression levels and the detection of 108 novel transcripts could speed up this process. For instance, gene expression analysis in the QTL regions 109 identified by a RIL population from reciprocal cross between two accessions (C24 and Te) showing high 110 variation in freezing tolerance, identified a novel negative regulator of freezing tolerance REVEILLE1 111 (Meissner et al., 2013). The vast majority of RNA expression analyses for O<sub>3</sub> responses have been 112 performed with full-length cDNA arrays or oligonucleotide arrays which limit the analysis to known 113 transcripts (Heidenreich et al., 2005; Mahalingam et al., 2006; Ahlfors et al., 2009; Blomster et al., 2011). 114 Compared to these techniques RNA-sequencing has several advantages to explore the transcriptome and 115 de novo transcript changes, such as greater sensitivity, larger coverage and detection of single nucleotide 116 polymorphisms (SNP) (Wang et al., 2009). 117 In this study, we utilized both classic QTL mapping and RNA-seq to analyze O<sub>3</sub> induced changes of transcriptome and cell death in accessions C24 and Tenela (Te). We would like to answer 118 1) What are the genetic bases of O<sub>3</sub> induced cell death in Arabidopsis accessions other than Col-0? 119 120 2) How does transcriptome-reprogramming influence O<sub>3</sub> sensitivity and which biological processes have 121 altered expression in C24 and Te? 122 3) What is the role of SA signaling in regulating  $O_3$  response and cell death in  $O_3$  sensitive and tolerant 123 accessions?

# 125 MATERIAL AND METHODS

#### 126 Plant materials and growth conditions

Seeds of *Arabidopsis thaliana* accessions C24, Tenela (Te), Cvi-0, Col-0 were obtained from Nottingham
Arabidopsis Stock Centre (NASC). A RIL population of 316 individual lines was previously generated via
reciprocal crossing between C24 and Te (Meissner et al., 2013). One F2 population was created by crossing
between C24 (pollen donor) and Te; and one backcross population was made by crossing between C24
(pollen donor) and the RIL CT101.

- 132 Seeds were sown on 1:1 peat: vermiculite, stratified for three days, and then grown at 22°C/19°C for a
- 133 week. Subsequently the geminated seedlings were transplanted into new 1:1 peat: vermiculite mixture,
- 134 one plant per pot (6cm x 6cm), and eight plants per genotype. All plants were randomly placed, grown in

- 135 controlled chamber (Weiss Bio1300; Weiss Gallenkamp), grown at 22°C/19°C, and relative humidity of
- 136 70%/90%, under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle for two weeks.

#### 137 SA treatment and quantification of cell death and ROS accumulation

- 138 To explore the effect of SA on regulation of cell death upon O<sub>3</sub> treatment, three weeks old plants (C24, Te,
- 139 CT101, Col-0 and Cvi-0) were treated with 0.1mM and 1mM SA for 16 h before O<sub>3</sub> treatment. Samples were
- 140 harvested immediately after 6 hours of O<sub>3</sub> treatment and leaf damage was quantified with ion leakage
- 141 (Overmyer et al. 2000). Trypan blue staining was performed as previously described (Cui et al., 2013).
- 142 Visualization of hydrogen peroxide  $(H_2O_2)$  in the leaf was performed with DAB (3,3'-Diaminobenzidine)
- staining as previously described (Cui et al., 2013). The experiments were repeated three times.

#### 144 Stomatal conductance

Stomatal conductance of 23-27 days old C24, Tenela and CT101 plants was recorded with a custom-built gas-exchange measurement system (Kollist et al., 2007). After two hours, when stomatal conductance had stabilized, 400 nmol mol<sup>-1</sup> ozone was applied to plants for 4h. Ozone uptake rate and cumulative ozone dose were calculated as previously described (Kollist et al., 2007). Four biological replicates and 10-11 technical replicates for each genotype were used for analysis.

#### 150 RNA isolation and quality inspection

Ten to 15 rosettes per genotype (Col-0, C24, Te, CT101) from control and two-hour O<sub>3</sub> treatment (350 nL L<sup>-1</sup>)
 were pooled and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Three biological replicates of RNA were isolated with GeneJet
 Plant RNA purification Mini Kit (Fermentas, now part of Thermo Scientific). Quality and quantity of RNA was
 assessed using Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and NanoDrop spectrophotometer.

#### 155 RNA-seq and data analysis

156 Three biological repeats were used for RNA-seq analysis. The library preparation and next-generation 157 sequencing of transcriptome were done at the Finnish Microarray and Sequencing Centre core facility. 158 Libraries were prepared from 100 ng of total RNA with Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit v2 according to 159 the kit manual. Briefly, the mRNAs were purified and fragmented and used as a template for synthesis of 160 double stranded cDNA. Subsequently, end-repair of the cDNA molecules was carried out after which the 3'-161 ends were adenylated. The adapters containing sample-specific indexes were ligated and the samples were 162 PCR amplified and purified. The samples were normalized, pooled and the automated cluster preparation was carried out with Illumina c-Bot station. The samples were sequenced with 1x50 bp chemistry and 163 164 Illumina HiSeg2500 instrument. Raw data can be accessed at Gene Expression Omnibus 165 (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) with accession number GSE61542. RNA-seq data analysis was

- 166 performed with several packages in the JAVA-based client-server system Chipster (Kallio et al., 2011).
- 167 Briefly, the quality of raw reads (51bp) was firstly inspected with FastQC (Andrews, 2010), then reads were
- 168 trimmed (first 15 nucleotides) and filtered with FastX package (Gordon and Hannon, 2010). Next, the single
- 169 end reads were aligned to Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 Genome reference (TAIR 10.22) using Bowtie 2 with
- default setting, allowing two mismatches (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). The resulting BAM files were
- then sorted and indexed with SAMtools package (Li et al., 2009). Afterwards, the number of reads mapped
- to each gene was calculated with HTSeq package (Anders, 2014). Subsequently, the generated counts of
- the reads were normalized with Bioconductor DESeq packages in R (Anders et al., 2010), and the
- 174 normalized counts were subjected to principal component analysis (PCA) as implemented in this package.
- 175 To determine differential expression, normalized counts were converted to log-cpm (counts per million)
- 176 with voom for linear modeling (Law et al., 2014). Gene expression of each genotype (C24, Te and CT101)
- was computed as  $\log_2$  fold changes between treatment and control, or between Col-0 and each genotype
- 178 (C24, Te and CT101) in clean air, using Bioconductor limma package in R (Smyth, 2005). GO term
- 179 enrichment was performed using the GO website with default setting
- 180 (http://bioinfo.cau.edu.cn/agriGO/analysis.php) (Du et al., 2010). Venn diagrams were generated using
- 181 Venny (http://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) (Oliveros, 2007).

# 197 **QTL** mapping and phenotyping of $O_3$ and water loss traits

- 198 Three weeks old plants, eight plants per RIL, parents (C24 and Te) and Col-0 and Cvi-0 (O<sub>3</sub> tolerant and sensitive controls) were exposed to 400 nL  $L^{-1}O_3$  for 6 h. The average number of 201 damaged leaves for each line was calculated for further QTL mapping. For phenotyping of the water loss 202 trait, two fully-expanded leaves (five plants per line) under clean air condition were detached and placed 203 abaxial side up in plastic tray at room temperature for 2 h. Water loss was measured as the ratio of weight 204 of detached leaves 2 h after cutting/total weight of detached leaves onset of cutting. The mean of the 205 normalized ratio to Te was used as input of phenotyping data for QTL mapping of water loss trait.
- 206 Genotyping and genetic map construction of RIL population were performed as previously described 207 (Meissner et al., 2013). For the development of SNP markers in the F2 and backcross populations (C24xTe 208 and C24xCT101), the Indexed BAM file of C24, Te and CT101 generated from Chipster were loaded into the 209 Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) and compared to Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 genome reference 210 (Tair10)(Robinson et al., 2011). The SNPs between C24 and Te/CT101 were visualized on chromosome 3 211 and the end of chromosome 2. dCAPS marker primer design and restriction enzyme selection were 212 performed in website (http://helix.wustl.edu/dcaps/dcaps.html) (Neff et al., 2002). Joinmap 3 (Van Ooijen & Voorrips 2001) and MapQTL 6 (Van Ooijen and Kyazma, 2009) 216 were used with default settings for construction of genetic linkage map and QTL analysis, respectively. The 217 genome-wide significance level for a QTL (P<0.05) was calculated by performing 1000 permutations using MapQTL 6. 218

# 219 **RESULTS**

#### 220 Phenotyping and QTL mapping for the response to $O_3$ and water loss

221 Most of the molecular details on the action of  $O_3$  in plants is derived from *Arabidopsis thaliana*, in particular 222 the accessions Col-0 and Ws and mutants in these backgrounds. Natural variation in the response to  $O_3$  in A. 223 thaliana was previously studied in a set of 93 accessions (Brosché et al., 2010). Among several traits 224 examined, including hormone and antioxidant levels,  $O_3$  induced leaf damage was positively correlated with 225 stomatal conductance and negatively correlated with the concentration of the stress hormone jasmonic 226 acid (JA) in one of the most  $O_3$  sensitive accessions, Cvi-0 (Brosché et al, 2010). To gain further insight into 227 the genetic basis of O<sub>3</sub> leaf damage and to move beyond the usual lab strain Col-0, two Arabidopsis accessions,  $O_3$  tolerant C24 and  $O_3$  sensitive Tenela (Te), were selected for this study. Notably a 228 229 recombinant inbred lines (RIL) population from a reciprocal cross between C24 and Te was developed for 230 mapping of freezing tolerance QTLs (Meissner et al., 2013), which offered a resource for our mapping

- approach. As expected from the parental phenotypic response to  $O_3$ , large variation in  $O_3$  induced leaf
- damage was observed in the RIL population illustrated by the O<sub>3</sub> hypersensitive RIL line CT101 (Fig. 1a and
- Figure S1). QTL mapping was performed using 316 RILs on  $O_3$  induced leaf damage, and since one
- determinant of O<sub>3</sub> damage relates to stomatal function (Brosché et al., 2010); phenotypic analysis with
- 235 respect to stomatal function was performed by measuring water loss in detached leaves.

236 For O<sub>3</sub> damage we detected three QTL regions on chromosomes 2 and 3, and three QTL regions on 237 chromosomes 1, 2 and 3 regarding stomatal regulation (Fig. 1b). The percentage of phenotypic variance 238 explained by the three identified QTL regions ranged from 3.7% to 9.4%. Consistent with the correlation 239 between O<sub>3</sub> damage and stomatal conductance (Brosché et al, 2010), two overlapping QTL regions between 240 the two traits were detected on the end of chromosomes 2 and 3, suggesting a phenotypic and genotypic 241 correlation between stomatal regulation and O<sub>3</sub> induced leaf damage. Moreover, the QTL region detected 242 on chromosome 2 overlaps with a previously identified  $O_3$  sensitivity and water loss QTL from Cvi-0 243 (Brosché et al, 2010). In order to verify target loci that control  $O_3$  induced responses, further QTL analysis 244 was performed using F2 and backcross populations: C24 crossed with Te, and C24 crossed with CT101. Given the lack of an available Te genomic sequence, very few published markers could be used for further 245 246 genotyping (Păcurar et al., 2012). However, since we sequenced the transcriptomes of C24, CT101 and Te 247 (see more below) we could use RNA-seq data to identify SNPs and design new markers. The parental lines 248 and CT101 were aligned against the reference Col-0 genome sequences using Chipster (See methods). SNPs 249 were searched in/nearby the target QTL regions on chromosomes 2 and 3 by Integrative Genomics Viewer 250 (IGV)(Robinson et al., 2011). Together with the published INDEL (UPSC2-18415) and three SSLP (bio2, F1P2, 251 nga162) markers (Păcurar et al., 2012), ten new dCAPS markers were designed for linkage map construction 252 of the F2 and backcross populations (Fig. 1c). Based on the mean for  $O_3$  induced leaf damage in 188 253 individuals from each of the F2 and backcross populations, the same three QTL regions as in the original 254 mapping were detected on chromosome 2 and 3. Each QTL region covers approximately 14.9 CM on 255 average, and the  $O_3$  sensitive alleles were from Te.

#### 256 Stomatal conductance correlates with O<sub>3</sub> induced leaf damage

Partly overlapping QTL areas for stomatal regulation with O<sub>3</sub> induced leaf damage indicate the amount of O<sub>3</sub> entering the plant as one determinant of O<sub>3</sub> damage (Fig. 1b). Water loss was used for QTL mapping since it is a fast method applicable to hundreds of plants required for QTL mapping. To more directly determine the role of stomata in O<sub>3</sub> damage we measured stomatal conductance and O<sub>3</sub> uptake rate in parental lines and CT101. Indeed, stomatal conductance was strongly correlated with O<sub>3</sub> induced leaf damage in the tested lines, and the hypersensitive CT101 exhibited the highest stomatal conductance (Fig. 2a). O<sub>3</sub> treatment of Arabidopsis leads to both rapid and long term stomatal closure (Ahlfors et al., 2004;

- Kollist et al., 2007: Vahisalu et al., 2010). Accordingly, the stomata closed in the parental lines and CT101
- when treated with O<sub>3</sub> (Fig 2A). However, the closure was fastest in CT101 and at the end of the O<sub>3</sub> exposure
- the stomatal conductance was close to C24 (Fig 2A). Overall, the O<sub>3</sub> uptake rate and the total cumulative O<sub>3</sub>
- 267 dose in Te and CT101 were higher than in C24 (Fig. 2B), which could contribute to the increased leaf
- 268 damage in Te and CT101.

# Transcriptome analysis unravels metabolic mechanisms and cellular processes altered in response to O<sub>3</sub>

- 271 Leaf damage in Arabidopsis caused by O<sub>3</sub> is the result of activation of programmed cell death (PCD), and is 272 at least partially executed via changes in gene expression, since cell death in an O<sub>3</sub> sensitive mutant can be 273 blocked by addition of an inhibitor of transcription (Overmyer et al., 2005). To gain further insights into 274 molecular mechanisms regulated by  $O_3$ , including potential regulators of PCD, we performed RNA-seq 275 analysis of C24, Te and CT101 after 2 h  $O_3$  and the corresponding 2 h control. The 2 h time point was 276 selected since it is the time point where  $O_3$  induces maximal changes in gene expression (Blomster et al., 277 2011); and the 2 h time point is more informative about outcomes in  $O_3$  cell death than later time points 278 (Broschélet al., 2014). For comparison, also Col-0 was sequenced to give further insights into the difference 279 between C24 and Te. Most of the analysis of Col-0 RNA-seq data will be published elsewhere. To visualize 280 the difference of O<sub>3</sub> sensitivity at the global transcriptome level, principal component analysis (PCA) was 281 applied to the parental lines and CT101 using DESeq (See material and methods). As shown in Fig. 3a, three 282 replicates of each genotype under each condition were close to each other based on normalized RNA-seq 283 data. O<sub>3</sub> treated samples were clearly separated from clean air samples in component 1 (PC1). Compared to 284 the sensitive genotypes, the difference between  $O_3$  treated and untreated C24 were relatively smaller, 285 suggesting an attenuated effect of  $O_3$  on C24 at the gene expression level. CT101 was in between the 286 parental lines and relatively closer to the sensitive parent Te in direction of genotype component (PC2). 287 Given the high similarity of three independent biological repeats (Fig. 3a), genes with significant changes in 288 transcript levels by O<sub>3</sub> were identified based on Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate, FDR<0.1 289 (Supporting Information Table S2), and a Venn diagram was used to reveal the unique and common 290 expression changes among the three genotypes. As shown in Figs. 3b and 3c, a high percentage of genes 291 were shared between sensitive and tolerant genotypes in response to  $O_3$  (3538 genes with increased
- expression and 2111 genes with decreased expression). There were also many genes with genotype specific
- expression; 187, 765 and 408 genes with increased expression in C24, Te and CT101, respectively.
- To gain deeper insight into physiological processes targeted by O<sub>3</sub>, we analyzed the GO term enrichment of
   the significantly regulated genes using agriGO (Du et al., 2010). Significantly enriched GO terms among

296 genes with respect to their expression to  $O_3$  were identified, including GO biological process (BP), cellular 297 component (CC) and molecular function (MF) (selected categories are shown in Figs. 3b and 3c, Supporting 298 Information Table S3 and S4 for full lists). In the shared genes with increased expression among the three 299 genotypes, enriched GO categories were related to defense response and various stimulus (BP), mainly 300 occurring in the membrane system (CC) and involved in activation of kinases and transferases (MF). In the 301 over 2000 common genes with decreased expression, the most enriched GO terms were highly associated 302 with energy metabolism, such as photosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism and hormone responses. This 303 indicates the presence of a fine-tuned trade-off between defense and growth, rapidly activated through 304 transcriptome reprogramming after 2 h  $O_3$  exposure.

#### 306 Elevated expression of SA related genes in C24

307 As a central signaling hub in various biotic and abiotic stress responses, production of the hormone salicylic 308 acid (SA) triggers multiple signaling networks leading to transcriptome reprogramming. C24 has previously 309 been shown to accumulate higher levels of SA than other accessions (Bechtold et al., 2010). To explore the 310 role of SA signaling in C24, Te and CT101, the GO category salicylic acid GO (461 genes, Supporting 311 Information Table S5) was selected for cluster analysis with various experiments related to stress and 312 hormone signaling (See material and methods). The expression changes (fold changes) for C24, Te and 313 CT101 in control conditions were calculated using Col-0 as the baseline reference. In addition the GO term 314 biological process was analyzed in all clusters (Supporting Information Table S5).

315 Five major clusters were obtained (Fig 4a), and consistent with the PCA analysis (Fig. 3a), Te and CT101 in 316 control conditions were clustered together without any major changes in gene expression. In stark contrast, 317 increased expression of many genes, especially in cluster 1 and 5, was indicative of activated SA and 318 defense signaling in control C24. - 332 SA was previously described as both a positive and negative regulator of O<sub>3</sub> induced cell death (Rao and 333 Davis, 1999). The genotype, dose and timing of SA treatment and other factors likely contribute to the 334 function of SA in cell death. - 341 Collectively, these observations imply an essential role of SA in signaling and regulating ROS homeostasis,

## 398 **DISCUSSION**

- 399 The responses of Arabidopsis to O<sub>3</sub> have been extensively studied through mutant analyses. O<sub>3</sub> sensitivity 400 can be a consequence of decreased antioxidant capacity; more open stomata and/or altered regulation of 401 cell death (Conklin et al., 2000; Overmyer et al., 2005; Overmyer et al., 2008). All mutant analyses for O<sub>3</sub> 402 sensitivity have so far been performed in the accessions Col-0 or Ws. A complementary approach to study 403 the responses of Col-0 is to use other Arabidopsis accessions coming from diverse geographic origins. We 404 used C24 from Portugal, an accession with increased pathogen resistance (Lapin et al., 2012) and Te from Finland, an accession with high freezing tolerance (Hannah et al., 2006). C24 is among the most  $O_3$  tolerant 405 406 of Arabidopsis accessions while Te is  $O_3$  sensitive (Brosché et al., 2010; Fig. 1). We used QTL mapping and 407 transcriptome analysis to identify the mechanisms behind  $O_3$  tolerance and sensitivity.
- 408 QTL mapping revealed three QTLs for  $O_3$  sensitivity on chromosome 2 and 3. Given the relationship
- between stomatal function and  $O_3$  sensitivity we also mapped QTLs for water loss and two QTLs were
- 410 overlapping. This reinforces the crucial role of stomata as the first line of defense against air pollutants.
- 411 However, the more closed stomata of C24 are most likely not the only reason for the extreme tolerance of
- 412 C24. One of the O<sub>3</sub> sensitivity QTLs was not overlapping with water loss indicating that stomatal
- 413 conductance is not the exclusive factor in determining the O<sub>3</sub> sensitivity, which is consistent with our earlier
- 414 finding that the accession Est-1 exhibited similar stomatal conductance as C24 but is more sensitive to O<sub>3</sub>
- 415 (Brosché et al., 2010). O<sub>3</sub> induced leaf injury is suggested to proceed through three steps: induction,
- 416 spreading and containment (Kangasjärvi et al. 2005). Thus, after O<sub>3</sub> enters the plant activation of various
- 417 defense signaling pathways will determine the output towards death or survival.
- The other mechanism(s) for O<sub>3</sub> tolerance were revealed through transcriptome analysis. Already in control
  growth conditions C24 had increased expression of defense genes related to SA and cell death (Figs 3 and 4;
  Bechtold et al., 2010). By having defenses already on, C24 could be better at handling a severe stress
  treatment such as an acute O<sub>3</sub> exposure. Detailed investigation of transcript levels for all genes in the QTL
  areas identified a few candidate genes that might explain altered defenses and execution of cell death.
- 423 These included genes related to antioxidant biosynthesis and re-cycling, transcription regulation and SA

14

424 biosynthesis (Fig. 6b).

425 In addition to classical antioxidants, such as glutathione and ascorbic acid, pyridoxine (vitamin B6) is a

- 426 potent scavenger of ROS and required for tolerance against osmotic and oxidative stress (Matxain et al.,
- 427 2006; Chen and Xiong, 2005; Titiz et al., 2006). PYRIDOXINE BIOSYNTHESIS 1.2 (PDX1.2) is an inactive
- 428 enzyme but has a crucial role to enhance the rate of vitamin B6 biosynthesis and if the gene is knocked out,
- 429 pyridoxine biosynthesis is not increased during stress (Moccand et al., 2014). Expression of *PDX1.2* in
- 430 response to O<sub>3</sub> was increased only in Te/CT101, indicating that these genotypes have a different strategy
- 431 for ROS scavenging than C24. Furthermore, MONODEHYDROASCORBATE REDUCTASE 3 (MDHAR3) involved
- 432 in recycling of oxidized ascorbic acid had approximately 10 times higher expression in C24 in control
- 433 conditions and was more highly induced by  $O_3$  in C24 than in Te/CT101 (Supporting Information Table S7).
- 434 Counterintuitively, Te has significantly higher levels of ascorbic acid than C24 (Brosché et al., 2010). Possibly,
- 435 C24 has optimized its ROS scavenging capacity through higher expression of key enzymes such as MDHAR3.
- 436 The candidate gene list contained only two transcription factors, WRKY46 and WRKY55. The WRKY 437 transcription factors are considered important regulators of abiotic and biotic stress responses (Rushton et 438 al., 2010). The target promoter element (the W-box) of WRKY transcription factors is highly enriched 439 among early  $O_3$  regulated genes (Blomster et al., 2011). Whereas the function of WRKY55 is currently 440 unknown, overexpression of WRKY46 leads to pathogen resistance (Hu et al., 2012). WRKY55 expression 441 was higher in C24 and WRKY46 higher in Te/CT101. Since WRKY transcription factors often act together 442 (Rushton et al., 2010), WRKY46 and WRKY55 (possibly together with other WRKYs) could be involved in 443 regulation of the  $O_3$  transcriptional response in C24 and Te/CT101.
- 444 Transcript levels of *DIN11* rapidly increase during dark-induced leaf senescence and after treatments with 445 several ROS-generating agents and O<sub>3</sub> (Gadjev et al., 2006). Increased expression of this gene was observed 446 in Te/CT101 upon O<sub>3</sub>, however the O<sub>3</sub> induced expression was diminished in C24 (Fig. 6b). Expression of 447 DIN11 is regulated in a jasmonic acid (JA) dependent manner (Köster et al., 2012), this indicates that JA 448 signaling is not impaired in Te/CT101 and that constitutive SA signaling in C24 could have an antagonistic 449 impact on JA signaling in this accession. Expression of At2q41090 (Calcium-binding EF-hand family protein, 450 CABP22) is induced by SA (Knoth et al., 2009). C24 exhibited high expression of this gene in clean air, which 451 was decreased by  $O_3$ ; whereas in Te/CT101 it had lower expression in clean air which was increased by  $O_3$ 452 (Fig. 6b, Supporting Information Table S7). Another gene with opposite regulation was PUB29 (At3g18710), a U-Box E3 ubiquitin ligase with a function in abiotic stress responses (Cho et al., 2008). PUB29 expression 453 454 increased by  $O_3$  in C24, but decreased in Te/CT101. As two examples of the few genes where  $O_3$  led to opposite regulation pattern, further study of these genes and their regulation might shed light on the 455 456 differential effects of  $O_3$  in C24 versus Te/CT101.

457 SA can be synthesized via two distinct pathways; the isochorismate pathway and the phenylalanine 458 ammonia-lyase (PAL) pathway (Wildermuth et al., 2001; Huang et al., 2010; Dempsey et al., 2011). The end 459 product of the shikimate pathway, chorismate, serves as precursor for formation of Trp, Phe, Tyr, SA, 460 phylloquinone and folate (Maeda and Dudareva, 2012). Chorismate can be converted to isochorismate by 461 isochorismate synthase (ICS) and to phenylalanine by chorismate mutase (CM); both serve as precursors for 462 the production of SA (Wildermuth et al., 2001; Maeda and Dudareva, 2012). The key enzyme of shikimate 463 pathway, ENOLPYRUVYL SHIKIMATE PHOSPHATE SYNTHASE At2g45300 (EPSPS1) exhibited higher induction 464 in Te/CT101 than C24 in response to  $O_3$  (Fig. 6b and Supporting Information Table S7). In addition, one of 465 the phenylalanine ammonia lyase genes (PAL2) had significantly higher expression in Te/CT101 in response 466 to O<sub>3</sub> compared to C24 (Fig. 6b). Increased expression of both SA biosynthesis routes in Te/CT101could lead 467 to higher *de novo* SA biosynthesis in Te/CT101 than C24 in response to  $O_3$ . This is consistent with our earlier 468 finding that Te had substantially higher amount of SA upon  $O_3$  than C24 (Brosché et al., 2010).

469

470 The role of SA was further explored through combined SA and O<sub>3</sub> treatments. Interestingly Te/CT101 471 pretreated with 1mM SA had less O<sub>3</sub> damage and H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> accumulation (Figs 4b and c). A similar antagonistic 472 relationship between SA and  $O_3$  was observed at the level of gene expression, where increased SA led to 473 decreased induction of  $O_3$  responsive genes (Xu and Brosché, 2014). In contrast to the protective effects of 474 SA in sensitive genotypes, SA pretreatment of C24 did not alter the extent of cell death or  $H_2O_2$ 475 accumulation, reinforcing the conclusion that C24 already has SA defenses active and additional SA cannot 476 further protect C24. How does increased SA protect against O<sub>3</sub> damage? The answer is likely to be a 477 combination of several mechanisms including increased expression of defense genes (Fig. 4) and altered 478 redox balance. Previously, oxidative modulation of glutathione redox status by H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> was shown to be part 479 of the feedback loop between SA and H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> in regulation of defense mechanisms (Han et al., 2010). The 480 constitutively elevated SA and  $H_2O_2$  levels in C24 correlate with a high amount of reduced glutathione (GSH) 481 (Bechtold et al., 2010). As indicated above, some QTL candidate genes are directly involved in modulation of the ascorbic acid – glutathione cycle (MDHAR3), and thus the interplay between antioxidants, SA and 482 483 ROS could decide if a cell death program is going to be activated. In contrast to the protective role of SA in 484 Te and CT101, SA can also act to promote cell death for example in the CATALASE2 deficient mutant cat2 485 (Chaouch et al., 2010) or in the O<sub>3</sub> sensitive mutant *rcd1* (Overmyer et al., 2005). Thus, SA is likely to play a 486 dual role, enough SA and activation of SA signaling is required to execute defenses. However, at too large 487 SA concentration the balance is pushed towards cell death (Overmyer et al., 2003). Here, further study of C24 could be interesting, since it appears very tolerant to the effects of SA (Fig. 4). 488

In the absence of stress, mutants with elevated SA level including *siz1* and *dnd1* are usually accompanied by
 constitutive activation of defense, stunted growth and spontaneous cell death (Lorrain et al., 2003; Xu and

16

491 Brosché, 2014). However, unlike these lesion-mimic mutants, accession C24 exhibited constitutively 492 activated defense mechanisms without developing spontaneous lesions or severe growth retardation (Fig. 493 4a) (Bechtold et al., 2010). A similar phenomenon was also observed in the transgenic Populus tremula x 494 alba that hyperaccumulate SA and SA conjugates (Xue et al., 2013), implying that constitutive activated 495 defense can be uncoupled from cell death. These observations raise the question of how C24 avoids the 496 development of spontaneous cell death. One option could be that a negative regulator of cell death is 497 simultaneously induced with the constitutive activation of defense or that a positive regulator is down-498 regulated. Of these two options it is more likely that a negative regulator would be up-regulated since 499 analysis of the GO category cell death in Fig. 5 revealed that genes with decreased expression in strong 500 lesion mimic mutants had unaltered expression in C24. A cluster of genes (At4g16890, At4g16920, 501 At4g16940, At4g16960) encoding TIR-NB-LRR-type R proteins showed high expression only in C24 502 (Supporting Information Table S7). One of these genes, SUPPRESSOR OF NPR1-1, CONSTITUTIVE 1 (At4q16890/SNC1) was isolated in a screen for suppressors of the essential regulatory component of 503 504 systemic acquired resistance and SA signaling, the redox regulated protein NPR1. The gain-of-function 505 mutant snc1 is an R gene mutant that constitutively expresses PR genes and renders R genes constitutively 506 active without the plant developing spontaneous lesions (Li et al., 2001; Zhang et al., 2003). In addition to 507 gain-of-function mutations in *snc1* leading to constitutive defense activation, duplication of the gene or 508 increased expression of SNC1 also lead to constitutive defense activation (Li et al., 2007; Yi and Richards, 509 2009; Bartels et al., 2009). Since SNC1 was not identified as a candidate gene regulating  $O_3$  induced cell 510 death (Fig. 1), this indicates that SNC1 is likely to be not directly involved in this process. We propose that 511 increased expression of SNC1 (or one of the other TIR-NB-LRR genes in the same cluster) is, at least partly, 512 responsible for the high accumulation of SA and constitutive defense activation in C24. This could be tested 513 in future experiments by introducing an artificial miRNA construct to silence SNC1 in C24. 514 To conclude, QTL mapping and transcriptome analysis revealed that the difference in sensitivity between 515 C24 and Te/CT101 to acute O<sub>3</sub> treatment is determined by a combination of low stomatal conductance 516 leading to a smaller O<sub>3</sub> dose entering the plant; and pre-activation of SA accumulation and SA mediated 517 defense signaling in C24. The pre-activated SA signaling in C24 could be mimicked by exogenous SA 518 application prior to  $O_3$  exposure leading to protection from  $O_3$  damage in sensitive genotypes Te/CT101. A

- 519 combination of transcriptome profiling and QTL mapping analysis identified several potential key regulators 520 involved in  $O_3$  responses, and uncovered *SNC1* as a potential regulator for conferring the 'defense no death'
- 521 phenotype in C24.
