### Abstract:

MicroRNAs are a class of non-coding small RNAs involved in negative regulation of gene expression and playing critical roles in developmental and metabolic pathways. Studies in several plants have identified few microRNAs and other small (s) RNAs that target regulators of phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway called MYB transcription factors. However, it is not well-understood how sRNA mediated regulation of MYBs influences accumulation of specific secondary metabolites. Using sRNA-sequencing, degradome analysis, mRNA-seq and proteomic analysis, we establish that grape lines with high anthocyanin content express two abundant MYB-targeting miRNAs resulting in differential expression of specific MYB proteins. miR828 and miR858 target coding sequences of specific helix motifs in the mRNA sequences of MYB proteins. Targeting by miR828, caused MYB RNA decay and production of cascade secondary siRNAs that depend on RNA dependant RNA polymerase 6. MYB suppression and cascade silencing was more robust in grape lines with high anthocyanin content when compared to a flavonol-rich grape line. We establish that miRNA-mediated silencing targeted repressor class of MYBs to promote anthocyanin biosynthesis in grape lines with high anthocyanins. We propose that this process regulates expression of appropriate MYBs in grape lines to produce specific secondary metabolites.

Keywords: miRNA, R2R3 MYB, grapes, secondary silencing, anthocyanins, flavonols

**Highlight:** This manuscript describes regulation of secondary metabolites like anthocyanins and flavonols in grapes by miRNAs by targeting the R2R3 MYB transcription factors.

## Introduction

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are key regulators of gene expression in both plants and animals. Plant miRNAs are processed by a specific Dicer-like nuclease from long RNA precursors with base-paired fold back structures to an active form of 21- to 24nucleotide (nt) RNAs (Baulcombe, 2004). These miRNAs form a ribonucleoprotein complex with Argonaute (AGO) family member AGO1, that can subsequently bind by base pairing to a target RNA with high complementarity (Baumberger and Baulcombe, 2005; Bartel, 2009). The consequence of targeting by the ribonucleoprotein complex depends on the nature of the targeted RNA as well as the extent of complementarity with the miRNA. Usually, the target RNA is cleaved to result in reduced protein accumulation if there is near-complete complementarity between the miRNA and its target. Such an outcome may also require complete complementarity at positions 10 and 11 of the miRNA. A lower complementation percentage between the miRNA and its target also leads to reduced protein production through translational suppression, usually without turnover of the target RNA (Brodersen et al., 2008). A third outcome can be miRNA-mediated targeting of chromatin-associated RNAs that leads to specific epigenetic modifications (Khraiwesh et al., 2010; Wu et al., 2010; Creasey et al., 2014). However, most striking among the functions of plant miRNAs is in their ability to initiate regulatory cascades with multiple mRNA targets (MacLean et al., 2010). The first step in these cascades requires conversion of an otherwise non-functional miRNA targeted RNA fragment into a double-stranded RNA molecule by an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RDR; usually RDR6 in Arabidopsis thaliana and other eudicots) (Howell et al., 2007; Qu et al., 2008). Not all miRNA-mediated targeting results in the cascade silencing. There are at least three known reasons for cascade silencing initiation: when the initiator miRNA duplex structure is asymmetrical (Manavella et al., 2012), if the initiator miRNA is atypical 22-nt long rather than 21-nt (Chen et al., 2010; Cuperus et al., 2010), or if there are two distinct target sites for miRNA within the target RNA (Axtell et al., 2006). Once initiated, the cascade silencing generates abundant secondary siRNAs of usually 21- or rarely 24-nt length. These siRNAs associate with specific AGO proteins based on their 5' nucleotide and/or length bias of specific AGOs to target RNAs with high complementarity (Chen et al., 2007).

The most conserved secondary siRNA loci that is seen across distantly related plants is a loci named TAS3 (Cuperus *et al.*, 2011). TheTAS3-derived secondary siRNAs target ARF mRNAs in a process that likely evolved in a common ancestor of seed plants (Axtell and Bartel, 2005). TAS3-derived siRNAs are crucial for plant development by regulating auxin signaling (Axtell *et al.*, 2006; Felippes and Weigel, 2009). Other secondary siRNA loci in rice (Song *et al.*, 2011), tomato (Shivaprasad *et al.*, 2012*a*), soybean (Arikit *et al.*, 2014; Fei *et al.*, 2015) maize (Dotto *et al.*, 2014; Zhai *et al.*, 2015), strawberry (Xia *et al.*, 2015), wheat (Yao *et al.*, 2007) and *Brachypodium* are speciesspecific and are likely to have arisen more recently (Johnson *et al.*, 2009; Vogel *et al.*, 2010).

In dicots, secondary siRNAs known as transacting siRNAs (tasi-RNAs) or phased siRNAs (phasi-RNAs) target pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) and auxin response factor (ARF) mRNAs (Axtell *et al.*, 2006). Surprisingly, both PPR and ARF mRNAs are targeted separately by secondary siRNAs and also by miRNAs such as miR161, miR160, or miR167 (Rhoades *et al.*, 2002; Allen *et al.*, 2005; Yoshikawa *et al.*, 2005). Although it is not entirely clear why cascade silencing targets are targeted by multiple small RNA species, it is possible to speculate that the secondary siRNAs play a role in either reinforcing or coordinating the action of primary sRNAs or miRNAs.

In addition to their role in development and disease resistance, cascade loci have been implicated in specific metabolic processes by regulating MYB transcription factors. The MYB superfamily are a diverse group of transcription factors represented in eukaryotes that are known to control processes like epidermal cell differentiation, flavonoid biosynthesis, stress tolerance and pathogen resistance. MYB proteins are classified into four classes 1R, 2R (R2R3), 3R (R1R2R3) and 4R (Dubos *et al.*, 2010), depending on the number of adjacent repeats homologous to animal c-MYB (Klempnauer *et al.*, 1982). In plants, the MYB family has selectively expanded through the R2R3-MYBs. R2R3-MYB transcription factors regulate secondary metabolism, while others regulate stress responses and development. The R2R3-MYBs along with two other proteins, bHLH and WD40, can act as both activators and repressors of the phenylpropanoid pathway (Ramsay and Glover, 2005). The phenylpropanoid pathway is an essential

pathway in higher plants, required for the production of metabolites like lignin, flavonols, proanthocyanins and anthocyanins (Fraser and Chapple, 2011).

A well conserved low abundant secondary siRNA loci named TAS4, initiated by miR828 regulates expression of transcription factors of MYB family in Arabidopsis and apple. (Rajagopalan et al., 2006; Espley et al., 2007; Xia et al., 2012). Studies have suggested that few Arabidopsis MYBs like MYB11, MYB12 and MYB113 are targeted by miR156 (Gou et al., 2011) and MYB111 by miR858 (Sharma et al., 2016), thereby regulating anthocyanin accumulation. In Arabidopsis (Rajagopalan et al., 2006; Hsieh et al., 2009), lotus (Zheng et al., 2014) and apple (Xia et al., 2012), siR81(-) (Luo et al., 2012), one of the TAS4-derived siRNAs, was shown to target RNAs coding for MYBs such as MYB75 and MYB113 (as in *Arabidopsis*), or MYB90 and MYB113 (as in potato), that are associated with anthocyanin accumulation (Luo et al., 2012; Rock, 2013; Li et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2016). Due to the absence of clear phylogenetic relationship among MYB proteins in different plant species, it is not possible to predict which subgroup of MYBs is being targeted in these plants. It has been speculated that these miRNAs and secondary siRNAs regulate phenylpropanoid pathway during normal development as well as part of responses against stresses (Shin et al., 2013). There is strong correlation between number of MYBs in a given plant and number of small RNAs that are capable of targeting some of the MYBs. However, it is not known how miRNA/siRNA-mediated regulation of MYBs and other regulators of phenylpropanoid pathways results in the production of specific secondary metabolites. This is largely due to lack of studies involving contrasting genotypes producing diverse secondary metabolites as well as lack of genetic approaches.

The objective of this study was to understand the functional significance of miRNAmediated regulation of MYB RNAs in grapes. We performed differential sRNA, RNA-seq and proteome analysis of three closely related grape varieties with varied anthocyanin and flavonol contents. Our results indicated that grape varieties with high anthocyanin levels have 2 previously known miRNAs abundantly expressed in both young leaf and fruit peel tissues. During the analysis we also identified a novel MYB repressor named VvMYB114 that is a target of miRNAs in anthocyanin-rich grape varieties. VvMYB114 when expressed in tobacco, repressed the expression of enzymes such as DFR, UFGT that promote anthocyanin accumulation. Since MYB114 homologs with EAR (ERFassociated amphiphilic repression motif) repressor motifs are well-known among dicots, often as targets of miRNAs, we predict that targeting of MYB repressors might be a general strategy to allow activator MYBs to shift the pathway towards anthocyanin biosynthesis.

#### Materials and Methods

#### **Plant materials**

Three grape lines with contrasting phenotypes i.e., Dilkhush (DK), Red globe (RG) and Bangalore blue (BB), were used in this study. The plant tissues were collected from IIHR Campus, Bangalore and grown in a greenhouse under natural light conditions and 70% RH at 23°C. *Nicotiana benthamiana* WT and *RDR6i* plants and *Nicotiana tabacum* (Wisconsin 35) were grown in a growth chamber (Panasonic) at 23°C under a light-dark cycle of 16/8 h and 70% RH.

## Plasmid clones and constructs

For generating overexpression constructs, cDNA was prepared from grape young leaf samples using Superscript III (Invitrogen), according to manufacturer's instructions. Amplified products were cloned into modified pBIN19 vector (Bevan, 1984) under CaMV 35S promoter and NOS terminator. Primer pairs used for cloning are listed in Supplementary Table S4. For the generation of mutations in EAR motif, to substitute 87th L residue of MYB114 to A and I residues, respectively, PCR based ligation method was performed using primers pairs incorporating mutations. The binary vectors were then transformed into *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* strain LBA4404 with pSB1 (pSB1 harbors extra copies of *vir* genes for better efficiency, Stachel and Nester 1986), and used for further transient and stable transformation experiments. Details of the restriction sites incorporated and used for cloning and mutations are given in Supplementary Table S4.

## Multiple sequence alignments and phylogenetic tree

The protein sequences of MYB-family belonging to R2R3 family were retrieved from NCBI and a multiple sequence alignment was performed using Geneious 8 tool. The aligned peptides from the R2R3 regions were extracted and phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA 6 with maximum likelihood algorithm and bootstrap 100.

#### Total RNA extraction and library preparation for RNA-Seq and sRNA-seq

Isolation of total RNA from grape young leaves and fruit peels was performed using Qiagen RNeasy Plant Mini Kit according to Manufacturer's instructions with slight modifications as described by Čepin et al. (2010). Concentration of RNA and purity of the samples were estimated using Nanodrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific) and Qubit Flurometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific). An aliquot of the samples from young leaves and fruit peels were separated on an Agilent RNA Bioanalyzer chip to check for integrity. RNA sequencing libraries with two biological replicates were constructed according to the NEXT flex Rapid directional mRNA-Seq bundle library protocol outlined in "NEXT flex Rapid directional mRNA-Seq sample preparation guide"(Trapnell *et al.*, 2012) at Genotypic Technology Pvt Ltd, Bangalore, India, and sequenced on Illumina NextSeq500 platform. sRNA sequencing libraries with two biological replicates were fully and sequenced on Illumina NextSeq500 platform.

## RNA blot analysis

The RNA blots were performed as described by Akbergenov et al. (2006) and Shivaprasad et al. (2012b). Briefly, about 10- or 15-µg of total RNA extracted as mentioned earlier were air dried and resuspended in 8µl loading buffer (0.10% bromophenol blue, 0.10% xylene cyanol in 100% de-ionised formamide), heated at 95°C for 1min and loaded on to a 15% denaturing polyacrylamide gel (a 19:1 ratio of acrylamide to bisacrylamide and 8M urea). The gel was run at 100 V for 3 h and then transferred to a Hybond N<sup>+</sup> membrane (GE Healthcare) by electro-blotting (Bio Rad) at 10V overnight at 4°C. The RNA hybridisation was performed at 35°C for 12h using UltraHyb-oligo buffer (Ambion) containing appropriate radiolabelled short DNA oligo probes (Supplementary Table S3), end-labelled with <sup>32</sup>P-ATP (BRIT, India) by polynucleotide kinase (NEB) and purified through MicroSpin G-25 columns (GE Healthcare). The blot was washed twice with 2X SSC, 0.5% SDS for 30min at 35°C. The signal was detected after exposure on a phosphorimager screen using a Molecular Imager (GE Healthcare). For repeated hybridisation, the membrane was stripped and re-probed.

## **Quantitative RT-PCR analysis of phenylpropanoid genes**

Isolation of total RNA from tobacco leaves was performed using TRIzol Reagent (Ambion) as per manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was prepared using random hexamers and Superscript III (Invitrogen), according to manufacturers' protocol. Primer pairs used for the amplification are provided in supplemental table S4. GAPDH was used the control for each gene.

## Degradome library preparation

Approximately 35 ug of total RNA were used to prepare degradome library as described by Zhai et al. (2014) with the following modification. Approximately 120 µg of total was taken for the analysis. For the size selection of 128 bp PARE ligated products, Agencourt® AMPure® XP, Beckman-Coulter beads were used instead of PAGE purification.

## Processing of Illumina sRNA-seq reads and expression analysis

The small RNA-Seq reads were processed for adapter removal and filtered for length range of 16-nt to 35-nt using The UEA small RNA Workbench Version-3 (Moxon *et al.*, 2008; Stocks *et al.*, 2012). The miRNA expression analysis was performed using miRProf tool in The UEA small RNA Workbench. A cut-off of log2(fold change)>2 was used for differential expression analysis of miRNA. Novel miRNAs were identified using miRcat tool from UEA workbench.

# Processing and mapping of Illumina mRNA-seq reads and gene expression analysis

The RNA-Seq reads produced by the Illumina Sequencer were processed to remove adaptors and low quality bases. The reads obtained after preprocessing are aligned to grape transcriptome (downloaded from https://urgi.versailles.inra.fr/Species/Vitis) and analyzed further as mentioned in Cole Trapnell et al. (2012).

#### miRNA target prediction, sequence alignments and phased sRNA analysis

The target of the miRNAs was predicted using psRNA tool (Dai and Zhao, 2011) and tapir tool (Bonnet *et al.*, 2010). The sequences in the target regions were extracted and the LOGOs of miRNA target region were generated with WebLogo (Schneider and Stephens, 1990). Sequence alignments and phylogenetic analysis were performed using maximum-likelihood method.

Loci producing phased siRNAs were identified using UEA small RNA workbench version 3. Using TASI prediction tool, phasing analysis of sRNA sequencing datasets was performed with a stringent cut off of  $P \le 0.01$ .

#### Transient and stable expression

Leaves of 3 to 4 weeks old *N. tabacum* Wis 35 plants were infiltrated with *Agrobacterium* strain LBA4404 (pSB1) harboring constructs to express genes of interest. The cultures of *Agrobacterium* were adjusted to  $OD_{600}$  - 1.0 using 10mM MgCl<sub>2</sub> (pH5.6). Before infiltration to the leaves, 60 µM of acetosyringone was added to the bacterial culture and incubated for 30 mins. Infiltrated tissues were collected after 3 days for RNA analysis. Transformation of tobacco leaf discs (*N. tabacum* cv. Wis 35) was performed using *A. tumefaciens* (LBA4404 with pSB1 and appropriate gene of interest) as described previously (Sunilkumar et al. 1999). Transgenic plants were regenerated from kanamycin-resistant calli and maintained in a growth chamber (70% RH at 28°C).

#### Anthocyanin estimation

The method was performed as described by Nakata and Ohme-Takagi (2014). About 5 volumes of extraction buffer (45% methanol and 5% acetic acid) was added to the plant tissues and mixed thoroughly. Centrifugation was performed twice at 12,000 g for 5 minutes at room temperature (RT). The absorbance of the supernatant was measured

at 530nm and 657 nm and the anthocyanin content was calculated by using the formula (Abs530/g F.W.) by [Abs530 - (0.25xAbs657)] x 5.

#### Total flavonol estimation

The method was performed as described Koyama et al. (2012) with modification as described below. The samples were ground into powder (0.5 g) using liquid nitrogen and extracted with 10 ml of 75% ethanol for 3 hrs. The filtrate was dried and dissolved in 5 ml of 75% ethanol. To 0.1 ml of this extract, 0.5 ml of NaNO<sub>2</sub> (50 mg/ml) was added and mixed vigorously followed by incubation for 6 min. To the reaction mixture, 0.5 ml of aluminum nitrate was added, and further incubated for 6 min. The reaction was stopped by the addition of 2.5 ml 1M NaOH and the absorbance was measured at 510 nm after 15 mins of incubation. A standard curve with varying amount of quercetin from 0-100  $\mu$ g was used to quantify the amount of quercetin produced in the samples by measuring the absorbance.

## 4-Dimethylaminocinnamaldehyde (DMACA) and toluidine blue O (TBO) staining

Berries at different developmental stages were collected (Supplemental Fig 1) and hand sectioned for stained for detection of proanthocyanidins and their precursor molecules (J. H. Jun et al., 2015). These sections were stained using 0.01% (w/v) 4-dimethylaminocinnamaldehyde (DMACA) in absolute ethanol containing 0.8% w/v hydrochloric acid for 20 min. DMACA stains the proanthocyanidins and flavan-3-ols green or blue (S. W. Abeynayake et al., 2011). The sections were also stained for 5 seconds using 0.5% (w/v) toluidine blue O in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.2. TBO stains polyphenolic compounds such as PAs greenish-blue colour (D. Isabelle et al., 2003).

#### Protein extraction and total proteome analysis

Extraction of protein was performed according to Wang et al. (2006), with some modifications specifically optimized for grape tissues. Briefly, leaf and fruit peel samples were ground in a mortar under liquid nitrogen. Ground samples were washed with 10%

TCA/acetone mixture, vortexed and centrifuged at 16000 g for 10 min. This washing step was repeated until maximum color from the samples disappeared. Samples were further washed with 80% methanol containing 0.1 M ammonium acetate followed by centrifugation at 16000 g for 10 min. The final wash was performed with 80% acetone followed by 10 min centrifugation at 16000 g. The resulting pellet was air dried at RT for 20 min to remove residual acetone. For protein precipitation a 1:1 ratio of phenol (pH 8.0) and SDS buffer was added to the air dried pellet, mixed thoroughly and incubated at RT for 5 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 16000 g for 7 min after which upper phenol phase was transferred to a fresh tube. To the collected supernatant, 5 times the volume of methanol containing 0.1 M ammonium acetate was added. Total proteins were precipitated overnight at -20°C and pelleted next morning by centrifugation at 16000g for 10 min. The resulting pellet was carefully washed with 100% methanol and followed by 80% acetone and air dried and dissolved in 0.1M ammonium bicarbonate buffer for further analysis. In-solution digestion of the total protein was performed using 13ng/µl trypsin in 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate containing 10% (v/v) Acetonitrile.

About 500 ng of the prepared samples were subjected to LC-MS/MS, LTQ Orbitrap XL (Thermo Scientific), C-18 column, 15cm length for 180 minutes. The raw LC-MS/MS data for the three replicates were imported into Progenesis QI P (version 2.0, Nonlinear Dynamics Limited). All 6 tissues with 3 biological replicates, were included in the analysis. The young leaves and fruit peel sample of DK was chosen as reference and all other ion intensity maps from other grape lines were automatically aligned to the reference. Considering the good initial alignment quality, dataset was not subjected to any further manual correction such as vector editing. Relative quantitation using Hi-3 was selected for automatic processing of the software. After successful alignment, no further filtering was applied for subsequent quantification steps in the software. Default settings such as, no protein grouping and quantitation from non-conflicting features were used for protein building. For peptide identification, MASCOT protein search engine was used against the combined target-decoy database with the following parameters: Enzyme: trypsin; maximum missed cleavage: three; variable modifications: deamidation, oxidation. Mascot Search tolerance parameters were as follows: peptide

#### Results

#### 1. Profiling of sRNAs from three grape lines

We performed sRNA profiling using three distinct grape varieties commonly grown in the Indian sub-continent. These include Dilkhush (DK), a selection from Anab-e-shahi that produces berries with negligible anthocyanins; Bangalore Blue (BB), a variety wellknown for high anthocyanins, and a variety named Red Globe (RG) that has medium levels of anthocyanin in between DK and BB (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1A). Anthocyanin accumulation initiated 6 weeks before fruit ripening in these grape lines. Until ripening, berries of these lines were morphologically indistinguishable other than slight variation in shape and size (Supplementary Fig. S1B). DK accumulated very high levels of flavonols while BB accumulated lowest amount of flavonols (Supplementary Fig. S1C). In order to understand the regulation of anthocyanin development in grape berries, we carried out detailed staining of anthocyanin intermediates during various stages of berry development. Accumulation of proanthocyanin intermediates (Supplementary Fig. S2), and precursors of proanthocyanins were also higher in various stages of berry development in BB than in grape lines with reduced anthocyanins (Supplementary Fig. S3). We profiled small RNAs from young leaves as well as fruit peels from all three grape lines. More than 60 million reads were obtained from deep sequencing of young leaf and fruit peel-derived sRNA libraries (Supplementary Table S1). In young leaves, BB sample had abundant 21-nt sRNAs and a minor peak at 24-nt siRNAs, a profile similar to most dicots (Supplementary Fig. S4A) including grapes (Pantaleo et al., 2010; Picardi et al., 2010; Sun et al., 2015). However, in DK and RG samples, there was a major peak of 21-nt and a prominent peak at 23-nt with comparatively lesser accumulation of 24-nt siRNAs. Previous reports have recorded few grape varieties with a major peak at 23-nt instead of a prominent 24-nt peak (Belli

Kullan *et al.*, 2015). In our analysis, a major 23-nt peak was not observed in fruit peel tissues, indicating that their accumulation is spatiotemporally regulated (Supplementary Fig. S4B). In order to identify the source of 23-nt peak from RG and DK samples, we mapped all 23-nt sRNA reads to grape genome. We identified a single 23-nt read that majorly corresponded to the 23-nt peak at the genomic locus Chr17:65148-65171.

Similar to other plants, 5' terminals of 21-nt sRNA reads had prominent U bias and that of 24-nt fraction with bias for A (Supplementary Fig. S4C and D). In all three varieties, most sRNAs originated from intergenic regions, introns and transposons (Supplementary Fig. S4E and F). Unlike most plant genomes, grape has significantly higher percentage of genome covering introns and transposons as observed previously (Supplementary Fig. S4G) (Pantaleo *et al.*, 2010). The distribution of sRNAs was also identical across grape varieties and matched published reports for grapes (Pantaleo *et al.*, 2010).

## 2. MYB targeting miRNAs accumulate at higher levels in grape varieties with high anthocyanin levels

In order to understand the role of miRNAs in the development of grapes, we identified conserved and less-conserved miRNAs using bioinformatics tools as described in methods section. Grape lines exhibited high accumulation of well conserved miRNAs such as miR159, miR166, miR168 and miR319 (Supplementary Table S2). Expression levels, measured as reads per million genome matching reads (RPM), varied significantly between the young leaves and fruit peels as expected. However, there was minor variation in abundance of most conserved miRNAs between grape varieties in a given tissue.

Analysis of miRNA abundance using miRProf (Moxon et al., 2008; Stocks et al., 2012) indicated that there were many miRNAs that were comparable such as miR168, miR166 and miR319 among grape lines. However, there were several miRNAs that were differentially expressed between the grape lines. Among the miRNAs that accumulated substantially differently between samples, few were associated with secondary metabolic pathways. miR828, miR3632, miR403 and miR858 predominantly accumulated at higher levels in anthocyanin-rich BB when compared to RG and DK

## 4. Differential accumulation of MYB RNAs and proteins between grape cultivars

We performed whole genome strand-specific RNA-seq to understand dynamics of miRNA-mediated regulation of MYBs. We used two different tissues of three grape lines for this analysis (Fig. 1). As expected, most of the tissue specific-RNAs were expressed between all three grape varieties (Supplementary Fig. S9A and 9B), confirming their similarity in gene expression patterns. However, in leaves and fruit-peel derived samples, approximately 300 to 500 mRNAs expressed differentially with a cut off of log<sub>2</sub> FC>2. A full list of differentially expressed mRNAs deduced from this analysis are provided in Supplementary Data Sets 1 and 2).

Among the differentially expressed genes, several MYBs including targets of miRNAs accumulated at significantly different levels (p-value 0.05) between grape lines (Supplementary Data Sets 1 and 2). Colored grape varieties BB and RG had low expression of specific MYBs such as VvMYB114, VvMYB48, VvMYB39 and VvMYB108. It is important to note that among the differentially expressed RNAs, VvMYB114 alone is targeted by both miR828 and miR858. In addition to MYBs, genes involved in phenylpropanoid pathway were significantly different in their expression between grape lines. Several key genes such as UFGT (UDP glucose flavonoid-3-Oglucosyltransferase) and FAOMT (flavonoid 3',5'-methyltransferase) were expressed at higher levels in BB and RG that also have higher accumulation of anthocyanins (Fig.

3A). RNA-seq analysis was verified by RT-PCR analysis for selected phenylpropanoid pathway members (Supplementary Fig. S9 and Supplementary Table S4). Taken together, these results indicate a surprisingly diverse expression patterns for several key genes previously reported to be essential for anthocyanin accumulation. Most MYB genes that are targeted by miRNAs exhibited negative correlation in expression in anthocyanin-rich BB line as expected.

In order to verify if indeed RNA-seq analysis can be correlated with corresponding accumulation of proteins in these lines, we performed a high-throughput proteome analysis using LC-MS/MS, specifically optimized for the grape tissues used in this study (methods). Similar to RNA-seq, most proteins displayed comparable levels of abundance, while few of candidates showed differential accumulation (Supplementary Fig. S10A). Several MYB proteins and phenylpropanoid pathway enzymes were differentially expressed between grape lines (Supplementary Table 5). Many proteins involved in phenylpropanoid pathway also accumulated at varied levels between grape lines and these are likely downstream of key MYB regulators (Supplementary Fig. 10B). This is an anticipated result since MYBs regulate phenylpropanoid pathway at multiple levels, and any deviation in accumulation of a single MYB could potentially alter expression patterns of multiple phenylpropanoid pathway genes. Most strikingly, VvMYB114 and other MYBs had low accumulation in colored grape lines BB when compared to DK (Fig. 3B), consistent with the high sRNA abundance and reduced RNA expression. Thus, expression pattern of MYBs in both RNA and protein level negatively correlated with expression of miRNAs in grape lines with high anthocyanin accumulation.

### 5. miR828 initiates RDR6-dependent secondary silencing in MYB114 loci

It has been well documented that MYBs regulate expression of other MYBs at transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels (Ramsay and Glover, 2005). Among the miRNAs targeting MYBs, miR828 is a 22-nt miRNA that is capable of targeting many MYBs including MYB114 and WER. It has been shown independently that miRNAs of 22-nt length are capable of initiating secondary siRNAs in their target loci that are capable of targeting other members of the target gene family (Chen *et al.*, 2010;

Cuperus *et al.*, 2010; Shivaprasad *et al.*, 2012*a*). miR828 is highly conserved across dicots and initiates TAS4 secondary silencing pathway. We hypothesized that miR828 targeted MYBs might be sources of secondary siRNAs that might modulate expression of other regulators of anthocyanin biosynthesis including MYBs.

We predicted all secondary silencing loci among grape datasets (methods; Moxon et al. 2008). Many conserved TAS loci such as TAS3 and TAS4 were abundantly represented in all grape varieties as expected (Table 1). There were unique secondary loci among the grape lines, for example, VvMYB114 was a strong secondary silencing locus in BB when compared to DK (Fig. 4A). BB had more than 4-fold higher abundance of MYB114 derived secondary siRNAs than DK. Although TAS4 pathway is well-conserved across dicots, VvMYB114 was not identified previously as phased secondary siRNA locus. MYB114 locus produced siRNAs that are characteristic of TAS loci in *Arabidopsis* and other dicots. For example, phasing register (Fig. 4B) and size of secondary siRNAs fulfilled criteria set for such loci in model and non-model plants (Fig. 4C). There were other secondary loci derived from MYBs such as WER that also accumulated varied levels of secondary siRNAs across grape lines and tissues.

Since MYB114 was a novel secondary siRNA loci, we explored if the biogenesis of secondary siRNAs derived from this RNA required silencing associated genes such as RDR6 (Yoshikawa *et al.*, 2005). Grape is an unusual model system where tools such as mutant lines and T-DNA insertion lines are not readily available. Added to this, transformation of grape lines used in this study has not been achieved in spite of efforts from the host lab. Hence, we used tobacco as a model system to study if indeed RDR6 is required for the production of secondary siRNAs from VvMYB114 loci targeted by miR828. We cloned miRNAs and several of their target MYBs such as MYB114 and WER in binary vectors (methods, Supplementary Fig. 11). We introduced miR828 and MYB114 or WER genes to WT *N. benthamiana* and RDR6-silenced line (Schwach et al., 2005). Similar experiments have been carried out previously to indicate role of RDR6 in miRNA-mediated silencing (Shivaprasad et al., 2012a and Chenna et al., 2018).

17

miR828 was extremely abundant in transiently infiltrated tobacco leaves, while levels of endogenous tobacco miR828 accumulated at negligible, often below detection levels as seen in control un-infiltrated leaves. WT plants where RDR6 is active produced secondary siRNAs from MYB114 loci abundantly, while *RDR6i* plants were unable to generate secondary siRNAs. Multiple probes looking at several secondary siRNAs did not produce any positive signal in *RDR6i* plants (Fig. 4D). Similar results were obtained for another miR828 target MYB named WER, that exhibited all characteristic features of secondary loci (Supplementary Fig. S12). These results indicate that grape cascade silencing is anRDR6-dependent cascade silencing mechanism, similar to other plants studied earlier.

#### 6. MYBs targeted by miRNAs are repressors of anthocyanin accumulation

It has been studied extensively in several plants how miRNAs efficiently target RNAs coding for MYBs. Many such interactions resulted in secondary silencing (Hsieh et al., 2009) such as the ones observed here. However, it is not known why MYB transcription factors that are generally known to promote anthocyanin accumulation are also targets of miRNAs. Several landmark studies have proposed that some MYBs act as repressors of anthocyanin accumulation (Jin et al., 2000; Aharoni et al., 2001; Deluc, 2006; Matsui et al., 2008; Albert et al., 2011, 2014b, 2015). Since VvMYB114 is a novel candidate that accumulates at varied levels in grape lines, we hypothesized that MYB114 might be a repressor of anthocyanin accumulation. Such a prediction was mainly based on the observation that MYB114 is targeted at higher levels in anthocyanin-rich grape lines and its RNA and protein accumulated at lower levels. Using phylogenetic analysis, we found that indeed VvMYB114 falls in a clade with other known MYB repressors. A characteristic feature of MYB repressors is the presence of a unique EAR motif in the C terminal end (Albert, 2015). Unlike canonical repressor MYBs, VvMYB114 had a consensus LxLxL type active repressor EAR motif in its Nterminal end. Presence of EAR motif in N-terminal end is more common among repressor transcription factors than at C-terminal end (Kagale and Rozwadowski, 2011). It is important to note that peach MYB114, studied previously as a promoter of anthocyanin (Yao et al., 2017) is in a different clade along with MYB activators such as MYBA1 and not along with VvMYB114 (Supplementary Fig. S13). We introduced

18

multiple MYBs to tobacco that might act as activators or repressors of anthocyanin based on previous reports. Well-known MYB activators such as VvMYBA1 and VvMYBA7 (Matus *et al.*, 2008, 2017), when introduced into tobacco produced abundant anthocyanins, while MYBs such as VvMYB114 and VvMYBC2 did not result in the accumulation of anthocyanins (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. S14A and B). This indicates that VvMYB114 is not a promoter of anthocyanin and likely a repressor class of MYB.

We further explored if indeed VvMYB114 is a repressor that can potentially alter and compete with the existing activator MYB:bHLH:WD40 complex. We co-infiltrated VvMYB114 along with activator MYBs and other known repressors (Fig. 5B). MYBC2 as a repressor of anthocyanin biosynthesis, reduced anthocyanin levels when co-infiltrated with anthocyanin activator VvMYBA7. Very interestingly, VvMYB114 also reduced anthocyanin accumulation when co-infiltrated with MYB activator MYBA7 (Fig. 5B). This reduction was observed along with co-expression of bHLH, a co-factor in the MYB complex. These results establish that VvMYB114 is a novel repressor of anthocyanin accumulation in grapes. It is not clear how exactly VvMYB114 acts as repressor, although it has the canonical EAR motif. Albert et al. (2015a) and others have proposed that MYB repressors might work in several ways. These repressor MYBs have the potential to compete with MYB activators for co-factors such as bHLH and WD40 (Hartmann *et al.*, 2005; Gonzalez *et al.*, 2008; An *et al.*, 2012; Albert *et al.*, 2015). Additionally, repressor MYBs have been implicated in destabilizing activator MYB complexes (Albert, 2015).

In order to understand the specific role of repressor MYBs with N-terminal EAR motif in regulating secondary metabolites, we constructed two EAR mutants (Supplementary Table S4) where the residues LxLxL in the EAR motif were mutated to LxAxL (mEAR1), and LxIxL (mEAR2), respectively. EAR motif in VvMYB114 is at the N-terminal, a region that is involved with bHLH interaction. We constructed mEAR2 mutant to understand the significance of lysine in the middle position (LxLxL), specifically to see if bHLH interaction and repression activity are coupled. We co-infiltrated MYB114 and the MYB114 EAR mutants along with MYBA7 and bHLH complex into WT tobacco leaves (Fig. 5C and D). The mEAR1 mutant showed increased anthocyanin content and reduced flavonols when compared to WT MYB114, suggesting that VvMYB114 is likely

to be a repressor of anthocyanin due to the presence of N-terminal EAR domain. VvMYB114 repressed the anthocyanins indicating that it is a crucial factor in phenylpropanoid pathway that is capable of shifting the final product even in tissues that are producing abundant anthocyanins. To further understand the role of N-terminal EAR motif in changing the flux for flavonol pathway, we estimated the flavonols in the same tissues (Figure 5D). The mEAR2 mutant also showed increased anthocyanin content when compared to WT MYB114, but did not significantly reduce the flavonol levels, suggesting the importance of lysine for its anthocyanin repression activity. We hypothesized that VvMYB114 might be activating directly or indirectly, promoters of genes involved in flavonol biosynthesis.

## 7. Over-expression of VvMYB114 shifts metabolism to flavonol biosynthesis.

To understand the role of VvMYB114 in flavonol pathway, we generated transgenic plants expressing MYB114 and measured anthocyanins and flavonols in these plants (Fig. 5E and 5F). Activator VvMYBA7 served as a control. Surprisingly, there was high accumulation of flavonols in MYB114 expressing transgenic plants, while MYBA7 expressing plants were enriched both in anthocyanins and flavonols (Fig. 5E and 5F). This indicated that VvMYB114 induces flavonoid biosynthesis in transgenic plants and might be a promoter of flavonol biosynthesis from dihydroxyflavonol intermediates. Several activator MYBs act as upstream factors to promote production of both anthocyanins and flavonols. VvMYBA7 promoted phenylpropanoid pathway to activate production of both anthocyanins and flavonols, clearly indicating that it is an upstream regulator of phenylpropanoid pathway. On the other hand, VvMYB114 promoted production of only flavonols like quercetin, indicating that it is a regulator specific to branch point of phenylpropanoid pathway that regulates flavonols (Supplementary Fig S15C). The qRT-PCR analysis of phenylpropanoid pathway genes from leaf tissues showed that MYB114 included upregulation of genes involved specifically in flavonol production from dihydroxyflavonol intermediates, such as flavonol 3 monooxygenase (F3MO) to quercetin and myceretin derivatives (Supplementary Fig. S15C). It is intriguing and worth speculating how exactly a MYB that has EAR motif for transcriptional repression represses some promoters, while promoting expression from few others. It is possible that upregulation of flavonol promoting genes is likely not

through the direct action of MYB114, but through feedback regulation or through mechanisms that are still unknown. However, these results collectively indicate that expression of VvMYB114 in the absence of miRNA targeting in specific grape lines results in abundant accumulation of flavonols.

## Discussion

In this study, we show that targeting of MYBs by miRNAs and siRNAs has the potential to specifically alter the end product of phenylpropanoid pathway in grapes. Although it was well known that several MYBs from several model and non-model plants were targets of miRNAs and tasi-RNAs, it was not known how exactly such a targeting might influence changes in the anthocyanin and flavonol levels in reproductive tissues such as berries. Our results indicate that miRNA-mediated MYB regulation shifts the pathway towards either anthocyanins or flavonols and match the predictions made previously (Rock, 2013). We anticipate that these results help us understand how phenylpropanoid pathway is regulated at the molecular level to fine-tune production of specific metabolites in specific tissues of plants.

## miRNAs and evolution of their target MYBs

In many plants rich in secondary metabolites there is a high representation of MYBs of R2R3 type as well as abundant expression of miRNAs that have the potential to target them. It was previously proposed that this correlation might have been a result of co-evolution. Indeed many miRNAs that target MYBs reside in close proximity to their targets (Allen *et al.*, 2004; Du *et al.*, 2012*a*).

Several plants that have colored fruits were specifically favored during domestication (Aharoni *et al.*, 2004; Feng *et al.*, 2010). It is interesting to note that plants with anthocyanin-rich fruits have higher number of MYB genes. This has been previously reported for apple (Xia *et al.*, 2012), grape (Azuma et al. 2008; this report), peach (Rahim *et al.*, 2014), soybean (Du *et al.*, 2012*b*), maize (Du *et al.*, 2012*a*), and strawberry (Lin-Wang *et al.*, 2010). In many related plant species such as Medicago, Citrus, and Aquilegia, number of MYB genes are low (Martin and Paz-Ares, 1997; Du *et al.*, 2012*a*). It has been previously proposed that this enrichment is likely due to selection for colored fruits during domestication (Dias *et al.*, 2003; Feller *et al.*, 2011).

In agreement with this, anthocyanin-rich fruit plants such as grape and apple encode some of the highest number of MYBs (Espley *et al.*, 2007; Allan *et al.*, 2008). Such a selection might result in targeted evolution of MYBs that regulate anthocyanin production along with specific miRNAs and siRNAs to regulate their expression. Most fruit crops such as grapes depend on coloration for fruit dispersal. It is possible that domestication of grapes might also have led to artificial evolution of MYBs and miRNAs to induce production of specific secondary metabolites. In addition to humans, artificial selection might have also worked due to other animals involved in fruit dispersal. Birds are well-known to spot black and red colored fruits than those that are not very colorful (Duan *et al.*, 2014; Renoult *et al.*, 2014).

#### Identification of a novel grape repressor MYB

A major target of miRNAs in our analysis is a relatively unknown MYB gene named VvMYB114. MYB114 expression was significantly lower in anthocyanin-rich grape lines due to targeting by miRNAs and potentially by secondary siRNAs and such an outcome was likely reinforced due to RDR6-dependent cascade silencing. VvMYB114 has a classical LxLxL type EAR motif that is previously implicated in transcriptional repression (Albert et al., 2014a). EAR motif is one of the first repressor motif discovered in plants a decade ago and represented in more than 20% of transcriptional repressors in plants. EAR motif has been implicated in repressor activity through its interaction with TOPLESS, a cofactor of Histone Deacetylase (HDAC) that is responsible for chromatin remodeling (Kazan, 2006; Kagale and Rozwadowski, 2011). However, this EAR motif is in N-terminal end of VvMYB114 unlike other canonical repressor MYBs such as MYBC2 and WER, where EAR motifs are observed in C-terminal ends (Fig. 6A). However, in many well-studied transcriptional repressors such as an AUX/IAA protein, NINJA and AFP, EAR motifs are observed in N-terminal ends (Kagale and Rozwadowski, 2011). It appears that activator MYBs are not active targets of miRNAs unlike repressor MYBs in reproductive tissues. miRNA target regions in helix 3 of MYB repressors such as MYB114 and WER are slightly different in nucleotide and amino acid sequence from those of MYB activators, indicating that presence of EAR motif and specific RNA motif coding for helix 3 might have co-evolved in MYB repressors to ensure their targeting by sRNAs to promoter anthocyanin accumulation. Alternative possibility is that VvMYB114like repressors alone co-evolved with miRNAs to specifically activate flavonols. Our results do not exclude presence of other layers of MYB regulation. Further studies are required to understand why such a targeting prominently selects MYB repressors in grapes and other plants.

In our studies, MYB activator such as MYBA7 and MYBA1 acted as inducers of both anthocyanins and flavonols. This indicates that MYB activators are upstream of dihydroxyflavonol conversion and have the potential to enhance both flavonols and anthocyanins. While BB line had negligible levels of flavonol, RG line had appreciable levels of anthocyanins and flavonols. In DK line, there was very low accumulation of activators MYBs while MYB114 repressor was most abundant. In transgenic tobacco expressing MYB114 there was higher accumulation of flavonols. Taken together, these results suggest that MYB114 is a key regulator mediating production of flavonols by converting dihydroxyflavonols to flavonols, thereby shifting the phenylpropanoid pathway to flavonol biosynthesis. In line with this, regulators of flavonol biosynthesis from dihydroxyflavonols such as F3MO were highly upregulated in MYB114 expressing transgenic plants. VvMYBF1 has been reported to induce flavonols in grape tissues by inducing the transcription of FLS (flavonol synthase) gene (Czemmel et al., 2009). However, VvMYB114 induces flavonols by inducing expression of FLS and F3MO, a regulator downstream of FLS. Transgenic plants expressing MYB114 induced expression of FLS and F3MO significantly. It will be of tremendous interest to understand how exactly MYB114 promotes flavonol pathway genes and directly or indirectly negatively regulates anthocyanin pathway genes.

## Finetuning of MYB expression in grape tissues

It is also intriguing that many MYBs are targeted by two classes of sRNAs, namely, miRNAs and siRNAs, in plants. Why is that this regulation requires network of multiple miRNAs and multiple siRNAs to target MYBs redundantly? The answer might not be simple. It appears that such a system arose due to feedback systems that commonly operate in MYB mediated regulations. Having multiple means to regulate single MYB RNA might ensure robust targeting to permit right combination of MYBs express in a given tissue. When we analyzed MYBs that are targeted by miR828 and miR858, we

found at least 5 MYBs as common targets of both miRNAs. This included MYB114, MYB82, MYBCS1, MYBPA1 and MYBPA2. We studied MYB114 in some detail; however, other candidates are equally interesting since all 5 MYBs are expressed at higher levels in flavonol rich DK fruit peels (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Data Set 1), strongly correlating with reduced miRNA levels. MYBPA1 and MYBPA2 promote ANR and LAR1, candidates that are involved in accumulation of precursors of flavonoids (which includes flavonols, proanthocyanins and anthocyanins). Functions of MYBCS1 and MYB82 are relatively unknown, however, MYBCS1 has an N-terminal EAR motif similar to MYB114. Co-targeting by multiple miRNAs in addition to strengthening silencing at two different levels provide robustness and probably spatio-temporal regulation if the miRNAs are tissue-specific. In this case, all 5 common targets achieve a common goal of producing higher flavonols collectively, as in DK lines, thus strengthening the idea that co-targeting by miRNAs achieves robustness.

Targeting of single mRNAs by both miRNAs and multiple phased siRNAs is not uncommon in plants. In fact most phased pathway genes, both coding and non-coding, are targeted by multiple miRNAs and multiple siRNAs. A common feature of phased siRNAs is that the initiator miRNA is capable of targeting multiple targets of a single gene family to generate a pool of target-derived siRNAs that ultimately target same family members or rarely other targets. In tomato, secondary siRNAs derived from nucleotide binding site-leucine rich repeat (NBS-LRR) RNAs targeted other regulators of resistance pathway, so that single miRNA ensures silencing of multiple targets that can be reversed by a single switch when needed (Shivaprasad *et al.*, 2012*a*). Detailed functional analysis of secondary siRNAs, including their targeting abilities in multiple cascade silencing pathways in plants is likely to answer these questions.

In our analysis, we also observed upregulation of miR828 by certain MYBs such as MYB114 and MYBA1 (Supplementary Fig. S14C), indicating that MYB expression is constantly regulated through feedback mechanisms. Such a feedback loop involving miR828 has also been identified *Arabidopsis* (Hsieh *et al.*, 2009). In grapes, induced expression of miR828 by MYBs such as MYB114 might provide robust secondary targeting. In such a scenario, miRNAs targeting of MYBs ultimately results in reduction in the expression of inducer miRNA.

Surprisingly, we observed that abundance of miRNAs that target R2R3 MYBs responsible for anthocyanin and flavonol accumulation in reproductive tissues were very high in some plants, while related species within the clades and families expressed these miRNAs at lower levels. Most of these species produced naturally or through domestication associated selection, high levels of anthocyanins or flavonols. Based on the observations deduced here for grape lines, it is tempting to speculate that in other species of plants too, miRNAs might be targeting MYB114-like MYB genes or related species-specific members of MYBs that regulate branch points of phenylpropanoid pathway (Fig. 6B). It is also possible, as described above, a strong selection for colorful fruits might have led to targeting of specific MYBs to promote production of specific metabolites in some plants, while retaining abilities to produce flavonols in others.

In multiple plants, anthocyanin fortification has been achieved spectacularly. It is also well-known that in some plants it is possible to enhance accumulation of flavonols. Our results open up a new avenue where it is possible to regulate secondary metabolites by specifically expressing activator or MYB repressors or through engineering of specific miRNAs. This understanding might pave way for further fine-tuning of flavonol and anthocyanin engineering in crop and other plants to promote nutraceutical fortification.
