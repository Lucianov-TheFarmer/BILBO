## SUMMARY

Among universal diseases affecting apples, blue mold caused by *Penicillium expansum* is a major concern resulting in yield and quality losses due to the production of the mycotoxin patulin. Despite characterization of the patulin biosynthetic gene cluster at both the molecular and chemical levels, the underlying regulation of patulin biosynthesis in *P. expansum* or mechanisms of apple colonization remains largely obscure. Recent work has indicated that sucrose, a carbon catabolite repressive metabolite, is a critical factor in regulating patulin synthesis. Here CreA, the global carbon catabolite regulator, was assessed for virulence and patulin synthesis both *in vitro* and *in vivo*. We show that loss-of-function *creA* strains were nearly avirulent and did not produce patulin in apples. Based on RNA-seq analysis and physiological experimentation, these mutants are unable to successfully colonize apples for a multitude of potential mechanisms including, on the pathogen side, a decreased ability to produce proteolytic enzymes, and acidify the environment and impaired carbon-nitrogen metabolism and, on the host side, an increase in the oxidative defense pathways. Our study defines CreA or its downstream signaling pathways as promising targets for the development of strategies to fight against the development and virulence of this post-harvest pathogen.

#### **INTRODUCTION**

The plant pathogenic fungus *Penicillium expansum* is the causal agent of blue mold storage rot in apples and other agricultural products and a major concern for global food security due to its widespread occurrence and ability to produce numerous toxic

secondary metabolites (SMs), with patulin generally regarded to be the most problematic (Puel *et al.*, 2010). The gene cluster coding for the synthesis of patulin has been identified and characterized in this species (Tannous *et al.*, 2014, Ballester *et al.*, 2015, Li *et al.*, 2015), however little is known concerning its transcriptional or post-transcriptional regulation. Recently we have demonstrated that the general inductor of fungal secondary metabolism biosynthesis, LaeA, is required for the production of not only patulin but also other putative SMs produced by *P. expansum* and contributes to virulence on apples (Kumar *et al.*, 2016). In addition to LaeA, other regulatory proteins known to impact natural product biosynthesis in fungi, including transcription factors CreA, AreA and PacC associated with broad responses to carbon availability, nitrogen availability and pH respectively, were shown to be expressed during the biosynthesis of patulin which is significantly affected by C and N sources as well as pH (Barad *et al.*, 2016, Kumar *et al.*, 2016, Xia *et al.*, 2017).

Colonization and mycotoxin accumulation on fruit by *P. expansum* is affected by increasing sugar content levels occurring during maturation and ripening (Kumar *et al.*, 2016). Apple fruits contain several sugars that contribute to the total soluble solids, and one of the key components is sucrose, which was found positively correlated with decreased patulin synthesis and decreased *laeA* expression but increased *creA* transcription (Kumar *et al.*, 2016). Therefore, the potential of CreA to regulate *P. expansum* patulin synthesis and virulence on apple is reasonable. Carbon catabolite repression (CCR) is a global regulatory mechanism found in a vast array of microorganisms, which guarantees the preferential utilization of rapidly metabolized sugars like D-glucose and sucrose over less favorable carbon sources by preventing the expression of genes required for the utilization of these secondary substrates (Dowzer and Kelly, 1991).

The CCR pathway is driven by CreA (Cre1) in filamentous fungi and first described in the genetic model organism *Aspergillus nidulans* (Dowzer and Kelly, 1989). CreA/Cre1 proteins all contain two Cys2His2 zinc fingers domains (Tudzynski *et al.*, 2000) and have been isolated and characterized from other filamentous fungi including *Aspergillus niger* (Drysdale *et al.*, 1993), *Sclerotinia sclerotiorum* (Vautard *et al.*, 1999), *Botrytis cinerea* (Tudzynski *et al.*, 2000), *Gibberella fujikuroi* (Tudzynski *et al.*, 2000), *Fusarium oxysporum* (Jonkers and Rep, 2009), *Penicillium canescens* (Chulkin *et al.*, 2010), *Neurospora crassa* (Sun and Glass, 2011), *Trichoderma reesei* (Portnoy *et al.*, 2011), *Penicillium oxalicum* (Zhang *et al.*, 2016), and *Aspergillus fumigatus* (Beattie *et al.*, 2017).

A regulatory effect of CreA on secondary metabolite biosynthesis was observed in some fungal genera. In *Penicillium chrysogenum*, penicillin biosynthesis was found to be subject to carbon catabolite regulation exerted by glucose where CreA negatively regulated the transcription of the penicillin biosynthesis genes *pcbAB*, *pcbC* and *penDE* (Gutiérrez *et al.*, 1999). Later, Cepeda-García et al. (2014) showed that CreA was responsible for the carbon source regulation of penicillin biosynthesis in this fungus. However, this was not the case in *A. nidulans*, where CreA did not play a key role in carbon catabolite repression of penicillin biosynthesis (Espeso *et al.*, 1993). In three *Aspergillus* spp., *A. ochraceus*, *A. carbonarius* and *A. tubingensis*, ochratoxin production was reported to be strongly influenced by the nature of the carbon source (Medina *et al.*, 2008), and putatively mediated by CreA (Wang *et al.*, 2016). In addition, a de-regulation of *cre1* in *Acremonium chrysogenum* has been reported to be putatively responsible for increased cephalosporin C production (Jekosch and Kück, 2000). CreA has also been implicated as a negative repressor of several secondary metabolite gene clusters in *P. oxalicum* where its deletion in a *AlaeA* background restored SM gene expression (Zhang *et al.*, 2016).

Furthermore, CreA has been found to affect pathogenicity in some plant and animal pathogens. A CreA-mediated catabolite repression controlling the virulence of Alternaria citri has been described by Katoh et al. (2007). In this study, the overexpression of creA resulted in the colonization of the juice sac area of citrus fruit, a phenomenon not observed by the wild type strain (Katoh et al., 2007). However, the deletion of creA in the plant pathogenic fungus Alternaria brassicicola did not significantly affect its virulence on brassica plants (Cho et al., 2009). Studies of biocontrol fungi, including the insect pathogen Beauveria bassiana and the microbial pathogen Trichoderma have also reported varying results of the impact of CreA on virulence. For example, the study of the creA mutant of B. bassiana showed a 50% reduction in mortality of Galleria mellonella larvae, as compared to the wild type parental strain (Luo et al., 2014) whereas a separate study - using different experimental parameters - did not find a role for CreA in virulence for this fungus (Jin et al., 2011). Cre1 may play a role in mycoparasitism in Trichoderma harzianum (Lorito et al., 1996) although a complete study is yet to be conducted. CreA has been found to affect virulence in a host dependent way in the human pathogenic fungus Aspergillus fumigatus, where the deletion of creA resulted in a reduction of virulence in some murine models of invasive aspergillosis (Beattie et al., 2017).

Following up on our hypothesis that CreA would impact virulence and patulin synthesis (Kumar *et al.*, 2016), we undertook this study to determine whether this global transcription factor regulates carbon metabolism in *P. expansum* via CCR, and, further, explore if CreA impacts virulence and secondary metabolism in *P. expansum*. The loss of patulin and near avirulence of  $\triangle creA$  mutants on apple, complemented by a comprehensive transcriptome analysis reveals insight into both pathogen and host mechanism(s) of attack and defense pathways respectively.

### Creation and validation of ku70 and creA deletion strains of Penicillium expansum

Although *P. expansum* has a described genetic system, the efficiency of gene deletion in this species is poor and we first set out to create a  $\Delta ku70$  strain of this pathogen to increase homologous recombination events as described for other fungi (Sidhu *et al.*, 2015). The confirmed  $\Delta ku70$  strain (detailed in method S1, S2 and Figure S1a,b) was named TJT1.1 and used and referred to as the wild type (WT) control for the ensuing experiments. In order to confirm that the  $\Delta ku70$  strain behaves as the parental wild type strain Pe-21, the physiological characteristics of both strains were evaluated where the two strains were found to be largely similar in growth patterns (Figure S2). To assess any functional role of the *P. expansum* transcription factor CreA in the physiology and virulence of this pathogenic fungus, we deleted *creA* in TJT1.1, obtaining several correct deletion mutants (detailed in method S1, S2 and Figure S3a, b).

#### CreA is required for colonization and patulin contamination of apples.

CreA/Cre1 mutants in other filamentous pathogenic fungi have displayed varying results such that in some fungi, but not others, this protein is required for full virulence (Katoh et al., 2007, Luo et al., 2014, Beattie et al., 2017). Colonization of Golden Delicious cv. apples by  $\Delta creA$  strains showed a significant reduction in the rot colonized area compared to creA WT strains (Figure 5a,b). Six days after inoculation the  $\triangle$  creA strains showed an inhibition of rotted area up to 50% compared to control strains and this inhibition increased up to 70% after 14 days inoculation (Figure 5a). Microscopic examination of the infection site of both the PhleoR control and *AcreA* strain (TJT2.2) after 14 days of culture at 25°C revealed an inhibition of germination of some  $\Delta creA$  spores in contrast to the control strain exhibiting only hyphal growth (Figure 5c). Analysis of patulin accumulation in the inoculated tissue 7 days after inoculation revealed no patulin synthesis by the  $\Delta creA$  strains compared to both WT and PhleoR control strains which accumulated between  $0.12 - 0.08 \,\mu g$  patulin/g apple FW, respectively (Figure 5d). Because citrinin has been occasionally reported as a contaminate of apples (Pepeljnjak and Ozegovic, 2002, Gimeno, 2005), we also assessed infected apples for this mycotoxin but could not detect it in either the PhleoR control strain or the  $\triangle creA$  mutant (Figure S6b).

To gain additional understanding of the potential mechanism underlying the reduced pathogenicity of the  $\triangle creA$  strains, the mutants were assessed for several physiological attributes that have previously been linked with virulence in this pathogen. A proposed virulence mechanism of *P. expansum* is its ability to reduce the pH of infected apple tissue through the production of gluconic acid (GLA) (Prusky *et al.*, 2004, Hadas *et al.*, 2007). Here the growth of both WT and PhleoR control strains in the presence of high sucrose level at an initial pH 7, showed a reduction of almost 3 pH units after 24 h, and in the following 48 h the pH remain between 4.5 and 5.0. This acidification of the media was accompanied by an accumulation of 1g/L GLA 24 h post- inoculation and a further increase of GLA accumulation up to 1.3-1.7g/L 48 h later (Figure 6a,b). In contrast, the  $\triangle creA$  strains showed no acidification of the medium and no GLA accumulation at any time point.

Given that GLA formation requires glucose oxidase activity (Barad *et al.*, 2012), the lack of *gox2* (glucose oxidase, XM\_016741098) expression in the  $\Delta creA$  strains explains the absence of GLA formation (Figure 6c). Furthermore, since pectolytic enzyme activities are also associated with *P. expanusm* virulence, the expression levels of two genes encoding a polygalactouronase (*pg*, XM\_016740409) (Prusky *et al.*, 2004) and a pectate lyase A (*pelA*, XM\_016738006) (Ballester *et al.*, 2015) were also followed during fungal growth during the acidification process described before and both were down-regulated in all the  $\Delta creA$  strains compared to the control strains (Figure 6d,e). Accordingly, the  $\Delta creA$  strains showed a clear reduction in polygalactouronase activity in a plate assay (Figure 6f).

All RNA-seq raw data were deposited at NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) under bioproject accession number: PRJNA434179. Hierarchical clustering of RNA-Seq data of both pathogen and host shows good correlations among replicates (Table S4, Figure 7).

Overall a total of 399 *P. expansum* transcripts were found significantly differentially regulated in the  $\Delta creA$  strain compared to the PhleoR control strain (FDR < 0.05) during infection, of which 244 were up-regulated and 155 were down-regulated (Table S5). (i) **Down-regulated genes**. Fourteen of the down-regulated genes encode for putative transporters including the major facilitator superfamily (MFS) PEXP\_041670, ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters and amino acid transporters (PEXP\_025200). Glucose transporters belong to the major facilitator superfamily (MFS) and represent one of the key group of genes that were significantly down-regulated by the deletion of *creA*. Genes involved in carbohydrate catabolism and showing a decrease in transcript levels include those encoding for glycoside hydrolases (Figure 7) and Concanavalin A-like lectin/glucanases – both being enzymes with roles in the hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds in complex sugars (e.g. cellulose, pectin, lectin, etc.) and therefore likely pathogenesis mechanisms. This result aligns well with our finding that the pectinolytic enzymes, PG and PL, are repressed in the  $\Delta creA$  mutant (Figure 6). The regulation of these two specific genes on apples followed the same trend but were not statistically significant in transcriptome analysis.

In addition, loss of *creA* resulted in a significant down-regulation of genes implicated in the degradation of amino acids, such as L-lysine 6-monooxygenase/L-ornithine 5monooxygenase (PEXP\_057130) (Figure 7) and pyridine nucleotide-disulfide oxidoreductase encoding genes (PEXP\_071850), which showed 7 fold reduction in expression in the  $\Delta creA$ mutant compared to the PhleoR control strain (Table S5). A gene encoding a glutamine synthetase (GS) (PEXP\_052140) which is putatively responsible for the reduction of organic nitrogen compounds and ammonia production also showed significant reduction in the  $\Delta creA$ strain (Table S5). Although one secondary metabolite gene cluster encoding a putative epipolythiodioxopiperazine-like metabolite (PEXP\_058600- PEXP\_058530) was downregulated in the  $\Delta creA$  strain (Table S7), in general there was not a large repression of secondary metabolite gene clusters in the mutant.

(ii) **Up-regulated genes.** As expected, we observed an increase in transcript levels of genes involved in the utilization of alternative carbon sources, including aldehyde dehydrogenase encoding genes that were found to be about 14 fold up-regulated (Figure 7, Table S5). Consistent with a role of CreA in the regulation of CCR, the transcriptome profile of  $\Delta creA$  shows that the glyoxylate cycle and gluconeogenesis are active in this strain despite the presence of glucose in apples. Genes encoding for principal enzymes of the glyoxylate cycle, isocitrate lyase (PEXP\_065930) and malate synthase (PEXP\_011610) and the gene encoding for pyruvate kinase (PEXP\_077890), a regulatory enzyme for gluconeogenesis were found significantly up-regulated in the  $\Delta creA$  mutant strain compared to the control strain (Table S5). Other notable trends were the large number of transporters (34) up regulated in the mutant (highlighted in yellow in Table S5).

Two biosynthetic gene clusters also showed an increase of expression in the  $\Delta creA$  mutant including the *pat* cluster (PEXP\_094320- PEXP\_094460) and the citrinin cluster (PEXP\_005510- PEXP\_005590) (Table S7) but as shown earlier, neither of these mycotoxins were produced in apple infected by the  $\Delta creA$  mutant. The increase in *pat* gene expression from the RNA-seq data largely supported the *in vitro* data where although many *pat* genes were increased in expression in the *creA* mutant (Note S3, Figure S5), patulin itself was greatly downregulated (Figure 3a and Figure 5). Genes involved in andrastin, roquefortine and communesin biosynthesis were largely unaffected in the  $\Delta creA$  mutant, thus showing similarity to our *in vitro* assessment of their backbone genes (Figure S8).

We verified transcript levels of a subset of genes with qRT-PCR and found that patterns of transcript levels between the PhleoR control strain and the  $\Delta creA$  strain were consistent between RNA-sequencing and qRT-PCR results (Figure S10).

Five hundred and forty seven genes were differentially expressed in apple between WT and  $\Delta creA$  infections (Table S8). The GO enrichment analysis of significantly regulated genes in the host was done using AgriGo website (Tian *et al.*, 2017) (Table S9).

(i) Genes up-regulated in *AcreA* infections compared to wild-type *P. expansum* infections. Apples infected with the *AcreA* mutant showed an up-regulation of the transcript level of 340 genes compared to infections with the control strain (Table S8). Two predominant functional categories belonged to (i) the oxidoreductase family and (ii) membrane transport proteins (Table S9). The first sub-group of genes belonging to the oxidoreductase family (oxidoreductase activity; FDR < 0.01) includes the catechol oxidase genes (catechol oxidase activity; FDR < 0.00002), a family of enzymes usually known as polyphenol oxidases which trigger the reduced activity of the single phenols (oxidoreductase activity, acting on diphenols and related substances as donors, oxygen as acceptor; FDR < 0.00002). Other sub-groups of oxidoreductase-encoding genes included those that catalyze the transfer of electrons using NADP or NAD+ as cofactors and those encoding for laccases which are oxidase enzymes that contribute to the formation of lignin by promoting the oxidative coupling of monolignols, a family of naturally occurring phenols present in apple tissue (Walker and Ferrar, 1998).

The second predominant category of apple genes up-regulated upon infection by the  $\Delta creA$  mutant include those encoding for ATPase driven flux, specifically ABC transporters, ABC-type efflux permeases and ABC-type efflux porters (Table S9), proteins that assist in

trans-membrane movement of substances by facilitated diffusion or active transport. Out of the 340 up-regulated genes, 54 genes sub-grouped into 4 groups of 13, 13, 13, and 15 genes, contribute to this type of efflux activity. Besides these ABC-type transporters, genes encoding for proteins transporters were also found up-regulated (i.e. protein translocases), driven by the hydrolysis of the diphosphate bond of ATP, or another nucleoside triphosphate.

One of the most up-regulated genes (14 fold) encodes a putative 1aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate oxidase (MDP0000151113), that is an enzyme in the synthesis of ethylene and is associated with the activation methyl jasmonate-induced defense responses in tomato fruits (Yu et al., 2011). Other genes also putatively related to defense responses included those encoding for the heat shock protein 83 (MDP0000303430), the thaumatin-like protein (MDP0000782642) and the pathogenesis-related protein1-like (MDP0000711379), which are all Pathogenesis-Related (PR) proteins assumed to play a role in plant defense system to various biotic and abiotic stresses (Wang et al., 2010). Other upregulated genes that potentially contribute to resistance to  $\Delta creA$  infection are those encoding for a synthase flavanone 3-hydroxylase-like (MDP0000508761) and an anthocyanidin 3-oglucosyltransferase 5-like isoform (MDP0000933711). Those two genes belong to the myb family involved in the enhancement of the biosynthesis of flavonols and anthocyanidins and host resistance (Daniel et al., 1999, Mehrtens et al., 2005) (Table S8). The activation of those genes explain as well the up-regulation of laccase-encoding genes that contribute to lignan and antioxidant synthesis, both conferring defense response in several plant systems (Zhao et al., 2013, Malik et al., 2014). Interestingly, genes encoding for the NAC domain-containing protein (MDP0000868419) resulting in activation of a large number of stress-related genes induced by pathogen attack and wounding (Collinge and Boller, 2001) were also found upregulated.

(ii) Genes down-regulated by  $\triangle creA$  infections compared to control *P. expansum* infections. 207 apple genes were significantly down regulated in  $\triangle creA$  versus control infections (Table S8). Analysis of the host GO enrichment analysis of down regulated genes showed several GO term that were significantly enriched (Table S9), these include genes involved in response to oxidative stress (FDR = 0.01), polysaccharide binding (FDR < 0.003), oxidoreductase activity, acting on peroxide as acceptor (FDR = 0.0031), oxidoreductase activity, acting on diphenols (FDR= 0.000018), active transmembrane transporter activity (FDR = 0.016), catechol oxidase activity (FDR = 0.00018) and heat shock protein binding (FDR < 0.05).

Specific gene analysis indicated significant down regulation of genes responsible for host response related to cell wall metabolism. Beta-glucosidase (MDP0000659743) is considered to be a key enzyme component present in cellulase and completes the final step during cellulose hydrolysis by converting the cellobiose to glucose. This down regulation suggests possible reduced cell wall cellulose content which could impact the host response. A putative linoleate 9S-lipoxygenase 5 (MDP0000263884) was also down regulated. These types of enzymes are involved in a number of diverse aspects of plant physiology including growth and development, and senescence or responses to wounding (Babenko *et al.*, 2017). Another gene that was down regulated in the host encoded a putative polygalacturonase (MDP0000152045) that could contribute to cell wall maceration and hence tissue firmness and susceptibility.

The identification and characterization of the transcriptional regulatory networks governing the physiology, adaptation and pathogenicity of a fungus is a prerequisite for understanding its behavior. Blue mold is a common postharvest disease of stored apples caused primarily by the pathogen P. expansum (Baert et al., 2007, Menniti et al., 2010). Several fungal factors have been correlated with disease development on apples including production of patulin (Barad et al., 2014, Snini et al., 2015), acidification of apple tissue by the fungus (Prusky et al., 2004, Hadas et al., 2007), sugar content (Kumar et al., 2016) as well as the global regulator of secondary metabolism and development, LaeA (Kumar et al., 2016). In the latter study, an increase of the sucrose molarity in the culture medium from 15 to 175 mM negatively regulated *laeA* expression and patulin accumulation, but conversely, increased *creA* expression leading to the hypothesis that CreA could be involved in *P*. expansum patulin biosynthesis and virulence, possibly through negative regulation of LaeA (Kumar et al., 2016). We found evidence for CreA transcriptional regulation of laeA (Figure 3c) but this was not correlated with patulin production either in vitro (Figure 3a) or in vivo (Figure 5d) and thus suggested that CreA regulation of patulin was independent of LaeA. Moreover, the near loss of virulence of the *creA* mutants (Figure 5) was much more striking than the relatively mild decrease in virulence of the laeA mutants (Kumar et al., 2016), suggesting a major requirement for CreA regulated process in colonization of apples.

#### Physiological effects of creA deletion in P. expansum

Loss of CreA had a profound effect on growth, sporulation and germination of *P*. *expansum*. Our findings are consistent with some previous studies which reported an impairment of growth due to the loss of *creA/cre1* in the model fungus *A. nidulans* (Shroff *et* 

al., 1997, David et al., 2005) and cellulytic species *Trichoderma reesei* (Nakari-Setälä et al., 2009). In this latter study, the  $\Delta cre1$  mutant of *T. reesei* was not able to sporulate on PDA, which was not the case with *P. expansum*. Similarly to our study, Ziv et al. (2008) demonstrated that deletion of *cre-1* in *Neurospora crassa* resulted in a delayed germination process and a reduced growth rate. Surprisingly, a deletion of *creA* in the plant pathogenic fungus *Alternaria brassicicola* did not result in any differences in growth when compared with the wild type strain (Cho et al., 2009); however loss of *creA* did impair growth, aerial mycelial production and sporulation in the entomopathogenic fungus *B. bassiana*, depending upon nutrient conditions (Luo et al., 2014).

A study describing changes in transcriptome and proteome profiles at the germination stage in *P. expansum* was recently published by Zhou et al. (2017). A total of 3026 genes involved in different cellular, metabolic and single-organism processes were found differentially expressed in samples collected at 6 hours (swollen spores) post-inoculation compared to those collected at 12 hours (end of germination). A comparison between this set of data and our transcriptomic results revealed a number of common genes that were found significantly regulated by the deletion of *creA* in *P. expansum* and may represent genes important in the germination process of this fungus.

*CreA* deletion eliminates patulin synthesis both *in vitro* and in apple but has smaller impact on other secondary metabolites.

One striking finding was the loss of patulin production in *creA* deletants in all media examined (Figure 2b). These results were opposite to our earlier prediction that CreA could be a negative regulator of patulin synthesis (Kumar *et al.*, 2016). In fact, assessment of *pat* gene expression in 50 mM sucrose showed that only 4 out of 15 genes in the patulin cluster

showed a significant down-regulation in the absence of *creA* (Figure S5). This inconsistent regulatory impact on the *pat* gene cluster was in contrast to the *\DeltalaeA* profile where all of the *pat* genes were down regulated in the *\DeltalaeA* background (Kumar *et al.*, 2016). Although an examination of *pat* promoters for putative CreA binding sites (5'-SYGGRG-3') (Cubero and Scazzocchio, 1994) suggested that there were many putative sites for each gene excepting *patA* (Table S10), this does not reveal if any of these sites are biologically meaningful.

The disconnection of transcript and product formation was also observed on host tissues. Our RNAseq data from apple indicated that most *pat* genes were up-regulated in the *creA* mutant during infection despite lack of patulin synthesis (Figure 5d, Table S7). Overall there was no one conclusion on how CreA might transcriptionally regulate the individual pat genes and we suggest CreA regulation of patulin is largely indirect through a posttranscriptional route. The failure to produce a metabolite while the corresponding biosynthetic genes are transcribed has been previously observed in other filamentous fungi. For example, several sterigmatocystin production mutants in Aspergillus nidulans still show sterigmatocystin gene expression but do not produce sterigmatocystin (Butchko et al., 1999). In some cases, this has been shown to be due to availability of precursor pools (Zhang and Keller, 2004) and we postulate that this could also be the reason behind the lack of patulin synthesis and citrinin synthesis as well in the  $\Delta creA$  mutant where, like patulin, citrinin genes are expressed but no citrinin is produced *in vitro* (Figure S6a). On the other hand, citrinin was not detected in apples infected with either the PhleoR control strain or the  $\Delta creA$  mutant (Figure S6b), although its production in apples has been previously reported (Pepelinjak and Ozegovic, 2002, Gimeno, 2005). It remains unclear whether the non-detection of citrinin by the control strain in apples is due to a deficiency in production or stability inside of apple tissues or a lack of efficient and reliable recovery and detection.

In addition to the negative impact of creA deletion on patulin and citrinin biosynthesis, metabolic profiling of the  $\Delta creA$  strain compared to the control strain revealed a significant number of changes in levels of many uncharacterized metabolites (Figure 4). This was expected considering the pleotropic phenotype of these mutants on various carbon sources (Figure 2). The production of some known secondary metabolites, andrastin A, roquefortine C and communes in D, were produced to slightly different but significant levels in the creA deletion strain although this was not reflected in the transcript levels of the synthase genes required for these metabolites (Figure S8), possibly due to the relatively minor changes in production of these metabolites. The RNA-seq data from apple infections supported a role for CreA transcriptional regulation of two gene clusters, the citrinin and epipolythiodioxopiperazine-like clusters, during pathogenesis (Table S7), although citrinin was not detected in apples. In general, CreA has not been frequently cited to have a role in regulation of fungal secondary metabolism although some examples exist including CreA regulation of cephalosporin C in A. chrysogenum (Jekosch and Kück, 2000), penicillin in P. chrysogenum (Cepeda-García et al., 2014), and pullulan in Aureobasidium melanogenum (Wang et al., 2017).

#### Deletion of creA impairs pathogenicity in P. expansum

Pathogenicity analysis conducted *in planta* with the  $\Delta creA$  strains revealed a severe decrease in virulence compared to the control strains, significantly greater than reported for other gene deletion strains of *P. expansum* including  $\Delta laeA$  (Kumar *et al.*, 2016),  $\Delta patL$  (Snini *et al.*, 2015),  $\Delta PeSte12$  (Sánchez-Torres *et al.*, 2018),  $\Delta veA$  (El Hajj Assaf *et al.*, 2018) and a recently described mutant in which the *pks* gene of the citrinin cluster was inactivated (Touhami *et al.*, 2018). One putative factor leading to the near avirulent

phenotype of  $\Delta creA$  was the inability of some spores to germinate in apple tissue compared to the PhleoR control strain (Figure 5c). However, this was but part of the mechanism as the inoculation of apples by agar mycelial plugs reflected similar results to those obtained by spore inoculation assays, e.g. a significant decrease in virulence (Figure S9).

The deletion of creA also may impact pathogenesis of P. expansum through the downregulation of cell wall-degrading enzymes, polygalacturonase and pectate lyase (Figure 6d,e). Similar findings are reported in Fernandez et al. (2012), where CCR control of cell wall-degrading enzymes was found to be important for the pathogenicity of the rice blast fungus Magnaporthe orvzae. Likewise, Cre1 has been shown to be involved in isocitrate lyase and cell wall-degrading enzyme production in the tomato pathogen Fusarium oxvsporum (Jonkers and Rep. 2009). On the contrary, despite that loss of creA in the fungus B. bassiana resulted in de-repression of a secreted protease and lipase, enzymes critical in mediating pathogenesis, the mutant displayed virulence defects in insect bioassays (Luo et al., 2014). These results indicate that creA regulates B. bassiana pathogenicity using yet-unknown mechanisms. Recently, Beattie et al. (2017) hypothesized that the reduced pathogenicity of the A. fumigatus  $\Delta creA$  mutants could be putatively due to the alteration of the polysaccharide rich cell wall, which is closely associated with virulence in fungi. This hypothesis was supported by the fact that  $\Delta creA$  mutants were more sensitive to the cell wall perturbing agents (Beattie et al., 2017). In our study, the lack of patulin production by the  $\Delta creA$  mutants may also contribute to a reduction of virulence in these strains although, in this regard, it is worth reiterating that the reduction in pathogenicity of the P. expansum patulin mutant remains relatively minor (Barad et al., 2014, Snini et al., 2015) compared to that observed with the  $\Delta creA$  mutants.

CreA plays a major role in transcriptional regulation of transportation and fungal carbon-nitrogen metabolism and its loss elicits expression of host defense mechanisms.

To further explore the mechanism underlying the *in vivo* growth defects of the *creA* knockout mutant, we investigated comprehensive infection-responsive expression profiles for both host (Golden Delicious) and *P. expansum* wild type and  $\Delta creA$  strains. This analysis revealed a number of genes that were differentially regulated in the  $\Delta creA$  mutant compared to the control strain. As mentioned earlier, a subset of secondary metabolism genes were differentially regulated, chiefly the patulin, citrinin and one epipolythiodioxopiperazine-like biosynthetic gene cluster. Gene expression was not linked with either patulin or citrinin (recently described to modestly modulate virulence, (Touhami *et al.*, 2018)) output in the  $\Delta creA$  mutant but whether epipolythiodioxopiperazine-like synthesis is altered in the mutant is unknown. Overall, it is unlikely that the changes in secondary metabolism are a significant source of the reduced virulence of this strain.

The deletion of *creA* also resulted in alteration in transcript level of many genes related to carbon and nitrogen metabolism. On the one hand, a set of genes encoding for sugar and amino acid transporters, genes encoding for enzymes acting on glycosidic linkages and others encoding for amino acid degradation enzymes, were found to be significantly repressed in the  $\Delta creA$  mutant (Table S5). The repression of these mechanisms in  $\Delta creA$  might potentially restrain the extracellular uptake of sugar and amino acids by the fungus. On the other hand, in the presence of abundant amounts of glucose and sucrose in apples, genes encoding for enzymes responsible for the utilization of alternative carbon sources were found up-regulated in the  $\Delta creA$  strain (Table S5), referring to an altered CCR mechanism in this mutant. The main function of CreA as a mediator of CCR has been

established years ago and with many fungi (Dowzer and Kelly, 1989, Drysdale *et al.*, 1993, Vautard *et al.*, 1999, Tudzynski *et al.*, 2000, Jonkers and Rep, 2009, Chulkin *et al.*, 2010, Portnoy *et al.*, 2011, Sun and Glass, 2011, Zhang *et al.*, 2016, Beattie *et al.*, 2017), whereas its role in amino acid transport and nitrogen assimilation has only recently been described (Ries *et al.*, 2016). Taken together, the down regulation of several of these processes are likely contributing to the reduced pathogenicity of  $\Delta creA$ .

On the host side, a set of defense processes related to the activation of processes including those induced by ethylene and jasmonate-induced defense processes together with activation of PR proteins regulated processes were clearly observed. Other processes include the activation of of myb family that is involved in the synthesis of flavonols and anthoxyanidins also contributing to the host defense process. This activation explains as well the up regulation of oxidoreductase genes, notably genes encoding polyphenol oxidases (PPOs), in infected apples with the  $\Delta creA$  mutant. These enzymes catalyze the oxidation of monophenols and/or o-diphenols which triggers the generation of lignin. In plants, PPOs have been related to defense mechanisms against pathogens by generating ROS as secondary products (Boeckx et al., 2015). A set of anti-oxidative stress response genes were also found significantly down-regulated in  $\triangle creA$  infected apple (Table S8). Genes up-regulated in apples infected with  $\Delta creA$  mutant encoded for MFS transporters and ABC-type transport systems in almost 10% of the genes out of the up-regulated genes, potentially due to the increased production of oxidative waste compounds produced within the cell. Considering all these data, the host-response to the  $\Delta creA$  mutant suggests an increase in oxidative processes involving host phenolic compounds that we suggest limit of fungal growth by generating ROS. A question that arises here is whether the reduced pathogenicity of the  $\Delta creA$  mutant is due to the altered carbon/nitrogen metabolism leading the fungus to starve to death and/or to the host-response to  $\Delta creA$  infection by generating ROS. Finally, it is also possible that apple genes down regulated during  $\Delta creA$  infections such as those encoding cell wall enzymes such as cellulase and polygalacturonase, may contribute to the reduced colonization by the mutant.

### CONCLUSION

Although some progress has been made in understanding *P. expansum* infection process, many unanswered questions remain. In this study, the global regulatory protein CreA has been identified as a critical factor for *P. expansum* infection of apples. We show that *P. expansum* strains lacking the *creA* gene are nearly avirulent on Golden Delicious apples; this phenotype is associated with an impairment in germination and reduced synthesis of pectolytic enzymes and the mycotoxin patulin. Transcript analysis of *P. expansum* infection of apples shows that CreA regulates several fungal processes potentially involved in the reduced pathogenicity of the mutant strains. Furthermore, deletion of this gene yields a *P. expansum* strain that elicits a novel response in the host transcriptome. We suggest that the alteration of carbon-nitrogen metabolism in the  $\Delta creA$  mutant may contribute to nutrient deprivation to the colonizing fungus and that an enhanced host oxidative response towards this strain may also limit fungal growth.

#### **EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES**

#### Fungal strains, media and cultivation conditions

All strains used in this study (Table S1) were maintained as glycerol stocks at -80°C. Cultures intended for protoplast preparation were inoculated with  $10^8$  conidia and grown at 25°C and 160 rpm for 14 h in 25 mL of Yeast Glucose (YG) medium (10 g dextrose, 5 g yeast

extract per liter). Fungal growth and sporulation were evaluated on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) medium (Barad *et al.*, 2016) as well as on Glucose Minimal medium amended with 10 mM ammonium tartrate (GMA) (Shimizu and Keller, 2001). Spore germination assay was carried out in GMA broth only. To test the effect of different carbon sources on the growth of *P. expansum* strains (GMA media), glucose was replaced with 1% of each of the alternative sugars described below. Physiological analysis performed on these strains are detailed in Method S3. Moreover, the different strains were assessed for several physiological attributes that have previously been linked with virulence in this pathogen (Method S4a, b). Cultures intended for patulin and citrinin analysis, RNA extraction and Real Time PCR assays were grown on solid or liquid Secondary Medium (SM) (Kumar *et al.*, 2016) (Methods S5,S6).

#### **Metabolic profiling**

The metabolome of one  $\Delta creA$  strain ( $\Delta creA2$ ) and the PhleoR control strain were analyzed using liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LCMS). Sample preparation and UHPLC-MS analysis are detailed in Method S7. Expression of backbone genes from characterized secondary metabolite gene cluster was measured by semiquantitative RT-PCR (Method S8).

#### Colonization and pathogenicity experiments

'Golden Delicious' apples, obtained from local supermarket in Madison, WI, USA or in Israel were subjected to asepsis using 70% ethanol solution for 1 min, and immediately rinsed in sterile distilled water. A spore suspension (10  $\mu$ L) containing 10<sup>6</sup> spores/mL of either the WT the three  $\Delta creA$  strains or the phleoR control strain was injected directly into the sterilized apples at 0.3- 0.5 cm depth according Kumar et al. (2016). The inoculated fruits were incubated at 25°C for 2 weeks and the diameters of the rotten spots were recorded daily.

In order to compare the ability of spores and mycelia to infect apples, pathogenicity assays were carried out as described earlier, but replacing spore inoculum with 0.3 mm mycelial agar plugs of the different *P. expansum* strains (Pe-21, WT, PhleoR and  $\Delta creA1$ , 2, 3) cultured for 7 days in GMA at 25°C. Infected apples were incubated at 25°C and growth was monitored regularly over a 14 days period by measuring the diameter of the rotten spots.

To analyze patulin accumulation in colonized apple tissue, the same weight of fresh tissue of each decay area was taken, 5 mL of double-distilled water was added and the tissues were homogenized. The final pH was measured in the homogenized samples of *in vivo* and *in vitro* experiments and the patulin accumulation was evaluated as described and reported before by Barad et al. (2014).

#### **Histological analysis**

Apples infected with PhleoR control strain and  $\Delta creA2$  were collected after 14 days of inoculation. Cuts of about 2 cm were executed at the rotten part of each apple using a razor blade. Tissue processing and staining with Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) for fungus (Dring, 1955) were performed in the University of Wisconsin- Madison School of Veterinary Medicine histology services laboratory. Images were captured using an inverted microscope (DMi1; Leica Microsystems Inc., Buffalo Grove, IL 60089 United States). Apples were wound inoculated with the WT and  $\Delta creA2$  isolates. Total RNA was extracted from apple fruits according to Yang et al. (2008), with minor modifications. RNA isolation, library preparation and RNA sequencing are described in Method S9.

### **Statistical analysis**

For all experiments, values are stated as mean  $\pm$  SEM of three independent replicates unless otherwise indicated. For statistical analysis, the unpaired student t-test was used with significance defined as a *P* value (\*P < 0.05; \*\*P < 0.01; \*\*\*P < 0.001). All statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software (La Jolla, CA, USA).
