### HIGHLIGHT

Cytokinin promotes photomorphogenesis independent of ethylene acting through the two-component signaling system and requiring a functional light signaling system.

## ABSTRACT

The transition of skotomorphogenesis to photomorphogenesis is induced by the perception of light and is characterized by the inhibition of hypocotyl elongation and opening of cotyledons. Although it is known that the plant hormone cytokinin, when applied in high concentrations, inhibits hypocotyl elongation in dark-grown Arabidopsis plants, it is unclear to what extent this response is the result of cytokinin alone or cytokinin-induced ethylene production. We show here that cytokinin-induced inhibition of hypocotyl elongation is largely independent of ethylene and suggest a close connection between the cytokinin two-component system and the light signalling networks. We show that this cytokinin signal is mainly mediated through the cytokinin receptor ARABIDOPSIS HISTIDINE KINASE3 (AHK3) and the ARABIDOPSIS RESPONSE REGULATOR1 (ARR1) in combination with ARR12. Interestingly, mutation of COP1 (CONSTITUTIVELY PHOTOMORPOGENIC1), DET1 (DE-ETIOLATED1) and CIN4 (CYTOKININ INSENSITIVE4)/COP10 renders plants insensitive to cytokinin and these factors are indispensable for the transcriptional response during cytokinin-induced de-etiolation which indicates that a functional light signaling pathway is essential for this cytokinin response. In addition, the cytokinin effect on hypocotyl elongation is strongly dependent on the light conditions where higher light intensities causes a switch in the response to cytokinin from an inhibitor to a promoter of hypocotyl elongation.

### INTRODUCTION

A seedling's transition from skotomorphogenic to photomorphogenic growth (deetiolation) after the exposure to light marks the transition from heterotrophic to photoautotrophic growth. Phenotypically, this developmental switch results in dramatic changes including unfolding of the apical hook, rapid inhibition of hypocotyl elongation, opening and expansion of the cotyledons and the promotion of chloroplast development and is driven by genome-wide transcriptional reprogramming (Ma *et al.*, 2001; Sullivan and Deng, 2003).

In Arabidopsis, to perceive the incoming light and regulate de-etiolation, there are four major classes of photoreceptors sensing UV-B (UVR8 receptor) (Rizzini et al., 2011), blue (cryptochromes and phototropins) (Briggs and Christie, 2002; Yu et al., 2010), and red/far-red (phytochromes) (Wang and Deng, 2004) light. In addition to these photoreceptors, a number of positive and negative regulators of photomorphogenesis have been revealed in a series of genetic screens over the last decades. COP1 (CONSTITUTIVELY PHOTOMORPOGENIC1), DET1 (DE-ETIOLATED1) and FUS (FUSCA) were identified as negative regulators of light signaling due to the photomorphogenic phenotype of their respective mutants in darkness (for review: (Kim et al., 2002)). COP1 directly associates with SPAs (SUPPRESSOR OF PHYA) to act as a substrate receptor of a CUL4-DDB1 (CULLIN4-DAMAGED DNA BINDING PROTEIN1)-based E3 ubiquitin ligase complex which targets photomorphogenesis-promoting transcription factors such as LONG HYPOCOTYL5 (HY5) for degradation (Chen et al., 2010). DET1, CIN4/COP10 and DDB1 form a separate complex (CDD) that acts through CUL4 to promote the action of the COP1-SPA-DDB1-CUL4 E3 ubiquitin ligase (Chen et al., 2006; Yanagawa et al., 2004). Light signals perceived by the photoreceptors repress the activity of COP/DET/FUS, leading to the accumulation of transcription factors promoting photomorphogenesis (Lau and Deng, 2012). Besides the COP/DET/FUS proteins, PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTORs (PIFs) are also essential for the repression of photomorphogenesis in darkness (for review: (Leivar and Monte, 2014)).

Due to the importance of light throughout the plant life cycle (Sullivan and Deng, 2003) it is not surprising that the light signaling networks are closely interconnected with several phytohormones (for review: (Cortleven and Schmülling, 2015; Lau and

Deng, 2010)). Treatment with inhibitors of both brassinosteroid and gibberellin signaling (Alabadi *et al.*, 2004; Li *et al.*, 1996) and the application of either strigolactone (Tsuchiya *et al.*, 2010), jasmonic acid (Zheng *et al.*, 2017) and cytokinin (CK) (Chory *et al.*, 1994), all have been shown to inhibit skotomorphogenesis, resulting in seedling de-etiolation in the dark.

CK regulates numerous developmental processes such as the cell cycle, shoot and root meristem size and activity and leaf senescence (for review: (Werner and Schmülling, 2009)). The CK signal is perceived by three membrane-localized sensor histidine kinases, ARABIDOPSIS HISTIDIN KINASE2 (AHK2), AHK3 and CYTOKININ RESPONSE1/AHK4 (CRE1/AHK4), which are predominantly located in the endoplasmatic reticulum and have both distinct and overlapping functions (Higuchi *et al.*, 2004; Inoue *et al.*, 2001; Nishimura *et al.*, 2004; Riefler *et al.*, 2006; Suzuki *et al.*, 2001; Wulfetange *et al.*, 2011, for review: see Heyl *et al.*, 2012). The CK signal is further transmitted by a two-component signaling system via HISTIDINE PHOSPHOTRANSFER PROTEINS (AHPs) to B-type RESPONSE REGULATORS (B-type ARRs), which are transcription factors that regulate CK responsive genes (Argyros *et al.*, 2008; Sakai *et al.*, 2001).

The link between photomorphogenesis and CK was first made by Chory et al. (1994), who showed that *Arabidopsis* seedlings germinated in the dark on CK-containing medium resembled the phenotype of *cop/det* mutants. Another mutant with increased endogenous CK levels, *amp1*, also shows a de-etiolated phenotype in darkness (Chin-Atkins *et al.*, 1996). Further, the *cin4/cop10* mutation makes *Arabidopsis* insensitive to the induction of the ethylene-mediated triple response by CK in the dark (Vogel *et al.*, 1998), linking CK action to light signaling as COP10 has a central role in repressing photomorphogenesis (Lau and Deng, 2012). Furthermore, it has been suggested that the interaction between light and CK is mediated by HY5, a transcription factor that acts downstream of cryptochrome and phytochrome (Vandenbussche *et al.*, 2007).

Although these reports all support a role for CK as a positive regulator of photomorphogenesis, inhibition of hypocotyl elongation caused by CK in darkness has been attributed to increased ethylene signaling (Cary *et al.*, 1995). Ethylene induces the triple response in dark-grown seedlings which is characterized by a strongly curved apical hook, a shortening of the roots as well as the shortening and

thickening of the hypocotyl (Guzman and Ecker, 1990; Wang et al., 2002). Being a derivative of methionine, ethylene is produced in three steps involving S-1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic adenylmethionine synthetase, acid (ACC) synthase and ACC oxidase (S F Yang and Hoffman, 1984). Furthermore, its biosynthesis is induced by CK (Woeste et al., 1999) which increases the stability of ACC synthase (Hansen et al., 2009) and this response is mediated through CRE1/AHK4 and ARR1. Due to this CK-induced ethylene production, CK is thought to achieve part of its influence on hypocotyl elongation through ethylene. In fact, the inhibitory effects of BA on root and hypocotyl elongation was shown to be partially blocked by the action of ethylene inhibitors or ethylene-resistant mutations (Cary et al., 1995). Besides ethylene biosynthesis, also a close correlation exists between CK and ethylene signaling. In particular, arr2 mutant seedlings are hyposensitive to ethylene comparable to *ein3* mutants in a hypocotyl assay suggesting that ARR2 is a signaling component functioning downstream of ETR1 in ethylene signal transduction (Hass et al., 2004). All these reports support the notion that CK induces ethylene production and suggest that CK might achieve part of its influence to regulate hypocotyl elongation by stimulating the ethylene pathway.

In this study, we have re-examined the effect of CK on dark-grown seedlings and revealed that the photomorphogenic effect of CK is largely independent of ethylene. Treatment of etiolated seedlings with CK in presence of ethylene inhibitors (e.g. AgNO<sub>3</sub>) clearly resulted in an inhibition of hypocotyl elongation. To investigate the ethylene-independent transcriptional response to CK in more detail, RNA-seq analysis was performed and revealed that CK treatment causes a similar transcriptional response as light. Moreover, we could show that this CK response is mediated through AHK3 and the B-type ARRs, ARR1 and ARR12. In addition, we explored the relationship between the known light signaling networks and CK treatment and found that the *cop1*, *det1*, and *cin4* mutants are insensitive to CK-induced hypocotyl inhibition in dark and these components of the light signaling pathway are necessary for the transcriptional response to CK. With this work, we provide a molecular framework for the ethylene-independent CK-induced inhibition of hypocotyl elongation linking this CK response to the major negative regulators of photomorphogenesis.

#### MATERIAL AND METHODS

#### Plant material, growth and treatment conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 was used as the WT. The CK receptor mutants (*ahk2-5*, *ahk3-7*, *cre1-2*, *ahk2-5 ahk3-7*, *cre1-2 ahk2-5* and *cre1-2 ahk3-7*) (Riefler *et al.*, 2006), B-type *arr* mutants (*arr1-4*, *arr10-5*, *arr12-1*, *arr1-3 arr10-5*, *arr1-3 arr12-1* and *arr10-5 arr12-1* (Ishida *et al.*, 2008; Mason *et al.*, 2005), ethylene insensitive mutant (*ein2-1*) (Guzman and Ecker, 1990) and the light signaling mutants, *cop1-4*, *cop1-6* (McNellis *et al.*, 1994), *det1-1* (Chory *et al.*, 1994), *cin4* (Vogel *et al.*, 1998), *hy5-215* (Oyama *et al.*, 1997) have been described previously. *ahk2-5 ahk3-7 ein2-1* and *arr1 arr12 ein2-1* mutants were obtained by genetic crosses of *ein2-1* to *ahk2-5 ahk3-7* and *arr1 arr12* respectively and *cop1-4hy5-215* was obtained by crossing *cop1-4* with *hy5-215*. Seeds were surface sterilized, sown on half MS medium (Murashige and Skoog, 1962) (1% Agar) without sucrose and stratified in darkness for 3 days at 4°C before given a two hours white light pulse (75 µmol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>). The half MS medium was supplemented with either 3 µM BA and/or 10 µM AgNO<sub>3</sub> unless stated otherwise. Seedlings were grown in complete darkness in a growth cabinet (Percival AR66L; Percival Scientific, Perry, IA, USA) at 22 °C.

### Hypocotyl length and cotyledon size measurements

For experiments in darkness, hypocotyl length and cotyledon size measurements of 5-day old seedlings grown on vertical plates was measured using ImageJ Software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). For experiments in light, seedlings were grown for five days in long day (16L/8D) under white light conditions (50  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) before the length of the hypocotyl was measured. Experiments were performed at least twice.

### Analysis of transcript levels by RNA-seq and real-time qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from 4-days old etiolated seedlings grown on medium containing 10  $\mu$ M AgNO<sub>3</sub> in presence or absence of 3  $\mu$ M BA using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) as described in the user's manual including an on-column DNAse treatment.

For RNA-seg analysis, RNA was isolated in three biological replicates for each treatment. The isolated RNA was sent to BGI (Beijing Genome Initiative, Hong Kong, China) for RNA quality and integrity control, library synthesis, high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatics analysis. In brief, RNA concentration, integrity and extent of rRNA contamination were monitored by the Nanodrop NA-1000 and the Bioanalyzer Agilent 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). After DNAse I treatment, mRNA was enriched by using oligo(dT) magnetic beads and fragmented into shorter fragments. First strand cDNA was synthesized by using random hexamer primers, followed by second strand synthesis. After purification, end reparation and 3'-end single nucleotide A (adenine) addition, sequences adaptors were ligated. Following PCR amplification and quality control by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, USA), the library products are sequenced via the Illumina HiSeq<sup>™</sup>4000 platform. More than 22 million raw sequencing reads were obtained for each sample. After removal of adaptors and low quality reads, the obtained clean reads (approximately 21 million) were stored as FASTQ format (Cock et al., 2010). Sequences were aligned to the TAIR10 Arabidopsis reference genome using Bowtie2 (Langmead et al., 2009). Gene expression levels were quantified using RSEM (Li and Dewey, 2011) and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the NOISeg method (Tarazona *et al.*, 2011) with the following default criteria: fold change  $\geq 2$  and diverge probability  $\geq$  0.8. Gene Ontology Annotation was performed using DAVID 6.7 (Huang et al., 2009a, b). For analysis of the overlap between the BA and cop1-4 regulated genes, DEGs co-regulated in two independent experiments using dark grown cop1-4 was used (Wang et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2017) and for comparison with a 6h light treatment or *det1-1* one published dataset was used (Dong *et al.*, 2014). RNA-seq data are deposited in NCBI's Gene Expression Omnibus and are accessible through GEO Series accession number GSE108912.

#### Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS v.9.2 (SAS Institute GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) and Prism6 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, USA). Data were analyzed by a one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's Post Hoc test. Pairwise comparisons were made between the different treatments (control, BA, Ag, BA+Ag) for each genotype. #### RESULTS

#### CK promotes photomorphogenesis largely independent of ethylene

In order to re-examine the effects of exogenously applied BA on photomorphogenic development in relation to ethylene, we treated wild type (WT) and ethylene resistant *ein2-1* seedlings with 3  $\mu$ M BA with or without the addition of AgNO<sub>3</sub> as an inhibitor of ethylene signaling. As previously shown, BA treated seedlings grown in darkness have a short hypocotyl and exaggerated apical hook, reminiscent of the ethylene triple response (Fig. 1, A-B). Strikingly however, BA treatment of the *ein2-1* mutant still results in a strong inhibition of hypocotyl elongation. Likewise, BA-treated WT plants grown on media containing AgNO<sub>3</sub> or AVG (aminoethoxyvinylglyine), two known inhibitors of ethylene signaling and biosynthesis respectively, also caused a strong reduction of hypocotyl elongation,

suggesting the activation of an ethylene independent pathway (Fig. 1, A-B and Fig. S1B). The effectiveness of the AgNO<sub>3</sub> treatment was confirmed by its ability to completely inhibit a seedling response to 10  $\mu$ M ACC in darkness (Fig. S1A). Besides its ethylene-independent effect on hypocotyl elongation, BA treatment of *ein2-1* also results in opening of the cotyledons and a marked increase in cotyledon size (Fig. 1, B-C). In this response, ethylene and CK appear to work antagonistically. Both BA treatment or inhibition of ethylene signaling by AgNO<sub>3</sub> alone result in a slight increase of cotyledon area in both WT and in *ein2-1*. However, BA treatment in addition to AgNO<sub>3</sub> (or BA treatment of the *ein2-1* mutant) results in further expansion (Fig. 1C).

From these data, we conclude that CK, in addition to activating ethylene signaling and biosynthesis, CK induces photomorphogenic development in dark-grown seedlings (short hypocotyl, open/expanded cotyledons) largely independent of ethylene.

## CK treatment causes a similar transcriptional response as light treatment

In order to investigate the ethylene-independent transcriptional response to BA, RNAseq was performed on 4-days-old etiolated seedlings grown on medium containing 10  $\mu$ M AgNO<sub>3</sub> and 3  $\mu$ M BA compared to 10  $\mu$ M AgNO<sub>3</sub> treated control seedlings. This resulted in the identification of 2463 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that were significantly upregulated (1140) or downregulated (1323) in response to 3 µM BA (Supplementary Data S1). First, we analyzed CK metabolism and signaling genes in the RNA-seq data set and found that most CKX and A-type ARR genes were strongly upregulated, consistent with the BA treatment (Fig. S2A). Interestingly, GO term analysis of the full dataset revealed a set of GO terms (Fig. 2A) of which many are related to activated light signaling pathways (photosynthesis, response to light stimulus, response to red light, flavonoid biosynthesis and cell wall modification). Since many GO terms are related to light signaling, we further compared our dataset to a previously published dataset of transcripts differentially expressed in response to a six hours light treatment (Dong et al., 2014) which resulted in a significant overlap of 758 genes (p<0,0001 using Fisher's exact test) between light and CK-regulated genes (Fig. 2B). Of these genes, 83% were co-regulated (51% co-upregulated, 32% co-downregulated) in response to BA or light (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, GO analysis of this overlap suggested that both BA and light treatment results in the upregulation of genes related to photosynthesis, response to light stimulus, photosynthetic electron transport chain, chlorophyll biosynthetic process, response to red light, while downregulated genes are related to cell wall modification and hydrogen peroxide catabolic process (Fig. S2, B-D). As many GO terms were related to photosynthesis and light perception, and a large overlap is apparent with published RNA-seq data investigating the transcriptional responses to light treatment, we conclude that CK treatment of dark-grown seedlings with blocked ethylene signaling results in partly similar transcriptional changes as light treatment.

In order to confirm our RNA-seq data and the independence of CK action from ethylene as well as the similarity to light treatment, we performed real-time gPCR on selected targets from the BA – 6 h light RNA-seq data overlap (Fig. 2B), looking at the effects of CK treatment with and without AgNO<sub>3</sub> in darkness and at the effect of a 6 h white light treatment. Total RNA was extracted from 4-days-old etiolated WT plants grown on medium containing 3  $\mu$ M BA with or without 10  $\mu$ M AgNO<sub>3</sub> in addition to plants grown on control medium with or without a 6 h white light treatment. Based on the GO term analysis, transcript levels were measured of chloroplast related genes (LHCB1, PETC), of chlorophyll biosynthesis genes (CHLI1, GUN4), anthocyanin biosynthesis genes (CHS, F3H) and genes involved in cell wall elongation (XTH30, XTH33) (Fig. 3). The upregulation of the expression of the Atype ARR, ARR5, which is an often used marker gene for CK induction, indicates that our BA treatment result in a strong CK response (Fig. 3A). In most cases, inhibition of the ethylene signaling by AgNO<sub>3</sub> did not change the transcriptional response to CK in the investigated genes suggesting that the BA-dependent induction of these genes is ethylene independent. Transcript levels of genes involved in chloroplast function (Fig. 3, B-C) and anthocyanin biosynthesis (Fig. 3D) are strongly upregulated upon both BA and light treatment. Genes involved in cell wall elongation (Fig. 3E) display a strongly reduced transcript level after BA or light treatment consistent with the observed inhibition of hypocotyl elongation (Fig. 1). Lastly, we also analyzed SAUR9 and SAUR14, which are predominantly expressed in the hypocotyl and cotyledons respectively (Sun et al., 2016) and observed that while SAUR9 is inhibited by both BA and light, SAUR14 is upregulated (when ethylene signaling is inhibited), consistent with the inhibition of hypocotyl elongation and cotyledon expansion observed under these conditions (Fig. 3F and Fig. 1C). While we initially compared the response to BA and light using our RNA-seq data and that of others (Fig.2), the consistent co-regulation of BA and light treatment observed by qPCR in selected targets (Fig. 3) confirm the predictability of the RNA-seq comparison made.

Thus, we conclude that similar to the phenotypic response (Fig.1), CK (BA treatment) resembles light treatment on the transcriptional level, independent of ethylene.

# Etiolated CK signaling mutants show a reduced photomorphogenic response to CK

Although all three CK receptors and some B-type ARRs (ARR1, ARR10, ARR12) previously have been shown to be important for the CK-dependent inhibition of hypocotyl elongation in the dark, these experiments have been conducted without the inhibition of ethylene signaling (Argyros *et al.*, 2008; Riefler *et al.*, 2006). Therefore, in order to determine which CK receptor is responsible for the ethylene-independent response to BA in darkness, *ahk2, ahk3* and *cre1* single and double mutants were analyzed under our conditions after five days growth in darkness (Fig. 4A and Fig. S3A). Analysis of the single receptor mutants resulted in BA responses similar to the WT regardless of the addition of AgNO<sub>3</sub> (Fig. S3A). However, analysis of double mutants suggesting that AHK3 in combination with AHK2 or CRE1 plays a dominant role in the ethylene independent BA response (Fig. 4A).

Similarly, although the B-type ARR mutants *arr1* and *arr12* might show a slightly reduced response to BA when not treated with AgNO<sub>3</sub>, the ethylene independent response appeared largely similar to WT in *arr1*, *arr10* and *arr12* single mutants (Fig. S3B). To account for possible redundant functions we further analyzed the double mutants and found that the *arr1 arr12* mutant showed a reduced responsiveness to CK treatment, independently of ethylene signaling (Fig. 4B). As the *arr1 arr12* mutant exhibited a reduced CK sensitivity, we further performed a dose response curve with increasing BA concentrations. While a strong gradual inhibition of hypocotyl elongation with increasing BA concentration on media supplemented with 10 µm AgNO<sub>3</sub> can be seen in WT, this inhibition is much weaker in the *arr1 arr12* mutant (Fig. S3C). Lastly, to rule out any unknown effects of AgNO<sub>3</sub> in these experiments, we generated the *ahk2 ahk3 ein2* and *arr1 arr12 ein2* to compare their BA

responsiveness to *ahk2 ahk3* and *arr1 arr12* double mutants and found that the introgression of *ein2* had no effect on the BA response in the double mutants (Fig. 4, C-D). Together, these results indicate that the receptor AHK3, in combination with either AHK2 or CRE1, and the B-type response regulators ARR1 and ARR12 play a prominent role in the regulation of hypocotyl elongation in response to CK.

To see if ARR1 and ARR12 are also important for the transcriptional response to BA, transcript levels of marker genes (see also Fig. 3) were measured in the *arr1 arr12 mutants* (Fig. 5). The transcript level of the CK-responsive *ARR5* gene was reduced in the *arr1 arr12* mutant in comparison to WT, however there was still a slight induction by BA treatment in comparison to control conditions (Fig. 5A). This indicates that for *ARR5*, ARR1 and ARR12 act redundantly with other B-type ARRs to induce its expression which is consistent with previous reports (Mason *et al.*, 2005). Transcript levels of genes involved in chloroplast function (*LHCB1, CHLI*) were strongly reduced in *arr1 arr12* mutants in response to BA treatment in comparison to WT (Fig. 5, B-C). Also *SAUR14* and *CHS* were less responsive to BA in *arr1 arr12* mutants, indicating that also for these genes, gene expression is mediated through ARR1 and ARR12. In line with our previous results (Fig. 3), the BA dependent regulation of these light responsive genes is largely independent on ethylene signaling and suggests that the CK-dependent regulation of the investigated genes is mediated through ARR1 and ARR12.

### The photomorphogenic mutants *cop1*, *det1* and *cin4* are CK-insensitive

Our results suggests that BA treatment of dark grown seedlings results in a shortening of hypocotyl length and expansion of cotyledons as well as a transcriptional response partially similar to light treatment (Fig. 1). In order to identify a possible point of convergence between the CK and light induced pathways, several light signaling mutants were tested for altered responses to CK treatment in darkness. As being a major positive regulator of photomorphogenesis and shown to accumulate after BA treatment (Vandenbussche *et al.*, 2007), the CK-effect on hypocotyl elongation was investigated first in *hy5* (Fig. S4) and *hy5 hyh* (results not shown) mutants. Both mutants responded to BA treatment like WT, suggesting that HY5 and its close homologue HYH are not responsible for the CK-induced inhibition of hypocotyl elongation. Then, we investigated the hypocotyl elongation in response

to BA in the photomorphogenic mutants *cop1*, *det1* and *cin4/cop10*. In comparison to WT, the reduction in hypocotyl elongation upon BA treatment is almost completely lost in these mutants already having a strongly reduced hypocotyl under etiolated conditions (Fig. 6A). To investigate if the already short photomorphogenic hypocotyl of *cop1* is the reason for the non-responsiveness to BA, the *hy5* mutant was crossed with *cop1* mutant which partially rescues the short hypocotyl phenotype of *cop1* under control conditions. Although significantly longer than *cop1-4* when grown in darkness, also the *hy5 cop1-4* mutant is largely CK insensitive consistent with a role for COP1 in the ethylene-independent CK regulation of hypocotyl elongation (Fig. S4). Together, these results indicate that a group of negative regulators of photomorphogenesis are necessary for the ethylene-independent CK-response on hypocotyl elongation in the dark.

Based on the above described phenotypic observations, we would expect that *cop1*, det1 and cin4 mutants would also be largely insensitive on the transcriptional level to BA treatment in the dark. Furthermore, as these mutants phenotypically resembles BA + AqNO<sub>3</sub> treated WT seedlings (Fig. 6B), there might be a transcriptional overlap between the mutants and BA-treated WT seedlings. To test this hypothesis, we used two RNA-seq datasets comparing *cop1-4* to the WT grown in darkness from the literature (Wang et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2017). By comparing these two datasets, we found 2953 genes consistently two-fold deregulated in cop1-4. Furthermore, we explored a list of *det1-1* regulated genes in seedlings grown in darkness (Dong *et al.*, 2014). By comparing these datasets with the BA regulated genes we found significant overlaps between both cop1-4 – BA (780 genes) and det1-1 – BA (908 genes) (Fig. 6C, Supplementary Data 2). Furthermore, 90% and 89% of these genes were co-regulated in *cop1-4* and *det1-1* compared to BA treatment, respectively, suggesting that the mutation of these genes results in a partly similar response to that of WT treated with BA (Fig. 6, D-E). Lastly, GO term analysis of genes coregulated by BA and *cop1-4* or *det1-1* showed a remarkable similarity to the GO term analysis of all genes regulated by BA (Fig. S5, A-B and Fig. 2A) which indicates that COP1 and DET1 might be essential for the photomorphogenic CK-response.

To provide further support for the transcriptomic analysis and to investigate the sensitivity to BA of these mutants on the transcriptional level, the ethyleneindependent CK effect was analyzed based on the transcript levels of marker genes (see Fig. 3) in 4-days-old etiolated seedlings. Although suppressed in *cop1* and *det1* under control conditions, we confirmed that *ARR5*, a transcriptional marker for central CK response, is still upregulated in response to BA in *cop1*, *det1* and *cin4* when grown on AgNO<sub>3</sub>, suggesting that the CK signaling pathway is fully functional in these mutants (Fig. 7A). Transcript levels of *LHCB1*, *PETC*, *CHS*, *F3H* and *SAUR14* are promoted in response to BA in WT, while *XTH30* and *SAUR9* are suppressed (Fig. 7, B-H). A similar observation for transcriptional behavior was also shown previously in WT after CK treatment (Fig. 3).Furthermore, in line with the suggested transcriptional overlap (Fig. 6), *cop1*, *det1* and *cin4* mutants show a regulation under the control conditions (AgNO<sub>3</sub>) similar to BA treated WT (AgNO<sub>3</sub> + BA), as these mutants have a constitutive active light signaling, which again confirmed the light-mimicking effect of CK. Importantly, in most cases, changes in basal expression and a strikingly reduced response to the BA treatment was observed in the three mutants, suggesting that COP1, DET1 and CIN4 not only regulate the same genes as BA in darkness, but also are required for BA responsiveness.

Taken together, these results together with the phenotypic analysis of the mutants suggests that COP1, DET1 and CIN4 might be important for the transcriptional response to CK.

### Light triggers a switch in CK function

As our results suggest that COP1, DET1 and CIN4 are required for CK dependent photomorphogenesis in darkness, we hypothesize that light, which inhibits the function of these factors, also would inhibit the effects of CK on suppressing hypocotyl elongation. To test this hypothesis, we grew WT and *ein2* seedlings with or without 3  $\mu$ M BA under increasing fluence rates of white light. As previously shown, addition of BA in darkness resulted in a strong inhibition of hypocotyl elongation in both the WT and the *ein2* mutant (Fig. 1 and Fig. 8A). Upon increasing the fluence rate of light up to 10  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>, the relative effect of addition of BA decreases to a point of almost no response. This is consistent with the hypothesis that light, possibly acting through COP1/DET1/CIN4, inhibits the effects of BA (Fig. 8A). Interestingly, by further increasing the fluence rate of light (50  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) we observed a CK dependent promotion of hypocotyl elongation (Fig. 8A-B). As this effect was also observed in the *ein2* mutant, albeit to a slightly less degree, we conclude that in light,

the CK dependent promotion of hypocotyl elongation is independent of ethylene similar as the CK-dependent inhibition of hypocotyl elongation in darkness.

To investigate if the promotion of hypocotyl elongation induced by CK is regulated by the same B-type ARRs as the inhibitory effects seen in darkness, WT, *ein2*, *arr1 arr12* and *arr1 arr12 ein2* mutants were grown in darkness and under 50 µmol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> of white light for 5 days. Compared to WT, the *arr1 arr12* seedlings showed a strongly reduced response in light as well as a strongly reduced response in darkness as has been previously shown (Fig. 4, B and D, Fig. S4C and Fig. 8C), suggesting that ARR1 and ARR12 are important regulators of both these responses. Furthermore, as the *arr1 arr12 ein2* mutant behaved similarly to the *arr1 arr12* mutant in both conditions, we conclude that the CK-dependent regulation of hypocotyl elongation is largely ethylene independent, regardless of the light conditions (Fig. 8C).

In conclusion, in line with the importance of COP1/DET1/CIN4 in the CK-induced inhibition of hypocotyl elongation in darkness, addition of light suppresses this BA-dependent phenotypic response. However, higher amounts of light surprisingly results in a switch of the response where CK action changes from an inhibitor to a promoter of hypocotyl elongation.

#### DISCUSSION

In this study, we have re-examined the CK response in dark grown seedlings by using hypocotyl elongation as a proximation for this CK response. CK promotes photomorphogenic development largely independent of ethylene signaling. The deetiolated phenotype of CK-treated seedlings correlates with a transcriptional response resembling a light treatment or a mutation in the *COP/DET/FUS* loci. Furthermore, inhibition of the COP1/CDD module by mutation resulted in CK insensitivity both in regards to hypocotyl elongation and transcriptional regulation of marker genes suggesting a prominent role of these factors in the pathway by which CK promotes photomorphogenesis. A model describing our findings is shown in Figure 9. These intricate connections between the light and CK signaling networks provide an initial molecular framework for further investigation.

### CK promotes photomorphogenesis largely independent of ethylene signaling

Although it is known that CK to promote photomorphogenic development in darkness (Chory *et al.*, 1994), the underlying molecular networks mediating this response are still largely unknown. Moreover, it was suggested that this photomorphogenic response to CK is mediated by increased ethylene biosynthesis (Cary *et al.*, 1995). However, the fact that high CK levels induces opening of cotyledons and emergence of true leaves (Chory *et al.*, 1994; Riefler *et al.*, 2006) suggests the presence of an ethylene-independent pathway by which CK induces photomorphogenic development in dark-grown seedlings.

Here, we have re-examined the role of CK and ethylene in the inhibition of hypocotyl elongation in darkness. Like previously reported (Cary *et al.*, 1995), BA-treatment resulted in a strong inhibition of hypocotyl elongation and a seedling morphology resembling the ethylene triple response (Fig. 1, A-B). However, inhibition of ethylene signaling by AgNO<sub>3</sub>, AVG or the use of the *ein2* mutant revealed that the majority of the CK-induced inhibition of hypocotyl elongation is largely ethylene-independent (Fig. 1, A-B and Fig. S1B). Thus, these data suggest that the triple response observed when seedlings are treated with CK might mask CK-dependent photomorphogenic development which can be overcome either by the addition of very high CK levels (Chory *et al.*, 1994) or by inhibiting the ethylene signaling (this study).

To further characterize the ethylene-independent de-etiolation response to CK, we performed RNA-seq analysis which identified 2463 DEGs in many biological processes but especially related to light signaling, photosynthesis and cell wall modifications in GO term analysis (Fig. 2A and Fig. S2D). In addition, comparing these DEGs with genes responsive to a 6 hours light treatment revealed a significant overlap, indicating that CK treatment might result in the activation of light signaling networks (Fig. 2B-C).

In conclusion, with this set of experiments, we have demonstrated that CK promotes de-etiolation in dark grown seedlings largely independent of ethylene signaling and that this response both phenotypically and on the transcriptomic level resembles a light treatment.

### The COP1/CDD module links CK and light signalling

Although CK is known to regulate many light-induced responses including inhibition of hypocotyl elongation, chlorophyll and anthocyanin accumulation, few direct links between CK and the light signaling networks have been described to date (Chory *et al.*, 1994; Cortleven *et al.*, 2016; Das *et al.*, 2012). An exemption is the role of the central positive regulator of photomorphogenesis HY5, which was shown to be required for CK-induced anthocyanin accumulation under blue light (Vandenbussche *et al.*, 2007). Moreover, HY5 protein levels accumulate in response to CK treatment in WT seedlings while no additional accumulation was observed in the *cop1* mutant, suggesting that COP1 might play a role in mediating the role of CK in anthocyanin accumulation. Although our results rule out a role of HY5 in the response to CK in darkness, the negative regulators of photomorphogenesis, COP1, DET1 and CIN4/COP10, appear to be largely required for this response (Fig. 6 and Fig. S4). By comparing the DEGs in response to BA with genes misregulated in *cop1* and *det1*, we found that ~50% of the BA regulated genes were also regulated by either COP1

and/or DET1 (Fig. 6C-E). Importantly, we further showed that the transcriptional response to CK in the *cop1*, *det1* and *cin4* mutants were strongly reduced (Fig. 7). As these constitutive photomorphogenic mutants are largely insensitive to CK both on the phenotypic and on the transcriptional level, we conclude that CK promotion of deetiolation might require a functional COP1/CDD module. This is in concordance with a study demonstrating that a mutant of the pea *COP1* homologue, namely *LIP1*, also shows a reduced response to isopentenyladenine. This also suggests the requirement for a functional COP1 in CK responses (Sullivan and Gray, 2000).

The ability of COP1 to target positive regulators of photomorphogenesis for degradation is inhibited by light-dependent nuclear exclusion (Pacin et al., 2014). Interestingly, under light conditions, ethylene inhibition of photomorphogenesis as a result of decreased HY5 stability is achieved through the promotion of COP1 nuclear localization (Yu et al., 2013). Although appealing, a mechanism by which CK regulates COP1 cellular localization appears unlikely as it has previously been shown that COP1-GUS is unaffected by CK treatment in etiolated seedlings (von Arnim et al., 1997). Alternatively, COP1 and the CDD complex could either be transcriptionally down-regulated or directly inhibited by CK (via B-type ARRs) in the nucleus. Although a transcriptional regulation is not supported by our RNA-seq experiment (Data S1), in these scenarios CK treatment would results in accumulation of photomorphogenesis promoting COP1 targets other than HY5, as our data suggests that HY5 is not required CK-induced inhibition of hypocotyl elongation (Fig. S4). Lastly, we cannot rule out the possibility that the CK signaling pathway interferes with some of the noncanonical functions of these factors. For example, DET1 has been reported to both act as a transcriptional repressor and in addition involved in chromatin remodeling (Lau et al., 2011; Benvenuto et al., 2002). Together, we have clearly shown that a functional COP1/CDD module is necessary for the CK-mediated light induced response in terms of hypocotyl elongation. However, by which mechanism this is regulated is as yet unknown.

## Light-dependent switch of CK action resembles ethylene signaling

As our data have demonstrated that the COP1/CDD module is required for the ethylene-independent CK response we hypothesized that light, which inhibits

COP1/CDD action, would also result in desensitization of the response to CK (Osterlund et al., 2000; Pacin et al., 2014; Yanagawa et al., 2004). Supportive of this hypothesis, we demonstrated that light levels up to 10 µmol m<sup>-2</sup>s<sup>-1</sup> resulted in a gradual decrease of relative CK sensitivity (Fig. 8A). In higher levels of light however, a striking switch of the CK response was observed where BA treatment resulted in an elongation of the hypocotyl (Fig. 8). A similar observation concerning the effect of CK on hypocotyl elongation in light has been reported (Smets et al., 2005). However, in contrast to our observations, it was shown that the hypocotyl elongation was completely dependent on the presence of the ethylene inhibitor  $AgNO_3$  in the medium. Regardless, this observed light-dependent switch appears analogous to ethylene-dependent regulation of hypocotyl elongation, where ethylene inhibits elongation in the dark and promotes elongation at fluence rates of light over 10 µmol m<sup>-2</sup>s<sup>-1</sup> (Zhong et al., 2012). However, the fact that the ein2 mutant shows only a partially reduced response compared to the WT, suggests that the effect of CK also in light is largely independent of ethylene signaling (Fig. 8). Intriguingly, recent studies have described the direct interaction and activation of the TAA1 promoter by ARR1, ARR10 and ARR12 (Reyes-Olalde et al., 2017; Yan et al., 2017). As reduced TAA1 transcript levels in the arr1 arr10 arr12 mutant was only evident after a light treatment, this suggests that these B-type ARRs might promote auxin driven cell elongation specifically in light grown seedlings. In addition, CK treatment indirectly promotes the transcription of YUC8, a rate limiting enzyme in auxin biosynthesis, through ARR1/ARR12 dependent transcriptional regulation of PIF4 (Di et al., 2016). Thus it is possible that the influence of CK on hypocotyl elongation might switch from a COP1/CDD dominated pathway in the dark to being auxin driven under light.

# Fig. 9. Model for the CK-dependent photomorphogenic response in etiolated *Arabidopsis* seedlings.

Dark-grown seedlings undergo skotomorphogenesis, which is characterized by an elongation of the hypocotyl, closed cotyledons and formation of an apical hook. In presence of CK, a photomorphogenic response results in the inhibition of hypocotyl elongation. This response can be either ethylene-dependent (Cary et al, 1995; Woeste et al., 1999; Hansen et al., 2009) or ethyelene-independent (this study). In the ethylene-independent signaling pathway, the CK signal is mediated through AHK3, in cooperation with either AHK2 or CRE1/AHK4, via the B-type ARRs ARR1 and ARR12 to cause the inhibition of hypocotyl elongation via the modulation of COP1/DET1/CIN4.
