#### **ABSTRACT**

In the model plant *Arabidopsis thaliana*, four Dicer-like proteins (DCL1-4) mediate the production of various classes of small RNAs (sRNAs). Among these four proteins, DCL4 is by far the most versatile RNaseIII-like enzyme and previously identified *dcl4* missense alleles were shown to uncouple the production of the various classes of DCL4-dependent sRNAs. Yet, little is known about the molecular mechanism pertaining this uncoupled production. Here, by studying the subcellular localization, interactome and binding to the sRNA precursors of three distinct *dcl4* missense alleles, we simultaneously highlight the absolute requirement of a specific residue in its helicase domain for efficient production of all DCL4-dependent sRNAs, and identify, within its PAZ domain, an important determinant of DCL4 versatility that is mandatory for efficient processing of intramolecular foldback dsRNA precursors but dispensable for the production of siRNAs from RDR-dependent dsRNA susbtrates. This study not only provides insights into DCL4 mode of action but also delineates interesting tools to further study the complexity of RNA silencing pathways in plants and possibly other organisms.

#### **INTRODUCTION**

RNA silencing or RNA interference (RNAi) refers to a variety of mechanisms mediated by small RNA (sRNA) molecules that lead to sequence-specific suppression of targeted RNA or DNA expression. It plays essential roles in many biological processes including development, maintenance of genome stability or antiviral defence. Among the plethora of proteins involved in these pathways, Dicer or Dicer-like (DCL) proteins are the primary key factors, as evidenced by their conservation in all RNA silencing-competent organisms (Mukherjee et al., 2013). The role of these RNase III-like enzymes is to excise 21-24 nucleotide (nt) sRNA duplexes from structurally distinct double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) precursor molecules. To successfully achieve this key reaction, Dicer or DCL proteins contain several functional domains that typically consist of a DExD/H-box helicase domain, a DUF283 domain which has been annotated as a novel RNA-binding domain (Qin et al., 2010), a PAZ domain, two RNase III domains and one or two dsRNA-binding domains (dsRBDs). In animal Dicers, the PAZ domain and the adjacent Platform domain have been shown to contain two cleft termed the 3' binding pocket and the phosphate binding pocket that are, respectively, responsible for the recognition and binding of the 2-nt 3' overhang (Lingel et al., 2004; Ma et al., 2004; MacRae et al., 2007; MacRae et al., 2006; Song et al., 2003; Yan et al., 2003) and 5' phosphate end of the dsRNA substrates of Dicer (Park et al., 2011; Tian et al., 2014). Structural data of Dicer from *Giardia intestinalis* has revealed that when one end of the dsRNA is anchored into the PAZ domain, the dsRNA stem is placed along the positively charged Platform domain to reach the catalytic centre of Dicer (MacRae et al., 2007; MacRae et al., 2006), where the two RNase III domains form a dimer that cleaves at a fixed distance the dsRNA substrates to produce the 2-nt 3' overhangs and 5' phosphate extremities characteristic of Dicer products (Zhang et al., 2004). More recently in Drosophila, the C-terminal dsRBD of DmDicer-2 was shown to bind the proximal body region of long dsRNA substrates in order to properly align the RNA to the RNA cleavage active site in the RNaseIII domains for efficient and high-fidelity siRNA production (Kandasamy et al., 2017).

In the model plant *Arabidopsis thaliana*, four DCL (DCL1-4) proteins with specialized functions produce the various classes of endogenous or exogenous sRNAs, which confer the sequence specificity of the diverse RNA silencing pathways (Bologna and Voinnet, 2014). While DCL1 produces the majority of (mostly 21-nt) micro (mi)RNAs from relatively short imperfect stemloop RNA precursors (Fahlgren et al., 2007; Kurihara and Watanabe, 2004), DCL3 produces 24 nt long small interfering (si)RNA from polIV- and RDR2-dependent short dsRNA precursors derived from heterochromatic regions of the genome (Blevins et al., 2015; Zhai et al., 2015). The endogenous function of DCL2 is more elusive and this protein is generally considered as a surrogate of DCL3 and DCL4 (Bouché et al., 2006; Gasciolli et al., 2005; Henderson et al., 2006), with the notable exception of the endogenous inverted-repeat (endoIR)-siRNA pathway where DCL2 acts, in conjunction/competition with DCL3 and DCL4, to specifically produce the 22-nt long endoIR-siRNA species from long and RDR-independent dsRNA precursors originating from endogenous loci configured as inverted-repeat (IR) transcripts (Bologna and Voinnet, 2014; Chapman and Carrington, 2007; Henderson et al., 2006; Kasschau et al., 2007; Lindow et al., 2007; Montavon et al., 2017). The fourth DCL protein encoded by the *Arabidopsis* genome, DCL4, is by far the most versatile RNase III-like enzyme in plants. It is involved in the production of 21-nt trans-acting (ta-)siRNAs through sequential processing of long dsRNA precursors generated by the action of RDR6 on single-stranded RNA (Allen et al., 2005; Gasciolli et al., 2005; Xie et al., 2005; Yoshikawa et al., 2005), of 21-nt exogenous (exo) and endogenous (endo) IR-derived siRNAs (Chapman and Carrington, 2007; Dunoyer et al., 2005; Henderson et al., 2006; Kasschau et al., 2007; Lindow et al., 2007; Montavon et al., 2017), of 21-nt viral-derived (v)siRNAs (Blevins et al., 2006; Deleris et al., 2006) and transgene-derived siRNAs (Fusaro et al., 2006; Moissiard et al., 2007) and of some miRNAs (Rajagopalan et al., 2006). DCL4 is also involved in the biogenesis of transposon-derived siRNAs that specifically target transposon (TE) transcripts and, on occasion, endogenes when TEs are epigenetically reactivated (Creasey et al., 2014; Mari-Ordonez et al., 2013; McCue et al., 2012; McCue et al., 2013).

While the reason(s) for the versatility of DCL4 is still not clearly defined, differences in terms of subcellular localization can potentially explain, at least partly, this property. Indeed, as opposed to the three other DCL proteins, which were shown to be exclusively located in the nucleus

(Fang and Spector, 2007; Pontes et al., 2006; Pumplin, 2016; Song et al., 2007; Xie et al., 2004), DCL4 is found in both cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments (Montavon et al., 2017; Pumplin, 2016), and could, therefore, potentially access more types of dsRNA precursor molecules. Another, not mutually exclusive, possibility for this versatility may reside in the intense positive selection observed in the PAZ domain of DCL4, as opposed to the PAZ domain of the other DCL proteins (Mukherjee et al., 2013). These recurrent adaptations of the DCL4 PAZ domain, throughout monocots and dicots, have been postulated to be the consequence of a long-term evolutionary arms race between the main antiviral DCL protein and plant viruses or viroids (Mukherjee et al., 2013). Therefore, given the role of the PAZ domain in recognizing specific RNA motifs (Lingel et al., 2004; Ma et al., 2004; Wang et al., 2009), these changes in its amino acid sequence, to maintain efficient recognition of the various pathogenic RNA molecules, have most likely led to variation in RNA recognition and/or binding properties of this protein (Mukherjee et al., 2013). Finally, DCL4 is known to physically and functionally interact *in vivo* with dsRNA-binding protein 4 (DRB4) (Nakazawa et al., 2007; Qin et al., 2010), which was shown to be required for accurate and efficient production of ta-siRNAs (Adenot et al., 2006; Nakazawa et al., 2007), DCL4-dependent miRNAs (Pelissier et al., 2011; Rajagopalan et al., 2006) and vsiRNAs (Curtin et al., 2008; Jakubiec et al., 2012). However, the existence of additional cofactors that may selectively contribute to the recognition, binding and/or processing of one or the other DCL4 dsRNA substrates cannot currently be discarded.

In order to gain more insights into the molecular basis of this versatility, we studied side-byside three distinct DCL4 missense alleles identified in the course of previous genetic screens (Cuperus et al., 2010; Dunoyer et al., 2007; Dunoyer et al., 2005). Using genetic and biochemical approaches, we show, here, that each of the studied alleles displayed specific defects in DCL4 dependent sRNA production that cannot be attributed to a change in the intracellular localization of DCL4 or a defect in its association with DRB4. More specifically, we found that a specific residue in the PAZ domain of DCL4 is required for efficient processing of intramolecular foldback dsRNA precursors but dispensable for the production of siRNAs from RDR-dependent dsRNA susbtrates. Importantly, we show that this mutated residue does not impact, per se, the binding of DCL4 to those intramolecular foldback structures, suggesting that impaired processing of these specific precursors might be due to their improper accommodation within DCL4 to efficiently reach the RNaseIII catalytic domains. The present study also reveals the requirement of a functional DExD/H-box helicase domain for efficient production of all DCL4 dependent sRNAs, including miRNAs. Collectively, this study provides several new insights into DCL4 ability to process distinct dsRNA substrates and simultaneously delineates interesting tools to further study the complexity of plant RNA silencing pathways. It also further underlines the interest of studying missense mutations to decipher the activity of multifunctional proteins.

### **RESULTS**

# **Distinct DCL4 missense mutations uncouple the production of various DCL4-dependent sRNAs**

Previous genetic screens have allowed the identification of several DCL4 missense alleles that seem to display distinct pattern of DCL4-dependent sRNA accumulation. For instance, whereas some of these alleles can uncouple the production of exoIR-siRNAs from that of tasiRNAs (Dunoyer et al., 2007; Dunoyer et al., 2005), others were shown to be deficient for 21-nt TAS3 tasiRNA production while remaining competent for TAS1-tasiRNA accumulation (Cuperus et al., 2010). In order to gain more insights into the molecular mechanism pertaining this uncoupled production of DCL4-dependent sRNAs, we selected and characterized in more details three distinct missense alleles, namely *dcl4-6*, *dcl4-8* and *dcl4-70b1*. The *dcl4-8* mutant plant carries a mutation within the DEAD-helicase domain (Dunoyer et al., 2007), whereas *dcl4-6* and *dcl4- 70b1* mutant plants exhibit mutations within, or adjacent to, the PAZ domain of DCL4, respectively (**Figure 1A**) (Cuperus et al., 2010; Dunoyer et al., 2005).

We first performed northern analysis of different classes of DCL4-dependent endogenous sRNAs in these mutant backgrounds and found that although *dcl4-6* was still competent to produce TAS1- and TAS3-derived tasiRNAs to WT or near WT levels respectively, accumulation of the DCL4-dependent miRNA miR822 was almost completely abolished (**Figure 1B**). More intriguingly, and as previously reported (Cuperus et al., 2010), we observed that *dcl4-70b1* mutant plants accumulate TAS1-derived tasiRNAs to WT or near WT level, but were strongly impaired in the production of 21-nt TAS3-derived tasiRNA (**Figure 1B**). In addition, we found that production of the DCL4-dependent miRNA miR822 was also strongly compromised in this mutant background (**Figure 1B**). Finally, accumulation of all these DCL4-dependent endogenous sRNAs was strongly impaired in *dcl4-8* mutant plants (**Figure 1B**) (Montavon et al., 2017). Of note, none of these mutations altered the steady-state level of DCL1-dependent miRNA accumulation (**Figure 1B**), including miR173 and miR390, which are specifically required for the production of TAS1- and TAS3-derived tasiRNAs, respectively (Allen et al., 2005). Moreover, the altered ability of these alleles to produce specific DCL4-dependent sRNAs does not result from a destabilization of the mutated DCL4 present in these backgrounds, as revealed by western analysis (**Figure 1C**).

Next, we assessed whether these *dcl4* missense mutations were also impaired in the production of DCL4-dependent endogenous and exogenous IR-derived siRNAs. As opposed to *dcl4-6* and *dcl4-8*, the *dcl4-70b1* mutation was not retrieved from a genetic screen that allowed to score its

effect on exoIR-siRNA accumulation (Cuperus et al., 2010; Dunoyer et al., 2007). Therefore, we introduced this mutation into the SUC:*SUL* reporter system used to isolate the two other missense alleles. In this system, an IR construct driven by the phloem-companion cell-specific AtSUC2 promoter is processed by DCL4 and DCL3 to generate 21- and 24-nt siRNAs, respectively. Of the two siRNA species, only the former is required to trigger RNAi of the endogenous *SULPHUR* (*SUL*) mRNA 10-15 cells beyond the vasculature (Dunoyer et al., 2007; Dunoyer et al., 2005). SUC:*SUL/dcl4-70b1* mutant plants displayed no *SUL*-silencing phenotype (**Figure 2A**) and northern analysis revealed that production of the DCL4-dependent 21-nt *SUL* siRNAs was impaired in this background (**Figure 2B**), indicating that *DCL4-70b1,* like *DCL4-6* and *DCL4-8,* is also unable to efficiently process exoIR-siRNA precursors. Similarly, production of endoIR-siRNAs from several endogenous loci configured as inverted-repeats was found to be strongly compromised in *dcl4-6*, *dcl4-70b1* and *dcl4-8* mutant backgrounds compared to wildtype plants (**Figure 2C),** indicating that all three mutations severely impair DCL4 processing on both endogenous and exogenous IR-derived dsRNA precursors. By contrast, and as expected, DCL3-dependent 24-nt endoIR-siRNAs production was unaffected.

Collectively, the above results support that specific mutations in DCL4 are able to genetically uncouple the production of the diverse DCL4-dependent sRNAs. They also suggest the existence of a gradient of severity in the way each mutation affects DCL4-mediated processing of its various dsRNA substrates (**Figure 1D**). For instance, the observation that all endogenous DCL4 dependent sRNA accumulation was strongly compromised in *dcl4-8* suggests that a functional DEAD-helicase domain is mandatory for efficient processing of all DCL4 substrates and that the residue mutated in *dcl4-8* is a key determinant for the helicase function. On the other hand, the residue mutated in the PAZ domain of *dcl4-6* seems required for efficient processing of intramolecular foldbacks structures (as for miR822, endoIR- and exoIR-siRNA production) but dispensable for the processing of RDR6-dependent dsRNA substrates (e.g. TAS1- and TAS3 derived tasiRNA precursors).

## **Impact of the distinct DCL4 missense mutations on viral-derived siRNA production**

Given that DCL4 is the main antiviral DCL protein (Blevins et al., 2006; Deleris et al., 2006), we also decided to assess how these specific *dcl4* alleles were affected in the production of vsiRNAs. For this purpose, we decided to test two distinct RNA viruses, namely the *Tobacco rattle virus* (TRV) and the *Turnip mosaic virus* (TuMV). In order to follow more easily the spread of these viruses, we used either a modified TRV containing a fragment of the *Arabidopsis* phytoene desaturase (*PDS*) gene, which triggers photobleaching of the infected tissues through virusinduced gene silencing of the *PDS* mRNA (Liu et al., 2002), or a GFP-tagged TuMV (Garcia-Ruiz et al., 2010).

In TRV-infected plants, northern analysis revealed that DCL4-dependent 21-nt vsiRNA accumulation was similarly impaired in *dcl4-6*, *dcl4-8* and *dcl4-70b1* mutant backgrounds than in *dcl4-2* knock-out (KO) mutant plants (**Figure 3A**). As expected, based on the surrogate role of DCL2 during antiviral silencing and the strong accumulation of DCL2-dependent 22-nt vsiRNAs in these backgrounds, none of these mutants exhibited a strong increase in viral RNA accumulation, which was only visible upon genetic inactivation of both DCL4 and DCL2 (**Supplementary Figure S1**) (Deleris et al., 2006).

Interestingly, the situation in TuMV-infected plants was slightly different. Indeed, while the production of DCL4-dependent 21-nt TuMV-derived vsiRNAs was as compromised in *dcl4-8* than in the *dcl4-2* mutant background, a high proportion of 21-nt vsiRNAs was found to accumulate in *dcl4-6* and, although to a lower extent, in *dcl4-70b1* (**Figure 3B**). The difference observed regarding TuMV- versus TRV-derived 21-nt vsiRNA production in *dcl4-6* mutant background could potentially be explained by the nature of the main viral dsRNA substrates processed by DCL4 in these infected cells. Indeed, by analysing the proportion of vsiRNAs of sense and antisense polarity, it has been previously reported that TRV vsiRNAs predominantly derive from intramolecular foldback structures of the viral positive-strand RNA (Donaire et al., 2008). In sharp contrast, no obvious strand bias could be observed for TuMV-derived vsiRNAs suggesting that, for this virus, the antiviral DCL proteins mostly process intermolecular viral dsRNA substrates, produced either by the viral- or plant-encoded RDRs (Garcia-Ruiz et al., 2010).

Therefore, these results further support the specific deficiency of *dcl4-6* in processing intramolecular foldback structures as opposed to its ability to process RDR-dependent dsRNAs, as observed above for endogenous sRNA production (**Figure 1B, 2B and 2C**).

# **Uncoupled production of DCL4-dependent sRNAs does not result from a change in DCL4 intracellular localisation**

These observations prompted us to investigate the molecular mechanism at the basis of the uncoupled production of the various DCL4-dependent sRNAs. As a first step, we decided to test whether this could be due to a change in the subcellular localisation of DCL4.

It has been recently reported that DCL4, when expressed from its endogenous promoter, is mostly localized in the cytoplasm (Montavon et al., 2017; Pumplin, 2016). In addition, DCL4 is, on occasions, also found in the nucleus, almost exclusively as discrete nuclear foci (Montavon et al., 2017; Pontes et al., 2013; Pumplin, 2016). Although the cytoplasmic localisation of DCL4 agrees with its documented role during antiviral silencing against a wide range of RNA viruses that exclusively replicate in the cytoplasm (Blevins et al., 2006; Deleris et al., 2006), and concurs with the cytoplasmic localisation of other components of the tasiRNA biogenesis pathway that produce the TAS dsRNA precursors processed by DCL4 (Jouannet et al., 2012; Kumakura et al., 2009), the function of the nuclear DCL4 is yet unclear.

To address the subcellular localisation of the DCL4 proteins produced by these *dcl4* alleles, we generated transgenic lines expressing, under its own promoter, the genomic sequence of DCL4wt, DCL4-6 and DCL4-8 fused in C-terminal to GFP, in a *dcl4-2* KO mutant background. Unfortunately, we did not manage to obtain the *DCL4-70b1:GFP* fusion construct and were, therefore, unable to assess its intracellular localisation. We selected two independent transgenic lines for each construct that accumulate somewhat similar or slightly lower levels of DCL4 than our positive control, pDCL4-*DCL4wt:GFP* (**Figure 4B**). Importantly, we first verified and confirmed that these GFP fusion proteins behave like their corresponding *dcl4* alleles (**Figure 1B**). For instance, expression of DCL4-6:GFP complemented the deficient production of TAS1 and TAS3-tasiRNAs, whereas miR822 accumulation remained strongly compromised, as observed in *dcl4-6* mutant plants (**Figure 4A**). By contrast, and as previously reported (Montavon et al., 2017), expression of DCL4-8:GFP was unable to complement the defects of sRNA production observed in *dcl4-2*, whereas DCL4wt:GFP restores production of all DCL4 dependent sRNAs back to wild-type levels (**Figure 4A**).

Confocal microscopy analysis of an average of 30 roots per transgenic lines did not reveal any obvious differences in terms of subcellular localisation. Indeed, DCL4-6:GFP and DCL4-8:GFP display the same cytoplasmic localisation as the one previously reported for DCL4wt:GFP construct (**Figure 4C**) (Montavon et al., 2017; Pumplin, 2016). Similarly, DCL4-6: and DCL4- 8:GFP fusions were also observed on occasions in the nucleus, as discrete nuclear foci (**Figure 4C, arrows**). Therefore, these observations strongly argue that the impaired production of DCL4-dependent sRNAs in *dcl4-6* and *dcl4-8* is unlikely to result from a change in the subcellular localization of these DCL4 alleles.

#### **The missense DCL4 mutants are still able to interact with DRB4**

We next decided to assess whether the uncoupled production of the various DCL4-dependent sRNAs observed in the allelic series of *dcl4* could result from a disruption in the association of DCL4 with either (i) its cofactor, DRB4 (Fukudome et al., 2011; Nakazawa et al., 2007), or (ii) other unknown and pathway-specific interactors.

To address the first possibility, we decided to perform co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments. For that purpose, we generated *dcl4-2* transgenic lines expressing, under its own promoter, the genomic sequence of DCL4wt, DCL4-6, DCL4-8 and DCL4-70b1, fused in Cterminal to two Flag and two HA tags (FHA). As for the GFP fusion constructs (**Figure 4A**), we first verified that the FHA fusion proteins behave like their corresponding mutant alleles by complementing the production of the DCL4-dependent sRNAs for which they are competent (**Figure 1B**). As northern analysis revealed that this was indeed the case (**Figure 5A**), we immunoprecipitated these various DCL4:FHA fusion proteins and analysed the level of DRB4 retrieved in the IP fractions (**Figure 5B**).

Western blot analysis revealed no significant difference in the association of this cofactor between wild-type and mutated DCL4 alleles, indicating that the specific defects on sRNA production, triggered by the *dcl4* missense mutations studied here, are unlikely to result from a problem in the formation of the DCL4/DRB4 complex (**Figure 5B**). This result was more or less expected, at least for *dcl4-6* and *dcl4-70b1* mutant plants, based on their specific pattern of sRNA accumulation (**Figure 1B, 2B-C and 3A-B**), as opposed to the general requirement of DRB4 for the efficient production of all DCL4-dependent sRNAs (Adenot et al., 2006; Dunoyer et al., 2007; Eamens et al., 2009; Nakazawa et al., 2007). Moreover, DRB4 was shown to interact with the DUF283 domain of DCL4 that is distinct from the domains mutated in these alleles (Qin et al., 2010).

Having discarded a potential defect in the interaction with DRB4, IP fractions were also sent to mass spectrometry (MS) analysis in order to identify putative new protein partners of DCL4 that might be specifically involved in the biogenesis of one of the DCL4-dependent sRNAs and for which interaction might be selectively lost with one, or more, of the *dcl4* missense alleles impaired in this/these specific pathway(s). Unfortunately, at least in our IP conditions, mass spectrometry analysis of four independent experiments failed to reveal an obvious loss of interaction with potential pathway-specific cofactors, arguing against the above hypothesis to explain the selective defects of DCL4-dependent sRNA production observed in these mutants.

Of note, however, and in line with the co-IP results (**Figure 5B**), DRB4 was found to be the top scoring protein, with very high coverage and spectral count, in all wild-type and mutated DCL4:FHA IP fractions (**Supplementary Table S1**), further supporting that none of the studied mutations impaired DCL4 interaction with DRB4. Interestingly, very high coverage and spectral count of peptides corresponding to a protein called NFD2/RTL4 (AT1G24450), belonging to the Ribonuclease III family, was also reproducibly and consistently found associated in the IP fractions of both wild-type and mutant alleles of DCL4:FHA fusion constructs (**Supplementary Table S1)**, suggesting that this protein might be a new cofactor of DCL4, or that DCL4 might be involved in some of the function operated by NFD2 (Portereiko et al., 2006). Similarly, very high coverage and spectral count of the HSP70-2 protein (AT5G02490) was also retrieved in three out of the four MS/MS analysis of both wild-type and mutated DCL4 IP fractions (**Supplementary Table S1**). However, northern analysis of various DCL4-dependent sRNA accumulation failed to reveal any differences between wild-type and *hsp70-2* KO mutant plants **Accepted Article**

(**Supplementary Figure S2**), leaving open the function, if any, of the DCL4/HSP70-2 interaction observed here. Finally, DRB2 was also found in two out of the four IP-MS/MS analysis suggesting that this DRB can also potentially interact with DCL4. Although relatively low score and spectral count of DRB2 was retrieved in these IP fractions, suggesting weak or transient interaction, this observation could potentially be at the basis of the documented role of DRB2 in the accumulation of DCL4-dependent miRNAs (Pelissier et al., 2011).

# **Mutation in the PAZ domain of DCL4-6 does not impair binding of specific dsRNA substrates**

We then sought to determine whether the selective defects in DCL4-dependent sRNA production exhibited by some of the mutant alleles studied here, could result from an impaired ability of these proteins to bind specific dsRNA substrates. This was an attractive hypothesis, particularly for *dcl4-6* as this allele (i) is able to process RDR-dependent dsRNA substrates but not intramolecular RNA foldback structures (**Figure 1 and 2**) and (ii) carries a mutation within the PAZ domain that was shown, in animal Dicer, to be the module that binds the ends of dsRNA precursors (MacRae et al., 2007; MacRae et al., 2006).

To test this hypothesis, we performed RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) experiments using the pDCL4-*DCL4-6:FHA*/*dcl4-2* transgenic lines and determined the immunoprecipitated RNAs by high-throughput sequencing (**Figure 6 and Supplementary Figure S3**). As a positive control for these RIP experiments, we decided to use the pDCL4-*DCL4-8:FHA*/*dcl4-2* transgenic line, which was previously shown to efficiently bind to, and stably interact with, the various DCL4 dsRNA substrates as a result of its inability to process them into sRNAs (Montavon et al., 2017). In parallel, wild-type Col-0 plants were used as negative control.

As expected, based on the wild type or near wild-type levels of ta-siRNA production observed in DCL4-6-expressing plants (**Figure 1B, 4A and 5A**), very low number of reads corresponding to various ta-siRNA precursors (TAS1a, TAS1b, TAS1c, TAS2 and TAS3) were retrieved in the DCL4-6:FHA IP fraction, as a result of their efficient processing into sRNAs by this mutant allele (**Figure 6A and Supplementary Figure S3**). In parallel, analysis of the DCL4-8:FHA IP fraction revealed a robust association of the various TAS-dsRNA precursors with this allele (**Figure 6A and Supplementary Figure S3**). Of note, and in agreement with the two-hit trigger hypothesis (Axtell et al., 2006), both 5'- and 3'- border of the TAS3 dsRNA precursor bound by DCL4-8 very nicely match with the two miR390 target sites of the TAS3 transcript (**Figure 6A and Supplementary Figure S3)**, which define the region converted by RDR6 into dsRNA (Adenot et al., 2006; Allen et al., 2005; Montgomery et al., 2008a; Rajeswaran and Pooggin, 2012). Similarly, the 5'-border of the TAS1a/b/c and TAS2 dsRNA precursors bound by DCL4-8 coincide with the miR173 target sites of these transcripts (Montgomery et al., 2008b; Yoshikawa et al., 2005).

However, for these precursors, we could not observe a clear 3'-border, normally defined by the TAS1c 3'D6(-) siRNA target site (Rajeswaran et al., 2012), as production of this specific siRNA, like the other DCL4-dependent sRNAs (**Figure 1B, 4A and 5A**), is most likely strongly compromised in DCL4-8-expressing plants.

Interestingly, and in sharp contrast to the results obtained for the TAS precursors, DCL4-6 was found to efficiently bind to, and stably interact with, various intramolecular foldback precursors (IR71, IR2039 and pre-miR822) (**Figure 6A**), as revealed by the similar amount of reads corresponding to these substrates retrieved in both DCL4-6:FHA and DCL4-8:FHA IP fractions (**Figure 6B**). Comparable results were obtained in both seedling (**Figure 6**) and flower tissues (**Supplementary Figure S3**). These observations, therefore, strongly suggest that the selective defects in sRNA production observed in *dcl4-6* are unlikely to result from an impaired ability of this allele to bind specific dsRNA precursors (i.e. intramolecular foldback structures).

RIP experiments followed by northern blot analysis further confirmed the sequencing results. Indeed, IR71 and miR822 precursors were found similarly associated to DCL4-6 and DCL4-8, whereas the TAS1 and TAS3 precursors were barely detectable in DCL4-6:FHA IP fraction, but were strongly stabilized in association with DCL4-8 (**Figure 7**). Of note, analysis of DCL4-70b1 in these RIP experiments revealed that this allele behaves like DCL4-6 regarding its association to IR71, miR822 and TAS1 precursors. However, despite being as impaired as DCL4-8 for TAS3 derived tasiRNA production (**Figure 1B, 2B and 5A**), the level of TAS3 precursor bound by DCL4-70b1 was much lower than the one found in DCL4-8:FHA IP fraction (**Figure 7**). This observation suggests that, as opposed to the intramolecular foldback-derived sRNA, the defective production of TAS3-derived tasiRNAs could be due to impaired binding of DCL4-70b1 to this specific precursor.

## **DISCUSSION**

By analysing *in vivo* the intracellular localization, interactome and processing activity of three distinct DCL4 missense alleles, this study not only provides interesting new information concerning the mode of action of the most versatile DCL protein in plants, but also delineates potential tools that might be useful to the community to further study the complexity of RNA silencing pathways in plants, and possibly other organisms.

In animals, the function of the DExD/H-box helicase domain of Dicers has been extensively studied. For instance, *Drosophila* DmDicer-1, which processes miRNA precursors, lacks a functional helicase domain and does not require ATP for activity (Lee et al., 2004), whereas the siRNA-generating enzyme, DmDicer-2, requires ATP and a functional helicase domain to efficiently cleave long dsRNA (Jiang et al., 2005; Lee et al., 2004; Liu et al., 2003). Similarly, the helicase domain of the *Caenorhabditis elegans*-encoded Dicer seems required for the production of some endogenous siRNAs but dispensable for miRNAs maturation (Ma et al., 2008; Pavelec et al., 2009; Sinha et al., 2015; Welker et al., 2011; Welker et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2002). These observations agreed with the notion that the helicase domain is required to efficiently translocate Dicer proteins along the length of a long dsRNA precursor, in order to produce multiple siRNAs, but is unnecessary for the single cleavage event required for miRNA processing in animals.

In plants, the contribution of the helicase domain of DCL proteins has been, so far, only studied for the miRNA-generating enzyme DCL1. Genetic and biochemical characterization of missense *dcl1* mutants, retrieved in two independent genetic screens, suggested that the helicase domain of DCL1 might have an autoinhibitory function (Liu et al., 2012; Tagami et al., 2009), like in the human Dicer (Kennedy et al., 2015; Ma et al., 2008). This autoinhibitory function of the DCL1 helicase domain may be required to slow down DCL1 translocation along the miRNA precursor, in order to ensure accurate processing and miRNA production (Liu et al., 2012). By contrast, very little is known regarding the requirement of the helicase domain for the functions of the siRNA-generating DCLs in plants. A previously identified missense mutation in the helicase domain of DCL4 (E560K) was shown to trigger a strong decrease in ta-siRNA accumulation (Yoshikawa et al., 2005). Unfortunately, the effect of this mutation on the production of other DCL4-dependent sRNAs was not tested. The findings made in the present study reveal that the *dcl4-8* helicase mutation (G587D) does not alter DCL4 stability (**Figure 1C**), intracellular localization (**Figure 3**), interaction with DRB4 (**Figure 5 and Supplementary Table S1**), or binding to the DCL4-dependent sRNA precursors (**Figure 6, 7 and Supplementary Figure S3**), yet it strongly compromises the production of all DCL4-dependent endogenous and exogenous sRNAs (**Figure 1, 2 and 3**). Therefore, collectively, these results strongly suggest that a functional helicase domain is required for the efficient processing of the various dsRNA substrates of DCL4 and that the dichotomy observed in animals regarding the requirement of the Dicer helicase domain during siRNA versus miRNA production also applies to plants. Incidentally, the glycine residue mutated in *dcl4-8* (G587D) is highly conserved across species (**Supplementary Figure S4A and Supplementary Methods**) and the *C. elegans* strain *dcr-1*(*mg375*), defective in endogenous siRNA production but competent for miRNA accumulation, harbours a mutation of the same glycine residue (G492R) (Pavelec et al., 2009; Welker et al., 2011). This observation suggests that mutation of this conserved residue will, most likely, inactivate the processivity and catalytic activity of any siRNA-generating Dicer protein, without impairing the binding to its dsRNA substrates. Given the stable interaction observed between *DCL4-8* and its various endogenous dsRNA targets (**Figure 6 and 7**), this property can be useful to the community to determine the full range of substrates of a given siRNA-generating Dicer, in

either whole tissues, specific cells, normal or various stress conditions. For instance, in plants, expression of an epitope-tagged DCL2 missense allele, harbouring a mutation in the G487 residue (**Supplementary Figure S4A**), may help to delineate the full-spectra of its endogenous substrates and, in turn, contribute to a better understanding of its endogenous functions, including the reasons of its deleterious effects on development in the absence of DCL1 and DCL4 (Bouché et al., 2006).

This approach can also potentially be used to determine the primary viral targets of the antiviral Dicer or DCL proteins. Indeed, although not tested in the present study, the stable association of DCL4-8 to the various endogenous DCL4-dependent sRNA precursors (**Figure 6 and 7**) strongly suggests that exogenous viral substrates will be similarly stabilized in association with this allele. Therefore, plants expressing a G587D mutant version of DCL4, or its DCL2 equivalent, can be used through RIP-seq experiments to empirically determine what are the primary viral targets of the two main antiviral DCL proteins in plants (i.e. replication intermediates versus intramolecular foldback structures within single-stranded viral RNA). This approach may also be implemented in *Drosophila*, for the antiviral DmDicer-2.

In line with this, another interesting observation that arises from the characterization of the *dcl4-8* mutant plants is their apparent, and somewhat counter-intuitive, increased resistance against TuMV. This increased resistance is characterized by a clear delay in TuMV systemic infection compared to wild-type plants or the other *dcl4* mutants tested here (**Supplementary Figure S5**). Given that production of the various DCL4-dependent sRNAs, including vsiRNAs, is similarly impaired in *dcl4-8* than in the *dcl4-2* KO mutant plants (**Figure 1, 2 and 3**), we speculate that the increased virus resistance displayed by *dcl4-8* results from the expression, in this background, of a stable, dsRNA binding-competent, yet catalytically inactive, DCL4 protein. These properties will, somehow, lock DCL4-8 on its viral RNA targets and potentially affect their translation and/or replication. Interestingly, expression of a catalytically inactive Drosha (the RNaseIII-like enzyme responsible for the initial cleavage of miRNA precursors in animals) has recently been shown to trigger an increased resistance against several positive-strand RNA viruses (Aguado et al., 2017). In the case of Sindbis virus-infected cells, this increased resistance was achieved through preferential binding of a specific hairpin structure at the 5' end of the viral genome, which reduces viral RDR processivity by 50% (Aguado et al., 2017). It is, therefore, tempting to speculate that similarly to Drosha, DCL4-8 can act, independently of its enzymatic activity, as an antiviral clamp to dampen viral infections.

Study of the two other *dcl4* alleles also provided interesting information. The findings that DCL4-6 is competent to efficiently process RDR-dependent dsRNA substrates but deficient for the processing of intramolecular foldbacks structures (**Figure 1, 2 and 3**), identify the arginine R1028 as an important determinant of DCL4 versatility. Indeed, mutation of this residue uncouples the production of the DCL4-dependent sRNAs from these two distinct categories of precursors, without altering DCL4 localization (**Figure 4**), interaction with DRB4 (**Figure 5**) or precluding DCL4 binding to the intramolecular foldback dsRNA substrates (**Figure 6 and Supplementary Figure S3**). Therefore, the most straightforward interpretation of these results is that R1028 somehow contributes to properly accommodate or position intramolecular foldback structures within the DCL4 PAZ domain, such that the two RNase III domains can efficiently cleave this type of substrates.

In the absence of raw structural data regarding DCL4, we can only infer, from computationally predicted model of its PAZ domain (Mickiewicz et al., 2017) and work done in other organisms, what could explain the requirement of R1028 in this process. For instance, alignment of the DCL4 PAZ domain sequence with those from humans, *C. elegans* and *Drosophila* Dicer(s), reveals that this residue corresponds to R1003 in hDicer (**Supplementary Figure S4B**). The human Dicer R1003 residue has been shown to participate in the formation of the basic 5'-phosphate binding pocket (Park et al., 2011), which plays an important role for site-specific cleavage of pre-miRNAs containing thermodynamically unstable ends (Tian et al., 2014). We can therefore speculate that, similarly to the R1003 in hDicer, R1028 in DCL4 may participate in the formation of a phosphate-binding pocket that is required to properly position dsRNA substrates with thermodynamically less stable ends. Incidentally, intramolecular foldback structures, as opposed to RDR-dependent dsRNA substrates, have greater chance to fulfil this thermodynamic criterion.

In parallel, two homology-based models of the Platform, PAZ, connector helix and RNaseIII domains of DCL4, have recently been generated (Mickiewicz et al., 2017). This study suggested the existence of a long PAZ loop, made of residues 1019 to 1041, that performed extensive interactions with dsRNA in two potential ways. In the first model, this long PAZ loop interacted with the RNA major groove and contributed to the formation of a putative 5'-binding pocket made of electropositive residues, whereas in the second model, the PAZ loop interacted with backbone atoms at RNA minor groove site, far from the RNA 5'-end (Mickiewicz et al., 2017). Interestingly, the authors of this study pinpoint R1028 as a residue engaged in the interaction with RNA. They also predicted that the long PAZ loop, to which R1028 belongs, is involved in directing the 3' RNA strand into the PAZ 3'-binding pocket and that substitution of this specific

residue may result in the alteration of RNA binding ability (Mickiewicz et al., 2017). The results presented here seem to, at least partly, support these predictions.

Finally, study of the *dcl4-70b1* allele revealed that the G1062R mutation strongly impairs DCL4 activity. Indeed, out of the different endogenous and exogenous dsRNA susbtrates tested here, DCL4-70b1 was only able to process TAS1 dsRNA precursor, yet to slightly reduced level than wild-type DCL4 (**Figure 1B, 2B, 3, 5A**). Of note, although not conserved between DCLs, G1062 is highly conserved among the DCL4 from various plant species (Mukherjee et al., 2013), suggesting that G1062 is an important residue for DCL4 function or folding. Using the computationally predicted model of DCL4 (Mickiewicz et al., 2017), it appears that G1062 is spatially close to the R872 residue (Cα-Cα distance of 13Å), which is predicted to be important for protein-RNA recognition through formation of hydrogen bonds with the 5'-end of dsRNA substrates (**Supplementary Figure S6).** Therefore, the G1062R substitution in *dcl4-70b1* can potentially destabilize this interaction through electrostatic repulsion between the positively charged side chains of these two arginine residues. Alternatively, the R1062 residue of DCL4- 70b1 can compete with R872 to form these hydrogen bonds with the 5'-end of dsRNA, which should, in turn, lead to a change in the positioning of the substrates within DCL4.

Although full understanding of DCL4 versatility will only be achieved upon resolution of its structure, in-depth analysis of additional missense mutations, when available, should really be considered as an asset to decipher its specificity.

### **EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES**

#### **Plant materials, transformation and virus inoculation**

Knock-out (KO) T-DNA mutant line *dcl4-2* (GABI\_160G05) and missense mutant *dcl4-6, dcl4-8*  and *dcl4-70b1* were described previously (Cuperus et al., 2010; Dunoyer et al., 2007; Dunoyer et al., 2005). *Arabidopsis thaliana* reference ecotype used was Columbia. The 35S-CFP:HA (Wave1C) line (Col-0 ecotype) was previously described (Geldner et al., 2009). Plant seeds were stratified for 2–4 days at 4°C before growth under standard conditions at 22°C with a 16 h light / 8 h dark cycle, either in soil or on Murashige and Skoog (MS) agar plates supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/ml), or phosphinothricin (10 μg/ml). *Arabidopsis* were transformed using the floral dip method (Bechtold and Pelletier, 1998).

Binary vectors carrying the infectious clone of TRV-PDS or TuMV-GFP were described previously (59, 60). These vectors were mobilized into Agrobacterium strain GV3101 and used for virus infection in *Arabidopsis*. Infected systemic leaves were collected at 12 dpi for TuMV-GFP infection and 18 dpi for TRV-PDS infection.

#### **DNA constructs**

The DCL4:FHA/GFP and DCL4-8:FHA/GFP contructs were previously described (Montavon et al., 2017; Pumplin, 2016). For the DCL4-6:FHA/GFP and DCL4-70b1:FHA constructs, the genomic coding sequence of DCL4 from the corresponding mutant plants (Cuperus et al., 2010; Dunoyer et al., 2007; Dunoyer et al., 2005) was cloned, in fusion with GFP or double-epitope tag (2xFlag 2xHA), as C-terminal fusions under the control of its endogenous promoter (1,5 kb upstream of the ATG), in pB7GW34 vector using the ''MultiSite Gateway Three- Fragment Vector Construction Kit'' (Invitrogen).

#### **RNA analysis**

Total RNA was extracted from *Arabidopsis* tissues with Tri-Reagent (Sigma, St Louis, MO) according to manufacturer's instructions. RNA gel blot analysis of high and low molecular weight RNA was on 5 and 45 μg of total RNA, respectively, and was conducted as described previously (Schott et al., 2012). Radiolabelled probes for detection of the IR71, IR2039, At4g21366, At5g22960, TuMV-GFP, TRV-PDS or SUL siRNAs were made by random priming reactions (Promega) in the presence of α-32P-dCTP. The template used was a 400-600-bp-long PCR product amplified from either the Arabidopsis gDNA (IR71, IR2039, At4g21366, At5g22960, PDS, SUL) or the mGFP5 ORF (TuMV-GFP). DNA oligonucleotides complementary to miRNAs or trans-acting siRNAs (Table S1) were end-labelled with γ-32P-ATP using T4 PNK (Thermo Scientific). Detection of the *Arabidopsis* U6 small nuclear RNA was used to confirm equal loading. Source data can be found with the Supporting information.

### **Protein extraction and analysis**

Total proteins were extracted from *Arabidopsis* flower buds as previously described (Hurkman and Tanaka, 1986) and were resolved on SDS–PAGE. After electroblotting onto Immobilon-P membrane (Millipore), protein gel blot analysis was carried out using the appropriate antiserum. The specificity of DRB4 and DCL4 antibodies used in this study has been verified by western blot analysis on protein extracts from wild type and the corresponding T-DNA mutant plants (Schott et al., 2012). Source data can be found with the Supporting information.

#### **Protein and RNA immunoprecipitation**

For immunoprecipitation, 0.1 g of flower buds was ground in liquid nitrogen, and homogenized in 1 ml of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100) containing 1 tablet/50 ml of protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) for 15 min at 4°C. Cell debris was removed by two successive centrifugations at 13 000 rpm at 4°C for 10 min. After the second centrifugagtion an aliquot of supernatants was taken for input fraction. The remaining extracts were incubated with magnetic microparticles coated with monoclonal GFP or HA antibodies (MACS purification system, Miltenyi Biotech) at 4°C for 30 min. Samples were passed through Mcolumn (MACS purification system, Miltenyi Biotech) and an aliquot of the flow-through fractions was taken. The Mcolumn were then washed 2 times with 500 μl of lysis buffer and 1 time with 100μl of washing buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5). To elute the immunoprecipited proteins, 95°C pre-warmed western blot loading buffer (10 % glycerol, 4 % SDS, 62,5 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 5 % (v/v) 2-β-mercaptoethanol, Bromophenol Blue) was passed through the Mcolumn. Proteins were analysed by western blotting. For RNA extraction from immunoprecipitated proteins, magnetic beads were eluted with 50°C pre-warmed Tri-Reagent (Sigma, St Louis, MO) and RNA were extracted according to manufacturer's instructions. RNA were analysed by northern blotting or sequencing. Source data can be found with the Supporting information.

#### **Sequencing and bioinformatics analyses**

**Accepted Article**

Immunoprecipitated RNA from Col-0, pDCL4-*DCL4-6:FHA*/*dcl4-2* and pDCL4-*DCL4-8:FHA*/*dcl4- 2* seedlings or flowers was extracted with Tri-Reagent (Sigma) and used for library preparation. The libraries were constructed using the TruSeq SBS kit V3 from Illumina following manufacturer's instructions and the libraries were sequenced as single-end 50 nt reads using an Illumina HiSeq 2500 (Fasteris, Switzerland). Reads were mapped using Hisat2 (v.2.0.5) (Kim et al., 2015), with the default parameters, to the TAIR10 assembly. Genome coverage of the loci of interest were made with genomeCoverageBed from bedtools suite (v.2.25.0) (Quinlan and Hall, 2010). Data were normalized with the total output number of reads obtained for each corresponding library (**Supplementary Table S2**). Coordinates of tasiRNA-producing loci were found in tasiRNAdb (Zhang et al., 2014). Coordinates of endoIR-siRNA-producing loci were taken from (Montavon et al., 2017). Coordinates of the DCL4-dependent miRNA-producing loci were found in miRBase (v.21) (Griffiths-Jones et al., 2006). The data reported in this paper have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo (accession no. GSE106383).

#### **Imaging**

Imaging of fluorescent protein fusions was performed in roots of 2 weeks old stablytransformed plants using confocal microscope LSM700 or LSM780 from Carl Zeiss with a 40x objective and a 488-nm laser for excitation. Images were converted in .tif and scale bars added using ImageJ software (NIH). At least 30 roots of each genotype were analyzed.
