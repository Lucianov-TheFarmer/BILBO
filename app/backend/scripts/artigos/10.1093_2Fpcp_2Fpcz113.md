# **The Role of RNA Binding Protein** *Os***Tudor-SN in Post-Transcriptional Regulation of Seed Storage Proteins and Endosperm Development**

### **Abstract**

Tudor-SN is involved in a myriad of transcriptional and post-transcriptional processes due to its modular structure consisting of 4 tandem SN domains (4SN module) and C-terminal Tsn module consisting of Tudor-partial SN domains. We had previously demonstrated that *Os*Tudor-SN is a key player for transporting storage protein mRNAs to specific ER subdomains in developing rice endosperm. Here, we provide genetic evidence that this multi-functional RBP is required for storage protein expression, seed development and protein body formation. The rice EM1084 line, possessing a nonsynonymous mutation in the 4SN module (SN3 domain), exhibited a strong reduction in grain weight and storage protein accumulation, while a mutation in the Tudor domain (47M) or the loss of the Tsn module (43M) had much smaller effects. Immunoelectron microscopic analysis showed the presence of a new protein body type containing glutelin and prolamine inclusions in EM1084, while 43M and 47M exhibited structurally-modified prolamine and glutelin protein bodies. Transcriptome analysis indicates that *Os*Tudor-SN also functions in regulating gene expression of transcriptional factors and genes involved in developmental processes and stress responses as well as for storage proteins. Normal protein body formation, grain weight, and expression of many genes were partially restored in EM1084 transgenic line complemented with wildtype *Os*Tudor-SN gene. Overall, our study showed that *Os*Tudor-SN possesses multiple functional properties in rice storage protein expression and seed development and that the 4SN and Tsn modules have unique roles in these processes.

**Keywords:** RNA binding protein, Rice, Tudor-SN, Storage proteins, Protein body, Mutant analysis, Rice transformation, Transcriptomics.

### **Introduction**

Rice is unique among flowering plants in that its seeds synthesize and accumulate substantial quantities of two major types of storage proteins: the acid- and alkali-soluble glutelins and the alcoholic-soluble prolamines as well as minor amounts of the saline-soluble -globulins (Muench et al. 1997). Prolamines accumulate as ER-lumenal intracisternal granules (PB-I), whereas glutelins and globulins are deposited in the protein storage vacuole (PB-II) (Krishnan et al. 1986; Yamagata and Tanaka 1986). The mRNAs that encode for prolamines, glutelins and globulins are targeted to morphologically distinct ER membranes in developing endosperm cells. Prolamines and globulin mRNAs are asymmetrically distributed to the rough ER membranes (PB-ER) delimiting the prolamine intracisternal granules while glutelin mRNAs are located on adjacent cisternal-ER (Choi et al. 2000; Hamada et al. 2003a; Li et al. 1993; Washida et al. 2012a). The distribution of prolamine and glutelin mRNAs to distinct subdomains of the cortical ER contribute to the distribution of their protein products to different locations within the endomembrane system (Washida et al. 2012b; Washida et al. 2004).

RNA transport is a multi-step processes that requires *cis*- (Singer 1993) and *trans*elements for modulating its localization (Eliscovich and Singer 2017; Hermesh and Jansen 2013; Martin and Ephrussi 2009; Medioni et al. 2012). Results from previous studies from this laboratory (Hamada et al. 2003b; Washida et al. 2009; Yang et al. 2014) have shown that specific *cis*-sequences (zipcodes), located in the coding sequence and 3'UTR of the prolamine and glutelin mRNAs, are required for targeting of these mRNAs to their respective ER subdomains. Several RBPs that recognize and bind to these zipcode sequences have been identified as potential *trans*-factors (Crofts et al. 2010; Doroshenk et al. 2009; Doroshenk et al. 2014; Tian et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2014). These RBPs bind to the zipcode sequences and form a ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex soon after transcription in the nucleus.

In addition to RBPs that recognize zipcode mRNA sequences, additional RBPs are required for basal processes of mRNA transport and localization. One such RBP is *Os*Tudor-SN, which was initially identified as a major cytoskeletal RBP in developing rice seeds that binds specifically to the 3'UTR sequences of prolamine and glutelin mRNAs (Sami-Subbu et al. 2001; Sami-Subbu et al. 2000). The role of this RBP in mRNA localization has been suggested via *in vivo* analysis of RNA-IP and immunofluorescence studies of GFP-tagged prolamine mRNA (Wang et al. 2008). Direct evidence for a role for *Os*Tudor-SN in mRNA transport was provided

by the partial disruption of storage protein localization on the cortical-ER in mutant endosperm cells (Chou et al. 2017). In addition to mRNA movement, our earlier study of RNAi and overexpression transgenic rice lines indicated that *Os*Tudor-SN is involved in transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory processes, thus modulating gene expression. Down-regulated expression of *Os*Tudor-SN reduced the levels of prolamine and glutelin storage proteins, whereas overexpression of *Os*Tudor-SN increased these storage protein amounts (Wang et al. 2008).

 Here, we provide evidence from the study of mutant and transgenic-complemented mutant lines that *Os*Tudor-SN functions in post-transcriptional regulation of storage protein expression and grain development as *Os*Tudor-SN mutant lines exhibit considerable differences in storage protein accumulation, grain weight and seed appearance. In addition to mis-localizing storage protein mRNAs, mutations in both the 4SN and Tsn modules perturb the packaging of the coded proteins in protein bodies.

#### **Results**

### *Os***Tudor-SN is function in regulating storage protein expression and seed development**

We had previously identified four rice lines containing mutations in the *Os*Tudor-SN gene (Chou et al. 2017). Three rice lines contained nonsynonymous mutations that changed amino acid sequence and, in turn, structure (Fig. 1). EM1084 contains a missense mutation where Glu at position 503 is replaced by a Lys (E503K) while PMT64 and 47M contain amino acid replacements, H753Q and P834L, respectively, in the Tudor domain of the Tsn module. A fourth rice line, 43M, contained a nonsense mutation in the SN4 domain resulting in premature termination of protein synthesis. To dissect the roles of the 4SN and Tsn modules of *Os*Tudor-SN during seed development, we studied in detail the biochemical and physiological properties of these rice mutant lines.

Fig. 2A depicts the protein expression of *Os*Tudor-SN and storage proteins in grains of wildtype and mutant plants via immunoblotting analysis (Fig. 2A). The nonsense mutation in 43M results in the disappearance of the 120 kDa *Os*Tudor-SN and Tsn polypeptides and the appearance of a truncated *Os*Tudor-SN identical in size to the 80 kDa N-terminal 4SN polypeptide present in developing wildtype grains (Fig. 2A). EM1084 accumulated lower amounts of intact 120 kDa *Os*Tudor-SN and 80 kDa 4SN module polypeptides than wildtype

(Fig. 2B). While amounts of the intact 120 kDa *Os*Tudor-SN were significantly lower in the 47M mutant than wildtype, 4SN and Tsn levels were higher suggesting elevated proteolytic cleavage of the 120 kDa protein in this line. PMT64 contained slightly higher amounts for the 120 kDa *Os*Tudor-SN but much higher amounts of 4SN and Tsn polypeptides. Overall, while the levels of intact 120 kDa *Os*Tudor-SN, 4SN and Tsn proteins varied in the individual mutant lines, the total content of *Os*Tudor-SN protein was not significantly altered in the various mutant lines.

The impact of these mutations on storage protein accumulation was assessed. EM1084 showed elevated accumulation of the glutelin precursor with corresponding decreases in processed glutelin acidic and basic subunits (Fig. 2B). 43M, PMT64 and 47M mutant lines showed substantially decreased glutelin expression especially at the acidic and basic subunit levels. As prolamine levels were also reduced in these four mutant lines (Fig. 2B), *Os*Tudor-SN is required for optimal storage protein gene expression.

All mutant four lines grew slower than wildtype with EM1084 showing the largest delay in flowering, indicating that the Tudor-SN mutations also affected plant growth and development in general. Unlike the hard-vitreous grains from wildtype and 43M, those from EM1084, PMT64 and 47M were chalky or floury in appearance (Fig. 2C). The seed morphology also correlated with the average grain weight in these mutant lines (Fig. 2D). Three of the mutant lines showed consistent decreases in 1000-grain weight with grains from EM1084 showing the most severe decreases attaining only about 60% (*p*=0.0002) of wildtype. 43M and 47M exhibited milder decreases in grain weight of 15% (*p*=0.015) and 12% (*p*=0.023), respectively. By contrast, seeds of PMT64 were 5% (*p*=0.019) higher in weight than wildtype. These significant differences in average grain weight exhibited by *Os*Tudor-SN mutant lines mediated by mutations in the SN3 domain (EM1084) and Tsn module (43M, 47M and PMT64) support the view that the 4SN and Tsn modules govern distinct molecular and physiological processes of plant growth and development.

#### **The Tsn module controls prolamine distribution and PB formation**

Prolamines and glutelins are deposited in protein body type I (PB-I, formed as an intracisternal granule of prolamine polypeptides) and the protein storage vacuole PB-II, respectively (Krishnan et al. 1986; Muench and Okita 1997; Tanaka et al. 1980). The formation of these protein bodies is facilitated by the segregation of the storage protein mRNAs to PB-ER

(prolamine mRNAs) and cisternal-ER (glutelin mRNAs) (Choi et al. 2000; Li et al. 1993; Washida et al. 2009), respectively. In addition to the asymmetric distribution of prolamine mRNAs to the PB-ER, the structure of PB-I is also influenced by the temporal expression patterns of the different prolamine polypeptide classes during seed development. The earliest expressing prolamines are the 10 kDa polypeptides, which occupy the central core region of PB-I .This is followed by the expression and deposition of 16 kDa and 13 kDa sulfur-rich and sulfur poor prolamines, which are deposited sequentially around the central 10 kDa core to form a spherical intracisternal granule exhibiting concentric layers of varying electron density (Nagamine et al. 2011; Saito et al. 2012). In view of the mis-localization of storage protein mRNAs in the *Os*Tudor-SN various mutant lines (Chou et al. 2017), we examined the features of PB-I and PB-II by immunoelectron microscopy. In wildtype endosperm, 10 kDa prolamine (15 nm gold particles) occupied the central core of PB-I (Fig. 3A and B) while 13 kDa prolamines labeled with 5nm gold particles were more concentrated to the peripheral region (Fig. 3A and C). In 43M and 47M, the distribution of the 10 kDa prolamines was disordered and were more randomly distributed throughout the intracisternal inclusion body (Fig. 3E-K). Protein deposition to PB-II was also disrupted. Whereas glutelins (5 nm gold particles) were deposited to compact, oblong PB-II structures (Fig. 3D), protein body morphology in 43M and 47M was highly irregularly-shaped and the protein deposits resided in highly vacuolated compartments (Fig. 3H and L).

#### **The 4SN module is crucial for PB development and storage protein accumulation**

The absence of the Tsn or missense mutations in this module had a significant impact on protein body formation and development. To determine whether the 4SN module also has a similar effect, we performed immunocytochemical analysis of EM1084. In wildtype, the spherical PB-I (Fig. 4A) and the oblong shaped PB-II were readily distinguishable (Fig. 4B), while a new type of protein body was readily evident in EM1084 (Fig. 4C). Unlike PB-I in the wildtype, this new type of PB lacked the spherical shape and lamellar structure of varying electron densities (Fig. 4C). The attachment of ribosomes on the surface of the ER-derived subdomain suggests that the new type of PB was derived from the same rough ER that gave rise to PB-I.

Immunocytochemical studies readily reveal that this new protein body consists of a spherical inclusion (circled in yellow) composed of prolamines (15 nm gold particles) embedded in a matrix of glutelin proteins (5 nm gold particles) (Fig. 4C). The presence of glutelins in the ER accounts for the abnormal accumulation of proglutelin evident on immunoblotting (Fig. 2A). The prolamine inclusion is uniform in electron density suggesting that the normal distribution of the various prolamine classes is disrupted in EM1084. These results suggest that protein body formation and glutelin export from the ER is altered by the mutation in the SN3 domain of *Os*Tudor-SN.

#### **Identification of the native promoter region of** *Os***Tudor-SN**

In order to gain further insight on the roles of *Os*Tudor-SN during rice growth and development, we analyzed the *Os*Tudor-SN promoter using a sequence-based promoter region analysis software (TSSP / Prediction of PLANT Promoters and Promoter 2.0 Prediction Server) (Knudsen 1999; Shahmuradov et al. 2003). The *Os*Tudor-SN promoter is relatively long (~ 2.1 kb in length) as it contains an intron of 1.1 kb located at the distal end of the 5'UTR (Fig. 5A). The promoter region contains several stress-response *cis*-elements located between -969 to -2160 from the transcriptional start site. The MBS motif is involved in drought-stress inducibility while the ABRE motif is required for abscisic acid responsiveness (Supplemental Table 1). The GC and ARE motifs are both capable of anoxic-specific inducibility while the GARE and TCA motifs are involved in gibberellin and salicylic acid responses, respectively (Supplemental Table 1). Seven light responsive regulating elements were identified including the LAMP, SP1, G-box, MNF-1, TCT and AE-BOX motifs as well as a CIRCADIAN motif involved in light response circadian control (Supplemental Table 1). The identified promoter region also contains the endosperm expression GCN4 motif (Fig. 5A) and several transcription enhancer CAAT motifs (Supplemental Table 1).

Plant gene expression studies have demonstrated that the intron sequences can enhance the level of protein expression (Chung et al. 2006; Rose 2004; Rose and Beliakoff 2000). To test for promoter activity, the intact 2.1 kb promoter sequence (pHL800) and two promoter variant forms (pHL801 and pHL802) were cloned upstream of a GUS reporter gene and transformed into rice (Fig. 5A). pHL801 contains the 1.1 kb intron along with the GCN4 endosperm motif while pHL802 lacks the 1.1 kb intron. GUS reporter gene expression in all three transgenic lines

was very low as viewed by histochemical staining in shoots or roots (Fig. 4B). It was prominently visualized in developing grains in pHL800 expressing plants but not from transgenic lines containing pHL801 and pHL802 (Fig. 5B). These results indicate that pHL800 is expressed predominantly in developing endosperm. The higher *Os*Tudor-SN promoter activity in developing grains compared to leaves is consistent with published RNAseq data (LOC\_Os02g32350, MSU Rice Genome Annotation Project).

Because of the low GUS reporter activity based on histochemical assays, analysis of promoter-driven GUS expression in the various transgenic rice lines was monitored by quantifying *gusA* RNA levels in mid-developing grains (14 days after flowering) from T2 homozygous plants via absolute quantification qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 5C). The intact *Os*Tudor-SN promoter (pHL800) showed a 2.7- to 3.1-fold higher expression than that of the variant promoter forms in pHL801 and pHL802 (Fig. 5C). Hence, optimal promoter activity requires the upstream cis-element region, GCN4 region and downstream intron. The transcriptional efficiency of the transgene *Os*Tudor-SN promoter in the transgenic line was nearly identical to that of the endogenous gene in wildtype plant. The number of *Os*Tudor-SN RNA transcripts from the transgenic line was 1,155,000 copies/µg RNA while those from the endogenous wildtype gene were 1,032,000 copies/µg total RNA (Fig. 5D). These numbers compared well to the number of GUS transcripts (~1,012,000 copies/µg total RNA) driven by the cloned intact *Os*Tudor-SN promoter (pHL800) (Fig. 5C).

# **Partial restoration of storage protein composition, seed morphology, protein body formation in mutant transgenic complemented with wildtype** *Os***Tudor-SN gene**

To obtain direct evidence for a role of *Os*Tudor-SN in storage protein gene regulation and seed development, we complemented the mutation in EM1084, which exhibits the most severe effects on these traits, with the wildtype GFP-*Os*Tudor-SN gene (Fig. 6A). Mature grains from wildtype and T2 homozygous plants of transgenic wildtype (pHL8042/WT#1, #14 and #15) and transgenic EM1084 (pHL8042/EM1084#1 and #2) were assessed for expression of the GFP-*Os*Tudor-SN transgene (Fig. 6B and C). The GFP-fused *Os*Tudor-SN was readily evident as a 147 kDa polypeptide in the transgenic plants but absent in wildtype or EM1084. Interestingly, levels of the endogenous mutant *Os*Tudor-SN were elevated in the pHL8042/EM1084 transgenic line but not in the pHL8042/wildtype. The three pHL8042/WT lines and two pHL8042/EM1084

lines showed consistently elevated storage protein content compared to the parental wildtype and EM1084, respectively (Fig. 6C). In contrast, levels of the ER chaperones BiP and PDI were relatively unaffected in pHL8042/WT and pHL8042/EM1084 transgenic lines compared to the wildtype (Fig. 6C).

In order to validate the role of *Os*Tudor-SN in seed development, the grain morphology of mature grains from wildtype and mutant transgenic plants was assessed (Fig. 6D). Compared to the shrunken and floury phenotypes displayed by EM1084 grains, those from pHL8042/EM1084-expressing lines were plumb and larger in size (Fig. 6C). By contrast, grain morphology was similar between wildtype and pHL8042/WT lines, although grain weight was elevated 3.9 % (*p* = 0.052) in the transgenic line compared to wildtype. Although the grains of pHL8042/EM1084 lines still retained their floury appearance, grain weight was increased 20% over EM1084 (*p* = 0.0007) (Fig. 6D). The increase in grain weight mediated by complementation of EM1084 mutation suggests a broad role of *Os*Tudor-SN in seed development.

Electron microscopy studies of pHL8042/EM1084 showed that the abnormal protein bodies were no longer present and spherical PB-I type were readily evident (Fig. 4D). Although retaining their spherical shape, the structure of PB-I in pHL8042/EM1084 differed from wildtype. Although some PB-I structures showed a slight increase in electron density in the core region others were electron lucent (Fig. 4C).

## **DISCUSSION**

Tudor-SN is an evolutionarily conserved protein and plays diverse roles in gene expression through its involvement in several steps of RNA metabolism. It was initially identified as a transcriptional co-activator (Goenka et al. 2007; Leverson et al. 1998; Paukku et al. 2003; Tong et al. 1995; Wang et al. 2010) and later as a major component of RISC (Caudy et al. 2003; Tsuchiya et al. 2007), as a major cleavage activity of hyper-edited RNAs (Scadden 2005), as a splicing co-factor (Yang et al. 2007), and as a repressor of mRNA degradation via enhancing translation stability (Paukku et al. 2008). These multi-functional properties of Tudor-SN can be attributed to its unique structure consisting of multiple domains in the 4SN and Tsn modules (Gutierrez-Beltran et al. 2016). As a key component in RNA splicing, processing and editing, the 4SN module has RNA binding and RNA cleavage activities (Caudy et al. 2003; Li et al. 2008; Scadden 2005). The C-terminal Tsn module has also been suggested to participate in RNA splicing as it interacts with the core U1, U2, U4, U5 and U6 small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs) (Yang et al. 2007). The combined Tudor and SN3-SN4 domains bind proteins containing symmetrically dimethylated arginines (Liu et al. 2010a).

The multifunctional regulatory function of Tudor-SN is also readily evident in higher plants (Abe et al. 2003; Sami-Subbu et al. 2001). In Arabidopsis, Tudor-SN promotes cell viability and abiotic stress tolerance via regulating the expression level of stress associated genes (dit Frey et al. 2010; Liu et al. 2010b). This function may be contributed by the requirement of Tudor-SN for efficient storage and processing of RNAs under stress (Gutierrez-Beltran et al. 2015b).

In rice, Tudor-SN was suggested to play a prominent role in mRNA stability and mRNA localization in developing rice endosperm (Chou et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2008). The combined 4SN module's RNA binding and Tsn module protein interacting properties (Chou et al. 2017) are likely responsible for these functions in rice. Moreover, *Os*Tudor-SN was also suggested to have a role in responses to stress as suggested by its interaction with stress-responsive genes (Chou et al. 2017).

In the present study, the multi-functional properties of *Os*Tudor-SN were illuminated by evaluating the effect of mutations in *Os*Tudor-SN on seed phenotype, storage protein accumulation and protein body formation. Seed phenotype varied considerably among the four mutant *Os*Tudor-SN lines. EM1084 produced partially shrunken seeds having a floury appearance and significantly smaller grain size 40% of wildtype while those from 43M and 47M were slightly smaller than wildtype (Fig. 2C and 2D). The severity in the reduction in grain size among the mutant lines may correlate with the location of the mutation in the 4SN or Tsn modules. The absence of the Tsn module in 43M line indicates that this module is required for optimal grain filling but is not essential. Although the 4SN module is important in affecting grain weight, the mutation type likely influences the severity of the phenotype change. The mutation in EM1084 consists of a Glu to Lys at position 530 in the SN3 domain, which together with SN4 binds RNAs (Li et al. 2008). This amino acid substitution mediates a major change in surface charge in the SN3 domain, which may disrupt the interaction with RNA and stress-related proteins associated with plant growth (Chou et al. 2017).

Unlike the other mutant lines, PMT64 produced larger seeds than wildtype although they were chalky. The amino acid replacement in PMT64 is a His to Gln substitution at position 753. Interestingly, rice is the only plant examined that has a His at this position: the 10 Tudor-SN orthologs examined from other both dicot and monocot plant species share a conserved Gln (Chou et al. 2017). Hence, the Tudor-SN protein in PMT64 may function normally in many processes or may function better than the normal gene in elevating seed weight.

The various rice lines containing mutations in the *Os*Tudor-SN gene showed decreased storage protein expression especially of prolamine proteins compared to wildtype (Fig. 2A), substantiating its crucial role in modulating storage protein expression. Surprisingly, mutations in the SN domain conferred changes in protein patterns distinct from that seen for mutations in the Tudor domain or lacking this domain. The *Os*Tudor-SN mutant variants (47M and PTM64) containing missense mutations in the Tudor domain and 43M which expresses a truncated 4SN protein devoid of the Tsn module showed substantially decreased amounts of proglutelin and acidic glutelin expression (Fig. 2A). Although the amounts of basic glutelin subunits are also lower in these mutant lines, it is less evident due to the higher reactivity of the glutelin antibody to this protein compared to the acidic subunit. In contrast, EM1084, which expresses an *Os*Tudor-SN containing a missense mutation in the SN3 domain showed elevated accumulation of the glutelin precursor with the corresponding decreases in processed glutelin acidic and basic subunits (Fig. 2A).

As proglutelins are transported to the protein storage vacuole (PSV) via the Golgi apparatuses where they are proteolyzed into the acid and basic subunits (Kumamaru et al. 2010), the elevated amounts of proglutelin indicate a defect in protein transport to PB-II or in proteolytic processing. Immunocytochemical analysis of protein body formation in EM1084 indicates that normal PB-Is are not found but instead aberrant protein bodies containing prolamine inclusion granule embedded within a matrix of glutelin protein. The location of glutelins in inclusions surrounding prolamine in EM1084 indicates that a considerable amount of proglutelin fails to be exported from the ER to the Golgi and accounts for the elevated amounts of proglutelin and, in turn, reduced amounts of acidic and basic glutelin subunits (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, like EM1084, glutelin mRNAs are mis-localized to the PB-ER in the mutant rice lines PMT64, 43M and 47M (Chou et al. 2017), but do not exhibit the accumulation of proglutelin or aberrant glutelin-prolamine containing protein bodies. The possibility arises that, in addition to storage protein mRNA transport and localization, *Os*Tudor-SN also has a role in transporting protein cargo such as glutelin from the ER to Golgi. Mutation in the SN3 domain disrupting this role in membrane trafficking, whereas mutations in the Tudor domain or lacking the Tsn module having no effect on this function.

The molecular basis for the biogenesis of the aberrant glutelin-prolamine protein bodies in EM1084 is likely due to a combination of RNA mis-localization and protein folding. Glutelin mRNAs are mis-localized to the PB-ER where the protein is translocated to the lumen. At this PB-ER lumenal location, proglutelin polypeptides are unable to fold properly and attain competence for ER export as these processes requires protein disulfide isomerase PDIL1-1, a molecular chaperone whose location is restricted to the lumen of the cisternal-ER (Satoh-Cruz et al. 2010). The accumulation of unfolded proglutelin polypeptides assembles to form an inclusion separate from prolamine granule within the PB-ER.

We had previously demonstrated that mRNA localization facilitated protein localization within the endosome system of developing rice endosperm (Washida et al. 2009). For example, mis-localization of maize δ-zein mRNA from its normal PB-ER site to the cisternal-ER by the addition of glutelin zip code sequences results in the mis-localization of zein polypeptides from prolamine containing PB-I to glutelin containing storage vacuoles (PB II). However, our analysis

14

of the *Os*Tudor-SN mutants, with the exception of EM1084, showed that even though these storage protein mRNAs were mis-targeted from their normal ER subdomains, prolamine and glutelin polypeptides accumulated in their respective protein body types as in the wildtype *i.e.* prolamine polypeptides were deposited in PB-I while glutelins were deposited in PSVs (Fig. 3 and 4). These observations indicate that mRNA localization alone is not sufficient for the assembly of prolamine proteins within the ER and is controlled by other factors, which remain to be identified.

While prolamines and glutelins are targeted to their normal storage locales in the endosome system in 43M and 47M, there are noticeable alterations in their deposition within these sites. Rice prolamines are normally deposited in an orderly fashion. The 10 kDa prolamines, the first class to be synthesized during endosperm development, are concentrated at the central core of prolamine PB surrounded by successive layers of 13b, 13a, 16 kDa and 13b prolamines (Nagamine et al. 2011; Saito et al. 2012). Instead of the normal orderly packaging of 10 kDa and 13 kDa prolamines, these polypeptides are randomly distributed within PB-I in the 43M and 47M mutant lines (Fig. 3E-G and 3I-K). This abnormal protein distribution is likely due to spatial differences in translation and protein deposition. Unlike wildtype where prolamine mRNAs are translated at the site where its coded protein accumulates, prolamine mRNA translation on the cisternal-ER is displaced from the accumulation of prolamine intracisternal inclusion granule within the PB-ER.

Likewise, the normal packaging of glutelin polypeptides within PB-II and the shape of this storage organelle was also altered in the 43M and 47M rice lines (Fig. 3H and 3L). In wildtype, PB-II are compact protein deposits where glutelins form crystalline deposits surrounded by a matrix of α-globulins. The storage proteins accumulate as separate deposits within PB-II as they are synthesized at different ER subdomains. Similar to prolamines, α-globulins are synthesized at the PB-ER where they are rapidly exported to the Golgi and subsequently transported to PB-II. Although not studied here, the abnormal PB-II morphology in the 43M and 47M rice lines is likely due to disruption of protein packaging within this protein storage vacuole. The mislocalization of α-globulin mRNAs from the PB-ER to cisternal-ER where glutelins are also synthesized (in addition to the PB-ER) would enable these two storage proteins to co-transported to PB-II and assembled as a more homogeneous protein mixture in the 43M and 47M rice lines.

Complementation of EM1084 by the introduction of a wildtype *Os*Tudor-SN gene partially reversed grain weight and storage protein biosynthesis. Grain weight increased by 20% and proglutelins were more efficiently processed to acidic and basic subunits (Fig. 6). The restoration of grain weight and storage protein biosynthesis was only partially successful likely because the overall expression of the introduced wildtype *Os*Tudor-SN gene was much lower than the endogenous EM1084 mutant gene (Fig. 6). Moreover, the elevated levels of the mutant *Os*Tudor-SN in the transgenic line may also impede the activity of the wildtype protein. Despite the lower expression, it was sufficient to restore near normal storage protein mRNA localization patterns (Chou et al. 2017). While normal localization of glutelin mRNAs to the cisternal-ER was restored in pHL8042/EM1084 line, prolamine mRNAs were only partially restored.

This capacity to complement the EM1084 mutation despite the lower expression of the wildtype *Os*Tudor-SN gene is also evident for other genes especially those involved in developmental processes and stress responses (Table 1 and Supplemental Table 4). While expression of the rice nitric oxide catalase (*Os*CATC, LOC\_Os03g03910) is 24-fold lower in EM1084, its expression level was completely restored in pHL8042/EM1084 (Supplemental Table 4). As *Os*CATC is required for regulating the nitric oxide-mediated leaf cell death pathway, the defects in growth and yellowish leaves in EM1084 may be attributed to the downregulation of this gene. Other substantially downregulated gene sets are the dehydrins (LOC\_Os11g26780, LOC\_Os11g26790, LOC\_Os11g26570), which are substantially suppressed 2.7 to 58.9-fold in EM1084 mutants and restored in pHL8042/EM1084. As dehydrins are essential for cell development and abiotic stress response (Graether and Boddington 2014), the lower expression of this gene may delay normal development of flowers and seeds as observed in EM1084. Lastly, the expression of twelve storage protein genes was depressed in EM1084 and then restored in pHL8042/EM1084 (Supplemental Table 4). These changes at the gene level is consistent with the changes in storage protein accumulation (Fig.6B and C) and further substantiates the role of *Os*Tudor-SN in storage protein mRNA localization and posttranslational regulation (Chou et al. 2017).

Consistent with its role in transcription and stress as mentioned above, six stress responsive-associated transcription factors were regulated by *Os*Tudor-SN in our transcriptome analysis (Table 1 and Supplemental Table 4). The MYB family transcription factors (LOC\_Os01g74590, LOC\_Os05g37060, LOC\_Os01g64360) are associated with plant responses to phosphate starvation, UV-B irradiation, salt and drought stress (Wang et al. 2017). While three zinc finger transcription factors (LOC\_Os07g43740, LOC\_Os09g12720, LOC\_Os04g58320) are associated with drought, desiccation, salt, heat and chilling responses (Ham et al. 2013; Shankar et al. 2016).

Interestingly, genes were found to be significantly up-regulated or down-regulated by complementation of EM1084 by wildtype *Os*Tudor-SN. One possible explanation for this apparent anomalous behavior in gene expression was that increases in the mutant *Os*Tudor-SN protein in pHL8042/EM1084 rice lines (Fig. 6B). The elevated levels of the mutant variant would compete for binding of RNAs or interaction with other proteins, events that would affect expression of downstream genes.

Overall, our finding suggested that *Os*Tudor-SN is an essential factor during grain development. Such a role likely occurs to interact with other transcription factors, protein partners or RNAs for the regulation of gene expression.

#### **Materials and Methods:**

#### **Plant material**

The rice (*O.* sativa L.) Tudor-SN mutant lines, EM1084, 43M, PMT64 and 47M were generated by N-methyl-N-nitrosourea (MNU) mutagenesis (Satoh et al. 2010) of the japonica cultivar Taichung65 (TC65) as described in more detail in Chou et al. (2017).

#### **Bioinformatics analysis of** *Os***Tudor-SN promoter and vector cloning**

The 2.16 kb promoter region (-2160 to -1) was identified upstream of the ATG start codon of *Os*Tudor-SN (LOC\_Os02g32350) using TSS /Prediction of PLANT Promoters (RegSite Plant DB, Softberry Inc.) and Promoter 2.0 [\(http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/Promoter/\)](http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/Promoter/). The 1.1 kb region containing the upstream cis-acting elements and GCN4 motif (-2160 to -1107) was amplified (primers details see Supplemental Table 2) and cloned into the T-vector pHL380 (Chou et al. 2017) to give pHL389. The GCN4 motif and the remaining 1.1 kb region that contains the 5'UTR and the first intron (-1296 to 0) were amplified and cloned into pHL380 to give pHL394. Cloned promoter fragments in pHL389 and pHL394 were purified and ligated into pCAMBIA1301 respectively via the replacement of 35S promoter at the HindIII and NcoI to give pHL802 and pHL801, respectively.

 The intact promoter used to drive GUS in pCAMBIA1301 was constructed as follows. DNA fragments containing the cis-acting elements and GCN4 motif (-2160 to -1270) and the 5'UTR containing the first intron (-1270 to -1) were prepared and purified from pHL389 (HindIII/ApaI) and pHL394 (ApaI/NcoI), respectively. These fragments were then ligated together at the HindIII and NcoI sites of pCAMBIA1301 replacing the 35S promoter to give pHL800 to drive GUS reporter gene.

#### **Plant transformation and growth condition**

Plasmids were mobilized into *Agrobacterium tumefaciens AGL1* using electroporation. Embryogenic calli of TC65 were used for *Agrobacterium*-mediated transformation and regeneration (Hiei et al. 1994; Rashid et al. 1996; Toki 1997). Regenerated transgenic rice plants were first cultured in a growth chamber under a day/night temperature regime of 28/24<sup>o</sup>C and a light period of 14 h at 400 μmol/m<sup>2</sup> /s. For molecular and biochemical studies, all transgenic and wildtype rice plants were grown in a growth chamber with 12 to 14 daylength with day temperatures ranging from 25 to 32<sup>o</sup>C and night temperatures from 20 to 25<sup>o</sup>C.

#### **Seed protein isolation and immunoblotting analysis**

Proteins were extracted from mature seeds using urea extraction buffer containing 0.125 M Tris-HCl, 4% SDS, 4 M urea and 5% b-mercaptoethanol, pH6.8 (Ushijima et al. 2011). The proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE, electrophoretic transferred to nitrocellulose membranes and then incubated with 3% fat-free milk for 30 min at room temperature. Membranes were incubated overnight with primary rabbit antibody and then with goat anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Pierce Biotechnology, www.piercenet.com) for 1 hour at room temperature. The blot was washed three times with TBST and antibody-antigen reaction detected using the SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate (Pierce Biotechnology) and a Fujifilm LAS-3000 image analyzer ([www.fujifilm.com](http://www.fujifilm.com)).

#### **RNA extraction and Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)**

Total RNA was extracted from fourteen-days old developing seeds using a modified phenol/chloroform protocol (Chou et al. 2011). Rice seeds were ground to a powder under liquid N2 and then suspended in TENS buffer containing 80 mM Tris, 16 mM EDTA, 160 mM NaCl,

4% SDS and 16 mM DTT. 1 µg of total RNA was subjected to cDNA synthesis using iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.). Real time PCR was performed with a CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) using iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) under the following conditions: 50<sup>o</sup>C for 2 min followed by 95<sup>o</sup>C for 2 min, and then subjected to 40 cycles consisting of 15 sec at 95<sup>o</sup>C followed by 30 sec at 60<sup>o</sup>C for annealing and extension. Melting curve was monitored by ramping the temperature from 55<sup>o</sup>C to 99<sup>o</sup>C with fluorescence measured at every 1<sup>o</sup>C interval. A range of six different dilutions of plasmids from vectors of pHL399 (GUS) and pHL300 (Tudor) was used to generate standard curves for absolute quantification of transcript copy numbers. The amplification of rice actin gene (LOC\_Os03g50885) was performed as internal control to normalize all data. The mean values for the expression levels of the target genes were calculated from three independent experiments and expressed as mean ± standard error. Primer information is provided in Supplemental Table 3.

### **Immunoelectron Microscopy**

### *Transmission immunoelectron microscopic analysis*

For immunoelectron microscopic analysis, seed samples were chemically fixed as described previously (Takemoto et al. 2002). Ultrathin sections (90–100 nm) were obtained with a diamond knife and placed on 200 mesh grids. The grids were treated with blocking buffer (TBS, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.2, 500 mM NaCl and 0.3% Tween) containing 1% BSA for 60 min. The grids were then incubated overnight with the appropriate antibodies diluted with blocking buffer. Unreacted antibodies were removed by washing the sections four times in a drop of blocking buffer. The grids were then incubated for 60 min with secondary antibodies, protein A-gold conjugate 5 nm or 15 nm (BBI Solutions Cardiff, UK), diluted with blocking buffer as described above, then sequentially stained with 0.25% KMnO4 and 1% uranylacetate. The sections were then observed by JEM-1220 transmission electron microscope at 80 kV.

## **RNA-seq and data processing**

Total RNA samples of wild type, EM1084 and pHL8042/EM1084 were extracted from fourteen-days old rice developing seeds as described previously (Chou et al. 2017). RNA-seq libraries were constructed using a ScriptSeqTM v2 RNA-Seq Library Preparation Kit (Illumina) following the manufacturer's instructions and sequenced on Illumina Hiseq instrument. RNAseq reads were trimmed and mapped to the rice genome database

(http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/index.shtml) using the CLC genomics workbench (Qiagen, US). Only unique reads were mapped to the rice genome and normalized as RPKM. Raw RPKM data of all genes are listed in Supplemental Table 6. Genes with fold-change greater than 2 and p-value lower than 0.01 were defined as differentially expressed genes (Supplemental Tables 4 and 5). GOslim annotation was retrieved from http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/index.shtml and categorized based on Arabidopsis GO slim terms (https://www.arabidopsis.org) with slight modifications. Some undefined genes were manually categorized based on putative function.
