# **Improved structural annotation of protein-coding genes in the** *Meloidogyne hapla* **genome using RNA-Seq**

Yuelong Guo1 , David McK Bird1,2, and Dahlia M Nielsen1,\*

Bioinformatics Research Center; NC State University; Raleigh NC USA; 2 Department of Plant Pathology; NC State University; Raleigh NC USA

**Keywords:** Meloidogyne hapla, annotation, NGS, RNA sequencing, trans-splicing

As high-throughput cDNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) is increasingly applied to hypothesis-driven biological studies, the prediction of protein coding genes based on these data are usurping strictly in silico approaches. Compared with computationally derived gene predictions, structural annotation is more accurate when based on biological evidence, particularly RNA-Seq data. Here, we refine the current genome annotation for the *Meloidogyne hapla* genome utilizing RNA-Seq data. Published structural annotation defines 14420 protein-coding genes in the *M. hapla* genome. Of these, 25% (3751) were found to exhibit some incongruence with RNA-Seq data. Manual annotation enabled these discrepancies to be resolved. Our analysis revealed 544 new gene models that were missing from the prior annotation. Additionally, 1457 transcribed regions were newly identified on the ends of as-yet-unjoined contigs. We also searched for trans-spliced leaders, and based on RNA-Seq data, identified genes that appear to be trans-spliced. Four 22-bp trans-spliced leaders were identified using our pipeline, including the known trans-spliced leader, which is the *M. hapla* ortholog of SL1. In silico predictions of trans-splicing were validated by comparison with earlier results derived from an independent cDNA library constructed to capture trans-spliced transcripts. The new annotation, which we term HapPep5, is publically available at www.hapla.org.

### **Background**

The first metazoan genome sequenced was that of the freeliving nematode worm *Caenorhabditis elegans*. Arguably, this is the only animal genome for which every base is known with high certainty. Because of their importance as parasites of humans, animals, and plants, draft sequences of a growing number of nematode genomes have been obtained.e.g.2-5 Included in this list are those of two root-knot nematodes (RKN), *Meloidogyne hapla*<sup>2</sup> and *Meloidogyne incognita*. <sup>3</sup> *Meloidogyne* is a large genus of plant parasites that are distributed worldwide and cause substantial loss to agricultural production. Despite being members of the same genus, *M. incognita* and *M. hapla* exhibit substantial differences in their parasitic ability including host range and resistance breaking. Comparison of these diverse species is predicated on robust annotation of the reference genome.

The *M. hapla* genome is diploid, consistent with sexual reproduction. The sequence spans 56Mb on 16 chromosomes,6,7 and accounts for > 98% of the large number of available ESTs.2 Empowering the sequence has been the development of a robust linkage map, 7 which permits the mapping of Mendelian and quantitative traits, and because the map and sequence are tightly anchored, the isolation of candidate genes. Collectively, these resources establish *M. hapla* as a model organism to study the mechanisms of plant parasitism. Further, *M. hapla* is an emerging model for studying cross-kingdom horizontal gene transfer.8,9

By contrast, *M. incognita* has both a complex karyotype that exhibits variable aneuploidy, and a complex genome organization that complicates assembly. First published as spanning 86 Mb,3 it now appears that the final assembly may approach 140 Mb.10 It is widely assumed that the complexity of the *M. incognita* genome is related to the obligate asexual lifestyle of this species. However, a study of polymorphisms in *Meloidogyne* mtDNA concluded that interspecific crosses occasionally occurred although at a frequency too low to repeat in a laboratory setting.11,12 Using more comprehensive methodology, a compelling case for *M. incognita* being derived from an interspecific cross has been made;10 modern *M. incognita* appears to be an allo-hexaploid, currently undergoing chromosomal decay.10,13

The current annotation of the *M. hapla* genome independently used the algorithms FgenesH and Glimmer, trained with EST data, to predict gene models in the genome assembly. Concordance of prediction was deemed evidence of a reliable gene model. To improve the reliability of gene predictions, the TimeLogic GeneDetective algorithm (Active Motif Inc.) was employed to yield the HapPep3 release. The algorithm

Redressing those deficiencies is the focus of this work.

As RNA-Seq is increasingly applied to biological studies, prediction of protein coding genes utilizing biological evidence, ideally full-length expressed mRNA sequences, has been made possible. Several studies improving genome annotation using RNA-Seq data have been published, e.g., references 15–18. Beyond simply predicting primary gene structure, annotation using RNA-Seq data has the potential to find non-coding transcripts, as well as alternative splicing and editing events.19,20 Important for nematode genomes, we also deduced trans-splicing events based on RNA-Seq data.

In many nematode species (perhaps all), trans-splicing is a mechanism in which a short (typically 22 nucleotide) leader is spliced to a primary transcript, thus creating the 5′-most exon of that gene. In *C. elegans*, this sequence, termed a "spliced leader" (SL), is transcribed as a capped transcript by RNA Polymerase III from a locus that is part of the 5S cluster. The 5′ end of this sequence, destined to be the SL, is 22 residues, ending with a splice donor. This donor transcript resembles a half exon. Transcripts to be trans-spliced have a splice acceptor sequence without a more 5′ splice donor motif. The standard cellular splicing machinery is able to splice the donor to the target mRNA acceptor, i.e., in trans.21

The role of trans-splicing is not fully explored. However, the discovery that many nematode genes reside in closely spaced gene clusters similar to bacterial operons points to one need. Such genes are transcribed together as a single poly-cistronic message, which must be disrupted to permit translational initiation, usually from the most 5′ ATG (depending on the Kozak motif). In *C. elegans* this conversion from a polycistronic transcript to monocistronic mRNA able to be translated is realized by "internal" trans-splicing with SL2.22 Other SL variants have been discovered23,24 and it has been estimated that about 70% of *C. elegans* mRNAs are trans-spliced to one of two 22 nucleotide spliced leaders.25 A spliced leader similar to SL1 in *C. elegans* has been identified in genus *Meloidogyne*, 3,26 and it is estimated at least 10% of *M. hapla* mRNAs are trans-spliced.27-30 However, to date, other SL, including those similar to SL2, had yet to be identified in *Meloidogyne*.

The main aim of this study was to improve the annotation of protein-coding genes in the *M. hapla* genome by incorporating Illumina RNA-Seq reads. To do this, RNA-Seq reads were assembled into transcripts, from which open reading frames were predicted. Annotation discrepancies between this approach and the current HapPep3 release were further arbitrated based on BLAST analysis, with HapPep3 models deemed to be incorrect only if additional evidence from BLAST showed that the newly predicted genes are more similar to a known protein. In addition, we applied a method for extracting novel trans-spliced leader sequences from high-throughput RNA-Seq data. For each identified spliced leader, genes that showed evidence of being transspliced based on the RNA-Seq data were recorded.

#### **Results and Discussion**

#### **Annotation based on RNA-Seq evidence**

*Updates to version 3 annotation*

We obtained Illumina RNA-Seq reads from 98 independent samples of RKN-infected *Medicago truncatula* plants. RNA-Seq reads were submitted to and stored in the NCBI SRA database (BioProject accession: PRJNA229407). Approximately 1 billion reads from the first 32 samples were used in this study, which provides sufficient coverage for gene structural annotation. We were able to map 149 million (15.2%) of these reads unambiguously to the *M. hapla* genome. The majority of the other mapped reads aligned to the host plant genome. Based on a reference-guided assembly of the *M. hapla* RNA-Seq reads, 16 896 transcripts, including products of alternative splicing, were identified using Cufflinks.31 Open reading frames (ORF) beginning with ATG and ending with a stop codon were found in 16 678 (98.7%) of transcripts. The current annotation (HapPep3) of the *M. hapla* genome predicts a total of 14 420 protein-coding genes.14 As summarized in **Table 1**, comparison of the new annotation with the HapPep3 models fully confirmed 2556, and partially confirmed 1321 HapPep3 genes. Additionally, new annotation confirmed the expression of 188 single exon genes, although due to the presence of 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions in mRNA, the start and stop codon may not be precisely evaluated solely based on RNAseq data. Split or merge gene model comparison as defined by the different number of gene at one locus were observed in 5343 HapPep3 genes. Three thousand three hundred and twenty-eight genes were merged into 1728 gene models at corresponding loci, while other 1136 genes were split into 2422 genes in the new annotation. Although the merge/split models were supported by RNA-Sequencing evidence, they may still represent incorrect predictions. *RNA-Seq evidence recovers expressed regions near ends of contigs*

These transcribed regions, however, can be identified using RNA-Seq data. A total of 1457 expressed regions that lie on both ends of 3452 contigs were identified in the new annotation. #### **Detection of trans-spliced leaders**

We employed a two step approach in detecting trans-spliced leaders in *M. hapla*. First, plausible spliced leaders (pSL) were identified using a protocol to detect and filter RNA-Seq reads

with ends that map to two different genomic locations (**Fig. 2**; also see Methods). To verify these pSL, we extracted all RNA-Seq reads (from the entire set of reads) that contained these pSL sequences or their reverse complement sequences. In order to evaluate whether these pSL appeared to be trans-spliced to several genomic loci we used BLAST to align the reads that contained these pSL to the *M. hapla* genome. It was observed that for six out of 10 identified pSL, more than 90% of the reads containing these pSL appeared to show evidence of cissplicing instead of trans-splicing (the non-pSL portion of these reads mapped to genomic locations adjacent to that of the pSL). Therefore, these pSL were considered to be false positives. For the other four pSL (listed in **Table 2**), the non-pSL portion of their RNA-Seq reads mapped to distal genomic locations, as expected for trans-splicing. It was also observed that sequences concatenated to these pSL mapped to multiple genes (i.e., multiple genes were trans-spliced by the same pSL). These four pSL were therefore retained as trans-spliced leaders. Two of the identified SL differ by one base pair from SL1 in *C. elegans*, and one of these concurs with the known SL1 in *M. hapla*. The other two putative SL are novel SL1 variants. It was observed that transcripts trans-spliced by SL1 may also be trans-spliced by the identified SL1 variants, although trans-splicing by SL1 variants are less abundant. Moreover, instances have been found that SL1 trans-splice to genes on the same strand that are less than 500 bp apart, i.e., likely resolving polycistronic genes within an operon. Given that no SL2-like splice leaders were identified, *M. hapla* could be one of the species that uses SL1 for all trans-splicing, including trans-splicing to downstream operonic genes, as discussed in Guiliano, et al.34

Genes that were determined, based on the RNA-Seq evidence, to encode transcripts that were subsequently trans-spliced were compared with sequences from an earlier SL-cDNA library that was derived independently.28-30 This cDNA library was constructed by PCR amplification of total RNA using a primer based on the known SL and an oligo(dT) primer, so that only mRNA sequences with the known spliced leader were amplified. Sequencing was then performed from both ends using the same primers. Upon aligning to the nematode genome, a total of 1193 genes were determined to be trans-spliced based on the cDNA library evidence. In contrast, 585 genes were identified as being trans-spliced based on the combined RNA-Seq data of 32 independent samples of RKN-infected plant root tissue. Although nearly half (266 out of 585) of the genes identified through the RNA-Seq data coincided with ones identified through the cDNA library, we had anticipated a higher degree of match. One possibility stems from a feature of our pipeline in which the identification of trans-splicing based on RNA-Seq data requires the presence of a full-length SL (~22 bp). While the fragmented cDNA short reads are around 78 bp, the probability of retaining a full-length SL is low during fragmentation before sequencing. Thus trans-spliced products, although present in the library, are likely not recognizable, especially for mRNAs with low steadystate levels. Because of this, lowly expressed genes are likely to be missed in the identification. In reexamining our data, we did observe that the number of RNA-Seq instances of each transspliced gene was low (with an average of 2.185 times coverage). Further, we performed the same analysis with 18 additional independent samples (50 out of total 98 available), to test whether more trans-spliced genes may be detected with more samples and coverage. With these data, a total of 1270 genes were identified as being trans-spliced and 494 out of 1270 (38.9%) genes were the same as the ones obtained from the SL cDNA library. From these results, we concluded that increasing RNA-Seq sample size results in increasing the number of genes identified as being trans-spliced, and that the list of genes identified as being transspliced is not complete. While it is likely this list also contain false-positive results, we expect that many newly identified transspliced genes are novel true positives, and thus, that RNA-Seq data can provide insight into trans-splicing events.

#### **Future directions**

RNA-Seq data has been proven to be valuable for the identification of gene structures, including alternative splicing. In regions with low expression however, often little or no information can be obtained. In this study, with approximately 149 million reads (about 200 fold coverage on average combining all samples), we failed to identify 3373 genes (out of 16 896, 19.96%) predicted in the HapPep3 annotation. Since all biological samples used in this study were in the same developmental stage, nematode genes that are not expressed 3 wk after inoculation will not be identified in our analysis. We expect that further improvements can be made in both *M. hapla* genome structural annotation and alternativesplicing identification by sequencing nematode transcriptomes sampled from different developmental stages.

Many nematode genes reside in closely spaced gene clusters similar to bacterial operons. Such genes are transcribed together as single poly-cistronic messages, which must be disrupted to permit translational initiation. One role of trans-splicing is that it creates mono-cistronic mRNAs that are translated separately. Although trans-spliced genes were successfully identified in this study, the low number of unclosed gaps in the existing *M. hapla* genome assembly creates difficulties for making further inferences about operons (i.e., total number of operons, average number of genes per operons) in *M. hapla*. As genes in the same operon are in close proximity, trans-spliced genes that are physically near to each other are likely processed from one operon. Upon closing the 3452 contigs of the current *M. hapla* genome assembly, it will be worth repeating the trans-spliced analysis and extending the methods to allow identification of operons.

#### **Conclusions**

Genome annotation is a process of continuous refinement. Although our updated structural annotation approach utilizing RNA-Seq data are not perfect, it has improved the HapPep3 annotation. In the new assembly, 3751 protein-coding genes were updated and 544 genes were newly predicted. Moreover, in the process of identifying trans-spliced leaders, four SLs, including one known previously, were successfully identified. Further experimental evidence will be needed to confirm the other three SL identified in this study and to acquire a complete list of transspliced genes.

### **Materials and Methods**

### **Experimental design**

*Medicago truncatula* plants were grown in sand in separate containers in a growth chamber for 16 h at 22 °C under 200– 300 µmol/s-2m-1 of light (day) and for 6 h at 18 °C in the dark (night); humidity 60%. Plants were watered daily and fertilized three times a week with half-strength Hoaglands solution. Plants were inoculated with 2 000 freshly hatched *M. hapla* J2 per plant. Three weeks after inoculation, roots were gently removed from pots, and sand removed by washing. Small portions of root (several mm) containing RKN-induced galls were resected by hand and immediately placed on dry ice. To reduce random variation (and facilitate identification of real signals), we pooled gall samples from three plants grown separately and infected with the same nematode sample. This pool was considered to be one replicate. Statistical power was obtained by performing 32 biological replicates. Total RNA was extracted using QIAGEN's RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) and examined on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent) for quality and concentration using a RNA Pico Chip. Libraries were prepared using Illumina's TruSeq RNA library prep kit v1 (Illumina). After quantification of the library, the fragments were annealed to a flow cell and clonally amplified using a cBot (Illumina). The amplified sequencing libraries were sequenced using Illumina's Genome Analyzer IIx sequencing platform as single reads for 68 cycles. Each sample was run on a separate lane. Single read runs of 76 nt were generated.

New evidence of gene structure was compared with HapPep3 annotations if inconsistent prediction occurred at the same genome locus.

In case split or merge gene structures were observed, i.e., HapPep3 annotation and new evidence differs in number of genes at a same locus, BLAST analysis similar to Li, et al. was used to determine whether the split or the merge gene model is more plausible.16 If two or more of the split gene model mapped to same top hits, then the merged gene model is preferred. While two or more of the split gene model mapped to different top hits, the split gene model is preferred. However, if only one of the split gene has BLAST hits, further comparison was made to identify the most plausible model. If the BLAST hit of merged model was longer than that of the split model, with extension to the other split genes, the merged model was determined more plausible. Otherwise, the HapPep3 gene model was remained unchanged.

In cases where the new evidence was incongruence with the HapPep3 annotation, but no split or merge gene structure was observed, the HapPep3 annotation was determined to be incorrect only if additional evidence from BLAST showed that the newly predicted genes displayed higher sequence similarity to a known protein. Genes assembled by RNA-Seq evidence and the ones annotated in HapPep3 were both BLASTed to the UniProt database of all nematodes. If BLAST returned no significant hits for either predicted protein, the current HapPep3 annotation was left unchanged. Newly identified genes not included in the HapPep3 annotation were also BLASTed against this protein database. Only predicted transcripts with significant hits were kept and recorded as protein-coding genes. Expressed regions near the ends of not-yet-closed contigs were recorded, but not definitively designated as being protein-coding genes.

#### **Detection of trans-splice leaders**

To detect trans-splicing events, a data filter followed by a twostep identification and verification procedure was applied to the RNA-Seq data. At the outset, reads that remained unmapped after performing the reference-guided transcript assembly were BLASTed against both the plant and nematode genome, and the subset of reads that mapped to the plant genome were discarded. All remaining reads (those that were not included in the reference-guided transcript assembly and that did not align to the plant genome) were used for the following trans-splicing event identification and verification steps.

In the identification step, the goal was to identify plausible spliced leaders (pSL). This was accomplished by searching for RNA-Seq reads for which one end of the read aligned (via BLAST) to one genomic location, while the other end of the read aligned to another, distal location (**Fig. 3**). Only reads for which both ends aligned (1) exclusively to nematode genome and (2) to two distinct genome locations were retained. To filter out reads that might have mapped by random chance, only reads that displayed 100 percent identity were considered. Several additional stringent filters were then applied to further reduce random mapping: (1) the match length of one of the ends of each read, hypothetically the spliced-leader sequence, had to be 22 ± 5 nt (**Fig. 3C–E**), as all of the currently known spliced-leaders in nematodes are around 22 nt; and (2) the overlap between the two halves that mapped to different locations had to be less than 5 nt (**Fig. 3E**).

The second step of the trans-spliced detection procedure was verification. Each pSL sequence identified in the first step was used as a probe against the entire set of RNA-Seq reads (every RNA-Seq read containing this pSL sequence was identified via BLAST). Each of these reads was then aligned via BLAST to the nematode genome, and the location of the non-pSL portion of these reads was recorded. These recorded locations were then used to determine if the non-pSL component of the reads (the sequences that were presumably derived from a transcribed gene) tended to be nearby the location of the pSL (i.e., *cis*) or if they tended to be distal (i.e., *trans*). If ≥ 90% of these reads aligned to a region adjacent to the location of the pSL, the pSL was considered to be a false positive and was discarded.
