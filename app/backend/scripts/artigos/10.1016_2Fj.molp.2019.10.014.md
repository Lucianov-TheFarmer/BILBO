### **ABSTRACT**

Suppression mechanisms employed by transcriptional repressors commonly exist in diverse phytohormone signaling pathways. In Arabidopsis thaliana, JASMONATE-ZIM DOMAIN (JAZ) proteins are transcriptional repressors that function as negative regulators of diverse JA responses. Novel Interactor of JAZ (NINJA) is an adaptor protein connecting JAZs with the co-repressor, TOPLESS (TPL), to mediate gene repression in JA-dependent root growth inhibition and defense pathways. However, whether NINJA or other adaptor proteins are employed in other JA responsive biological processes remains to be elucidated. In the present study, we demonstrate that a previously uncharacterized protein, ECAP (EAR motif-Containing Adaptor Protein), directly interacts with JAZ6/8 and enhances their transcriptional repression activities. We also provide evidence that ECAP is a novel adaptor protein for JAZ6/8 recruitment of the transcriptional co-repressor, TPR2, into a transcriptional repressor complex, to repress the WD-repeat/bHLH/MYB complex, an important transcriptional activator in the JA-dependent anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway. This novel insight, together with previous studies, reveals that specific adaptor proteins are critical for distinct JA responses by pairing different JAZs (which possess overlapping but also individual functions) with the general co-repressors, TPL and TPRs.

**Keywords:** Jasmonate signaling; transcriptional repressors; anthocyanin; adaptor proteins; ECAP; JAZ proteins; TPL/TPRs

### **INTRODUCTION**

Jasmonate (JA) is a vital phytohormone that triggers plant defense against pests and pathogens (Farmer and Ryan, 1992; Sarde et al., 2018) and also influences many plant development processes such as anthocyanin accumulation (Franceschi and Grimes, 1991), stamen development (McConn and Browse, 1996; Sanders et al., 2000), root growth and root hair development (Staswick et al., 1992; Zhu et al., 2006), and senescence (He et al., 2002). In the current model, JA-dependent removal of transcriptional repressors is required for the activation of diverse JA responses during plant development or defense processes (Kazan and Manners, 2012; Huang et al., 2017; Howe et al., 2018; Yan et al., 2018). At low JA levels, JASMONATE-ZIM DOMAIN (JAZ) proteins form a repressor complex with the general co-repressor, TOPLESS (TPL), either directly or mediated by an adaptor protein, to inhibit the function of JA-responsive transcriptional activators (Pauwels et al., 2010; Pauwels and Goossens, 2011; Shyu et al., 2012). Perception of JA by the F-box protein, CORONATINE INSENSITIVE 1 (COI1), and the other components of the JAZ co-receptor leads to ubiquitination and degradation of JAZ proteins through the 26S proteasome, releasing the inhibition of transcriptional activators in JA signaling pathway (Chini et al., 2007; Thines et al., 2007; Yan et al., 2009; Sheard et al., 2010).

To date, the most intensively studied transcriptional factors (TFs) in the core JA signaling pathway are the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) protein, MYC2, and its homologs including A. thaliana MYC3, MYC4, and MYC5, which are involved in diverse JA-mediated responses, such as root growth inhibition, defense, stamen development, and the biosynthesis of glucosinolate and anthocyanin, by regulating the expression of JA-responsive genes (Lorenzo et al., 2004; Dombrecht et al., 2007; Pozo et al., 2008; Fernandez-Calvo et al., 2011; Schweizer et al., 2013; Figueroa and Browse, 2015; Qi et al., 2015; Major et al., 2017). In the absence of JA, MYC TFs are repressed by JAZ proteins via a dual repression mechanism: direct inhibition of the binding of

MYCs to the co-activator MED25, which facilitates the recruitment of RNA polymerase II to the promoters of target genes (Zhang et al., 2015, 2017; An et al., 2017); and epigenetic modifications in the JA-responsive gene chromatin structure via TPL co-repressors (Pauwels et al., 2010). Previous studies have demonstrated that NINJA (Novel Interactor of JAZ), an EAR motif-containing protein, functions as an adaptor connecting JAZs with TPL. The NINJA-mediated JAZ-TPL repressor complex blocks the activity of MYC2, repressing the JA-dependent root growth inhibition and defense processes (Pozo et al., 2008; Pauwels et al., 2010; Kazan and Manners, 2012; Acosta et al., 2013; Ke et al., 2015). Interestingly, Acosta et al. (2013) found that NINJA is crucial for the repression of JA signaling in roots, but the significantly weaker shoot phenotypes of ninja null mutants suggest alternative mechanisms of repression in the aerial organs. This could be exerted via the exclusion of co-activators such as MED25, as is the case for JAZ-MYC. Alternatively, it could involve adaptor(s) other than NINJA or completely different mechanisms altogether (Acosta et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2015, 2017; Howe et al., 2018).

Anthocyanins are a group of small molecular secondary metabolites with antioxidant capacity (Wang et al., 1997). One of the strategies that plants have developed to cope with unfavorable environmental conditions is the synthesis of anthocyanins (Mierziak et al., 2014; Li et al., 2016; Li et al., 2017); several plant hormones including JA are involved in the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis (Shan et al., 2009; Qi et al., 2011; Gan et al., 2014; Boter et al., 2015). It is well known that a suite of TFs, including MYB, bHLH, and WD-repeat proteins, regulate anthocyanin biosynthesis in many plant species (Gonzalez et al., 2008). A WD-repeat/bHLH/MYB complex has been demonstrated to promote the expression of anthocyanin biosynthetic genes, including NADPH-dependent dihydroflavonol reductase (DFR), leucoanthocyanidin dioxygenase (LDOX, also known as ANTHOCYANIDIN SYNTHASE, ANS), and UDP-Glc flavonoid 3-Oglucosyltransferase (UF3GT) (Xu et al., 2014). As a general co-repressor, TPL has also been found to play a

role in the control of anthocyanin production (Zheng et al., 2019). Qi et al. (2011) reported that JAZ proteins interact with essential components of the WD-repeat/bHLH/MYB complex to repress JA-mediated anthocyanin accumulation in a COI1-dependent manner. Among these JAZ-interacting essential components, Glabra1 (GL1) and MYB75 are R2R3 MYB transcription factors; Transparent Testa8 (TT8), GL3, and Enhancer of GL3 (EGL3) are bHLH transcriptional factors (Qi et al., 2011). Given that anthocyanins are mainly synthesized in the aerial organs of the plant (Petrussa et al., 2013), whether there is an adaptor protein other than NINJA connecting JAZ proteins with TPL or TRP co-repressors to repress the activity of the WD-repeat/bHLH/MYB complex remains to be elucidated.

In the present study, we identified a previously uncharacterized protein, ECAP (EAR motif-Containing Adaptor Protein), and found that the EAR (ERF-associated amphiphilic repression) motif-containing ECAP acts as a COI1-dependent (at least partially) negative regulator of JA signaling by repressing the expression of many early JA-responsive genes (including some anthocyanin biosynthetic genes). We further demonstrated that ECAP directly interacts with JAZ6 and JAZ8, acting as a novel adaptor protein for the recruitment of TPR2 into a transcriptional repressor complex to repress JA-responsive anthocyanin accumulation.

**RESULTS** 

# **The ECAP protein is a negative regulator of JA-responsive anthocyanin**

## **accumulation in A. thaliana**

To further understand the mechanism underlying the transcriptional control of JA-responsive anthocyanin biosynthesis, we performed a genetic screen for mutants that accumulated more anthocyanins. Using a SALK transfer (T)-DNA insertion mutant library, we identified two mutants (named ecap-1 and ecap-2) that had greater anthocyanin content than the wild-type (Figure 1A and 1B). These two mutants harbored a T-DNA insertion in the exon of the At3G58110

gene on chromosome 3, which encodes a protein of unknown function (Figure 1C). Reverse transcription (RT) polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis demonstrated that both mutants were null alleles (Figure 1D). Furthermore, following 50 µM methyl jasmonate (MeJA) treatment, JA-induced anthocyanin accumulation in both mutants was markedly greater than that in wild-type seedlings (Figure 1A and 1B). To confirm that the anthocyanin phenotype could indeed be attributed to the loss of ECAP function, we generated two loss-of-function alleles, ecap-3 and ecap-4, using the CRISPR/cas9 technique (Wang et al., 2015) (Figure 1C). Mutant ecap-3 had a single nucleotide (T) insertion after the 296th bp in the ECAP sequence, which caused a frameshift resulting in an early termination (Figure 1C and 1E). A 16-nucleotide deletion after the 296th bp in the ECAP sequence was found in the ecap-4 mutant, and protein sequence alignment revealed a mismatch and early termination of ECAP protein translation (Figure 1C and 1E). An immunoblotting assay further confirmed that all four ecap alleles were knock-out mutants (Figure 1F and Supplemental Figure 1A). Similar to the ecap-1 and ecap-2 mutants, an increase in anthocyanin content and enhanced JA-responsive anthocyanin accumulation were detected in the ecap-3 and ecap-4 mutants, demonstrating a role for ECAP in the negative regulation of JA-mediated anthocyanin biosynthesis (Figure 1A and 1B).

To further investigate whether ECAP acts as a negative regulator of JA signaling, we crossed an ecap-2 mutant with the JA-production mutant, opr3 (Col) (Chehab et al., 2011), and the JA receptor mutant coi1-1, which has a defect in JA signal transduction (Xie et al., 1998). We measured the anthocyanin content and found that exogenous MeJA induced anthocyanin accumulation in opr3 (Col) and caused enhanced anthocyanin accumulation in the opr3 (Col) ecap-2 double mutant (Figure 1G and 1H). In coi1-1 mutant seedlings, anthocyanin accumulation was significantly decreased and insensitive to exogenous MeJA induction (Figure 1G and 1H, Shan et al., 2009; Qi et al., 2011). Notably, in the ecap-2 coi1-1 double mutant, the anthocyanin

content was dramatically increased as compared with the coi1-1 single mutant, regardless of MeJA treatment. Interestingly, in comparison with the untreated control, exogenous MeJA-induced anthocyanin accumulation in the coi1-1 ecap-2 double mutant was significantly less than that in the ecap single mutants (Figure 1G and 1H). These data suggest that ECAP not only acts downstream of COI1 in JA signaling, but also affects anthocyanin accumulation independently of COI1 and exogenous JA treatment. Indeed, when seedlings were challenged with sucrose, drought, or high salinity, anthocyanins were more greatly induced in the ecap-2 mutant than in the wild-type (Supplemental Figure 1B−G), suggesting that ECAP also mediates the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis via other signaling pathways. Moreover, the ecap-2 mutant showed partial infertility and enhanced resistance to Botrytis cinerea (Supplemental Figure 1H−L), suggesting a possible role of ECAP in JA-regulated fertility and defense. However, we did not detect any significant differences in root length or trichome number between ecap-2 and wild-type seedlings, irrespective of the presence of JA, indicating that ECAP is not involved in JA-induced root growth inhibition or trichome formation (Supplemental Figure 1M−O).

Taken together, we can conclude that ECAP is a negative regulator of anthocyanin biosynthesis and that JA-COI1 signaling induces anthocyanin accumulation, at least partially, via the release of inhibition by ECAP.

### **ECAP is an EAR motif-containing protein localized to the nucleus**

To better understand the function of ECAP in anthocyanin synthesis and its role in JA signaling, we first analyzed the predicted secondary structure of ECAP using the SMART tool (Letunic et al., 2015). As shown in Figure 2A, ECAP consists of 784 amino acids. There are two coiled-coil domains (CCD) located at the N-terminus (38−65 aa and 103−130 aa) and another two CCDs at the C-terminus (611−635 aa, 727−766 aa). An EAR motif (LELNLG) (Kagale and Rozwadowski, 2011) was identified at 374−379 aa of the ECAP protein

(Figure 2A). Previous reports have indicated that EAR motif-containing proteins, such as INDOLEACETIC ACID-INDUCED PROTEIN (IAA), NINJA, and ABI FIVE BINDING PROTEIN (AFP), play important roles in transcriptional repression processes (Tiwari et al., 2004; Pauwels et al., 2010; Lynch et al., 2017). The sequence alignment of ECAP, NINJA, IAAs, and AFPs exhibited a conserved EAR motif (LxLxLx) in these proteins (Supplemental Figure 2A), implying a possible role of ECAP in transcriptional repression in A. thaliana. Moreover, we used the BLASTP tool (McGinnis and Madden, 2004) to analyze ECAP-related proteins in the NCBI database and construct a phylogenetic tree. As shown in Supplemental Figure 2B, ECAP-related proteins were identified in bacteria, archaebacterial, plants, and animals, which are mostly uncharacterized proteins with no functional annotation. In A. thaliana, an ECAP-like protein (AT2G42370) showed 46% identity with ECAP; however, we did not detect any defects in anthocyanin accumulation in ecap-like null alleles as compared with the wild-type, irrespective of the presence of JA (Supplementary Figure 2B−F), indicating that ECAP-like protein is not involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis in A. thaliana.

Subsequently, we analyzed the organ/tissue expression pattern of ECAP. A total of six transgenic lines expressing β-glucuronidase (GUS) driven by the native promoter of ECAP (~2000 bp upstream of the ATG) were generated, of which two were used to analyze the expression pattern of ECAP. As shown in Supplemental Figure 2G, ECAP was constitutively expressed in 9-day-old seedlings, with relatively high levels observed in the leaves and hypocotyls. Additionally, quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis revealed that the transcript of ECAP was not affected by exogenous JA treatment (Figure 2B). Moreover, immunoblotting analysis demonstrated that treatment with 50 µM jasmonic acid had little effect on the protein expression level of ECAP, although it did lead to the degradation of the control JAZ6 protein (Figure 2C−F).

We then investigated the subcellular localization of ECAP in tobacco and

A. thaliana. To successfully clone the ECAP CDS into an expression vector, we performed sequence optimization according to Fuglsang (2003), without changing the amino acid sequence of the ECAP protein. The resulting optimized CDS (Supplemental Data 1) permitted insertion into the pGWB505 and pGWB506 vectors. Transient expression of GFP-tagged ECAP in tobacco leaf cells revealed a consistent nuclear localization of ECAP, regardless of whether the GFP molecule was fused to the N- or C-terminus of the ECAP protein (Supplemental Figure 2H). A similar localization pattern was observed in A. thaliana protoplasts in leaf mesophyll cells transiently expressing ECAP-GFP (Figure 2G) and in wild-type hypocotyl cells stably expressing GFP-ECAP (Figure 2H). Furthermore, the nuclear localization of ECAP was not altered by JA treatment (Figure 2H).

## **ECAP is a transcriptional repressor that represses the expression of JA-responsive genes**

The EAR motif is the predominant transcriptional repression motif in plants and is highly conserved in transcriptional negative regulators in diverse developmental and physiological processes throughout the plant kingdom. Therefore, we hypothesized that nuclear-localized EAR motif-containing ECAP participates in plant gene regulation as a transcriptional repressor of JA signaling. To test this hypothesis, we first investigated whether ECAP is capable of transcriptional repression activity using a protoplast co-transfection assay, as described previously (Tiwari et al., 2004). As shown in Figure 2I, a GUS reporter gene was driven by the 35S promotor containing both LexA and Gal4 DNA-binding (DBD) sites. ECAP was fused to a Gal4 DNA-binding domain (GD-ECAP), which served as an effector to evaluate whether its targeting of the reporter gene promoter could repress the activity of another effector, a transcriptional activator (LexA DBD fused to the Herpes simplex virus VP16 activation domain, LD-VP16) that targeted another site in the promoter. As revealed in Figure 2J, co-transfection of the LD-VP16 effector

gene and the control (GD only) resulted in strong activation of the GUS reporter gene. However, co-transfection of GD-ECAP and LD-VP16 led to a significant decrease in GUS activity, demonstrating a role for ECAP in the repression of GUS reporter gene expression (Figure 2J). We also generated a mutant form of ECAP (ECAPmEAR ) using site-directed mutagenesis, which harbors a mutation in the EAR motif (from 374LELNLG379 to 374AEANAG379) (Figure 2I), and subsequently fused ECAPmEAR to GD to perform a protoplast co-transfection assay. Co-transfection of GD-ECAPmEAR and LD-VP16 resulted in markedly higher GUS activity as compared with GD-ECAP co-transfection, indicating the importance of the EAR domain for ECAP transcriptional repression activity (Figure 2J).

Next, we performed RNA sequencing to compare the gene expression profiles of wild-type and ecap-2 seedlings in the presence and absence of exogenous 50 µM jasmonic acid treatment. In the absence of JA, 267 genes (including 72 early JA-responsive genes) were upregulated in the ecap-2 mutant. After 1 h of JA treatment, transcripts of 348 genes (including 94 early JA-responsive genes) were upregulated intensively in ecap-2 as compared with wild-type, and after 3 h of JA treatment, 385 genes (including 119 early JA-responsive genes) were upregulated more prominently in ecap-2 as compared with wild-type (Figure 3A, Supplementary Figure 3A). Among these induced early JA-responsive genes, the biological processes of "anthocyanin-containing compound biosynthetic process", "response to external biotic stimulus", and "response to jasmonic acid" were significantly over-represented in gene ontology (GO) analysis (Figure 3B and Supplemental Data 1). Selected genes are shown in Figure D, including anthocyanin biosynthesis genes (MYB75, DFR, UF3GT, and ANS), genes involved in the synthesis of jasmonates (OPR3, JAZ8, AOC2, and LOX2), and defense-related genes (VSP1, VSP2, and ERF2) (Figure 3D). These results indicate that JA signaling was enhanced in the ecap-2 mutant.

Additionally, many other genes were induced more prominently in ecap-2

than in wild-type, and there was a strong over-representation of GO terms related to "response to auxin" and "cellular response to iron ion starvation" (Figure 3C and Supplemental Data 1). Moreover, many genes were downregulated to a lower level in ecap-2 as compared with wild-type at different time points (Supplemental Figure 3B), with "cell differentiation" and "regulation of transcription, DNA-templated" among the significantly over-represented biological processes (Supplemental Figure 3B−D, Supplemental Data 1), suggesting a broad role of ECAP in different signaling and biological processes worthy of further investigation.

Subsequently, we validated our RNA-seq results by RT-qPCR. As shown in Figure 3E, loss of ECAP function led to the overexpression of MYB75, TT8, DFR, UF3GT, and ANS, which are related to anthocyanin biosynthesis, even without exogenous JA treatment. The induction of these genes by JA was more prominent in ecap-2 mutant than in wild-type seedlings. Additionally, induction of other genes involved in JA signaling was also enhanced in the ecap-2 mutant. For instance, the expression of JAZ8 was dramatically increased in ecap-2 mutant as compared with wild-type seedlings, regardless of whether exogenous JA was present (Figure 3E). Taken together, the RT-qPCR and RNA-seq results demonstrate a role of ECAP in the repression of JA signaling. To further understand the mechanism by which ECAP represses JA-responsive anthocyanin accumulation, we generated transgenic seedlings stably expressing GFP-ECAP and GFP-ECAPmEAR (Figure 3F and 3G) and treated them with 50 µM MeJA. Interestingly, MeJA-induced anthocyanin accumulation in ECAP-overexpressing seedlings was significantly less than that in wild-type seedlings; however, no significant difference in anthocyanin accumulation was detected between ECAPmEAR -overexpressing and wild-type seedlings, suggesting an important role of the EAR motif in ECAP repression activity (Figure 3H and 3I). Indeed, following transfer to a half-strength Murashige and Skoog media (1/2 MS plate) containing 50 µM MeJA for 1 h, expression of the anthocyanin biosynthesis gene, ANS, in GFP-ECAP

transgenic seedlings was significantly lower than that in wild-type seedlings, but GFP-ECAPmEAR seedlings had a similar ANS expression level to the wild-type seedlings (Figure 3J). Moreover, we crossed the ecap-2 mutant with GFP-ECAP-6- or GFP-ECAPmEAR -4-overexpressing lines, and found that the expression of GFP-ECAP but not GFP-ECAPmEAR could partially rescue the anthocyanin phenotype of ecap-2 (Supplemental Figure 3E and 3F), further demonstrating the requirement of the EAR motif for the functionality of ECAP. Taken together, we can conclude that ECAP is indeed a negative regulator of JA signaling and the EAR motif is of great importance for ECAP function.

### **ECAP protein interacts with JAZ6 and JAZ8**

The above results indicate that ECAP is a nuclear-localized EAR motif-containing protein that represses JA signaling; therefore, we hypothesized that it may act as an adapter protein, similar to NINJA, for the interaction with JAZ repressors. Using a yeast-two-hybrid (Y2H) assay, we found that two of the twelve screened JAZ proteins, JAZ6 and JAZ8, could directly interact with ECAP (Supplemental Figure 4A). The interaction between ECAP and JAZ6/JAZ8 was further confirmed by an in vitro pull-down assay (Figure 4A). To verify the JAZ-ECAP interaction in vivo, we generated transgenic plants expressing JAZ6-GFP or GFP alone to conduct a co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay. JAZ6-GFP or GFP was precipitated from plant extracts using a commercial anti-GFP antibody; the ECAP protein co-precipitated with JAZ6-GFP but not with GFP, as identified using an antibody that recognized ECAP (Figure 4B). Additionally, reconstituted MBP-JAZ8 purified from Escherichia coli pulled down ECAP from total protein extracted from wild-type A. thaliana seedlings but not from ecap-2 mutant seedlings (Figure 4C). Furthermore, a luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay was performed by transient co-expression of ECAP-nLUC and cLUC-JAZ6/8 in tobacco leaves; the results shown in Figure 4D−G further demonstrate the interaction between ECAP and JAZ6/8 in vivo. We also

confirmed the interaction of ECAP with JAZ6/8 in live cells using a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay. The CDS of JAZ6 or JAZ8 was fused to the N-terminal half of YFP (nYFP), and an optimized ECAP sequence was fused to the C-terminal half of YFP (cYFP). By co-expressing nYFP- and cYFP-fused protein pairs in tobacco leaf cells, reconstituted YFP signals indicated an interaction between ECAP and JAZ6/8 (Figure 4H). Interestingly, when we evaluated the interaction between ECAP and other JAZ proteins using the BiFC assay, several JAZs including JAZ1, JAZ2, JAZ5, JAZ10, JAZ11, and JAZ12 were found to be capable of interacting with ECAP, although such an interaction was not detected in the Y2H assay (Supplemental Figure 4A and 4B). Further analyses are needed to investigate whether these JAZs indeed interact with ECAP, in addition to revealing their function.

To elucidate which domain of ECAP is responsible for binding to JAZ proteins, truncated fragments of ECAP, D1 (1−130 aa), D2 (131−610 aa), and D3 (611−784 aa), were used as bait to probe putative interactions with JAZ6 and JAZ8 using a Y2H assay. As shown in Figure 5A, the D3 region containing two C-terminal coiled-coil domains was essential for ECAP binding to JAZ8; whereas either of the EAR motif-containing D2 and D3 regions was sufficient for ECAP binding to JAZ6 (Figure 5A). It is known that the ZIM domain of JAZ proteins is important for their interaction with the adaptor protein, NINJA (Pauwels et al., 2010). To determine whether it is also required for JAZ6/8 binding to ECAP, we generated truncated JAZ6/8 proteins with deletion of the ZIM domain (named JAZ6<sup>∆</sup>ZIM and JAZ8<sup>∆</sup>ZIM ), in addition to the ZIM domain fragments (named ZIMJAZ6 and ZIMJAZ8), and tested their interaction with ECAP using a Y2H assay. We found that both the full-length and ZIMJAZ8 fragment of JAZ8 directly interacted with ECAP, and the deletion of the ZIM domain almost completely abolished the interaction of JAZ8 with ECAP in yeast, indicating that ZIMJAZ8 is sufficient and necessary for JAZ8 binding with ECAP (Figure 5B). With respect to JAZ6, however, although the deletion of ZIMJAZ6 markedly reduced the interaction between JAZ6 and ECAP in yeast, ZIMJAZ6 alone

barely interacted with ECAP, indicating that other motifs in JAZ6 primarily contribute to its interaction with ECAP, whereas the ZIMJAZ6 domain plays an assistant role (Figure 5B).

Considering the interaction of NINJA with most JAZ proteins except JAZ7, and the importance of the ZIMJAZ domain in their interaction (Pauwels et al., 2010; Shyu et al., 2012), we subsequently evaluated whether ECAP and NINJA compete for binding with JAZ6/8. Using in vitro pull-down and in vivo BiFC assays, we found that the addition of NINJA had little influence on the interaction between JAZ6/8 and ECAP (Figure 5C−I).

### **ECAP is an adaptor protein in JAZ6- and JAZ8- repressor**

### **complexes repressing JA-induced anthocyanin accumulation**

Since ECAP is a negative regulator of JA-responsive anthocyanin accumulation and interacts with JAZ6 and JAZ8, we hypothesized that JAZ6 and JAZ8 are repressors in JA signaling to regulate anthocyanin biosynthesis. Recently, Qi et al. (2011) reported that JAZ proteins, including JAZ6 and JAZ8, can interact with essential components of the WD-repeat/bHLH/MYB transcriptional complex, including MYB75, EGL3, TT8, and GL1, which are known to be important TFs for anthocyanin biosynthesis. Further analysis revealed elevated levels of anthocyanins in both jazQ (a quintuple mutant with T-DNA insertion mutations in the JAZ1/3/4/9/10 genes) and jazD (a homozygous jaz1−7/9/10/13 decuple mutant) loss-of-function mutants (Campos et al., 2016; Guo et al., 2018). These results indicate that certain JAZ proteins negatively regulate anthocyanin biosynthesis. To discern the role of JAZ6 and JAZ8, we generated single loss-of-function mutants of JAZ6 and JAZ8 using the CRISPR/Cas9 technique. Two independent lines of each JAZ protein (jaz6−18, jaz6−31, jaz8−4, and jaz8−10) (Figure 6A, Supplemental Figure 5A−D) were used to analyze the anthocyanin phenotype. When grown on normal medium, the anthocyanin content of these mutants was similar to

that of the wild-type. However, following 50 µM MeJA treatment, higher levels of anthocyanin accumulation were observed in mutant seedlings as compared with wild-type seedlings (Figure 6A and 6B, Supplemental Figure 5E and 5F). These data provide genetic evidence that JAZ6 and JAZ8 are indeed repressors involved in JA-mediated anthocyanin accumulation. Interestingly, we crossed jaz6−18 with jaz8−10 and found that the resulting double mutant had greater anthocyanin accumulation than the single jaz6 and jaz8 mutants following JA treatment (Figure 6A and 6B), indicating that JAZ6 and JAZ8 function synergistically in the JA-induced anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway in A. thaliana.

To investigate the genetic interaction between JAZ6/8 and ECAP, we generated the ecap-2 jaz8−10 and ecap-2 jaz6−18 double mutants and the ecap-2 jaz6−18 jaz8−10 triple mutant to analyze the anthocyanin accumulation phenotype following MeJA treatment. As shown in Figure 6A and 6B, the jaz6 ecap-2, the jaz8 ecap-2 double mutants, and the jaz6 jaz8 ecap-2 triple mutant showed a similar level of anthocyanin accumulation to the single ecap-2 mutant, irrespective of the presence of MeJA, implying that both JAZ6 and JAZ8 negatively regulate JA-induced anthocyanin production via the ECAP protein. However, in contrast to ecap-2 having neither root length nor trichome formation phenotypes, analysis of the jaz6/8 mutants demonstrated a possible role of JAZ6/8 in JA-induced root growth inhibition and trichome formation (Supplemental Figure 5G−K), suggesting that JAZ6/8 may utilize different adaptor proteins or act directly to repress various JA signaling-mediated processes (Pauwels et al., 2011; Shyu et al., 2012; Thatcher et al., 2016).

Since JAZ6 and JAZ8 are EAR motif-containing repressors, we hypothesized that ECAP may act in conjunction with JAZ6 and JAZ8 to repress their targets. To verify the effect of ECAP on the transcriptional inhibitory activity of JAZ6 and JAZ8, we performed a protoplast co-transfection assay (Tiwari et al., 2004) to evaluate the transcriptional repression activities of JAZ6 and JAZ8. As shown in Figure 6C and 6D, overexpression of ECAP enhanced

the transcriptional repression activities of JAZ6 and JAZ8; however, ECAPmEAR was less efficient than ECAP in enhancing the repression activities of JAZ6 and JAZ8, indicating that the EAR motif is important for ECAP functionality. In comparison with the wild-type protoplasts, the relative GUS activity ratio (either GD-JAZ6 vs. GD or GD-JAZ8 vs. GD) was higher in ecap-2 mutant protoplasts, indicating that the transcriptional repression activities of JAZ6 and JAZ8 are partially dependent on ECAP (Figure 6E and 6F).

Due to the fact that the NINJA adaptor binds various JAZ proteins (Pauwels et al., 2010), we investigated whether NINJA was also involved in the repression of JA-induced anthocyanin biosynthesis. Two lines of NINJA loss-of-function mutants, ninja-5 and ninja-6, were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 technique (Supplemental Figure 6A−D). Seedlings of both lines had a similar short root length phenotype as previously reported for ninja-1 and ninja-2 (Supplemental Figure 6E and 6F, Acosta et al., 2013); however, no difference in the anthocyanin content was detected between the ninja mutants and the wild-type, irrespective of the presence of JA (Supplemental Figure 6G and 6H), indicating that NINJA is not involved in JAZ6/8 repression of JA-induced anthocyanin biosynthesis. These results are consistent with a previous report showing that NINJA plays a major role as a repressor of JA signaling in roots but not in shoots (Acosta et al., 2013). Our observations regarding ECAP other than NINJA being involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis in shoots further supports the view that the regulation of the JA pathway differs between the roots and the aerial parts of plants (Acosta et al., 2013).

### **ECAP is involved in the recruitment of the co-repressor, TPR2,**

### **to repress JA-induced anthocyanin accumulation**

JAZ proteins are known to form repressor complexes with the general co-repressor proteins, TPL and TPRs, either directly or via interactions with adaptor proteins (Pauwels et al., 2010; Causier et al., 2012; Shyu et al., 2012). In the JA signaling pathway, which modulates defense and root growth

inhibition, the EAR motif-containing NINJA protein is the adaptor protein that mediates JAZ recruitment of co-repressor TPL or TPR proteins. Given the results of the present study, ECAP is likely the adaptor protein for JAZ6 and JAZ8 recruitment of co-repressors to repress anthocyanin biosynthesis. TPL or TPR co-repressors have been reported to repress gene expression by modifying histone deacetylation of target chromatin (Pauwels et al., 2010); therefore, we analyzed the JA-induced changes in histone acetylation level on the promoters of JA-responsive JAZ8 and ANS genes in ecap-2 and wild-type plants. Using anti-H3K9ac to detect histone acetylation in vivo, we found that 50 µM jasmonic acid-induced enrichment of H3K9ac on the JAZ8 and ANS promoters in ecap-2 seedlings was significantly higher than that in wild-type seedlings (Figure 7A), implying that ECAP acts as an adaptor protein for JAZ6/8 recruitment of co-repressors (likely TPL or TPR proteins) that function in histone deacetylation.

Next, we performed a Y2H assay to evaluate whether ECAP can interact with any known co-repressors, and found that TPR2, but not TPL or other TPRs, could interact with ECAP in yeast (Figure 7B). Moreover, the interaction between ECAP and TPR2 was weakened by mutation of the EAR motif (Figure 7B). Subsequent in vitro pull-down and in vivo BiFC analysis confirmed the direct interaction between TPR2 and ECAP (Figure 7C and 7D). These data are also consistent with our hypothesis.

To further confirm the role of TPR2 in JA-induced anthocyanin biosynthesis, we generated a TPR2 loss-of-function mutant using the CRISPR/Cas9 technique, and two independent lines, named tpr2-9 and tpr2-17, were used for analysis (Supplemental Figure 7A and 7B). Following 50 µM MeJA treatment, the seedlings of both tpr2 mutant lines accumulated more anthocyanins than wild-type seedlings (Figure 7E), indicating that TPR2 is a transcriptional co-repressor involved in JA induced-anthocyanin biosynthesis. Notably, the anthocyanin accumulation in the tpr2 mutant was significantly lower than that in the single ecap-2 mutant (Figure 7E), indicating that ECAP is

likely also involved in other mechanism for the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis. Moreover, none of the jaz6, jaz8, jaz6 jaz8, or tpr2 mutant seedlings had an obvious infertility phenotype (Supplemental Figure 5L and 7C), implying the existence of functionally redundant JAZs in the regulation of fertility in these mutants. Considering the infertility phenotype observed in ecap-2 (Supplemental Figure 1H), ECAP may connect transcriptional repressor(s) and co-repressor(s) other than JAZ6/8-TPR2 pairs to regulate fertility in A. thaliana.

In conclusion, we identified ECAP as a novel adaptor protein that connects JAZ6/8 with the transcriptional co-repressor, TPR2. We propose that ECAP mediates JAZ6/8 recruitment of TPR2 to form a transcriptional repressor complex, which represses the WD-repeat/bHLH/MYB transcriptional complex as part of the JA-dependent anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway.

### **DISCUSSION**

Transcriptional repression is an important strategy for signal integration and regulation of gene expression in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Therefore, identifying members of transcriptional inhibitory complexes and illustrating transcription-repressing mechanisms provides insight into the regulation of gene expression and phytohormone control of plant growth and development.

### **ECAP is a novel adaptor protein in JA signaling**

In A. thaliana, it is well known that JAZ proteins act as transcriptional repressors of gene expression at low JA levels and are degraded in a SCFCOI1 -dependent manner when JA levels are elevated (Chini et al., 2007; Thines et al., 2007; Yan et al., 2009; Sheard et al., 2010). Previous studies have demonstrated that most JAZ proteins repress transcription via recruitment of co-repressors including TPL, TPR2, and TPR3 (Pauwels et al., 2010; Causier et al, 2012; Shyu et al., 2012). In JA-mediated defense and root growth inhibition, the recruitment of the co-repressor, TPL, to the SCFCOI1 -JAZ

complex is achieved via the EAR motif-containing adaptor protein, NINJA (Pauwels et al., 2010; Acosta et al., 2013). Here, we have identified a novel adaptor protein, ECAP, in the JAZ repressor complex, which negatively regulates JA-induced anthocyanin accumulation. ECAP has an EAR-containing motif that interacts directly with JAZ6 and JAZ8 (Figure 2 and 4). Loss of ECAP function resulted in overexpression of the JA-responsive anthocyanin biosynthetic genes, DFR, UF3GT, and ANS (Figure 3), which are target genes of the WD-repeat/bHLH/MYB transcriptional factor complex that has established roles in JA-mediated anthocyanin accumulation (Shan et al., 2009; Xu et al., 2014). Additionally, we found that JA induced higher-than-wild-type elevation in MYB75 and TT8 (members of the WD-repeat/bHLH/MYB complex) transcripts in the ecap-2 mutant (Figure 3). Furthermore, JA-induced enrichment of H3K9ac on the promotors of JA-responsive genes in ecap-2 seedlings was significantly higher than that in wild-type seedlings (Figure 7A). Taken together, we propose the working model shown in Figure 7F and 7G: in A. thaliana, JAZ6/8 and ECAP recruit co-repressor(s) to form a transcriptional repressor complex and inhibit the activity of WD-repeat/bHLH/MYB transcriptional factor complex members at low levels of JA. When JA levels increase, JAZ proteins are degraded via the 26S proteasome pathway, subsequently releasing the repression of the WD-repeat/bHLH/MYB complex members and inducing the expression of structural genes in anthocyanin biosynthesis. In the present study, the in vitro and in vivo analyses provided evidence that TPR2 is the co-repressor recruited to JAZ proteins by ECAP (Figure 7B−D).

Moreover, loss of ECAP function also led to defects in fertility and defense responses (Supplemental Figure 1H−L) and caused overexpression of genes (Figure 3D, Supplemental Figure 1J) involved in JA-mediated stamen development (such as MYB21) (Qi et al., 2015) or defense processes (such as VSP1 and VSP2) (Liu et al., 2005). These data suggest a possible broad role of ECAP in certain JA-mediated biological processes worthy of further

investigation.

### **ECAP and NINJA have distinct functions in JA signaling**

As a systemic defense hormone, JA signaling is activated by a variety of developmental and environmental signals and is involved in different plant physiological processes such as pest and pathogen defense, anthocyanin accumulation, and stamen development. JAZ family proteins are JA co-receptors and transcriptional repressors that interact with various proteins to regulate diverse aspects of the JA pathway. Recently, overlapping but also distinct functions of JAZ proteins were identified in JA signaling (Kazan et al., 2012; Shyu et al., 2012; Yu et al., 2016; Gimenez-Ibanez et al., 2017; Takaoka et al., 2018; Rodrigues Oblessuc et al., 2019). Many JAZ proteins recruit their co-repressors, TPL and TRPs, via adaptor protein(s) to form a transcriptional repressor complex. NINJA is a known adaptor protein that mediates JAZ repressor complex function in JA-mediated defense and root growth inhibition (Pauwels et al., 2010; Acosta et al., 2013). However, NINJA does not appear to participate in JA-dependent anthocyanin accumulation (Supplemental Figure 6G and 6H) and is believed to exert a much stronger influence in roots than in aerial tissues (Acosta et al., 2013), implying that NINJA-independent repression mechanisms exist in JA signaling. Indeed, in the present study, we found that ECAP was mainly expressed in the aerial tissues/organs of A. thaliana and acted as an adaptor protein connecting JAZ6/8 proteins with the TPR2 co-repressor to repress JA-dependent anthocyanin accumulation.

Although both NINJA and ECAP are EAR motif-containing proteins, there are obvious differences between them: NINJA has an N-terminal EAR motif (LSLGLS) but ECAP has an EAR motif (LELNLG) in the middle region. Mutation in the EARNINJA motif has been shown to completely abolish the interaction between NINJA and the co-repressor, TPL (Pauwels et al., 2010), indicating that the EARNINJA motif is sufficient and necessary for corepressor interaction. However, the mutant protein, ECAPmEAR , retained a weak

interaction with TPR2 (Figure 7B and 7C), suggesting that other motifs in ECAP besides EARECAP may also be involved in TPR2 interaction. Nevertheless, mutation of the EAR motif markedly reduced the repression capacity of ECAP (Figure 6C and 6D), and expression of ECAPmEAR did not complement the anthocyanin phenotype of the ecap mutant (Supplemental Figure 3E and 3F), demonstrating the importance of the EARECAP motif for ECAP functioning in the repression of anthocyanin biosynthesis in A. thaliana. Additionally, there is no coiled-coil domain in NINJA; however, at each terminus of ECAP, there are two predicted coiled-coil domains (Figure 2A). A coiled-coil domain is a highly versatile protein folding and oligomerization motif, with its architecture contributing to the specific biological functions of particular coiled-coil domain-containing proteins (Burkhard et al., 2001). How coiled-coil domains are related to the biological functions of ECAP is an interesting question to be answered in the future. These data indicate that ECAP possesses functions that are distinct from those of NINJA. Moreover, NINJA has been reported to interact with most JAZ proteins except JAZ7 (Pauwels et al., 2010; Shyu et al., 2012), and recruit the co-repressor, TPL, to form a transcriptional repressor complex that regulates MYC2 activity. Here, we found that ECAP interacts with JAZ6 and JAZ8, and recruits the co-repressor, TPR2. The resulting transcriptional repressor complex influences JA-mediated anthocyanin accumulation by regulating the transcripts and activities of essential components of the WD-repeat/bHLH/MYB transcriptional complex. Therefore, data from several previous studies and the present work indicate that different adaptor proteins mediate JA signaling in an organ- and cell type-specific manner, and are involved in distinct JA-dependent functions, likely by connecting various pairs of JAZ proteins and co-repressors.

Notably in Figure 6B, ecap mutants can be seen to accumulate greater than a 2-fold anthocyanin content as compared with the jaz6 jaz8 double mutant, suggesting that ECAP has other partners in JA signaling. Although our Y2H assay showed that ECAP interacts only with JAZ6/8, the interaction between

ECAP with other JAZ proteins, including JAZ1, JAZ2, JAZ5, JAZ10, JAZ11, and JAZ12, was detected using the in vivo BiFC assay (Supplemental Figure 4B). Of course, in order to draw a conclusion, it will be necessary to confirm the interaction between ECAP and other JAZ proteins besides JAZ6/8 with the use of multiple strategies. Moreover, phenotypic analyses of different JAZ proteins and ECAP double or multiple loss-of-function mutants are also needed in future studies.

On the other hand, JA may regulate different adaptor proteins in different ways. For instance, JA treatment can induce the expression of NINJA (Pauwels et al., 2010) but has no effect on the expression of ECAP (Figure 2B). However, neither the nuclear localization nor protein stability of either NINJA or ECAP are altered by JA treatment (Pauwels et al., 2010, Figure 2C, 2D, and 2H).

### **ECAP is involved in diverse signaling pathways**

Recently, NINJA was found to regulate leaf flatness, likely via the interaction with PPD2 to repress CYCD3;2 gene expression, in addition to its known function in JA signaling (Baekelandt et al., 2018), suggesting a broad role of adaptor proteins in many aspects of plant development.

In the present study, we found that ECAP also mediates the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis via other signaling pathways such as sucrose, salinity, and drought (Supplemental Figure 1B−G), which implies that ECAP is likely not specific to JA signaling in the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis. Notably, in addition to JA-responsive genes, the expression levels of some abscisic acid (ABA)-responsive genes, such as MYB112 and CYP707A2, were also upregulated in the ecap-2 mutant (data not shown). Moreover, in our RNA-seq profiles, the gene encoding DELLA-like protein, RGL1, was upregulated in the ecap-2 mutant (Figure 3D). RGL1 is reported to act as a negative regulator in GA signaling pathways but to play a positive role in JA signaling (Daviere and Achard, 2016). It has been shown that DELLA proteins

promote anthocyanin biosynthesis by sequestering MYBL2 and JAZ suppressors, thus releasing bHLH/MYB subunits to form an active MBW complex (Xie et al., 2016). These data, together with our results demonstrating an interaction between ECAP and MYBL2 in the Y2H assay (data not shown), suggest that ECAP may be involved in different phytohormone signaling or act as an important node for cross-talk among different hormonal and stress signals. Therefore, it is of great interest and importance to further investigate the biological function of ECAP, identify other partners of ECAP, and elucidate the mechanisms of ECAP-mediated transcriptional repression in the regulation and integration of diverse plant development processes and plant responses to various environmental stimuli.

### **METHODS**

### **Plant material and growth conditions**

A. thaliana ecotype Columbia-0 (Col-0) was used in the present study. T-DNA insertion mutants were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC) including the ECAP loss-of-function mutants, ecap-1 (SAIL\_136\_F10) and ecap-2 (SALK\_009978) and the ECAP-like loss-of-function mutants, ecap-like-1 (SALK\_114955), ecap-like-2 (SALK\_082744), and ecap-like-3 (SALK\_020762). The mutants were identified as homozygotes by PCR; the primers used to generate the genomic fragments (LP/RP primers) and obtain T-DNA sequencing (LB1.3/RP primers) are listed in Supplemental Table 1. The JA biosynthesis mutant, opr3 (WS), and JA-IIe receptor mutant, coi1-1, have been described previously (Xie et al., 1998; Chehab, et al., 2011). opr3 (Col) was obtained by crossing opr3 (WS) with the wild-type (Col-0) 3 times to replace the background.

Seeds were sterilized in 5% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite for 15 min and subsequently placed at 4°C for 3 days before sowing . Young seedlings were grown on 1/2 MS (Murashige and Skoog medium with 1% sucrose) culture plates in an incubator at 22°C with a 16 h/8 h ligh t/dark photoperiod before

transferring to soil. Plants were grown in soil at 22°C with a 16 h/8 h light/dark photoperiod in a greenhouse. To observe the JA-induced phenotype, seeds were sown on 1/2 MS plates containing 50 µM MeJA (Sigma, USA, Cat#39924-52-2) and grown for 6 days before being observed under an OLYMPUS stereomicroscope (SZ61).

### **Generation of constructs and transgenic plants**

To successfully clone the ECAP CDS into an expression vector, we performed sequence optimization according to Fuglsang (2003), without changing the amino acid sequence of the ECAP protein. The optimized ECAP gene was synthesized by GenScript (USA) and used for the following constructs. To generate the 35S::ECAP-GFP, 35S::JAZ6-GFP, and 35S::GFP-ECAP constructs, the coding sequences of ECAP and JAZ6 were first cloned into the pDONOR201 vector using the Gateway™ BP Clonase™ II enzyme mix (Thermo Scientific, USA, Cat#11789020) and subsequently cloned into the pGWB505 and pGWB506 vectors using the Gateway™ LR Clonase™ enzyme mix (Thermo Scientific, CAT#11791019). To mutate the EAR motif of pDONOR201-ECAP (LELNLG to AEANAG), a Q5 site-directed mutagenesis kit (NEB, USA, E0554S) was used, and the presence of the desired mutation was verified by DNA sequencing. To generate the ECAPpro::GUS construct, a ~2000 bp fragment upstream of the start codon of ECAP was amplified by PCR using the Q5 High Fidelity PCR master mix (NEB, Cat#E0552S) and cloned into the pCMBIA1391 plasmid using SalI (NEB, Cat#R0138V) and EcoRI (NEB, Cat#R0101V) digestion and subsequent ligation using T4 ligase (NEB, Cat#M0202T). To generate the ECAPpro::GFP-ECAP construct, the ClonExpress II One Step cloning kit (Vazyme, China, Cat#C112-02) was used to insert the GFP-ECAP fusion sequence into the pCAMBIA1300 plasmid. Wild-type plants were transformed with sequence-verified constructs using the Agrobacterium-mediated flower-dipping method (Clough and Bent, 1998). T3- or T4-generation homozygous transgenic lines were used in the experiments.

The ecap-2 GFP-ECAP-6 and ecap-2 GFP-ECAPmEAR -4 were obtained by crossing ecap-2 with the GFP-ECAP/ECAPmEAR -overexpressing line. The primers used to generate the constructs are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

### **CRISPR/Cas9 technique**

To select the targeting sequences, the CRISPR-P (Lei et al., 2014) technique was used. Two specific sites of each target gene were incorporated into forward and reverse primers for the assembly of two guide RNAs. The PCR fragment was amplified from pCBC-DT1T2 using the Q5 High Fidelity PCR master mix and subsequently cloned into pHEE401 using BsaI (NEB, Cat#R0535V) digestion and ligation using T4 ligase (NEB, Cat#M0202T) as described by Wang et al. (2015). Wild-type plants were transformed with the resulting constructs using the Agrobacterium-mediated flower-dipping method (Clough and Bent, 1998). For ecap-3, ecap-4, jaz6−18, jaz6−31, jaz8−4, jaz8−10, tpr2−7, and tpr2−19, T3- or T4-generation homozygous lines were used for the experiments. For ninja-5 and ninja-6, T2-generation homozygous lines were used for the experiments. The jaz6 jaz8 double mutant was generated by crossing jaz6−18 with jaz8−10. The primers are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

### **Yeast-two-hybrid (Y2H) assay**

To generate the constructs used in Y2H, JAZ (JAZ1−12 genes), ECAP/ECAPmEAR , TPL, TPR2, TPR3, and TPR4 coding sequences were amplified by PCR using the Q5 High Fidelity PCR master mix. JAZ and ECAP/ECAPmEAR were cloned into the pGADT7 plasmid (prey), and ECAP, TPL, TPR2, TPR3, and TPR4 were cloned into the pGBKT7 vector (bait) using the ClonExpress II One Step cloning kit (Vazyme, Cat#C112-02). Truncated JAZ6 and JAZ8 were synthesized and cloned into the pGADT7 vector by Genscript (USA). The ECAP-D1 (1−130 aa), D2 (131−610 aa), and D3 (611−784 aa) fragments were obtained by PCR and subsequently cloned into the pGBKT7 plasmid. Competent AH109 yeast cells were co-transformed with

bait and prey plasmids and grown for 4 days on -2 (SD-Leu-Trp) SD culture plates lacking leucine and tryptophan. Subsequently, the transformants were transferred to -3 (SD-Leu-Trp-His) and -4 (SD-Leu-Trp-His-Ade) selective culture plates at several dilutions and grown for 3 days at 30°C. The primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

### **RNA-seq assay**

RNA-seq was utilized to investigate the gene expression patterns in wild-type and ecap-2 mutants. Wild-type and ecap-2 seedlings were grown for 6 days on 1/2 MS plates and then transferred to plates containing 50 µM jasmonic acid for 0, 1, or 3 h. Samples were collected at the indicated times in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using an RNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, China, Cat#DP432). Subsequently, 5 µg each RNA sample was used to construct a library, and an Illumina Hiseq 2500 platform was employed for RNA-deep sequencing. Three independent experiments were performed to collect RNA sequencing data. The adaptor and low-quality sequences were disregarded. Approximately 6.0 GB clean-read data were generated from each sample. TopHat2 was used to analyze the clean data, comparing it with the A. thaliana reference genome database (Kim et al., 2013). The Cufflinks software was used to assemble the transcripts and analyze the differential expression of indicated samples (Trapnell et al., 2012). The DEG (differential expression gene) data were determined using DESeq to show the genes for which the expression exhibited a fold change ≥ 2 and an FDR ≤ 0.01 (Anders and Huber, 2010). The FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments) value of all A. thaliana genes are presented in Supplementary Data 1. Gene ontology (GO) analysis of enriched functional categories was performed using the PANTHER classification system (http://geneontology.org/) (Mi et al., 2019) and thresholded at FDR ≤ 0.05. The results are presented in Supplementary Data 1.

**RT-PCR and RT-qPCR** 

To determine the ECAP expression level in the ecap-1 and ecap-2 mutants, total RNA from 6-day-old seedlings was extracted using an RNA extraction kit (TIANGEN). Subsequently, RNA was reverse-transcribed using SuperScriptTM III reverse transcriptase (Thermo Scientific, Cat#18080044). RT-PCR was performed using the Q5 High Fidelity PCR master mix. ECAP-RT-F and ECAP-RT-R were used as primers to create a 1201-bp product. To investigate ECAP expression following hormone treatment, 6-day-old seedlings grown on 1/2 MS were transferred to fresh 1/2 MS plates with or without 50 µM jasmonic acid (Sigma, USA, Cat# 77026-92-7) for 0, 3, 6, 12, or 24 h. After extraction of the total RNA, RT-qPCR was performed on a 7500 Real-time PCR system using the Power SYBR™ Green PCR master mix (Thermo Scientific, CAT# 4368577). To detect the JA-responsive gene (MYB75, TT8, JAZ8, DFR, UF3GT, and ANS) expression level in Col-0 and ecap-2, 6-day-old seedlings grown on 1/2 MS were transferred to fresh 1/2 MS plates containing 50 µM jasmonic acid for 0, 1, or 3 h. RNA extraction and RT-qPCR were performed using EF1-*α* as an internal control. The primers are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

### **Confocal microscopy**

To determine the subcellular localization of GFP-tagged ECAP in cells, 6-day-old seedlings stably expressing GFP-ECAP were observed under a Leica SP5 confocal laser-scanning microscope, as previously described (Li et al., 2017). Images were acquired using the LAS AF Lite software (Leica, German). To detect GFP-fused ECAP or WRKY40-mCherry in A. thaliana protoplasts, a Zeiss 710 confocal laser-scanning microscope was employed, and the ZEN software was used for image acquisition. To observe ECAP-GFP and GFP-ECAP in tobacco leaf epidermal cells, N. benthamiana leaves were transiently transformed with the indicated plasmids (pGWB505 and pGWB506) via Agrobacterium-mediated injection (Waadt et al., 2008). After 36−48 h, the transformed leaves were observed using the Leica SP5 system, as described above.

### **BiFC assay**

For the BiFC assay, 35S::SPYNE (for JAZ1−12, and TPR2) and 35S::SPYCE (for ECAP) constructs were generated, and tobacco leaves were transformed using the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, as previously described (Waadt et al., 2008). At 36−48 h post-transformation, tobacco leaves were examined for YFP expression under a Leica SP5 confocal microscope (with a 514-nm excitation laser and 530−581-nm barrier). DAPI was used to label the nuclei.

### **Anthocyanin content measurement**

To detect the anthocyanin content in the seedlings, we used the method previously described by Lotkowska et al. (2015) with slight modifications. To detect JA- or sucrose-induced increase in anthocyanin, seeds were sown on 1/2 MS plates with or without 50 µΜ MeJA or containing 0, 30, or 60 mM sucrose, and grown for 6 days. The roots were subsequently removed from 20 mg seedlings, and the shoots were immersed in 3 mL 0.1 M HCl-ethanol and incubated at 98°C for 3 min. Samples were then plac ed in the dark overnight (10−12 h). Following centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at room temperature, the OD of the supernatant was measured at an OD of 530 and 650 nm. The formula, [OD530 nm − 0.25 × OD650 nm] × v (mL)/m (g), was used to determine the anthocyanin content. For the salt-induced anthocyanin measurement, 3-week-old wild-type and ecap-2 plants were treated with 300 mM NaCl for 6 days, following which the aerial part of the plants was collected for the measurement of anthocyanin content, as described above. For drought-induced anthocyanin measurement, 2-week-old wild-type and ecap-2 plants were deprived of water for 3 weeks, following which the shoots of the plants were collected for the measurement of anthocyanin content, as described above.

### **Botrytis cinerea inoculation**

As previously described (Ingle et al., 2015), we used plants grown in a greenhouse for 4 weeks at 22°C with a16 h/8 h light /dark photoperiod. Botrytis cinerea was subcultured on potato medium at 22°C for 2 weeks. S pores were then collected with half-strength grape juice at a density of 5 × 10<sup>4</sup>spores mL-1 . Subsequently, 5-µL aliquots were dropped onto the surface of the leaf blades. Lesions were photographed at 60 h post-inoculation, and lesion size was determined using ImageJ.

### **Root growth and trichome phenotype analyses**

Wild-type, ecap, jaz6, jaz8, and jaz6 jaz8 seeds were sown on 1/2 MS plates containing 50 µΜ MeJA and incubated in a growth chamber at 22°C wit h a 16 h/8 h light/dark photoperiod for 7 days. Subsequently, the plates were photographed and the primary root length of the seedlings was measured using the ImageJ software.

To determine the trichome number, seedlings were grown on 1/2 MS plates for 6 days and then transferred onto fresh 1/2 MS plates. A microfuge tube cap containing a piece of filter paper was placed in the center of each plate, and 150 nM MeJA was dropped onto the filter paper and left to evaporate. After a further 3 weeks of growth, the fifth true leaf was used to calculate the trichome number. An OLYMPUS stereomicroscope (SZ61) and tabletop TM3000 microscope (HITACHI, Japan) were used for image acquisition.

### **Luciferase complementation imaging assay**

The coding sequences of JAZ6 and JAZ8 were cloned into the 35S::cLuc plasmid and optimized ECAP was cloned into the 35S::nLuc plasmid using the ClonExpress II One Step cloning kit. The 35S::COP1-nLuc plasmid was used as the control, as described previously (Lian et al., 2017). The Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain, GV3101, was used to transform N. benthamiana leaves with various construct pairs, according to the previously described method (Waadt et al., 2008). Images were acquired with a CCD camera after 2 days (1300B, Roper, USA). The primers used for the nLUC and cLUC constructs are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

### **Preparation of the anti-ECAP antibody and immunoblotting**

The anti-ECAP antibody was generated and purified by Abclonal, China (https://abclonal.com.cn), based on a fragment (528−784 aa) of ECAP as the antigen. Total protein was extracted from 6-day-old wild-type or ecap seedlings, separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane. The anti-ECAP antibody detected a band at approximately 135 kDa in the wild-type but not the ecap extract. This is larger than the theoretical molecular weight (90.15 kDa) but could be due to high content of acidic amino acid in the protein. The antibody also detected a few nonspecific bands in both the wild-type and the ecap extract (Supplemental Figure 1A).

To analyze ECAP protein stability following JA treatment, 6-day-old wild-type seedlings were transferred to fresh 1/2 MS plates with or without 50 µM jasmonic acid for 0, 3, 6, or 12 h. To demonstrate that the treatment was effective, 6-day-old JAZ6-GFP-21 seedlings were used as a positive control, since JAZ6 is known to be degraded by JA. Total protein was extracted for immunoblotting, and ECAP was detected by the anti-ECAP antibody, JAZ6-GFP was detected by an anti-GFP antibody (ABclonal, AE011), and actin, used as the internal control, was detected by an anti-actin antibody (ABclonal, AC009).

For immunoblotting described here and in the following section, extracted proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred electrophoretically to nitrocellulose membranes. HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (ABclonal, AS003) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (ABclonal, AS014) were used as secondary antibodies. The protein bands were detected using a SuperSignalTM West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate reagent kit (Thermo Scientific) and visualized by a Tanon-5200 Chemiluminecence Imaging System (Tanon Science and Technology, China).

### **Pull-down assays**

To generate His-tagged JAZ6 and JAZ8 constructs, the coding sequences were cloned into the pET30a plasmid, and ECAP was cloned into the pML-c2 plasmid to generate maltose binding protein (MBP)-tagged ECAP. Escherichia coli (DE3) were transformed with all constructs (TIANGEN, Cat#CB108). Ni-NTA (Bio-Rad, USA, Cat#780-0800) and amylose (NEB, USA, Cat#E8021S) resins were used for protein purification. An anti-His antibody (ABclonal, Cat#AE028) was used to detect His-tagged JAZ6 and JAZ8. In brief, 10 µg purified MBP or MBP-ECAP were pre-incubated with 20 µL amylose resin in 200 µL PBS buffer (10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, 140 mM NaCl, pH7.4) for 1 h at 4°C, followed by incubation with 10 µg His-JAZ6, His-JAZ8, or His-RIC1 for a further 1 h at 4°C. The resulting re sin was subsequently washed 3 times with PBS. The prey proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and detected by immunoblotting using an anti-His primary antibody (ABclonal, AE003) and a secondary HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG.

For semi-in vivo pull-down assays, the JAZ8 coding sequence was cloned into the pML-c2 plasmid, and Escherichia coli (DE3) were transformed to initiate expression of MBP-tagged JAZ8. The total protein from 6-day-old wild-type plants were extracted using lysis buffer (100 mM Hepes, 5 mM EDTA, 10 mM Na3VO4, 10 mM NaF, 50 mM glycerol phosphate, 5% glycerol, 0.5% Triton X-100; 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail (Selleck, USA), 10 mM DTT, and 1 mM PMSF were added immediately before use) and incubated with 100 µg purified MBP or MBP-JAZ8 captured by 100 µL amylose resin. After incubation at 4°C for 2 h, the resin was washed 3-5 times with lysis buffer to remove the nonspecific proteins. Prey proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and detected by immunoblotting using an anti-ECAP primary antibody and a secondary HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ABclonal, AS014). ecap-2 seedlings were used as a negative control for the anti-ECAP antibody (ABclonal, China).

A competitive pull-down assay was performed as described previously (Hou

et al., 2010). In brief, 5 µg His-JAZ6 or His-JAZ8 were pre-incubated with 5, 10, or 20 µg His-NINJA prior to incubation with 20 µL amylose resin containing 5 µg MBP-ECAP in 500 µL PBS at 4°C for 2 h. The resin was washed 3 times with PBS, and the proteins were eluted from the resin by boiling at 100°C in 40 µL SDS-PAGE loading buffer. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and detected by immunoblotting using an anti-His primary antibody and a secondary HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG.

### **Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay**

To investigate the interaction between EACP and JAZ6, a Co-IP assay was performed. 35S::GFP and 35S::JAZ6-GFP seedlings were grown on 1/2 MS plates for 6 days, followed by total protein extraction using lysis buffer. GFP-binding resin (MBL, Japan, Cat#D153-10) was used to immunoprecipitate GFP and JAZ6-GFP at 4°C for 2 h, after which the resin was washed 3-5 times with lysis buffer at 4°C for 5 min. The final products were separated using SDS-PAGE and analyzed via immunoblotting using an anti-ECAP primary antibody and a secondary HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG.

### **Protoplast transfection assay**

Extraction and transfection of A. thaliana protoplasts were performed as described by Yoo et al. (2007). The coding sequences of ECAP, ECAPmEAR , JAZ6, and JAZ8 were cloned into GD effector plasmids using the ClonExpress II One Step cloning kit or T4 ligase. Two-week-old wild-type and ecap-2 mutant seedlings were used for protoplast extraction. To detect the transcriptional repression activity of ECAP, JAZ6, and JAZ8, protoplasts were co-transfected with the effectors (LD-VP and GD or GD-ECAP, GD-JAZ6, GD-JAZ8) and reporter [LexA(2×)-Gal4(2×)::GUS], as described previously (Tiwari et al., 2004). In brief, 10 µg each reporter and effector plasmid was used per 200 µL A. thaliana protoplasts. To inhibit the degradation of JAZ6 and JAZ8, 10 µΜ MG132 was added to the buffer after transfection. 35S::LUC was used as an

internal control. GUS activity was measured by a fluorometric assay using a Hitachi F-7000 fluorescence spectrophotometer, with 4-methyl-umbelliferyl-β-d-glucuronide (MUG) as the fluorogenic substrate. All transfection assays were performed as 3 independent experiments. Primers are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

### **Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays**

To detect H3K9 acetylation of the JAZ8 and ANS genes, ChIP assays were performed as previously described (Pazhouhandeh et al., 2010). Chromatin was prepared from 9-day-old wild-type and ecap-2 mutant plants grown on 1/2 MS plates with or without 50 µM jasmonic acid for 1 h. A H3K9ac antibody (ABclonal, Cat# A7255) was used to immunoprecipitate chromatin extracted from approximately 2 g frozen samples. The protein−chromatin immunocomplex was achieved using magnetic protein A/G beads (Biomake, USA, Cat#B23202), which were washed 3 times to remove nonspecific binding. DNA fragments after ChIP analysis were quantitated by RT-qPCR using a SuperReal PreMix Plus kit (TIANGEN, Cat# FP205) and subsequently normalized to the internal control, Actin7. The primers are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

### **Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis**

To align the EAR motif of ECAP with those of other related proteins, aa 361−398 were selected from ECAP proteins, aa 1−70 were selected from IAA9, IAA11, IAA18, IAA26, IAA27, and AFP1, aa 47−85 were selected from AFP2, and aa 54−90 were selected from AFP4. For the other proteins, aa 1−37 were selected (Pauwels et al., 2010). The sequence alignment was performed using the DNAMAN and BioEdit software.

The phylogenetic tree for ECAP, ECAP-like, and related proteins in other species was generated using MEGA6 (Tamura et al., 2013) by the neighbor-joining method, with bootstrap values out of 1000 replicates. Default settings were used. The ECAP-related proteins were selected using the

BLASTP tool (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) in the NCBI database. The protein sequences were aligned using ClustalX2, as shown in Supplemental Figure 8.

(F−G) Working model of ECAP in the repression of JA-mediated anthocyanin biosynthesis. When low levels of JA are present (F), ECAP connects JAZ6/8 with the co-repressor, TPR2, forming a JET (JAZs-ECAP-TPR2) transcriptional repressor complex. The JET complex represses the activity of MBW complex members (such as MYB75, TT8, and EGL3) that regulate the expression of anthocyanin biosynthesis-related genes. Additionally, certain JAZ proteins also interfere with the formation and function of the MBW complex by competitively interacting with its members (Qi et al., 2011). Elevated JA levels promote the COI1-dependent degradation of JAZ proteins (including JAZ6 and JAZ8) via the 26S proteasome pathway. Thus, the repression on MBW complex activity is released, resulting in elevated expression of anthocyanin biosynthesis-related genes, and the consequent accumulation of anthocyanin.
