# **Biosynthesis of riccionidins and marchantins is regulated by R2R3- MYB transcription factors in** *Marchantia polymorpha*

**Hiroyoshi Kubo1 · Shunsuke Nozawa1 · Takuma Hiwatashi2 · Youichi Kondou3 · Ryo Nakabayashi4 · Tetsuya Mori4 · Kazuki Saito4,5 · Kojiro Takanashi1,6 · Takayuki Kohchi7 · Kimitsune Ishizaki2**

Received: 8 March 2018 / Accepted: 7 May 2018 © The Botanical Society of Japan and Springer Japan KK, part of Springer Nature 2018

#### **Abstract**

R2R3-MYB transcription factors constitute the largest gene family among plant transcription factor families. They became largely divergent during the evolution of land plants and regulate various biological processes. The functions of R2R3-MYBs are mostly characterized in seed plants but are poorly understood in non-seed plants. Here, we examined the function of two *R2R3-MYB* genes of *Marchantia polymorpha* (*Mapoly0073s0038* and *Mapoly0006s0226*) that are closely related to subgroup 4 of the *R2R3-MYB* family. We performed LC/MS/MS metabolomics, RNA-seq analysis and expression analysis in overexpressors and knockout mutants of Mp*MYB14* and Mp*MYB02*. Overexpression of Mp*MYB14* remarkably increased the amount of riccionidins, which are specific anthocyanins in liverworts and a few flowering plants. In contrast, overexpression of Mp*MYB02* increased the amount of several marchantins, which are characteristic cyclic bis (bibenzyl ether) compounds in *M. polymorpha* and related liverworts. Knockouts of Mp*MYB14* and Mp*MYB02* abolished the accumulation of riccionidins and marchantins, respectively. The expression of Mp*MYB14* was up-regulated by UV-B irradiation, N deficiency, and NaCl treatment, whereas the expression of Mp*MYB02* was down-regulated by NaCl treatment. Our results suggest that the regulatory framework of phenolic metabolism by R2R3-MYB was already established in early land plants.

# **Introduction**

Land plants have many transcription factors that regulate their growth and development in unfavourable environments (Riechmann et al. [2000\)](#page-14-0). Among plant transcription factors, MYB transcription factors constitute the largest gene family in land plants. More than 100 *MYB* genes have been reported in seed plants (Dubos et al. [2010;](#page-13-0) Jin et al. [2017](#page-14-1)). On the other hand, the number of *MYB* genes is much lower in pteridophytes, bryophytes, and chlorophytes. Twenty-four and 69 *MYB* genes have been reported in *Selaginella moellendorffii* and *Physcomitrella patens*, respectively, and 16 and 9 *MYB* genes in *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* and *Chlorella* sp. NC64A, respectively (Jin et al. [2017\)](#page-14-1).

The MYB domain is composed of one to four conserved repeats of approximately 50 amino acids. R2R3-MYBs that contain two repeats in the MYB domain are prominently divergent in seed plants. Stracke et al. ([2001](#page-14-2)) identified 126 *R2R3-MYBs* in Arabidopsis and categorized them into 25 subgroups. Du et al. ([2015](#page-13-1)) classified 1,548 *R2R3-MYBs* of major eukaryotic lineages into 73 subfamilies. The complexity of the R2R3-MYB family indicates that gene duplication and diversification of their functions must have contributed to the evolution of the physiological and developmental processes of land plants. R2R3-MYB regulates various processes in angiosperms, such as the cell cycle (Brownfield et al. [2009;](#page-13-2) Mu et al. [2009\)](#page-14-3), secondary metabolism (Gonzalez et al. [2008;](#page-13-3) Stracke et al. [2007](#page-15-0)), response to phytohormones (Gubler et al. [1995\)](#page-13-4), response to environmental stress (De Vos et al. [2006;](#page-13-5) Lippold et al. [2009](#page-14-4)), and the development and differentiation of plants (Lee and Schiefelbein [1999](#page-14-5); Müller et al. [2006\)](#page-14-6). MYB3Rs that contain three conserved repeats are found in animals, fungi, and plants (Rosinski and Atchley [1998](#page-14-7)). Many of them are involved in the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis (Haga et al. [2007;](#page-13-6) Ito [2005;](#page-14-8) Kobayashi et al. [2015](#page-14-9); Weston and Bishop [1989](#page-15-1)). Some single-repeat MYBs regulate the accumulation of anthocyanin and the formation of trichomes and root hairs (Dubos et al. [2008](#page-13-7); Schellmann et al. [2002](#page-14-10); Wada et al. [1997\)](#page-15-1). Little is known about the function of MYB4Rs that contain four repeats (Dubos et al. [2010](#page-13-0)).

One well-known function of R2R3-MYBs is the regulation of the biosynthesis of phenolic compounds (Dubos et al. [2010\)](#page-13-0). R2R3-MYB directs the flow of substrates by activating multiple genes involved in the biosynthetic pathway (Fig. S1). In Arabidopsis, R2R3-MYBs in subgroups (SGs) 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 13 regulate the metabolism of phenolic compounds (Dubos et al. [2010](#page-13-0)). Production of anthocyanin pigment 1 (PAP1), which belongs to SG6, regulates the biosynthesis of anthocyanin through activation of the flavonoid late biosynthetic genes (LBGs), which encode dihydroflavonol reductase (DFR), anthocyanin synthase (ANS), and flavonoid 3-glucosyltransferase (3GT) (Gonzalez et al. [2008;](#page-13-3) Tohge et al. [2005\)](#page-15-1). AtMYB12 (SG7) regulates flavonol biosynthesis through activation of the gene encoding flavonol synthase (FLS) and the flavonoid early biosynthetic genes (EBGs), which encode chalcone synthase (CHS), chalcone isomerase (CHI) and flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H) (Stracke et al. [2007\)](#page-15-0). AtMYB123 (SG5) regulates tannin biosynthesis through activation of the genes encoding DFR, ANS, and anthocyanidin reductase (ANR) (Nesi et al. [2001](#page-14-11)). AtMYB61 (SG13) and AtMYB58 (SG3) regulate lignin biosynthesis (Newman et al. [2004;](#page-14-12) Zhou et al. [2009](#page-15-1)). AtMYB58 (SG3) activates multiple genes involved in phenylpropanoid and lignin biosynthesis (Zhou et al. [2009\)](#page-15-1). AtMYB4 (SG4) regulates phenylpropanoid biosynthesis through suppression of the gene encoding C4H (Jin et al. [2000\)](#page-14-13).

Land plants contain various phenolic compounds. The diversity of the phenolic compounds was established during the evolution of land plants. Bryophytes, which diverged from a common ancestor of vascular plants over 430 million years ago, have a relatively simple constitution of phenolic compounds. Flavone and flavonol derivatives have been identified in many mosses and liverworts (Markham and Porter [1974\)](#page-14-14). Although there are no typical anthocyanins, some bryophytes accumulate red pigments, such as deoxyanthocyanin of *Bryum cryophilum* (Bendz et al. [1962\)](#page-13-8) and riccionidins of some liverwort species, including *Ricciocarpus natans* (Kunz et al. [1994](#page-14-15)). The function of these anthocyanins in Bryophytes is not determined, but several hypotheses related to photoprotection, free radical scavenging and modulation of signalling cascades are proposed on the function of anthocyanin in vegetative organs (Hatier and Gould [2009](#page-13-9)). Vascular plants produce lignins, which are essential for the efficient transportation of water and gain in mechanical strength of the plant body (Douglas [1996\)](#page-13-10). Anthocyanin is widely spread among angiosperms as an attractant for pollinators (McClure [1975\)](#page-14-16). Thus, land plants have acquired chemical and mechanical capacities from novel phenolic compounds to adapt to the terrestrial environment. The evolution of the constituents of phenolic compounds is a subject of great interest.

*Marchantia polymorpha* L. belongs to liverworts, which represent the earliest diverging lineage of extant land plants (Ishizaki [2017\)](#page-14-17), and attracts interest as an emerging model plant among the basal land plants (Ishizaki et al. [2016\)](#page-14-18). Sixty MYB and MYB-related proteins are identified in the genome database of *M. polymorpha* (Bowman et al. [2017](#page-13-11)). Among them, MpR2R3-MYB14 (Mapoly0073s0038; hereafter MpMYB14) and MpR2R3-MYB02 (Mapoly0006s0226; hereafter MpMYB02) fall basal to the large phylogenetic clade containing all the Arabidopsis SG4, 5, 6, 7, 15, and 44 (Bowman et al. [2017\)](#page-13-11). Mp*MYB14* was shown to regulate riccionidin biosynthesis (Albert et al. [2018](#page-13-12)), but the function of Mp*MYB02* is not known. Here, we examine the functions of Mp*MYB14* and Mp*MYB02* and discuss the evolution of R2R3-MYB and their functions in phenolic metabolism.

#### **Materials and methods**

#### **Plant materials and growth conditions**

*Marchantia polymorpha*, accession Takaragaike-1 (Tak-1) was used. The thalli were grown on M51C medium (Ishizaki et al. [2008\)](#page-14-19) at 22 °C under a 16-h day length. For N-deficient medium, KNO3, NH4NO3, casamino acids, and l-glutamine were removed from the M51C medium. For carbon (C)-deficient medium, M51C medium without sucrose was used. For P-deficient medium, KH2PO4 was removed from the M51C medium. For UV-B treatment, the thalli grown on M51C medium for 8 days under white light conditions were irradiated with white light supplemented with UV-B (1.3 W m−2). UV-B was obtained from a UV-B fluorescent tube (FL20SE, KYOKKO DENKI, Tokyo, Japan). UV-B radiation (280–320 nm) was measured using a Solarmeter Model 6.2 (Solar Light Company, PA, USA). The thalli grown on vermiculite were watered with 0.05% Hyponex nutrient solution (Hyponex Japan, Osaka, Japan).

#### **Plasmid constructs and transformations**

cDNAs encoding MpMYB14 and MpMYB02 were amplified with Phusion DNA polymerase (NEB, MA, USA). MpMYB14start and MpMYB14term primers were used for Mp*MYB14* and MpMYB02start and MpMYB02term primers for Mp*MYB02*. The amplified cDNAs were cloned into the *Eco*RV site of pBluescript SK+. The cDNA was cut with restriction enzymes and inserted downstream of the *CaMV35S* promoter of a modified version of the binary vector pIG121-Hm (Hiei et al. [1994\)](#page-14-20). DNA sequences were determined with ABI3130xl (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA).

For the construction of a glucocorticoid receptor (GR) domain fusion, cDNA of the GR domain was amplified with GRFwd and GRRev primers using a pTA7002 plasmid as a template (Aoyama and Chua [1997\)](#page-13-13). The amplified cDNA was fused with the cDNA of Mp*MYB14*. Mp*MYB14-GR* was inserted downstream of the *CaMV35S* promoter of the pIG121-Hm vector.

Transformation of *M. polymorpha* was performed as described by Ishizaki et al. ([2008](#page-14-19)). Immature thalli of Tak-1 were co-cultured with *Agrobacterium* and then grown on a selection medium containing hygromycin as the antibiotic. Transformants (T1) were grown on the same selection medium to obtain gemmae (G1 generation). G1 gemmae were grown on M51C medium to obtain gemmae of the next generation (G2 generation). G2 and their descendant gemmae were used for the experiments.

## **Knockout of Mp***MYB14* **and Mp***MYB02*

An off-target search was performed using casOT (Xiao et al. [2014](#page-15-1)). Synthesized oligo DNA sets for target sites (202f\_F 5′-CTCGGTGGATCAACTACTTAAGGC-3′ and 202f\_R 5′-AAACGCCTTAAGTAGTTGATCCAC -3′, 455r\_F 5′-CTCGAGCACCAGTGTGAACGGAAG -3′ and 455r\_R 5′-AAACCTTCCGTTCACACTGGTGCT -3′, 203f\_F 5′-CTCGCTCCGTCCAGACCTAAAGCG-3′ and 203f\_R 5′-AAACCGCTTTAGGTCTGGACGGAG -3′, 427f\_F 5′-CTCGAGTGGGGAGCCATATATCGC-3′ and 427f\_R 5′AAACGCGATATATGGCTCCCCACT-3′) were annealed, inserted into the *Bsa*I site of entry vector pMpGE\_En03 and then introduced into destination vector pMpGE011 (Sugano et al. [2014\)](#page-15-2). Genomic DNA was isolated from transformed plants and amplified from the target region by PCR. The PCR product was used for sequencing with ABI3130xl (Applied Biosystems).

#### **RT‑PCR analysis**

For the extraction of total RNA, tissue was ground with extraction buffer (0.1 M Tris-HCl pH 9.0, 0.1 M NaCl, 1% SDS, 0.1% 2-mercaptoethanol) and extracted with phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) 3–4 times. RNA was precipitated with 2 M LiCl. After ethanol precipitation, the pellet was dried, dissolved with diethyl pyrocarbonate-treated water, and treated with DNase (Promega, WI, USA). For quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis under UV-B conditions, total RNA was extracted using a Plant Total RNA Purification Kit (Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan), and cDNA was synthesized using qPCR RT Master Mix with a gDNA remover (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). RNA concentration was measured with a Gene-Quant™ RNA/DNA calculator (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Uppsala, Sweden).

First-strand cDNA was synthesized by M-MuLV reverse transcriptase (NEB) with an oligo-d(T)15 primer. For quantitative RT-PCR, an ABI Step One Plus system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) and Thunderbird™ SYBR® qPCR mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) were used. The nucleotide sequences of the primers used for RT-PCR are shown in Table S1.

# **RNA‑seq analysis**

Total RNA was extracted from thalli grown for 6 weeks on M51C agar medium using an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen K.K., Tokyo, Japan). After DNase treatment, cDNA was prepared with an Ion Total RNA-seq Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

The quality and quantity of the resultant total RNA were evaluated using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and a Bioanalyzer RNA6000 Nano Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The sequence libraries were constructed with a TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit v2 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The quality and quantity of each library were determined using an Agilent Bioanalyzer with a High Sensitivity DNA kit (Agilent Technologies) and a KAPA Library Quantification Kit from Illumina (Illumina). Equal amounts of each library were mixed to make the 2 nM pooled library. Illumina sequencing was performed using a HiSeq2500 platform (Illumina) to produce 101 single-end reads. RNA-seq analysis was performed with three independent RNA samples in each line. All of the reads obtained have been deposited in the DDBJ BioProject and BioSample databases under accession numbers DRA005914 (DRA), PRJ DB5890 (BioProject), and SAMD 00084096 - SAMD 00084104 (BioSample).

The resultant reads were mapped to the draft genome of *M. polymorpha* version 3.1 by TopHat ver 2.1.1 (Kim et al. 2013) with default parameters. To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), the mapped reads from different samples were compared by Cuffdiff 2.2.1 (Trapnell et al. 2013) using default parameters and our in-house transcript annotation generated on the Marchantia genome assembly ver. 3.1 provided by the Joint Genome Institute. The supercomputers of the Academic Center for Computing and Media Studies at Kyoto University were used for computation.

#### **DEX experiment**

Thalli were incubated with shaking for 8 h in M51C liquid medium containing 10  $\mu$ M dexamethasone (DEX) with or without 100  $\mu$ M cycloheximide (CHX). Then, 0.01% ethanol was used for the mock treatment. After the treatment, total RNA was extracted and used for quantitative RT-PCR. The expression was examined by qRT-PCR using actin or Mp*EF1* as an endogenous control. Threshold cycle (C<sub>t</sub>) values were normalized with the corresponding value of an endogenous control to obtain a  $\Delta$ C<sub>t</sub> value. The  $-\Delta\Delta$ C<sub>t</sub> value was calculated using the  $\Delta$ C<sub>t</sub> value of a reference:  $-\Delta\Delta$ C<sub>t</sub> =  $\Delta$ C<sub>t</sub> (DEX) –  $\Delta$ C<sub>t</sub> (reference).

#### Metabolome analysis using LC-QTOF-MS

The fresh samples were extracted with 5  $\mu$ l of 80% methanol containing 2.5  $\mu$ M lidocaine and 10-camphorsulfonic acid per milligram fresh weight using a mixer mill with zirconia beads for 7 min at 18 Hz and 4 °C. After centrifugation for 10 min, the supernatant was filtered using an HLB  $\mu$ Elution plate (Waters, Milford, MA, USA).

The extracts (1 µl) were analysed using LC-QTOF-MS (LC, Waters Acquity UPLC system; MS, Waters Xevo G2 Q-Tof). The analytical conditions were as follows: LC: column, Acquity bridged ethyl hybrid (BEH) C18 (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm  $\times$  100 mm, Waters); solvent system, solvent A (water including 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile including 0.1% formic acid); gradient program, 99.5% A/0.5% B at 0 min, 99.5% A/0.5% B at 0.1 min, 20% A/80% B at 10 min, 0.5% A/99.5% B at 10.1 min, 0.5% A/99.5% B at 12.0 min, 99.5% A/0.5% B at 12.1 min and 99.5% A/0.5% B at 15.0 min; flow rate, 0.3 ml min<sup>-1</sup> at 0 min, 0.3 ml min<sup>-1</sup> at 10 min, 0.4 ml min<sup>-1</sup> at 10.1 min,  $0.4 \text{ ml min}^{-1}$  at 14.4 min, and 0.3 ml min<sup>-1</sup> at 14.5 min; column temperature, 40 °C; photodiode array, 200-500 nm; phenylpropanoid detection, 280 nm; flavonoid detection, 340 nm; and riccionidin detection, 490 nm. MS detection: capillary voltage, + 3.0 keV; cone voltage, 25.0 V; source temperature, 120 °C; desolvation temperature, 450 °C; cone gas flow, 50 l  $h^{-1}$ ; desolvation gas flow, 800 l  $h^{-1}$ ; collision energy, 6 V; mass range, m/z 50–1,500; scan duration, 0.1 s; interscan delay, 0.014 s; data acquisition, centroid mode; and polarity, positive/negative. Lockspray (Leucine enkephalin): scan duration, 1.0 s, and interscan delay, 0.1 s. MS/MS data were acquired in the ramp mode under the following analytical conditions: (a) MS: mass range, m/z 50–1,500; scan duration, 0.1 s; inter-scan delay, 0.014 s; data acquisition, centroid mode; and polarity, positive/negative; and (b) MS/ MS: mass range, m/z 50–1,500; scan duration, 0.02 s; interscan delay, 0.014 s; data acquisition, centroid mode; polarity, positive/negative; and collision energy, ramped up from 10 to 50 V. In this mode, the MS/MS spectra of the top-10 ions (>1,000 counts) in an MS scan were automatically obtained. If the ion intensity was less than 1,000, MS/MS data acquisition was not performed, and the scan moved to the next top-10 ions. Data acquisition was performed using MassLynx 4.1. Chemical assignment was performed using the exact mass of metabolites in M. polymorpha stored in KNApSAcK. Exact masses were searched for precursor ions  $([M+H]^+$  or  $[M-H]^-)$  with a tolerance of  $\pm 0.01$  Da. Candidate metabolites were characterized with an MS/MS pattern.

HPLC analysis of marchantins was performed by Xing et al. (2007). The analytical conditions were as follows: column, Inertsil ODS 80A (GL Sciences Inc., Tokyo, Japan); solvent A (water including 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile including 0.1% formic acid); gradient program, 40% B for 0–10 min, a linear gradient from 40 to 55% B for 10–30 min, 55–90% for 30–40 min, 90% B for 40–50 min, and then 90–40% B; and flow rate, 0.6 ml min<sup>-1</sup>.

## Measurement of total antiradical capacity

The total antiradical capacity of each thallus was evaluated by measuring the decrease in the absorbance of 1,1-diphenyl-2-picryl-hydrazyl (DPPH) (Yamaguchi et al. 1998). Thalli were grown for 6 weeks on M51C medium and extracted with 70% methanol. One hundred microliters of the extract was mixed with 700 µl of DPPH solution [0.1 mM DPPH, 50 mM MES (pH 7.0), 50% ethanol]. The absorbance of the mixture was measured at 520 nm. The absorbance without DPPH was used to compensate for the absorbance by endogenous substances.

#### **Construction of phylogenetic trees**

Alignment and phylogenetic analysis of amino acid sequences of the MYB domain were performed with ClustalW version 2.1 (Larkin et al. 2007) and PhyML 3.0 (Guindon et al. 2010), respectively. The bootstrap value was 100. The phylogenetic tree was displayed with NJplot version 2.3 (Perrière and Gouy 1996).

#### **Results**

## **R2R3‑MYB family of** *M. polymorpha*

Sixty MYB and MYB-related genes have been identi fied in the *M. polymorpha* genome (Bowman et al. [2017](#page-13-11)). We focused on the R2R3-MYB family of *M. polymorpha* and compared the amino acid sequence, intron position, and R2 structure with those of a basal charophyte alga (*Klebsormidium flaccidum*), a chlorophycean alga (*Chlamydomonas reinhardtii*) (Fig. S2a), and Arabidopsis (Fig. S2b). At the top of the phylogenetic tree in Fig. S2a, one clade composed of seven OTUs contains no algal MYBs. These R2R3-MYBs were dispersed in the phyloge netic tree for *M. polymorpha* and Arabidopsis (Fig. S2b).

R2R3-MYB families of *M. polymorpha* and Arabidop sis were classified according to Du et al. ([2015\)](#page-13-1) (Table [1](#page-4-0); Fig. S3). Because a-, b-, and c-type introns were closely related to one another, they were put into one category, "abc". The abc-type intron was remarkably increased in number in Arabidopsis. The number of members in each category was lower in *M. polymorpha*. Nevertheless, *M. polymorpha* had at least one member in most categories. The intron patterns of *K. flaccidum* and *C. reinhardtii* were variable, as has been reported (Fig. S2a) (Du et al. [2015](#page-13-1)).

Three conserved tryptophans in an MYB repeat are usually divided by 18 or 19 amino acid residues. The R2 structure of MYB3R and some plant R2R3-MYBs is W-x(19)-W-x(18)-W type. On the other hand, several plant R2R3-MYBs have a W-x(19)-W-x(19)-W type R2 struc ture (Williams and Grotewold [1997\)](#page-15-1). R2R3-MYBs of *M. polymorpha* and Arabidopsis were classified by their R2 structures. The W-x(19)-W-x(19)-W type was dominant in the R2R3-MYB family of land plants (Fig. S2b). In *K. flaccidum*, a few R2R3-MYBs had this R2 structure (Fig. S2a). No R2R3-MYBs had this type of R2 struc ture in *C. reinhardtii* (Fig. S2a). On the other hand, the W-x(19)-W-x(18)-W type was found in all the plants. The number of R2R3-MYBs with the W-x(19)-W-x(18)-W type R2 structure was 8, 12, 6, and 23 in *M. polymorpha, K. flaccidum, C. reinhardtii*, and Arabidopsis, respectively (Fig. S1a, b).

## **Mp***myb14* **and Mp***myb02* **mutants do not accumulate anthocyanins and marchantins, respectively**

Knockout mutants of Mp*MYB14* and Mp*MYB02* were made with the CRISPR/Cas9 system. We selected two target sequences, one located middle of the R2R3-MYB domain and the other located downstream of the R2R3-MYB domain (Fig. S6a, b).

Anthocyanin did not accumulate in the plant grown on normal M51C medium. When thalli were grown on nitrogen-removed medium, the Tak-1 and Mp*myb02* mutants accumulated red pigment, but both the Mp*myb14\_202f* and Mp*myb14\_455r* mutants completely lacked red pigment (Fig. [3a](#page-9-0)). However, the amount of luteolin and apigenin derivatives did not change significantly between Tak-1 and Mp*myb14* (Fig. [2](#page-6-0)).

Phenolic compounds of Mp*myb02\_203f* and Mp*myb02\_427f* mutants were analysed by HPLC. In Tak-1 and Mp*myb14*, several peaks were detected around RT 20–40 min, at which various marchantins were expected to be detected (Xing et al. [2007\)](#page-15-1). In Mp*myb02\_203f* and Mp*myb02\_427f*, however, no peak was detected in this region (Fig. [3b](#page-9-0)). LC/MS/MS analysis showed that the accumulation of all marchantins was abolished in Mp*myb02* (Fig. [2](#page-6-0)). Lunularic acid and prelunularic acid were slightly increased in Mp*myb02* (Fig. [2](#page-6-0)).

#### **Effect of environmental factors on the expression of Mp***MYB14* **and Mp***MYB02*

Because the biosynthesis of phenolics is regulated by various environmental factors through regulation by *R2R3-MYB* in angiosperms, we investigated whether Mp*MYB14* and Mp*MYB02* were involved in the regulation by environmental factors such as UV-B (Fig. [4](#page-10-0)a) and nutrients (Fig. [4b](#page-10-0)).

Albert et al. [\(2018](#page-13-12)) reported that strong white light promoted the expression of Mp*MYB14*. Here, we examined the effect of UV-B on the expression of Mp*MYB14* and Mp*MYB02*. When white light supplemented with UV-B was irradiated on the thallus of Tak-1, the expression of Mp*MYB14* was increased within 1 h and continued to increase for at least 6 h (Fig. [4a](#page-10-0)). On the other hand, the expression of Mp*MYB02* was not affected by UV-B (Fig. [4](#page-10-0)a).

Mp*MYB14* was up-regulated when thalli were grown on a medium from which all mineral nutrients were removed (Albert et al. [2018\)](#page-13-12). Therefore, we examined the effect of sucrose, nitrogen, phosphate and NaCl on the expression of Mp*MYB14* and Mp*MYB02*. In the thalli grown for 7 days on M51C medium without sucrose, the expression of Mp*MYB14* was slightly reduced compared with the thalli grown on M51C medium containing 2% sucrose (Fig. [4](#page-10-0)b). The expression of Mp*MYB14* was not affected when the concentration of sucrose was increased from 2 to 4% (data not shown). In the thalli grown for 7 days on nitrogen (N)-deficient medium, the expression of Mp*MYB14* significantly increased, and the expression of Mp*MYB02* slightly increased (Fig. [4b](#page-10-0)). When the thalli were treated with 200 mM NaCl for 24 h, the expression of Mp*MYB14* was slightly increased, but that of Mp*MYB02* was greatly decreased (Fig. [4](#page-10-0)b). Phosphate (P) deficiency did not significantly affect the expression of Mp*MYB14* or Mp*MYB02* (Fig. [4b](#page-10-0)).

# **Downstream genes of Mp***MYB14*

To examine whether Mp*MYB*s regulate biosynthetic genes under N-deficient conditions, we examined the expression of several biosynthetic genes for phenolics that showed high expression in RNA-seq experiments (Fig. [5](#page-11-0)). The expression of *PAL* (*Mapoly0005s0089*), *C4H* (*Mapoly0163s0018*), *CHS* (*Mapoly0021s0159*), *CHI-like* (*Mapoly0175s0004*) and *Dir* (*dirigent-like; Mapoly0078s0058*) increased under N deficiency in Tak-1. In the Mp*myb14* mutant, however, the increase in expression under N deficiency was less for *PAL, CHS* and *CHI-like*, indicating that these genes are partly regulated by Mp*MYB14*. On the other hand, the expression of *Di*r did not increase under N deficiency in the Mp*myb14* mutant, indicating that Mp*MYB14* is a dominant regulator of *Dir*.

Because *PAL, CHS, CHI-like* and *Dir* were suggested to be regulated by Mp*MYB14*, we utilized an inducible system in which protein nuclear localization is modulated by a glucocorticoid receptor (GR) domain, and the function of a transcription factor can be activated in response to dexamethasone (DEX) (Lloyd et al. [1994](#page-14-27); Schena et al. [1991](#page-14-28)). The Mp*MYB14-GR* transgenic line slightly accumulated anthocyanin 4 days after DEX treatment, showing that these transgenic lines are functional (Fig. S7).

The expression of the flavonoid-related genes was examined with two Mp*MYB14-GR* lines with or without DEX treatment. Transient induction by DEX treatment activated *PAL, CHS, CHI-like*, and *Dir* in Mp*MYB14*-*GR* (Fig. [6](#page-11-1)). DEX activated the expression of these genes in the presence of cycloheximide, suggesting that de novo protein synthesis is not required for the regulation of these genes.

From the phylogenetic tree, we can postulate the evolution of *R2R3-MYB* genes. The W-x(19)-W-x(18)-W type R2 structure found in SG21, 22, 23, and 25 is considered to be an ancient type (Du et al. [2015](#page-13-1)). The W-x(19)-W-x(19)- W type R2 structure seemed to appear before the terrestrialization of plants because this type was found in several charophyte algae, such as *K. flaccidum, Spirogyra pratensis* and *Coleochaete orbicularis* (data not shown). In early land plants, three intron types (abc, f, and g) were established in the *R2R3-MYB* genes coding W-x(19)-W-x(19)-W type R2R3-MYB. Then, SG14 with a W-x(20)-W-x(19)-W type R2 and AtMYB99 appeared from the abc group. *R2R3- MYB* genes with an f-type intron formed SG18. Members of SG18 were reported to regulate male organ development in *P. patens* (Aya et al. [2011](#page-13-22)) and flowering plants (Zhang et al. [2014\)](#page-15-1). The *R2R3-MYB* gene with a g-type intron was reported to regulate sperm cell specification in Arabidopsis (Rotman et al. [2005](#page-14-30)). On the other hand, *R2R3-MYB* genes with an abc-type intron show various functions, but many of them are related to phenolic metabolism, epidermal cell fate, and abiotic stresses (Dubos et al. [2010\)](#page-13-0). *R2R3-MYB* genes of this type in early land plants might have played an important role in the adaptation to terrestrial environments that impose UV and water stresses. To address this possibility, it is important to determine the function of *R2R3-MYB* genes in non-flowering plants.

Compared with Arabidopsis, the number of R2R3-MYBs was far less in *M. polymorpha*. Nevertheless, the classification of R2R3-MYBs by intron position and R2 structure showed that *M. polymorpha* keeps at least one member in most of the categories for R2R3-MYB families of land plants established by Du et al. [\(2015](#page-13-1)) (Table [1](#page-4-0)). This means that the common ancestor of liverworts and angiosperms had already acquired ancestral *R2R3-MYB* genes belonging to each category. The *R2R3-MYB*  genes of *M. polymorpha* showed low redundancy, and this species is a good example for examining the evolution of *R2R3-MYB* genes.

MpMYB14 and MpMYB02 were the most similar to SG4 members (Fig. S4a), and both conserved the C1 motif (Fig. S4b), suggesting that MpMYB14, MpMYB02, and extant SG4 members evolved from a common ancestor. [2013](#page-14-31)). This is reasonable because UV radiation would have been deleterious on the propagation of land plants since the terrestrialization of plants. Phenolic substances might have been important as a screen against UV-B and as an antioxidant.

In the biosynthesis of phenolics in flowering plants, many R2R3-MYB genes have been reported to be regulated by various environmental factors. Mp*MYB14* was shown to be regulated by UV-B, N-deficiency, and high salinity (Fig. [4](#page-10-0)a, b). The removal of sucrose slightly down-regulated the expression of Mp*MYB14* (Fig. [4](#page-10-0)b), indicating that sucrose also promotes the expression of Mp*MYB14*. [2005;](#page-14-32) Zhu [2001\)](#page-15-1). It is probable that Mp*MYB14* directly and indirectly regulates the response to oxidative stress caused by these environmental factors. This idea is

The responses of Mp*MYB14* to these environmental factors were quite similar to those of Arabidopsis *AtPAP1* (Cominelli et al. [2008](#page-13-25); Lea et al. [2007;](#page-14-33) Oh et al. [2011](#page-14-34); Teng et al. [2005\)](#page-15-1). It is probable that the common ancestor of Mp*MYB14* and *AtPAP1* had already developed the machinery against such stresses to overcome deleterious environmental factors that early land plants would have encountered. In the course of the evolution of land plants, these functions could have been shared between duplicated genes. This example is found in the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis in Arabidopsis. *AtPAP1* but not *AtPAP2* is up-regulated by UV-B (Cominelli et al. [2008\)](#page-13-25), whereas *AtPAP2* is up-regulated by N deficiency much more strongly than *AtPAP1* (Lea et al. [2007](#page-14-33)). *AtMYB112*, which plays a critical role during salt-induced anthocyanin biosynthesis, is up-regulated by salinity and high light stress but not by N deficiency and sucrose (Lotkowska et al. [2015\)](#page-14-35). It is interesting how the regulatory mechanism has developed during the evolution of land plants.

Although the regulation of Mp*MYB14* expression was similar to that of *AtPAP1*, the target genes did not seem to be conserved between MpMYB14 and AtPAP1. AtPAP1 regulates LBGs such as *DFR* and *ANS*, which is a common feature in SG6 of flowering plants (Quattrocchio et al. [2006](#page-14-36)). MpMYB14, however, was suggested to regulate EBGs such as *PAL, CHS*, and *CHI-like*, as well as *Dir*. RNA-seq experiments showed that no genes belonging to the 2OG-Fe(II) oxygenase superfamily were significantly up-regulated in Mp*MYB14*OX (Table S4), suggesting that MpMYB14 is not a dominant regulator of *flavanone 3-hydroxylase* and *ANS*, which belong to this superfamily. Interestingly, Mp*MYB14* was suggested to be a dominant regulator of *Dir* (Fig. [5\)](#page-11-0). In pterocarpan biosynthesis of legume, a dirigent-like protein catalyses a linkage between the B- and C-rings of 2′-hydroxyisoflavanol (Uchida et al. [2017](#page-15-1)). Riccionidin has a similar linkage between the B- and C-rings of anthocyanidin (Kunz et al. [1994](#page-14-15)). *Dir* might have a similar catalytic function. Elucidation of the biosynthetic pathway of riccionidin is important for further discussing the evolution of the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis in *M. polymorpha*.

The function of Mp*MYB02* appeared to be largely different from that of Mp*MYB14*. Overexpression of Mp*MYB02* increased compounds with the same m/z values as various marchantins (Fig. [2](#page-6-0); Table S2). In addition, the Mp*myb02* lines lost all these compounds. Mp*MYB02* may regulate a key step in the biosynthesis of marchantins. Identification of downstream genes of Mp*MYB02* may help to clarify the biosynthetic pathway of marchantin.

Marchantin has various biological activities such as anti-fungal, antibacterial and antioxidation activities and regulatory activities of various cellular processes (Asakawa et al. [2013\)](#page-13-26). Our results also showed that the total antiradical capacity was increased in Mp*MYB02*OX (Fig. [7](#page-11-1)). Mp*MYB02* may have important functions against biotic stress and oxidative stress. Kageyama et al. ([2015\)](#page-14-37) showed that the biosynthesis of marchantins was promoted by irradiation with UV-C but not with UV-B in *M. polymorpha*. UV-C is known to trigger the production of ROS (Gao et al. [2008\)](#page-13-27). The effect of UV-C and ROS on the expression of Mp*MYB02* is worthy of further study.

The expression of Mp*MYB02* was greatly reduced by treatment with 200 mM NaCl (Fig. [4b](#page-10-0)), but the significance of this regulation in the salt stress response of *M. polymorpha* remains unknown. Mp*MYB02* might have a suppressive effect on some steps in the salt stress response. Further molecular characterization of phenolic biosynthesis mediated by MpMYB14 and MpMYB02 should shed light on the origin and evolution of the mechanisms that regulate diverse secondary metabolism in land plants.
