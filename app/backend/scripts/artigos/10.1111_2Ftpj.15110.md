# **Summary**

Pathogens secrete effector proteins into host cells in order to suppress host immunity and promote pathogen virulence, although many features at the molecular interface of host-pathogen interactions remain to be characterized. In a yeast two-hybrid assay, we found that the *Pseudomonas syringae* effector HopZ1a interacts with the Arabidopsis transcriptional regulator Abscisic Acid Repressor 1 (ABR1). Further analysis revealed that ABR1 interacts with multiple *P. syringae* effectors, suggesting that it may be targeted as a susceptibility hub. Indeed, loss-offunction *abr1* mutants exhibit reduced susceptibility to a number of *P. syringae* strains. The ABR1 protein comprises a conserved APETALA2 (AP2) domain flanked by long regions of predicted structural disorder. We verified the DNA-binding activity of the AP2 domain and demonstrated that the disordered domains act redundantly to enhance DNA binding and to facilitate transcriptional activation by ABR1. Finally, we compared gene expression profiles from wild-type and *abr1* plants following inoculation with *P. syringae*, which suggested that the reduced susceptibility of *abr1* mutants is due to the loss of a virulence target rather than an enhanced immune response. These data highlight ABR1 as a functionally important component at the host-pathogen interface.

#### **Introduction**

Phytopathogen virulence derives from an ability to manipulate host metabolism in a manner that facilitates infection. When pathogens initially enter plant tissues, their presence is often betrayed by the detection of conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns such as bacterial flagellin, which are recognized by pattern recognition receptors that surveil the extracellular space (Peng *et al.*, 2018; Saijo *et al.*, 2018). The elicitation of pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) is associated with the production of antimicrobial compounds and reactive oxygen species, as well as callose deposition for reinforcement of the cell wall, all of which serve to prevent the establishment of an infection. To counteract this response, many pathogens have evolved collections of proteins known as effectors which are introduced directly into host cells and act to suppress PTI (Lewis *et al.*, 2009; Varden *et al.*, 2017; Khan *et al.*, 2018). The benefits of these effectors are host-dependent, however, because effector recognition by intracellular nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR)

proteins leads to effector-triggered immunity (ETI), a strong defense response that often culminates in localized plant cell death (Schreiber *et al.*, 2016; Lolle *et al.*, 2020). In turn, the evolution of additional effectors may facilitate the suppression of ETI. Such evolutionary dynamics have generated effectors with a wide range of targets in various subcellular locations, including structural components, proteins with signaling functions, and enzymes (Khan *et al.*, 2018).

As plants evolve under selection for reduced pathogen susceptibility, the impact of losing a virulence target will depend on the specific role of that protein in plant immunity. Many virulence targets are positive regulators of immunity that are inactivated by effectors, including several transcription factors that undergo effector-mediated inhibition, degradation, or relocalization (McLellan *et al.*, 2013; Block *et al.*, 2014; Gimenez-Ibanez *et al.*, 2014; Yang *et al.*, 2017; Qin *et al.*, 2018; Kim and Mudgett, 2019). Disruption of the genes encoding these proteins often confers increased susceptibility to infection, essentially mimicking the consequences of being targeted by effectors. Other proteins, classified as "susceptibility factors" (van Schie and Takken, 2014), function either as negative regulators of plant immunity or are activated in some manner to facilitate compatible plant-pathogen interactions. As such, the loss or mutation of susceptibility factors reduces the ability of a pathogen to cause disease. This enhanced resistance is inherited as a recessive trait, in contrast to NLRs which are genetically dominant and positively regulate plant immunity. One of the most well-studied susceptibility factors is encoded by *Mildew resistance locus o* (*Mlo)* genes. Loss-of-function *mlo* mutants exhibit non-race-specific resistance to infection by the powdery mildew pathogen *Blumeria graminis* f. sp. *hordei* in barley, and orthologous mutants in various other plant species also confer powdery mildew resistance (Kusch and Panstruga, 2017). Notably, Arabidopsis MLO2 interacts with the *Pseudomonas syringae* effector HopZ2, and is required for HopZ2-mediated virulence promotion (Lewis *et al.*, 2012). Host gene expression represents another potential susceptibility target, as illustrated by the binding of transcription activator-like (TAL) effectors to the promoters of host genes such as sugar transporters in order to promote pathogen proliferation (Verdier *et al.*, 2012). There is accumulating evidence that engineering promoters to eliminate TAL effector binding sites can enhance resistance to pathogen infection (Blanvillain-Baufumé *et al.*, 2017; Xu *et al.*, 2019). Transcription factors themselves can also be

susceptibility factors, as illustrated by the basic helix-loop-helix protein CHL1 from potato, which is indirectly upregulated by the *Phytophthora infestans* effector AVR2 to promote pathogen growth, and whose knock-down reduces susceptibility to infection (Turnbull *et al.*, 2017; Turnbull *et al.*, 2019). High-throughput interaction screens have identified additional transcriptional regulators that are directly targeted by effectors and which can be considered susceptibility factors (Weßling *et al.*, 2014).

One group of transcription factors with important roles in plant stress responses is characterized by a conserved APETALA2 (AP2) domain. This family is broadly divided into four subgroups with DNA-binding domains that are most similar to either dehydration-responsive element binding (DREB) proteins, ethylene response factor (ERF) proteins, APETALA2-like proteins (containing two AP2 motifs), or related to ABI3/VP1 (RAV1)-like proteins (which possess additional DNA-binding regions) (Kagaya *et al.*, 1999; Sakuma *et al.*, 2002; Gutterson and Reuber, 2004). Collectively, these proteins integrate numerous phytohormone signals to regulate developmental processes as well as responses to both abiotic and biotic stresses (Gutterson and Reuber, 2004; Xie *et al.*, 2019). The ERF subfamily is particularly responsive to invading pathogens, and the overexpression of ERF-type proteins is frequently associated with altered disease resistance phenotypes (Son *et al.*, 2012; Zhang *et al.*, 2016; Cao *et al.*, 2018; Zheng *et al.*, 2019). Given the contributions of these proteins to plant immunity, AP2/ERF transcription factors represent potentially important virulence targets for pathogen effectors.

While associations between pathogen effectors and host proteins are a key determinant of the outcome of host-pathogen interactions, both the composition of this molecular interface and its biological functions are not fully understood. The vast landscape of the effector-host protein interactome has been previously surveyed through selective pairwise screening of specific effectors against a defined group of Arabidopsis proteins (Mukhtar *et al.*, 2011; Weßling *et al.*, 2014). In this study, we sought to identify Arabidopsis proteins that interact with the *P. syringae* effector protein HopZ1a, an acetyltransferase whose enzymatic activity contributes both to its virulence function and its recognition by the NLR ZAR1 (Lewis *et al.*, 2010; Lee *et al.*, 2012; Lewis *et al.*, 2013). Notably, HopZ1a was not included in previous interactome screens, so the complete list of host proteins

targeted by this effector remains to be elucidated. Using large-scale yeast two-hybrid screens, we identified the AP2/ERF family transcriptional regulator Abscisic Acid Repressor 1 (ABR1) as a HopZ1a interactor, and verified this interaction *in planta*. Interestingly, Arabidopsis *abr1* T-DNA knockout plants are less susceptible to infection by *P. syringae* than wild-type plants. We further demonstrated that ABR1 interacts with multiple *P. syringae* effectors. Global transcriptional profiling revealed that ABR1 influences a wide range of biological processes that are manipulated by *P. syringae* in an effector-dependent manner. Altogether, these data indicate that ABR1 is a susceptibility factor that may be targeted by multiple effector proteins to promote pathogen virulence.

#### **Results**

## **Identification and initial characterization of ABR1**

A yeast two-hybrid interaction screen was previously performed using a Quantitative Interactor Screen Sequencing (QIS-Seq) approach, in which large-scale screens were coupled with nextgeneration sequencing (Lewis *et al.*, 2012). HopZ1a was included as one of the baits in this screen, and the Arabidopsis protein ABR1 (encoded by At5g64750) was identified as a putative HopZ1a interactor. We confirmed this association *in planta* by bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC), and noted that both wild-type HopZ1a and an acetyltransferase-deficient catalytic mutant (HopZ1aC216A) were capable of associating with ABR1 (Figure 1a, Figures S1 and S2a). Within the HopZ family of effectors, HopZ3 also interacted with ABR1, while HopZ2 did not. In addition, we used an ABR1-YFP fusion to confirm that ABR1 is primarily nuclear-localized, and that co-expression with effectors from the HopZ family does not significantly alter the subcellular localization of ABR1 (Figure S3).

To determine if *ABR1* had any functional relevance to plant immunity, we obtained an Arabidopsis T-DNA knockout line bearing a transposon insertion in the second exon of *ABR1*. Notably, *abr1* plants were phenotypically identical to wild-type plants in terms of growth and overall morphology (Figure S4). These plants were infiltrated with either *P. syringae* pv. *tomato* DC3000 (*Pto*DC3000), *P. cannabina* pv. *alisalensis* ES4326 (*Pcal*ES4326, formerly *P. syringae* pv. *maculicola* ES4326), or *P. syringae* pv. *cilantro* 0788-9 (*Pci*0788-9). Interestingly, the *abr1* mutant supported lower populations of all three strains compared to wild-type plants (Figure 1b). The highly virulent strains *Pto*DC3000 and *Pcal*ES4326 both multiplied to log 6.1 in wild-type plants, and their growth was reduced by 0.5 to 0.8 log colony-forming units (cfu) in *abr1* plants, representing a reduction of 59% and 85%, respectively. The weakly virulent strain *Pci*0788-9 achieved a population of log 4.2 on wild-type hosts, but consistently grew 0.25 log cfu less in *abr1* plants, which amounts to a 44% reduction in bacterial growth. We also examined plant responses to pathogen infection by quantifying *ABR1* gene expression in plants infiltrated with either 10 mM MgCl2, *Pto*DC3000, or *Pto*DC3000*hrcC*, which is unable to secrete any effector proteins. Relative to the MgCl2 control, *ABR1* expression was strongly induced by infiltration with *Pto*DC3000 (29-fold), while *Pto*DC3000*hrcC* had only a minor effect on *ABR1* expression (2.7-fold versus MgCl2) (Figure 1c). This suggests that the broad reductions in susceptibility to *Pseudomonas* spp. seen in *abr1* mutants are likely not due to an effect on PTI, and that the contribution of ABR1 to plant immunity is strongly influenced by pathogen effectors, perhaps as a target of manipulation to promote virulence.

#### **ABR1 interacts with multiple effectors**

Although we initially identified ABR1 as a HopZ1a interactor, it is important to note that *Pto*DC3000, *Pcal*ES4326, and *Pci*0788-9 all lack HopZ1a, so we hypothesized that additional effectors are involved in the manipulation of ABR1 at the host-pathogen interface. To test this idea, we conducted yeast two-hybrid experiments using pairwise mating to assess the interaction between ABR1 and 29 effectors from *Pto*DC3000. Relatively strong interactions were observed in yeast with the effectors HopAF1, HopP1, and HopQ1-1, while HopA1, HopB1, HopC1, HopE1, and HopN1 interacted weakly with ABR1 (Figure 2a). We used a GRAS family transcription factor (encoded by At3g46600) as a negative control to demonstrate the absence of non-specific interactions with the effector collection (Figure S5). We confirmed the ABR1-effector associations *in planta* by BiFC (Figure 2b, Figure S2b),

although some interactions were influenced by the orientation of the N- or C-terminal YFP fusions (nYFP and cYFP, respectively. Specifically, the ABR1-nYFP:HopQ1-1-cYFP association was relatively inconsistent (observed in four of eight experiments), and neither HopAF1-nYFP nor HopP1-nYFP reproducibly associated with ABR1-cYFP, despite convincing associations between the reciprocal fusions.

Structurally, ABR1 comprises an APETALA2 (AP2) domain flanked by regions that are predicted to be largely disordered (Figure 3a). The AP2 domain of ABR1 includes a conserved RAYD element that is thought to participate in protein-protein interactions as well as a DNA-binding YRG element containing a WAAEIRD sequence typical of ethylene response element-binding proteins (Okamuro *et al.*, 1997). Short stretches of potential secondary structure are predicted within the disordered regions, as are a number of possible protein-protein interaction interfaces. Several highly conserved short linear interaction motifs (SLiMs) were identified by the Eukaryotic Linear Motif resource (Gouw *et al.*, 2018) (Table S1), while the ANCHOR2 (Mészáros *et al.*, 2018), MoRFpred (Disfani *et al.*, 2012), and MoRF chibi (Malhis *et al.*, 2016) algorithms predicted seven longer interfaces known as molecular recognition factors (MoRFs) (Figure 3a). To determine which region(s) of ABR1 may be targeted by effectors, we tested HopZ1a against various ABR1 truncations in yeast two-hybrid assays. The truncations comprised either the N- or C-terminal disordered domain alone or in combination with the AP2 domain, as well as the AP2 domain alone. These analyses indicated that HopZ1a potentially binds most strongly to the N-terminal disordered region of ABR1 (amino acids 1-182), although other domains of ABR1 can interact with HopZ1a (Figure 3b, Figure S6).

#### **Functional characterization of ABR1 as a transcriptional regulator**

Since ABR1 has previously been studied as a transcriptional regulator (Pandey *et al.*, 2005), we sought to characterize its DNA-binding activity in yeast one-hybrid assays. Proteins containing an AP2 domain commonly bind to GC-rich motifs such as a GCC box (AGCCGCC), although a range of other DNA-binding sequences have been observed (Licausi *et al.*, 2013; Ohme-Takagi and Shinshi, 1995). As such, we prepared three yeast one-hybrid reporter constructs containing four copies of a GCC box (AGCCGCC), CGG box (ACGGCGG), or TCC box (ATCCTCC) within a minimal promoter upstream of the uracil biosynthetic gene *URA3*. These reporters were used to test three AP2 domain-containing proteins, including ABR1, Ethylene Response Factor1 (ERF1, At3g23240), and an uncharacterized protein belonging to ERF subfamily B-1 (designated here as ERF-B-1, At1g28160). Based on data from DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP-seq) experiments (O'Malley *et al.*, 2016), ERF1 is predicted to preferentially bind GCC box sequences, while CGG box sequences are preferentially bound by ERF-B-1. In yeast, ABR1 and ERF-B-1 appeared to exclusively activate the CGG box reporter, while ERF1 could activate *URA3* expression from either the GGC or CGG box promoter sequence (Figure 4a, Figure S7). This indicates that there is some flexibility in the sequence specificity of DNA binding in AP2 domain-containing proteins. We subsequently examined the ability of different ABR1 truncations to bind DNA and found that the N- or C-terminal disordered regions alone (ABR11-182 and ABR1245-391) were insufficient for DNA binding (Figure 4b, Figure S7). The AP2 domain alone (ABR1183-244) could weakly bind to the CGG box and activate *URA3* expression, but either the N- or C-terminal disordered region in combination with the AP2 domain (ABR11-244 or ABR1183-391) was capable of activating the reporter at levels similar to that observed with full-length ABR1. Interestingly, the truncation lacking the C-terminal disordered region (ABR11-244) also weakly bound the GCC box. This suggests that the disordered regions of ABR1 play important yet functionally redundant roles in DNA binding and may influence DNA-binding specificity.

It is important to note that the constructs used for these yeast one-hybrid assays expressed ABR1 as a fusion with the GAL4 transcriptional activation domain (GAL4 AD), and thus only provided an indication of DNA binding. To determine the ability of ABR1 itself to activate transcription, we expressed ABR1 in yeast one-hybrid assays without the GAL4 AD fusion. These assays showed that ABR1 is capable of mediating transcriptional activation (Figure 5a, Figure S8a). With the 4xCGG motif, the activity of a truncation lacking the C-terminal disordered region (ABR11-244) resembled that of full-length ABR1, while a truncation lacking the N-terminal disordered region (ABR1183-391) did exhibit some reductions in reporter activation (Figure 5a). Minimal transcriptional activation activity was observed for the AP2 domain alone (ABR1183-244). The disordered regions alone were not tested

due to their inability to bind DNA (Figure 4b). Nonetheless, these data revealed that the N- and Cterminal disordered regions are somewhat functionally redundant for mediating the activation of transcription in yeast. Interestingly, full-length ABR1 also weakly facilitated transcription from the 4xGCC motif, indicating that the previously tested GAL4 AD fusion likely influenced the ability of ABR1 to bind DNA. We also tested whether co-expression with HopZ1a would influence the activity of ABR1 in yeast, but found no impact on either DNA binding or transcriptional activation (Figure S9).

In order to validate our yeast one-hybrid observations, we prepared expression constructs for assessing DNA binding by ABR1 *in planta*. One set of constructs was designed to express putative transcriptional activators including ABR1, ERF1, and ERF-B-1, as well as GRAS1 as a negative control. A second group of constructs contained a firefly luciferase gene downstream of the same CGG/GCC/TCC box promoter motifs that were tested in the yeast one-hybrid assay. These motifs were embedded in a minimal *Cauliflower Mosaic Virus* 35S promoter, and a construct with the minimal promoter alone was included to measure baseline luciferase expression. The transcriptional activator and reporter constructs were introduced into *N. benthamiana* by agroinfiltration and luciferase expression was measured 48 h later. In concordance with the yeast one-hybrid data, we observed that ABR1 most strongly activated the luciferase reporter downstream of a 4xCGG motif (Figure 5b, Figure S8b). On the other hand, the *in planta* assay appears to be less sensitive than the yeast one-hybrid experiment, as ERF1 and ERF-B-1 only weakly activated luciferase gene expression, to a degree that was not statistically distinguishable from the minimal 35S negative control. Nonetheless, we confirmed that ABR1 can mediate the activation of reporter gene transcription. In addition, we evaluated the effects of HopZ1a co-expression on ABR1 function in the *in planta* assay. While the level of background expression from the minimal 35S promoter was consistently lower when ABR1 was co-expressed with HopZ1a, the background-adjusted luciferase reporter expression from a 4xCGG motif was statistically indistinguishable when ABR1 was expressed either with or without HopZ1a (Figure S9), which parallels our observations in yeast.

#### **Global transcriptomic analysis of the contribution of ABR1 to plant immunity**

The apparent function of ABR1 as a transcriptional regulator suggests that its manipulation by effectors might broadly impact patterns of gene expression in plants. To investigate this possibility, we inoculated Arabidopsis leaves with 1 x 10<sup>8</sup> cfu/mL suspensions of either wild-type *Pto*DC3000, a *Pto*DC3000 mutant that is unable to secrete effectors (*Pto*DC3000Δ*hrcC*), or 10 mM MgCl2 as a control. Tissues were collected at 12 h post-inoculation for gene expression profiling by Tag-Seq, a 3'-end RNA sequencing approach (Meyer *et al.*, 2011; Lohman *et al.*, 2016). To minimize artefacts in data analysis, we aligned sequencing reads to the Arabidopsis genome using either STAR or HISAT2 and identified differentially expressed genes with either the edgeR or limma-voom algorithm. The combination of the two aligners and two differential expression algorithms generated four datasets, and final lists of differentially expressed genes were assembled as the consensus between these four datasets. When we compared gene expression in inoculated tissues to control tissues infiltrated with 10 mM MgCl2, 4,115 genes exhibited statistically significant changes in expression under one or more conditions, including 1,950 upregulated and 2,206 downregulated genes (Figure 6), with an overlap of 41 genes that were either up- or downregulated depending on the experimental condition.

In the absence of infection, only a single gene (At5g44575) was identified as being differentially expressed between wild-type and *abr1* plants (Table S2), although we did not consistently observe this difference in RT-qPCR experiments. This result may be due to low ABR1 expression in untreated plants (Figure 1c), but it also suggests that the loss of ABR1 in *abr1* plants does not significantly alter basal gene expression patterns, and instead may prime plant immunity to facilitate more dramatic transcriptional changes following pathogen exposure. One welldocumented example of such priming is the phenomenon of systemic acquired resistance (SAR), in which localized infection stimulates the release of one or more signaling molecules to distal tissues, resulting in reduced susceptibility to infection throughout the plant (Shine *et al.*, 2019; Klessig *et al.*, 2018). Gene expression data are available from RNA-seq analyses of SAR-primed distal Arabidopsis leaves following inoculation with *P. syringae* (Bernsdorff *et al.*, 2016; Baum *et al.*, 2019). We compared two SAR datasets to our results from wild-type or *abr1* mutant plants inoculated with wild-type *Pto*DC3000, and found a modest correlation (r = 0.56-0.61) between the expression values

(Figure S10). The two SAR expression datasets showed a stronger association with each other (r = 0.84), despite major differences in experimental protocols. For both of these SAR gene lists, gene expression patterns between wild-type and *abr1* mutants were highly correlated (r = 0.95), implying that differential expression of typical SAR-primed genes does not fully explain the reduced susceptibility phenotype of the *abr1* mutant. Immune responses can also be primed by exogenously applied chemicals such as beta-aminobutyric acid (BABA), and BABA-primed genes have previously been identified from BABA-treated, *P. syringae*-inoculated tissues (Tsai *et al.*, 2011). The expression patterns of the BABA-primed genes did not correlate with those from wild-type or *abr1* mutant plants inoculated with *Pto*DC3000 (r = -0.34), while these genes were very similarly expressed in wild-type versus *abr1* plants (r = 0.97) (Figure S11). We also examined specific marker genes of different aspects of plant immunity, including PTI, SAR, and phytohormone signaling, yet found few differences in gene expression patterns between wild-type and *abr1* plants (Table S3). Altogether, this suggests that the reduced susceptibility of the *abr1* mutant is not significantly associated with the induction of a stronger immune response.

Previous characterization of ABR1 was performed in the context of plant responses to ABA and abiotic stimuli (Pandey *et al.*, 2005). To evaluate whether ABA-related gene expression is altered in *abr1* plants following *P. syringae* inoculation, we derived a gene list from a previously published comprehensive survey of ABA-responsive genes (Lumba *et al.*, 2014). We plotted log2 fold changes in gene expression following ABA treatment against the corresponding data from either wild-type or *abr1* mutants following *P. syringae* inoculation (Figure S12). Calculated correlations were relatively low (r = 0.48-0.50), while expression profiles of these ABA-responsive genes in wildtype and *abr1* plants were highly similar (r = 0.97). Broadly, therefore, the pathogen resistance phenotype of *abr1* is likely not derived from altered ABA responses.

Given that ABR1 is a transcriptional regulator with a conserved AP2 DNA-binding motif, we hypothesized that the promoters of genes exhibiting ABR1-dependent differential expression should be enriched for ABR1-binding motifs. A consensus motif for ABR1 was previously determined by DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP-seq) from Arabidopsis seedlings, including a variant approach using amplified libraries (ampDAP-seq) to identify binding sites in the absence of occluding methylation modifications (O'Malley *et al.*, 2016) (Figure S13a). We compared lists of ABR1-bound genes from the DAP-seq and ampDAP-seq datasets to genes that we observed to be differentially expressed in an *ABR1*- or *abr1*-dependent manner (Figure S13b). None of our gene lists exhibited statistically significant enrichment or depletion of ABR1-binding motifs (Table S4). Since the (amp)DAP-seq experiments recorded transcription factor binding throughout the genome, we used a genome-wide position weight matrix scanner (PWMScan) to computationally search for ABR1 binding sites in sequences 1000 nt upstream of every coding sequence in the genome. Relative to this "promoter-only" list, the lists of *ABR1*- or *abr1*-dependent genes were still not enriched/depleted in ABR1-bound sequences (Table S4). At the time point of 12 hpi, therefore, changes in the binding of ABR1 to the consensus motif cannot fully explain the altered susceptibility phenotype, which is in agreement with the results of our yeast one-hybrid and *in planta* reporter gene activation assays (Figure S9).

#### **Analyses of ABR1-dependent transcriptional responses**

After comparing our data to other datasets, we performed additional calculations to quantitatively differentiate the responses of wild-type and *abr1* plants to *P. syringae*. For a SAR experiment with various combinations of local and distal leaf inoculations, Bernsdorff *et al*. (2016) defined genes as primed in distal leaves if the difference between normalized read counts of localinoculated/distalinoculated and localinoculated/distalmock treatments is statistically greater than the difference between localmock/distalinoculated and localmock/distalmock treatments. The analogous calculation for our experiment would be: (*abr1*inoculated – *abr1*mock) versus (WTinoculated – WTmock), which would identify genes that we refer to as APETRA (Altered Pathogen-Elicited Transcriptional Response in *abr1*) genes. This analysis essentially enables a statistical comparison of gene expression values between host genotypes while correcting for any variations due to genetic background or priming treatment. A calculated difference of greater than zero would result if a gene is either more strongly upregulated in *abr1* or less strongly downregulated in *abr1* versus wildtype, and vice versa for differences of less than zero. Using a Mann-Whitney two-sided U test

(α=0.005), we identified 310 APETRA genes following wild-type *P. syringae* inoculation in *abr1* relative to wild-type (Table S5). For *Pto*DC3000Δ*hrcC*-inoculated plants, 283 genes were considered statistically different by these same criteria (Table S6), including 79 genes that were also identified in the wild-type *Pto*DC3000 analysis. After using this list to filter for effector-dependent genes, we found 110 genes with calculated differences greater than zero (">0") and 121 genes with differences less than zero ("<0") (Table S7). To functionally describe these effector-dependent APETRA genes, Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment was assessed using the online Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) (Huang *et al.*, 2009; Huang *et al.*, 2009). In the Biological Process category, the ">0" gene set was enriched for a small number of genes associated with response to karrikin and endoplasmic reticulum organization (Table 1). Several of the proteins encoded by the ">0" genes were localized to the chloroplast or mitochondrion. The "<0" APETRA genes represented a wide range of metabolic functions, with no enrichment for a particular subcellular location. Immunity-related GO terms were completely absent from these results.

We performed additional GO term enrichment analyses on specific subsets of differentially expressed genes. The primary focus was on genes with ABR1- and effector-dependent expression patterns, as well as genes uniquely differentially expressed in an *abr1* background in an effectordependent manner. In general terms, the ABR1-/effector-dependent upregulated group was dominated by DNA-binding proteins and hydrolase enzymes, while the downregulated gene list was enriched for protein folding and glycosyltransferase activities (Table 2). Proteins associated with ubiquitination processes were frequently encoded by the genes that were upregulated in an *abr1*- /effector-dependent manner (Table 3). Genes downregulated in this context encoded proteins with a diverse array of activities in several cellular compartments, including various components of the cytoskeleton.

At a global level, the quantitative differences between gene expression profiles of wild-type and *abr1* plants are relatively subtle. This is particularly evident in our calculation of effectordependent APETRA genes, which constitute just 5.6% of all differentially expressed genes (231 of 4,115). Furthermore, within specific host genotype/inoculum combinations, the proportion of differentially expressed genes with log2 fold changes beyond -2 or +2 only ranges between 12 and

25% (Table S2, Figure S14). Nonetheless, we sought to validate the expression of specific genes by RT-qPCR. We replicated our initial experiment by infiltrating wild-type and *abr1* mutant Arabidopsis plants with wild-type *Pto*DC3000, *Pto*DC3000Δ*hrcC*, or 10 mM MgCl2 as a control. To also investigate the specific influence of HopZ1a on gene expression, we expressed this effector in an otherwise effectorless *Pto*DC3000 polymutant (*Pto*DC3000 D36E) (Wei *et al.*, 2015). In order to avoid the activation of cell death responses arising from HopZ1a recognition, we infiltrated these bacteria into either *zar1* or *abr1zar1* mutant plants. We ultimately focused on three genes that exhibited reproducibly altered ABR1-dependent gene expression patterns (Figure 7). These genes encode a nuclear-localized MAP kinase phosphatase (At3g06110), a RING/FYVE/PHD-type zinc finger family protein (At3g52100), and a late embryogenesis-abundant family protein (At3g02480). Following exposure to *Pto*DC3000, both At3g06110 and At3g52100 were expressed at lower levels in *abr1* plants relative to wild-type, and this phenotype was effector-dependent due to the absence of such a difference following inoculation with *Pto*DC3000Δ*hrcC*. For At3g02480, the difference in expression between wild-type and *abr1* plants was not statistically significant, but this gene was more strongly upregulated in *abr1zar1* double mutants in response to *Pto*DC3000 D36E carrying *hopZ1a*. Interestingly, At3g06110 and At3g52100 lacked differential expression phenotypes specific to HopZ1a. As such, it appears that the full complement of *Pto*DC3000 effectors, which does not include HopZ1a, can influence ABR1-dependent host gene expression in a different manner than HopZ1a alone.

# **Discussion**

We initially identified ABR1 as a HopZ1a interactor whose absence in Arabidopsis was associated with reduced susceptibility to various *Pseudomonas* spp. The breadth of this immune phenotype could suggest an enhancement of some aspect of PTI, although our observations do not support this hypothesis. Firstly, *ABR1* gene expression is strongly induced following inoculation with wild-type *Pto*DC3000, while the type III secretion-deficient *Pto*DC3000Δ*hrcC* mutant only slightly induces *ABR1* (Figure 1c). In addition, the expression of several established PTI marker genes does not differ

substantially between wild-type and *abr1* plants after inoculation with either wild-type or the Δ*hrcC* mutant of *Pto*DC3000 (Table S3). As such, it is more probable that ABR1 represents a susceptibility factor that is bound by effectors to promote pathogen virulence.

The interaction of ABR1 with multiple effectors suggests that this protein is not just a susceptibility factor, but a susceptibility hub. Such interaction hubs have also been identified by recent large-scale yeast two-hybrid screens in which effectors from *P. syringae*, the oomycete *Hyaloperonspora arabidopsidis*, and the fungus *Golovinomyces orontii* were tested against a panel of Arabidopsis proteins (Mukhtar *et al.*, 2011; Weßling *et al.*, 2014). These studies indicated that multiple effectors from the same pathogen converge on a relatively small set of host proteins, and that these proteins are often targeted by effectors from different pathogens. Experimental validation of these interactions further revealed that disruption of the genes encoding highly targeted host proteins was often associated with significantly altered susceptibility to pathogen infection. Interestingly, a more focused yeast two-hybrid screen of 56 *P. syringae* effectors against 97 Arabidopsis ABA-regulated proteins found that several AP2 domain-containing proteins interact with multiple effectors (Cao *et al.*, 2018). Notably, none of these screens included ABR1, which we propose as an additional convergent target of *P. syringae* effectors.

While the AP2 domain is the only conserved sequence feature of ABR1, flanking regions of predicted structural disorder contribute significantly and redundantly to the function of this protein. The N-terminal disordered region appears to interact strongly with HopZ1a, although other sequences within ABR1 also facilitate interactions with this effector (Figure 3b). In yeast one-hybrid assays, we observed that the deletion of either the N- or C-terminal disordered region did not significantly impair DNA binding by ABR1, although the absence of the C-terminal disordered region appeared to relax ABR1 DNA-binding specificity somewhat (Figure 4b). Disordered regions are commonly found in transcription factors (Liu *et al.*, 2006; Minezaki *et al.*, 2006), where they regulate transcription factor function by mediating interactions with other transcription factors and proteins (Maiti *et al.*, 2019) as well as serving as platforms for regulatory post-translational modifications such as acetylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and sumoylation (Bah and Forman-Kay, 2016; Krois *et al.*, 2018). Disordered domains can also associate with the DNA-binding domain to limit

non-specific binding to DNA (De *et al.*, 2014; Krois *et al.*, 2018). Predicted regions of disorder often contain segments that can adopt a more defined structure upon interaction with other proteins. These interaction interfaces can be short, highly conserved sequences (SLiMs) or longer stretches (MoRFs) (Vacic *et al.*, 2007; Weatheritt and Gibson, 2012; Cumberworth *et al.*, 2013). Computational analyses of the ABR1 sequence predicted multiple SLiMs and MoRFs, which could provide platforms for interactions with several different effectors.

Based on analyses of specific ABA signaling genes, ABR1 was initially proposed to be a transcriptional repressor (Pandey *et al.*, 2005), although some evidence of weak transcriptional activation activity was later found using a CACCG DNA-binding motif (Li *et al.*, 2016). We demonstrated that ABR1 is capable of either directly or indirectly mediating the activation of transcription both in yeast and *in planta*. The disordered regions of ABR1 were also required for transcriptional activation in yeast one-hybrid assays. In combination with the AP2 domain, both disordered regions were capable of inducing expression of the *URA3* reporter gene, although the Nterminal region conferred stronger induction than the C-terminal disordered region (Figure 5a). Transcriptional activation domains are often less structured (Liu *et al.*, 2006), and multiple activation domains are occasionally observed within the same protein (Arnold *et al.*, 2018).

Intriguingly, our yeast one-hybrid assays revealed some variation in DNA-binding specificity for ABR1. When fused to GAL4 AD, ABR1 specifically bound a 4xCGG motif (Figure 4a), while ABR1 without GAL4 AD could bind both 4xCGG and 4xGCC motifs (Figure 5a). For other transcriptional regulators, conditional flexibility in binding specificity can be modulated by post-translational modifications, interactions with co-factors, or the formation of homo- or heteromeric protein complexes (Halder and Carroll, 2001; Shi *et al.*, 2007; Ea and Baltimore, 2009; Slattery *et al.*, 2011; Hosokawa *et al.*, 2015; Jolma *et al.*, 2015; Smaczniak *et al.*, 2017; Sri Theivakadadcham *et al.*, 2019), and the presence of GAL4 AD may have interfered with one or more of these phenomena. At the same time, we hypothesized that the interaction of ABR1 with effector proteins might influence the DNA-binding specificity and/or transcriptional activation behavior of ABR1. In reality, these phenotypes were unchanged in yeast one-hybrid and *in planta* assays that included HopZ1a coexpression, although a limited number of promoter motifs were tested. In addition, a consensus

ABR1-binding motif was neither enriched nor depleted in the promoters of genes exhibiting effectordependent differential expression between wild-type and *abr1* mutant plants (Table S4). These genes also did not share an obvious promoter motif that could arise if ABR1 had bound a different motif in an effector-independent manner. The results of this sequence-based analysis may be a consequence of the time point used, such that most differentially expressed genes represent indirect, downstream targets of ABR1. At this point, however, it is not clear how effectors might specifically impact the function of ABR1 as a transcriptional regulator.

In addition to the influence of effectors, we sought to more broadly characterize ABR1 in the context of plant immunity by performing transcriptional profiling of wild-type and *abr1* mutant plants following inoculation with *Pto*DC3000. We initially speculated that the loss of ABR1 may prime plants to execute a stronger immune response only upon exposure to an invading pathogen. Based on comparisons to datasets from pathogen- or chemical-induced priming experiments, as well as several key marker genes of plant immunity, this does not appear to be the case. The alternative hypothesis is that, as a susceptibility target, the absence of ABR1 would render a pathogen unable to manipulate this protein and thus deprived of a key mechanism for promoting its growth in plants. In this case, it would be most useful to examine genes whose differential expression is effectordependent in either a wild-type or *abr1* mutant background. Genes whose expression is altered in an ABR1-/effector-dependent manner may represent molecular events downstream of the targeting of ABR1 in wild-type plants. In an *abr1* background, genes that are differentially regulated in an effector-dependent manner may reflect effector off-targets whose downstream effects serve to impair virulence, and whose influence is unmasked only by the absence of a significant virulence target. In either host background, general biotic stress responses likely account for some of the effector-dependent differential gene expression. While it is tempting to speculate about the specific pathways and processes that could alter disease susceptibility in *abr1* mutants, the reality could be more complex due to the interaction of ABR1 with multiple effectors, and the possibility that reduced susceptibility to infection may arise as a cumulative effect of various subtle transcriptional changes in the *abr1* background. These challenges extend to *in planta* pathogen virulence assays, in which *P. syringae* strains lacking multiple ABR1-interacting effectors or expressing a single effector

exhibit insufficient levels of growth to statistically resolve the difference in susceptibility between wild-type and *abr1* mutant plants. Nonetheless, future studies will work towards a more mechanistic exploration of the ABR1-effector interaction.

Overall, we have demonstrated that ABR1 is a susceptibility factor that interacts with several *P. syringae* effectors, and that the loss of ABR1 in Arabidopsis confers reduced susceptibility to bacterial infection. These results are congruent with previous observations of reduced susceptibility to *P. syringae* infection in knockouts of highly targeted proteins such as the COP9 signalosome complex subunit 5A (CSN5A), the transcription factor TCP14, and the cell cycle regulator APC8 (Weßling *et al.*, 2014). Susceptibility factors are acknowledged as an important avenue for developing disease-resistant field crops with potentially durable phenotypes (Engelhardt *et al.*, 2018; Zaidi *et al.*, 2018). The modification of some susceptibility factors can have negative pleiotropic effects such as reduced plant fitness, but the apparent absence of such phenotypes in *abr1* plants suggest that it may be worth examining in a field setting.

#### **Experimental procedures**

#### **Cloning**

In general, PCR amplification of sequences for cloning was performed using Phusion High-Fidelity Polymerase (New England BioLabs, Inc., Ipswich, MA). All oligonucleotides were supplied by Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc. (Coralville, IA) and are listed in Table S8. Following electrophoresis, PCR amplicons were purified using EZ-10 Spin Column Gel Extraction Kits (Bio Basic Inc., Amherst, NY). All restriction enzymes, as well as T4 DNA Ligase for ligations, were supplied by New England BioLabs, Inc. All constructs were verified by sequencing.

Constructs for yeast two-hybrid analysis were prepared in the DupLEX-A system (OriGene Technologies, Inc., Rockford, MD) using the vectors pEG202 and pJG4-5 to express bait and prey proteins, respectively. A collection of 21 *Pto*DC3000 effectors in pEG202 was obtained as a generous gift from Dr. Darrell Desveaux (University of Toronto, Canada). An additional eight effectors from

*Pto*DC3000 were cloned into the *Eco*RI and *Xho*I sites of pEG202, and *hopD1* was introduced into this vector at the *Xho*I site alone. The *Eco*RI and *Xho*I sites of pJG4-5 were also used for cloning *ABR1* and all truncations.

The cloning of *ABR1* for yeast one-hybrid experiments required multiple vector modifications. Initially, *ABR1* was cloned into the *Nde*I and *Bam*HI sites of pGADT7 AD (Takara Bio USA Inc., Mountain View, CA), but yeast transformants could not be recovered using this construct, potentially due to the ADH1 promoter (pADH1) driving high levels of *ABR1* expression. To address this issue, a *GAL4 AD-1xHA tag-ABR1* fusion was amplified from the pGADT7 AD construct and a methionine-regulated promoter (pMet) was separately amplified from the yeast three-hybrid vector pBridge (Takara Bio USA Inc.). These two amplicons were joined by stitching PCR and cloned into pGADT7 AD at *Pac*I and *Bam*HI sites, which allowed the replacement of most of the pADH1 sequence of pGADT7 AD and the successful recovery of yeast transformants. To clone ABR1 in the absence of the GAL4 AD, a *Pac*I-pMet-*Xba*I-*Not*I fragment and a *Not*I-ABR1-*Xho*I fragment were introduced into the *Pac*I/*Xho*I sites of pGADT7 AD by a three-way ligation. An *Xba*I-6xHA-*Spe*I fragment was cloned into an *Xba*I site upstream of ABR1 to facilitate protein immunodetection. All ABR1 truncations were cloned into these vectors in the same manner.

A separate vector backbone was derived from pGADT7 AD to enable the co-expression of *ABR1* and *P. syringae* effectors. Primers for stitching PCRs were designed to prepare a *pADH1- 1xFlag tag-MCS-tADH* amplicon using pBridge as a template, where *MCS* corresponds to "multiple cloning site I" and *tADH* is a transcription termination signal from the *S. cerevisiae ADH1* gene. This amplicon was cloned into unique *Nhe*I and *Not*I sites in pGADT7 AD, located at nucleotides 4,495 and 4,517, respectively, in a region devoid of functionally important features between the LEU2 coding sequence and the pUC replication origin of the vector. To simplify subsequent cloning steps, this modified pGADT7 AD was digested with *Pac*I and *Xho*I, liberating a *pADH1-GAL4 AD-MCS* fragment that was replaced with a linker comprising: *Pac*I-GAL4BD primer (reverse orientation)-*Xho*I. We refer to this doubly modified vector as pGADT7 AD-II. *Pto*DC3000 effectors were cloned into pGADT7 AD-II first, generally by ligating an effector amplicon digested with *Eco*RI and *Xho*I into the *Eco*RI and *Sal*I sites of pGADT7 AD-II so that the *Xho*I-*Sal*I junction is not recleavable. The sole

exception here was *hopZ1a*, which was cloned into pGADT7 AD-II using *Eco*RI and *Bam*HI. The second stage of cloning involved the introduction of *pMet-6xHA-ABR1* sequences at the *Pac*I and *Xho*I sites of pGADT7 AD-II:effector constructs.

Three reporter constructs with different promoter sequences were prepared for yeast onehybrid analyses. Oligonucleotide duplexes comprising four copies of an ACGGCGG, AGCCGCC, or ATCCTCC motif separated by eight-nucleotide spacers were cloned into the *Pst*I and *Eco*RI sites of pYoh366 (Qian *et al.*, 2002).

Constructs for BiFC experiments were cloned into the *Xho*I and *Stu*I sites of previously described pBD-derived vectors (Lewis *et al.*, 2014). These vectors provide dexamethasone-inducible expression of proteins with C-terminal fusions to a 1xHA tag, plus either full-length YFP, nYFP (Nterminal 155 amino acids), or cYFP (C-terminal 87 amino acids). All constructs were introduced into *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* strain GV2260.

*In planta* dual luciferase experiments required two different sets of constructs. For expression of putative DNA-binding proteins, pCAMBIA1301 was first modified by introducing fragments containing *Xba*I-*Cauliflower Mosaic Virus* (CaMV) 35S promoter-*Spe*I-*EcoR*I and *EcoR*I-*Pac*I-NOS terminator-*Pst*I into the *Xba*I/*Pst*I sites of pCAMBIA1301 via a three-way ligation. An *Xba*I-6xHA-*Spe*I fragment was cloned into the *Spe*I site within this region such that a start codon preceded the 6xHA sequence. Genes encoding ABR1, ERF1, ERF-B-1, and GRAS1 were cloned into the *Spe*I and *Pac*I sites of this modified vector. Constructs for HopZ1a co-expression also included a *BamH*I-CaMV 35S-HopZ1a-NOS-*Kpn*I fragment generated by stitching PCR and introduced via *BamH*I/*Kpn*I sites. For luciferase reporter constructs, we obtained a modified pCAMBIA1302 vector, pC1302-pPIL1:LUC-35S:RLUC (Zhang *et al.*, 2013), as a generous gift from Dr. Yu Zhang and Dr. Peter Quail (University of California, Berkeley, USA). The pPIL1 promoter upstream of a firefly luciferase gene was excised via *Pst*I/*Sal*I digestion and replaced with an oligonucleotide duplex that includes a multiple cloning site upstream of a minimal CaMV 35S promoter (Li *et al.*, 2011): *Pst*I-*EcoR*I-mini35S. Oligonucleotide duplexes comprising four copies of an ACGGCGG, AGCCGCC, or ATCCTCC motif separated by eightnucleotide spacers were cloned into the *Pst*I and *Eco*RI sites of this vector. As a positive control, the pPIL1 promoter of pC1302-pPIL1:LUC-35S:RLUC was replaced with a full-length CaMV 35S promoter.

#### **Yeast assays**

All constructs for yeast two-hybrid analysis were transformed into yeast using Frozen-EZ Yeast Transformation II reagents (Zymo Research Corp., Irvine, CA) to introduce pEG202 constructs into yeast strain RFY206 (mating type A), while pJG4-5 constructs were introduced into strain EGY48 (mating type α). Transformants were recovered on Synthetic Defined (SD)+Glucose media lacking histidine and uracil to select for colonies containing pEG202 constructs, or lacking tryptophan to select for pJG4-5 transformants. Yeast matings were performed in YPDA media at 30°C overnight, followed by two rounds of diploid selection on SD+Glucose-HisUraTrp plates at 30°C overnight. To assay for protein-protein interactions, diploid yeast cells were first resuspended in 150 μL of sterile distilled water and adjusted to an OD600 of 2, followed by three serial 1:5 dilutions in water. Five microliters of each dilution were spotted on SD+Glucose-HisUraTrpLeu and SD+Galactose+Raffinose-HisUraTrpLeu plates, with yeast growth indicating the activation of a LEU2 reporter. Images of yeast plates were generally captured after three days of growth at 30°C. The *Pto*DC3000 effector HopF2 and its chaperone ShcF2 were used as positive controls because they are known to strongly interact (Shan *et al.*, 2004).

Yeast one-hybrid constructs were sequentially introduced into yeast strain W303a using Frozen-EZ Yeast Transformation II reagents (Zymo Research Corp.). Generally, reporter constructs were transformed first and recovered on SD+Glucose-Trp media. Protein (co-)expression constructs were subsequently introduced into these transformants and plated on SD+Glucose-LeuTrp to select for both plasmids. For the one-hybrid assays, yeast cells were resuspended in 150 μL of sterile distilled water and adjusted to an OD600 of 2, followed by three serial 1:5 dilutions in water. Five microliters of each dilution were spotted on SD+Glucose-LeuTrp and SD+Glucose-LeuTrpUra, with yeast growth indicating the activation a URA3 reporter. Images of yeast plates were generally captured after three days of growth at 30°C.

#### **Western blotting**

Protein expression in yeast was evaluated following shaking incubation of liquid cultures at 30°C overnight in SD+Glucose-HisUra (pEG202 constructs), SD+Galactose+Raffinose-Trp (pJG4-5), or SD+Glucose-Leu (pGADT7 derivatives) media. Cultures were processed for protein extraction according to a lithium acetate/sodium hydroxide pre-treatment protocol described by (Zhang *et al.*, 2011). Samples were resolved on 10% or 12% discontinuous SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and detected by α-HA (Roche 12CA5, Indianapolis, IN), α-Flag (Sigma-Aldrich F1804, St. Louis, MO), α-GAL4-TA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology SC1663, Dallas, TX), α-GFP (Roche 11814460001) or α-LexA antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology SC7544) in combination with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rat (Santa Cruz Biotechnology SC2065) or goat antimouse (Santa Cruz Biotechnology SC2005) secondary antibodies.

#### **Plant materials and bacterial inoculations**

Arabidopsis *abr1* (SALK\_012151C) and *zar1* (SALK\_013297C) T-DNA knockout lines were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center. The two lines were crossed to generate the *abr1zar1* double mutant. For plant growth, surface-sterilized Arabidopsis seeds were placed on moist Sunshine #1 soil (Sun Gro Horticulture, Agawam, MA) amended with 20:20:20 fertilizer, stratified for three days at 4°C, then moved to a growth room with a 9-h photoperiod (130 E∙m-2s -1) at constant 22°C and 60% RH. Surface-sterilized *N. benthamiana* seeds were placed on moist Sunshine Mix #1 soil (Sun Gro) amended with 14:14:14 fertilizer and grown under the same conditions without prior stratification.

Bacterial growth assays in Arabidopsis were performed using *P. syringae* pv. *tomato* DC3000 (*Pto*DC3000), *P. cannabina* pv. *alisalensis* ES4326 (*Pcal*ES4326, formerly *P. syringae* pv. *maculicola* ES4326), and *P. syringae* pv. *cilantro* 0788-9 (*Pci*0788-9). Bacteria were grown overnight at 28°C on King's B agar media (King *et al.*, 1954) supplemented with 50 g mL-1 rifampicin (*Pto*DC3000 and *Pci*0788-9) or 300 g mL-1 streptomycin (*Pcal*ES4326). For inoculations, bacteria were resuspended in 10 mM MgCl2, adjusted to OD600 = 0.0002 (1 x 10<sup>5</sup> cfu/mL), and the suspensions were infiltrated

into the leaves of six-week-old Arabidopsis plants using a needleless 1 mL syringe. Quantification of *in planta* bacterial growth was carried out as previously described.

For global transcriptome analysis, the leaves of six-week-old wild-type or *abr1* Arabidopsis plants were infiltrated with 10 mM MgCl2, or either wild-type *Pto*DC3000 or *Pto*DC3000Δ*hrcC* adjusted to OD600 = 0.2 (1 x 10<sup>8</sup> cfu/mL). Tissues were harvested at 12 h post-infiltration for RNA extraction.

To prepare inocula for BiFC assays, Agrobacterium strains were grown overnight at 28°C on Luria-Bertani agar media supplemented with 100 g mL-1 rifampicin and 50 g mL-1 kanamycin. Bacteria were resuspended in 1 mL of induction media (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM 2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid (MES) [pH 5.6], and 150 μM acetosyringone), diluted with induction media so that each strain was at OD600 = 0.45, and incubated at room temperature for three hours. For all inoculations, leaves of five- to seven-week-old *N. benthamiana* plants were pierced with a 22 gauge needle prior to infiltration with a 1 mL needleless syringe. Protein expression was induced by spraying plants with 20 M dexamethasone at 24 h post-inoculation and protein interaction was generally assessed 24 h after dexamethasone treatment.

Agrobacterium strains for *in planta* luciferase assays were prepared in a similar manner. Here, strains carried constructs for the expression of putative DNA-binding proteins (ABR1, ERF1, ERF-B-1, or GRAS1) or reporter constructs containing a firefly luciferase gene downstream of either a minimal CaMV 35S promoter ("m35S") or m35S plus four copies of an ACGGCGG, AGCCGCC, or ATCCTCC sequence. Agrobacteria were resuspended in induction media and combinations of DNAbinding proteins and reporter constructs were prepared such that each strain was at OD600 = 0.5. As a positive control, we included a reporter construct with a firefly luciferase gene driven by a fulllength CaMV 35S promoter. Inocula were introduced into the leaves of five- to seven-week-old *N. benthamiana* plants by piercing leaves with a 22 gauge needle prior to infiltration with a 1 mL needleless syringe. At 48 hpi, six leaf discs were collected for each sample with a #3 cork borer (1.5 cm<sup>2</sup> total), placed in a microfuge tube along with a 3 mm glass bead, and frozen in liquid nitrogen.

#### **Confocal microscopy**

Samples were prepared for BiFC analysis by using a #4 cork borer to collect leaf discs from infiltrated areas. Leaf discs were placed in a 5 mL syringe and infiltrated with water, then mounted on microscope slides. Images were captured using a Leica SP8 confocal laser-scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Inc., Buffalo Grove, IL) equipped with a 40x water-immersion objective (HC PL APO CS2 40x/1.10 WATER). A 514 nm argon laser line was used for YFP excitation, and fluorescence was recorded within a specific emission window of 520 to 600 nm. Leica Application Suite X software was used for image processing, including minor adjustments to normalize brightness and contrast across images.

#### **In planta luciferase assays**

Luciferase activity was quantified using reagents from the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI) and a protocol that was previously optimized for plant tissues (Nassuth *et al.*, 2014). Briefly, samples were homogenized in a Mini-Beadbeater-96 (BioSpec Products, Inc., Bartlesville, OK), resuspended in 300 μL of Passive Lysis Buffer, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Samples were centrifuged at 13,000 x g for 10 min, and the supernatant was diluted 75-fold in Passive Lysis Buffer. Ten microliters of this dilution was mixed with 50 μL of Luciferase Reagent II in a solid white flat-bottom 96-well plate, then luminescence was measured using a Tecan Spark 10M plate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). Fifty microliters of Stop & Glo Reagent was then added to each well and luminescence was measured again. The ratio of the first reading (firefly luciferase) to the second reading (*Renilla* luciferase) provides a normalized readout of reporter gene transcription.

#### **RNA-sequencing and expression validation**

TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA) was used to extract RNA from Arabidopsis leaves, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Samples were treated with DNase and further purified using RNA Clean and Concentrator kits (Zymo Research Corp.) and RNA quality was assessed on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). Library preparation and sequencing were performed by the University of California – Davis Genome Center, yielding 90 bp single-end reads from an Illumina Hiseq 4000 sequencer (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA).

To evaluate the expression of specific genes, cDNA was generated with Superscript III reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and anchored oligo-dT primers. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 thermocycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Herculues, CA) using EvaGreen fluorescent dye (Biotium, Inc., Fremont, CA) and ExTaq polymerase (Takara Bio USA Inc.).

#### **Data analysis**

Most of the RNA-seq data analysis was conducted in the Galaxy environment ([https://usegalaxy.org/\)](https://usegalaxy.org/). Read quality was assessed with FastQC (Andrews, 2010), and the first 12 nucleotides of each read were removed with Trimmomatic (Bolger *et al.*, 2014). Reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis TAIR10 genome with either STAR (Dobin *et al.*, 2013) or HISAT2 (Kim *et al.*, 2015) and read counts determined by the program featureCounts (Liao *et al.*, 2014). From these counts, differential gene expression was analyzed by either the edgeR (Robinson *et al.*, 2009) or limmavoom algorithm (Law *et al.*, 2014). For both of these analyses, genes with less than one count per million in any sample were filtered out, and the threshold for differential expression was set at a log<sup>2</sup> fold change of 1 with a false discovery rate (for edgeR) or adjusted p-value (for limma-voom) of less than 0.05. Lists of differentially expressed genes were parsed and further analyzed in R (R Core Team, 2019).

A variety of resources were utilized to analyze various features of the ABR1 amino acid sequence. Conserved domains were identified by Pfam (El-Gebali *et al.*, 2019), and secondary structure was predicted by PSIPRED (Buchan and Jones, 2019). A consensus of results from PONDR-FIT-VSL2 (Xue *et al.*, 2010) and DISOPRED3 (Jones and Cozzetto, 2015) defined regions of probable structural disorder. Putative interaction interfaces were predicted using the Eukaryotic Linear Motif resource (Gouw *et al.*, 2018), ANCHOR2 (Mészáros *et al.*, 2018), MoRFpred (Disfani *et al.*, 2012), and MoRF chibi (Malhis and Gsponer, 2015; Malhis *et al.*, 2016).

The DNA motif bound by ABR1 was previously characterized through DNA Affinity Purification Sequencing (DAP-seq) (O'Malley *et al.*, 2016). A list of genes bound by ABR1 was downloaded from the Plant Cistrome Database

([http://neomorph.salk.edu/dev/pages/shhuang/dap\\_web/pages/index.php\)](http://neomorph.salk.edu/dev/pages/shhuang/dap_web/pages/index.php). A nucleotide frequency matrix of this motif was obtained from the JASPAR transcription factor binding profile database (<http://jaspar.genereg.net/>) (Fornes *et al.*, 2019) and used as an input for the online program PWScan ([https://ccg.epfl.ch/pwmscan/\)](https://ccg.epfl.ch/pwmscan/) (Ambrosini *et al.*, 2018) in surveys of defined collections of promoter sequences.

A catalytic mutant of HopZ1a (C216A) was also tested, along with additional HopZ effectors (HopZ2 and HopZ3). An empty expression vector (EV) construct and a GRAS family transcription factor (GRAS1, At3g46600) were included as negative controls. Proteins were transiently expressed in *Nicotiana benthamiana* leaves by agroinfiltration as fusions to an N- or C-terminal portion of yellow fluorescent protein (nYFP or cYFP, respectively). Expression was induced by 20 μM dexamethasone at 24 h post-inoculation and images were collected 24 h later. Scale bars indicate 50 μm. Confirmation of construct expression is provided in Figure S1. (b) Growth of *Pseudomonas* spp. in wild-type (WT) versus *abr1* knockout plants. The leaves of six-week-old plants were inoculated by pressure infiltration with 1 x 10<sup>5</sup> cfu/mL suspensions of *P. syringae* pv. *tomato* DC3000 (*Pto*DC3000), *P. cannabina* pv. *alisalensis* ES4326 (*Pcal*ES4326), or *P. syringae* pv. *cilantro* 0788-9 (*Pci*0788-9), and bacterial populations were quantified at three days post-inoculation. Error bars represent the standard deviation from the mean of eight samples. For each strain, an asterisk indicates a statistically significant difference between WT and *abr1* plants as determined by a Student's t-test (α=0.05). (c) *ABR1* expression in response to bacterial infection. Six-week-old Arabidopsis plants were infiltrated with 10 mM MgCl2 or with 1 x 10<sup>8</sup> cfu/mL suspensions of either wild-type (WT) *Pto*DC3000 or a type III secretion-deficient mutant (*hrcC*), and leaf tissue was collected at 12 h post-inoculation. Following RNA extraction, RT-qPCR was used to quantify the expression of ABR1 normalized to a housekeeping gene (EF-1α) using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Error bars represent standard deviation for data from three biological replicates. All experiments were performed at least three times with similar results.

**Figure 2.** Arabidopsis Abscisic Acid Repressor 1 (ABR1) interacts with numerous effectors from *Pseudomonas syringae*. (a) Yeast two-hybrid analyses of ABR1-effector interactions. Effectors were constitutively expressed in yeast while ABR1 was expressed from a galactose-inducible promoter. In addition to HopZ1a, 29 effectors from *P. syringae* pv. *tomato* DC3000 (*Pto*DC3000) were tested against ABR1 by pairwise yeast mating. The *Pto*DC3000 genes *HopF2* and *ShcF* were included as a positive control, based on their previously documented

strong interaction (Shan *et al*., 2004). Yeast suspensions at OD600 = 2 and three five-fold serial dilutions were spotted on nutritional selection media, and images were generally captured after three days of growth. (b) Evaluation of ABR1-effector interactions by bimolecular fluorescence complementation assays in *Nicotiana benthamiana*. An empty expression vector (EV) construct and HopI1 were included as negative controls. Proteins were transiently expressed in *N. benthamiana* leaves by agroinfiltration as fusions to an N- or Cterminal portion of yellow fluorescent protein (nYFP or cYFP, respectively). Expression was induced by 20 μM dexamethasone at 24 h post-inoculation and images were collected 24 h later. Scale bars indicate 50 μm. All experiments were performed at least three times with similar results. Expression analyses for all constructs and additional yeast two-hybrid controls are provided in Figures S1 and S5.

**Figure 3.** Predicted sequence features of Abscisic Acid Repressor 1 (ABR1) and domain interactions with the effector HopZ1a. (a) Computational predictions of functionally relevant regions within ABR1. An APETALA2 (AP2) domain was identified by querying the Pfam database, while secondary protein structure was predicted using PSIPRED, as illustrated with purple and red boxes representing β-strands and α-helices, respectively. A consensus of results from PONDR-FIT-VSL2 and DISOPRED3 defined regions of predicted structural disorder (blue boxes). Within these regions, putative interaction interfaces known as molecular recognition features (MoRFs) were predicted using ANCHOR2, MoRFpred, and MoRF chibi (green boxes). (b) Yeast two-hybrid analyses of the interaction between HopZ1a and specific regions of ABR1. HopZ1a was constitutively expressed in yeast while ABR1 was expressed from a galactose-inducible promoter. Yeast suspensions at OD600 = 2 and three five-fold serial dilutions were spotted on nutritional selection media, and images were generally captured after three days of growth. The experiment was performed at least three times with similar results, and confirmation of expression for all constructs is provided in Figure S6.

**Figure 4.** Arabidopsis Abscisic Acid Repressor 1 (ABR1) preferentially binds a CGG box promoter motif. (a) Characterization of ABR1 binding sequence specificity by yeast one-hybrid analysis. Putative DNA-binding proteins were expressed as fusions to the GAL4 transcriptional activation domain (GAL4 AD), and included ABR1 as well as the APETALA2 (AP2) domain-containing proteins Ethylene Response Factor1 (ERF1, At3g23240) and a member of ERF subfamily B-1 (designated here as ERF-B-1, At1g28160). Yeast one-hybrid reporter constructs contained four copies of the motif ACGGCGG ("4xCGG"), AGCCGCC ("4xGCC"), or ATCCTCC ("4xTCC") within a minimal promoter upstream of the uracil biosynthetic gene *URA3*. Yeast

suspensions at OD600 = 2 and three five-fold serial dilutions were spotted on nutritional selection media, and images were generally captured after three days of growth. Media lacking leucine and tryptophan (SD-LW) confer plasmid selection for assessment of equal plating of yeast cells, while growth on media that also lack uracil (SD-LWU) indicates activation of the reporter construct. (b) Evaluation of the contribution of specific ABR1 domains to DNA binding. The domain architecture of ABR1 comprises an APETALA2 (AP2) domain (blue) flanked by regions of predicted disorder (black). Experiments were performed as in (a). All experiments were performed at least three times with similar results, and confirmation of expression for all constructs is provided in Figure S7.

**Figure 5.** Transcriptional activation by Arabidopsis Abscisic Acid Repressor 1 (ABR1). (a) Yeast one-hybrid analysis was performed using a 6xHA-ABR1 fusion expressed with reporter constructs containing four copies of the motif ACGGCGG ("4xCGG"), AGCCGCC ("4xGCC"), or ATCCTCC ("4xTCC") within a minimal promoter upstream of the uracil biosynthetic gene *URA3*. Yeast suspensions at OD600 = 2 and three five-fold serial dilutions were spotted on nutritional selection media, and images were generally captured after three days of growth. Media lacking leucine and tryptophan (SD-LW) confer plasmid selection for assessment of equal plating of yeast cells, while growth on media that also lack uracil (SD-LWU) indicates activation of the reporter construct. (b) Assessment of DNA binding by ABR1 *in planta*. *Nicotiana benthamiana* leaves were coinfiltrated with constructs expressing putative DNA-binding proteins (ABR1, ERF1, ERF-B-1, or GRAS1) as well as a reporter construct containing a firefly luciferase gene downstream of either a minimal *Cauliflower Mosaic Virus* (CaMV) 35S promoter ("m35S") or m35S plus four copies of an ACGGCGG ("4xCGG"), AGCCGCC ("4xGCC"), or ATCCTCC ("4xTCC") sequence. A reporter construct with a full-length CaMV 35S promoter ("35S") was included as a positive control. The ratio of firefly luciferase activity (FLUC) to the *Renilla* luciferase (RLUC) internal loading control provides a normalized readout of transcription from a given promoter motif. Error bars indicate standard deviation and an asterisk indicates a statistically significant difference in expression relative to the respective m35S control (Student's t-test, α=0.05). Experiments were performed at least three times with similar results, and confirmation of expression for all constructs is provided in Figure S8.

**Figure 6.** Summary of RNA-Seq analysis of the influence of Abscisic Acid Repressor 1 (ABR1) on transcriptional responses to pathogen infection in Arabidopsis. Either wild-type (WT) or *abr1* mutant Arabidopsis plants

were infiltrated with 10 mM MgCl2 or 1 x 10<sup>8</sup> cfu/mL suspensions of either wild-type (WT) *Pseudomonas syringae* pv. *tomato* DC3000 (*Pto*DC3000) or a type III secretion-deficient *Pto*DC3000 mutant (*hrcC*). Tissues were collected at 12 h post-inoculation for analysis by RNA-sequencing. Differentially expressed genes were identified by comparing gene expression in pathogen-inoculated tissues versus MgCl2-infiltrated control tissues. Venn diagrams indicate overlaps in the lists of differentially expressed genes between the four hostpathogen genotype combinations tested.

**Figure 7.** Abscisic Acid Repressor 1 (ABR1) influences gene expression in Arabidopsis in response to infection by different genotypes of the pathogen *Pseudomonas syringae*. Six-week-old Arabidopsis plants were infiltrated with 10 mM MgCl2 or with 1 x 10<sup>8</sup> cfu/mL suspensions of wild-type (WT) *P. syringae* pv. *tomato* DC3000 (*Pto*DC3000), a type III secretion-deficient mutant (*hrcC*), an effectorless *P. syringae* polymutant containing an empty expression vector (D36E+EV), or the effectorless polymutant expressing the effector HopZ1a (D36E+*hopZ1a*). To avoid immune recognition of HopZ1a, all D36E inoculations were performed in *zar1* or *abr1zar1* mutant plants. Leaf tissues were collected at 12 h post-inoculation. Following RNA extraction, RT-qPCR was used to quantify the expression of specific genes normalized to a housekeeping gene (EF-1α) using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Values are presented as log2 fold changes relative to MgCl2-infiltrated control plants. Error bars represent standard deviation for data from at least four biological replicates and asterisks indicate a statistically significant difference between host genotypes as calculated by a Student's t-test (α=0.05).
