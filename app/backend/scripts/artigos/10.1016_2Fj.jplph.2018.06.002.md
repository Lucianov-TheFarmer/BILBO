#### ABSTRACT

Cottonseed oil accumulated dramatically from 20 days post-anthesis (DPA) to 30 DPA in Upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.). To reveal the gene network of oil accumulation and fatty acid composition in developing embryos, embryos at 10, 20 and 30 DPA in cottonseed were sampled and used for transcriptome sequencing (RNA-Seq). In total, 8629, 7891, and 12,555 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the comparison sets of '20 DPA vs 10 DPA', '30 DPA vs 20 DPA', and '30 DPA vs 10 DPA', respectively. The gene network highlighted the dynamic expression profiles of oil accumulation in fatty acid (FA) synthesis, FA desaturation, and triacylglycerol (TAG) biosynthesis. WRI1 and NF-YB6 were suggested elite transcription factors in regulating lipid metabolism. Compared with the gene expression levels in developing seeds, GhPDAT was highly expressed and might play a more important role than GhDGAT in transforming diacylglycerol to TAG in cotton. Expression patterns of 12 FA-biosynthesis-related genes were validated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) method. To reveal the reason for the high content of linoleic acid (C18:2) in cottonseed oil, we carried out a comparative analysis of gene expression levels in Upland cotton, rapeseed (Brassica napus), and oleaster (Olea europaea). Compared with in rapeseed and oleaster, GhFAD2 genes were up-regulated and GhFAD3 genes downregulated in cottonseed, taking into account the relative high amount of C18:2 but low content of linolenic acid (C18:3) in Upland cotton. The present study offers new information to interpret the mechanism of the FA biosynthesis network and to alter FA composition in cotton breeding projects.

## 1. Introduction

Cotton is the worldwide donor of natural fiber for global textile industries. Meanwhile, cottonseed is an excellent source of oil for humans, due to its high content of unsaturated fatty acids; cottonseed oil contains 71% unsaturated fatty acids, with 58% linoleic acid (C18:2) and 13% oleic acid (C18:1), and also consists of 26% palmitic acid (C16:0) and 2% stearic acid (C18:0) (Cherry, 1983). The high content of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) helps to reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease in humans. In addition, cottonseed oil is an ideal material for the manufacture of biodiesel due to its richness in 16- to 18carbon chains. Therefore, the application of molecular breeding and biotechnology strategies that increase the cottonseed oil content are the goal for cotton breeding.

The process of fatty acid (FA) biosynthesis includes synthesis, transport, and desaturation, and it takes place mainly in plastids, cytosol, and /or endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membranes in plant seeds (Bourgis et al., 2011; Li-Beisson et al., 2013). This process involves enzymes encoded by a series of functional genes: overexpressed subunits of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACCase) contribute to oil accumulation in cottonseed (Cui et al., 2017); glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (GPAT9) catalyzes the first step of TAG biosynthesis in Arabidopsis thaliana (Shockey et al., 2016); acyl-CoA: diacylglycerol acyltransferase (DGAT1, DGAT2, DGAT3) and phospholipid: diacylglycerol acyltransferase (PDAT) are two parallel pathways that synthesize TAG, and ectopic expression of either of these pathways

Furthermore, some transcription factors (TFs) were involved in TAG metabolism and FA synthesis of FA metabolism: LEC1 [\(Mu et al., 2008](#page-11-1); [Shen et al., 2010](#page-11-2)), FUS3 [\(Roscoe et al., 2015](#page-11-3)), WRI1 [\(Shen et al., 2010](#page-11-2); [To et al., 2012](#page-10-6); [Grimberg et al., 2015\)](#page-10-7), Dof ([Wang et al., 2007;](#page-11-4) [Deng et al., 2015;](#page-10-8) [Su](#page-11-5) [et al., 2017\)](#page-11-5), and NF-Y [\(Siefers et al., 2009\)](#page-11-6) were reported in Arabidopsis, maize, tobacco, soybean, oilseed rape, and cotton, respectively. Additionally, some genes were proved to regulate FA composition: down-regulation of △9-stearoyl ACP desaturase (SAD) and △-12 fatty acid desaturase (FAD2) altered FA composition in cottonseed ([Liu et al.,](#page-11-7) [2002\)](#page-11-7); RNAi down-regulated KASII, increasing the content of C16:0 in Arabidopsis ([Pidkowich et al., 2007\)](#page-11-8) and Upland cotton [\(Liu et al.,](#page-11-9) [2017\)](#page-11-9); combination mutants of FAD2 and FAD3 genes produced high C18:1 and low C18:2 in soybean [\(Pham et al., 2012](#page-11-10)). However, the network and regulation mechanism involved in oil biosynthesis remains ambiguous and needs to be further elucidated in oil crops.

Transcriptome sequencing technology provides a new strategy for the study of expression profiling, allowing the identification and quantification of gene expression levels in developing seeds. Along with the booming of sequencing data, gene networks constructed by highthroughput sequencing in developing seeds have been reported in soybean ([Chen et al., 2012;](#page-10-9) [Jones and Vodkin, 2013\)](#page-10-10), rapeseed ([Troncoso-Ponce et al., 2011](#page-11-11); [Deng et al., 2015;](#page-10-8) [Wan et al., 2017\)](#page-11-12), oil palm [\(Dussert et al., 2013\)](#page-10-11), peanut ([Yin et al., 2013;](#page-11-13) [Gupta et al., 2016](#page-10-12)), and sesame (Wang [et al., 2014](#page-11-14)), demonstrating considerable progress towards uncovering the molecular mechanism in oil accumulation. In recent years, there has been a dramatic increase in cotton genome data, including diploid cotton species of D5 (Gossypium raimondii, [Wang et al.,](#page-11-15) [2012;](#page-11-15) [Paterson et al., 2012](#page-11-16)) and A2 (G. arboreum, [Li et al., 2014](#page-10-13)), as well as allotetraploid cotton species of Upland cotton (AD1) (G. hirsutum, [Li et al., 2015a](#page-10-14); [Zhang et al., 2015\)](#page-11-17) and Sea-Island cotton (AD2) (G. barbadense, [Liu et al., 2015;](#page-11-18) [Yuan et al., 2015](#page-11-19)). Global expression analyses of the cottonseed transcriptome have also been reported using diploid cotton D5 as a reference genome [\(Jiao et al., 2013;](#page-10-15) [Hovav et al.,](#page-10-16) [2015;](#page-10-16) [Hu et al., 2016\)](#page-10-17). The attempts to sequence transcriptomes of cottonseed are beneficial to elucidate the networks of oil synthesis metabolism, providing a theoretical basis for genetic improvement.

To avoid noise in the data caused by the seed coat, we first sampled the isolated cotton embryos and carried out pair-end reads by RNA-Seq technology to produce transcriptome data at 10, 20, and 30 days postanthesis (DPA). Meanwhile, we used the Upland cotton (G. hirsutum var. TM-1) genome as a reference to unearth more comprehensive information. We analyzed the gene expression network of oil accumulation to improve our understanding of the metabolic pathways of FA biosynthesis and the FA composition in cottonseed development.

#### 2. Materials and methods

#### 2.1. Plant materials

Upland cotton plants "11-0509" were grown at Hejian Experimental Station of China Agricultural University (Guoxin Seed Co. Ltd, Hejian, Cangzhou City, Hebei Province, China, 2016; 38°43′ N, 116°08′ E). Field management followed the local conventional field practices. Embryos were collected at 10, 20, and 30 DPA stage for RNA isolation, representing diff ;erent stages of seed filling and development. The temperature and photoperiod were around 25 °C in the night for about 10 h, and 30 °C in the daytime for about 14 h. We carefully isolated the embryos manually in a biosafety cabinet, put the embryos in a liquid nitrogen tank immediately, and stored them in a −80 °C freezer for RNA isolation. Three biological replicates were taken for each stage.

To detect the oil content, the embryos were isolated at 20, 30, 40, and 50 DPA, followed by drying in an oven. The oil content of the embryos was determined following the method described by [Hovav](#page-10-16) [et al. \(2015\).](#page-10-16) FA composition in mature cottonseed oil was detected by gas chromatography (GC) on a HP7890 A machine (Agilent, Palo Alto CA, USA) following the methods of [Yang et al. \(2010\).](#page-11-20)

#### 2.2. RNA sequencing and mapping to the reference genome

Total RNA was isolated by the cetyltrimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) method ([Zhang et al., 2011](#page-11-21)). A total of 100 ng purified mRNA was used for cDNA library preparation using NEB Next® Ultra™ RNA library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, Ipswich MA, USA), following the manufacturer's protocol carefully. After quality inspection, a 10-ng sample of the cDNA library was taken with TruSeq PE Cluster Kit (Illumina, San Diego CA, USA) in the cBot for cluster generation, applied for 150 base paired-end sequencing on the Illumina Hiseq™ 2500 platform. FASTX-Toolkit ([http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx\\_toolkit/\)](http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/) tools were used for adapter removing and the filtration of low-quality reads to produce clean reads. The clean data were submitted to the sequence read archive (SRA) database of the National Center of Biotechnology Information (NCBI) with BioProject no. PRJNA361371.

Cotton genome data of D5 ([Paterson et al., 2012](#page-11-16)), A2 ([Li et al.,](#page-10-13) [2014\)](#page-10-13), AD1\_BGI [\(Li et al., 2015a\)](#page-10-14), and AD1\_NBI [\(Zhang et al., 2015\)](#page-11-17) were downloaded from the CottonGen website [\(https://www.](https://www.cottongen.org/) [cottongen.org/](https://www.cottongen.org/)). The clean reads of each library were aligned to the reference genome by TopHat software (Version 2.0.1) [\(http://tophat.](http://tophat.cbcb.umd.edu/) [cbcb.umd.edu/\)](http://tophat.cbcb.umd.edu/). Gene expression levels were measured by Cufflinks (Version 2.2.1) of fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (FPKM) value following the protocol of [Trapnell et al. \(2012\).](#page-11-22)

#### 2.3. DEGs detection, gene function annotation, and enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected using an R package of DESeq [\(Anders and Huber, 2010](#page-10-18)) with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold < 0.01 and foldchange > 2. Gene Ontology (GO) function annotation was carried out by BLASTp algorithm against the Nr database (E-value cut-off, 1.0E-6) and then detected by blast2go software [\(Conesa et al., 2005](#page-10-19)). GO enrichment analysis was carried out by blast2go using the fisher method. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotation was detected by KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS) tool with the SBH (single-directional best hit) method ([Moriya et al., 2007](#page-11-23)), KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the ccNET website ([You et al., 2017\)](#page-11-24) at the level of p value ≤ 0.05.

## 2.4. Construction of gene network in oil accumulation and gene expression validated by quantitative real-time PCR

Genes involved in FA biosynthesis were detected and referenced to Arabidopsis acyl-lipid metabolism ([Li-Beisson et al., 2013](#page-10-2)). Gene expression levels were measured by FPKM value. The gene network in oil accumulation of cottonseed was constructed and modified based on the model in A. thaliana ([Li-Beisson et al., 2013](#page-10-2)) and in Camelina sativa ([Abdullah et al., 2016](#page-10-20)), respectively.

The expression levels of candidate genes were validated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). The total RNA of each sample was the same as that used for cDNA library preparation. The reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Dalian, China) following the manufacturer's protocol, and qRT-PCR reactions with triplet technique repeats were carried out with an ABI 7500 fluorescent quantitation PCR machine (Applied Biosystems, Foster City CA, USA) using the SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II kit (Takara Bio, Dalian, China) as a fluorescent reporter. The qRT-PCR reaction system contained 0.5 μL of each forward and reverse primers (10 μmol each), 5 μL of SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II (2×), 0.2 μL of ROX Reference Dye II (50×), and 1 μL of cDNA (300 ng/μL), topping up the reaction system to 10 μL with nucleic-acid-free water. The cDNA samples were amplified using gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table S1). The quantification of PCR products was conducted using the 2−ΔΔCT method ([Livak and Schmittgen, 2001](#page-11-25)). GhUBQ7 (GenBank ID: [DQ116441\)](http://DQ116441) was used as the internal control. The gene expression levels were normalized using the Z-score method.

## 2.5. Public genome and transcriptome data used for FA composition analysis

The genome and protein sequence data of oleaster (Olea europaea var. sylvestris) were downloaded from an olive genome website [\(http://](http://olivegenome.org/) [olivegenome.org/](http://olivegenome.org/)), and gene expression data of both leaf and fruit were downloaded from NCBI Sequence Read Archive ([https://www.ncbi.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) [nlm.nih.gov/sra](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra)) referenced by [Unver et al. \(2017\)](#page-11-26). The genome data of rapeseed (Brassica napus) were downloaded from a Brassica genome website (<http://www.genoscope.cns.fr/brassicanapus/>) with version 4.1. The transcriptome data on developing rapeseed were downloaded from the NCBI SRA website referenced by [Wan et al. \(2017\).](#page-11-12) The transcriptome data of different tissues in cotton were downloaded from NCBI SRA referenced by [Zhang et al. \(2015\).](#page-11-17) Detailed information on these public RNA-Seq data is described in Supplementary Table S2. The orthologous genes between Upland cotton, oleaster, and rapeseed were subjected to InParanoid ortholog analysis ([Remm et al., 2001](#page-11-27)). The gene expression levels were estimated by TopHat and Cufflinks software.

#### 3. Results

#### 3.1. Dynamic changes and oil accumulation in developing cottonseeds

The dynamic morphology of developing cottonseeds was investigated at 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, and 50 DPA. The volume of cottonseed expanded quickly before 25 DPA with cottonseed development and reached a steady size at 30 DPA [\(Fig. 1A](#page-2-0)). The dry weight of cottonseed increased continuously until 50 DPA, following a slight reduction at maturity (Supplementary Fig. 1A). In contrast, the water content of cottonseed reduced gradually from more than 80% at early stage (before 15 DPA) to about 5% at maturity (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

Total oil content was detected to explore the dynamic accumulation process; oil content accumulated rapidly from 20 DPA (about 7%) to 30 DPA (about 35%) [\(Fig. 1](#page-2-0)B). The relative content of seed oil reached its peak of about 40% at 40 DPA, following a decrease to about 35% at maturity ([Fig. 1B](#page-2-0)). In addition, fatty acid composition of oil in mature seeds was investigated. Cottonseed oil contains a high amount of C18:2 (57.64%), but also contains 22.92% of C16:0, 15.39% of C18:1, and 2.22% of C18:0 ([Fig. 1C](#page-2-0)). Other fatty acids, such as myristic acid (C14:0), palmitoleic acid (C16:1), linolenic acid (C18:3), and arachidic acid (C20:0) were detected at minor levels, each constituting less than 1% of the total seed oil ([Fig. 1](#page-2-0)C).

## 3.2. Gene expression and functional classification in developing cottonseeds

To detect the transcriptome profile of the developing embryos, nine cDNA libraries at different developmental stages (10 DPA, 20 DPA, and 30 DPA) with three biological replicates each were sequenced with high-throughput RNA-Seq approach using Illumina sequencing. A total of 131 million of 150-bp paired-end reads were produced. About 4 Gb transcriptome data were generated from each library, so that a total of 36 Gb data were deposited for sequence quality evaluation and GC distribution analysis. The results showed that the RNA-seq data were of high quality and suitable for further analyses (Supplementary Table S3).

Clean reads of each sample were aligned to Gossypium reference genomes of A2 [\(Li et al., 2014\)](#page-10-13), D5 ([Paterson et al., 2012\)](#page-11-16), AD1\_BGI ([Li](#page-10-14) [et al., 2015a](#page-10-14)), and AD1\_NBI [\(Zhang et al., 2015\)](#page-11-17) by TopHat software. The alignment showed that the genome data of G. hirsutum, AD1\_NBI ([Zhang et al., 2015](#page-11-17)) were more complete and of higher quality as a reference genome, compared with the other cotton genomes (Supplementary Table S4). Thus, G. hirsutum, AD1\_NBI was chosen as the reference genome for RNA-Seq aligning. The alignment showed that the mapped rate was around 90%, and the uniquely mapped pair rate was about 80% in nine libraries [\(Table 1](#page-3-0)). Then, gene expression levels were estimated by calculating the FPKM value. To remove the negative effects of expression noise, only the criterion of FPKM ≥ 1 in each library was considered an expressed gene. A total of 47,533 genes were expressed in seed development (Supplementary Fig. S2), accounting for 73.6% of total genes in the AD1 reference genome [\(Zhang et al., 2015](#page-11-17)). There were 42,277, 43,581, and 38,484 genes expressed at 10 DPA, 20 DPA, and 30 DPA, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S2A). The maximum number of genes was expressed at 20 DPA, in concordance with the public gene expression data on the developing ovule of Upland cotton (Supplementary Table S2). A total of 2,585, 1,930, and 937 genes were time-specifically expressed at 10 DPA, 20 DPA, and 30 DPA, respectively, with a continuously decreasing trend (Supplementary Fig. S2B). In total, 34,764 genes were expressed in all the three stages, showing the conservative function in cottonseeds.

#### 3.3. Gene expression profiles change in cottonseed developmental stages

To evaluate the correlation among different samples, nine libraries of gene expression data from different stage points were employed for hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) and principal component analysis (PCA). Gene expression data in the same stage were clustered together concordantly, and showed a similar expression pattern [\(Fig. 2](#page-4-0)A, B). The result reflects that gene expression data were uniform at each time point, and were appropriated for comparative analysis in developing embryos. To observe how different gene expression levels affect embryo development, the R package DESeq (FDR < 0.01, fold change > 2) was used to identify DEGs at cottonseed developmental stages. A total of 8629 genes were differentially expressed in the comparison of '20 DPA vs 10 DPA', with 4751 genes up-regulated and 3878 genes down-regulated. In the '30 DPA vs 20 DPA' comparison, a total of 7891 DEGs were detected, with 4335 DEGs up-regulated and 3556 DEGs down-regulated. In the comparison of '30 DPA vs 10 DPA' embryo libraries, there were 12,555 DEGs, with 6850 DEGs up-regulated and 5705 DEGs down-regulated [\(Fig. 2C](#page-4-0), D). In total, 17,430 genes were differentially expressed, with 743 DEGs steadily up-regulated and 317 DEGs continuously down-regulated in developing cottonseed [\(Fig. 2D](#page-4-0)).

The GO distributions of up- and down-regulated DEGs in the comparisons of '20 DPA vs 10 DPA', '30 DPA vs 20 DPA', and '30 DPA vs 10 DPA' were carried out to describe the distinct classification groups (Supplementary Fig. S4). Some of the GO distributions of DEGs in cottonseed exhibited significant differences during cottonseed development; for example, genes in the subcategory cell killing (GO: 0001906) were obviously up-regulated in '30 DPA vs 20 DPA', while genes involved in structural molecule activity (GO: 0005198) were more abundant in '20 DPA vs 10 DPA' and '30 DPA vs 10 DPA' upregulated DEGs. From the results it can be inferred that genes in different GO terms perform essential functions in cottonseed development. Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis was carried out on the different classes of DEGs in developing cottonseed (Supplementary Table S6). Some categories involved in oil metabolism were speculated to play a role in oil accumulation. For instance, in the '30 DPA vs 20 DPA' treatment, six out of 29 most enriched GO subcategories were related to lipid metabolism ([Fig. 3](#page-4-1)). Genes involved in these subcategories might contribute greatly to oil accumulation in cottonseed.

KEGG enrichment analysis was carried out to determine gene expression profiles at cottonseed development stages (Supplementary Table S7), with more attention being paid to the lipid metabolism pathway. In the group of down-regulated DEGs in the '20 DPA vs 10 DPA' comparison, four out of 20 significant enrichment pathways were involved in lipid metabolism pathways, including fatty-acid elongation (ko00062), synthesis and degradation of ketone bodies (ko00072), glycerolipid metabolism (ko00561), and cutin, suberine, and wax biosynthesis (ko00073). In the meantime, for the up-regulated DEGs in the '30 DPA vs 20 DPA' comparison, four out of 19 pathways exhibited significant enrichment, including the pathways of fatty acid degradation (ko00071), biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (ko01040), glycerolipid metabolism (ko00561), and linoleic acid metabolism (ko00591) (Supplementary Fig. S5).

A total of 1690 genes were involved in lipid metabolism in Upland cotton [\(You et al., 2017](#page-11-24)), of which 550 genes were differentially expressed in developing cottonseed. A heat map was generated to check the gene expression model at different stage points, which showed that genes were mainly up-regulated at 10, 20, and 30 DPA, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S6). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the up-regulated genes at specific stage were involved in different pathways. Here, we highlighted that more genes were involved in the pathways of linoleic acid metabolism and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids at the late stage (30 DPA) of cottonseed development, which coincided with a high content of unsaturated fatty acids at 30 DPA ([Hovav et al., 2015\)](#page-10-16).

#### 4. Discussion

Transcriptome analysis by high-throughput sequencing is an effective method to dissect candidate pathways and genes at different developmental stages. Compared with previous studies ([Jiao et al., 2013](#page-10-15); [Hovav et al., 2015](#page-10-16); [Hu et al., 2016\)](#page-10-17), the present study tries to uncover more complete transcriptome information on the regulation mechanism of oil accumulation in cottonseeds. [Jiao et al. \(2013\)](#page-10-15) compared the different gene transcript levels of cotyledons and embryo axis at 30 DPA, focusing on the metabolism in two developmentally diverse tissues. [Hovav et al. \(2015\)](#page-10-16) took four stages of cottonseed for RNA sequencing and compared gene expression models in developing cottonseeds. The analyses were mainly focused on the expression bias in At and Dt subgenomes, and gene expression patterns of PDHC, ACCase, FAT, and DGAT in cottonseed. [Hu et al. \(2016\)](#page-10-17) used two diploid and two allotetraploid cotton species to explore the substantial modification of genic co-expression under domestication in cottonseed. Seed coat might affect gene expression profiling analysis in seed ([Hovav et al.,](#page-10-16) [2015\)](#page-10-16), so we first provided the data originating from cotton developing embryos at different stages for pair-end RNA sequencing and employed newly released data of the Upland cotton genome [\(Zhang et al., 2015\)](#page-11-17) as a reference. In particular, gene expression patterns involved in oil accumulation and FA components were fully discussed for the detected developmental stages.

Cottonseed contains fatty acids, which make up 20% to 40% of the seed kernels ([Shang et al., 2015\)](#page-11-28). In the present study, oil content of cottonseed barely accumulated before 20 DPA, but rapidly increased from 20 DPA to 30 DPA. Gene expression analysis showed that more genes were expressed at 20 DPA compared with at 10 DPA and 30 DPA. A similar result was observed in developing rapeseed, which expressed more genes at four weeks post pollination (WAP) compared with at other stages (Supplementary Table S2). Homoplastically, oil content accumulated rapidly from four WAP in rapeseed ([Wan et al., 2017](#page-11-12)). Therefore, we propose that the conversion of carbohydrates to fatty acids requires higher gene expression and that the developmental time point of 20 DPA is crucial for oil accumulation in cottonseed. After 30 DPA, the majority of storage oil had been accumulated in cottonseeds ([Fig. 1B](#page-2-0)). From 40 DPA to maturity, oil content slightly decreased and exhibited fatty acid degradation at the ripening stage. This result is in accordance with previous reports on Arabidopsis ([Baud et al., 2002](#page-10-23)), soybean [\(Chen et al., 2012](#page-10-9)), oilseed rape [\(Chia et al., 2005](#page-10-24)), tree peony ([Li et al., 2015b](#page-10-25)), and Upland cotton [\(Reeves and Beasley, 1935;](#page-11-29) [Hovav](#page-10-16) et [al., 2015](#page-10-16)). The β-oxidation enzymes were active and abundant during ripening to degrade storage oil [\(Baud et al., 2002;](#page-10-23) [Chia et al.,](#page-10-24) [2005\)](#page-10-24).

Compared with down-regulated DEGs, there was a higher number of up-regulated DEGs, demonstrating that more genes were up-regulated than down-regulated during cottonseed development [\(Fig. 2\)](#page-4-0). To obtain a global view of DEG functions, GO and KEGG analysis were performed in each of the two comparative stages. A previous study showed that the period from 20 DPA to 30 DPA is the most dynamic stage for transcriptome analysis, and about 20% of genes showed homolog expression bias [\(Hovav et al., 2015\)](#page-10-16). The expression levels of oil-related genes changed in this period, perhaps contributing more to the accumulation of total storage oil. In our present experiment, GO enrichment analysis showed that more oil-related subcategories showed significant enrichment in up-regulated DEGs of the '30 DPA vs 20 DPA' comparison ([Fig. 3](#page-4-1)), in agreement with rapid oil accumulation in cottonseed ([Fig. 1](#page-2-0)B). The KEGG analysis showed that more pathways involved in lipid metabolism were significantly enriched in down-regulated DEGs of '20 DPA vs 10 DPA' and '30 DPA vs 10 DPA' comparisons. Many genes involved in FA metabolism were down-regulated in cottonseed developmental stages, in agreement with a study on soybean ([Chen et al.,](#page-10-9) [2012\)](#page-10-9). However, genes involved mainly functioned in pyruvate precursor's metabolism, while gene expression associated with FA biosynthesis increased generally in developing cottonseed (Supplementary Table S7). This result is consistent with a previous observation in cottonseed [\(Hovav et al., 2015\)](#page-10-16). In the group of up-regulated DEGs in the '30 DPA vs 20 DPA' comparison, it was noteworthy that four out of 19 significantly enriched pathways were involved in the class of lipid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. S5). Meanwhile, the biosynthesis pathways of unsaturated fatty acids and linoleic acid metabolism were also significantly enriched, in accordance with the high content of unsaturated fatty acids in cottonseed.

Pyruvate acts as the carbon source precursor for FA metabolism. PDHC is the main enzyme complex for acetyl-CoA production, and ACCase represents an important enzyme complex converting acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA for de novo FA synthesis in plastids of plants [\(Li-](#page-10-2)[Beisson et al., 2013\)](#page-10-2). Genes involved in these two complexes have been detected in Arabidopsis and soybean [\(Reverdatto et al., 1999](#page-11-30); [Ke et al.,](#page-10-26) [2000\)](#page-10-26). PDHC provides an acetyl-CoA precursor, which is required for de novo FA synthesis. Our investigation found that the subunits of PDHC genes exhibited a similar increasing expression tendency with temporal expression in cottonseed (Supplementary Fig. S7), in accordance with the result in A. thaliana [\(Ke et al., 2000\)](#page-10-26). Knockout of the E2 subunit (DHLAT) of PDHC resulted in a lethal mutation of the early embryo ([Lin](#page-10-27) [et al., 2003\)](#page-10-27), indicating that PDHC is essential for reproduction, since lipid metabolism is the vital and basal metabolism for plant growth. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase catalyzed the first step of FA synthesis. There are four subunits of heteromeric ACCase, in cotton: BCCP, BC, CT-α, and CT-β [\(Cui et al., 2017](#page-10-3)). In higher plants, BC, BCCP, and CT-α are encoded by nuclear genes ([Ohlrogge and Browse, 1995\)](#page-11-31), but the subunit of CT-β is encoded by a chloroplast gene ([Reverdatto et al., 1999](#page-11-30)). The subunits of CT-α and BCCP displayed coordinated expression patterns as in other oilseeds ([Troncoso-Ponce et al., 2011](#page-11-11)). The CT-α, BC, and BCCP showed constantly high expression levels during cottonseed development, as confirmed in Arabidopsis [\(Ke et al., 2000\)](#page-10-26) and in other oilseeds ([Troncoso-Ponce et al., 2011](#page-11-11)). For the carbon-chain elongation process, KAR reduces 3-ketoacyl-ACP to produce 3-hydroxyacyl-ACP, HAD catalyzes the dehydration of 3-hydroxyacyl-ACP for enoyl-ACP production, ENR re-reduces the enoyl-ACP to acyl-ACP, and then KASI utilizes the acyl-ACP and malony-ACP for the next cycle of elongation, resulting in two carbon atoms being added after each cycle[\(Li-Beisson](#page-10-2) [et al., 2013\)](#page-10-2). KAR and ENR were more abundant than HAD in our study, proposing a similar reductase function in FA synthesis. At the same time, HAD, ENR, and KASI showed a coordinated temporal pattern in seed development, suggesting a supporting function in carbon-chain elongation.

Triacylglycerol (TAG) is the main component of seed oil. TAG biosynthesis is the core pathway for TAG production and involves a lot of enzymes, mainly taking place in the ER membrane [\(Li-Beisson et al.,](#page-10-2) [2013\)](#page-10-2). In the first step, GPAT transfers an acyl moiety from acyl-CoA pool to the sn-1 position of glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) to produce lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), and LPA is acylated to phosphatidic acid (PA) by catalyzing LPAAT. Subsequently, phosphatidate phosphatase (PP) catalyzes PA to produce dephosphorylated diacylglycerol (DAG), followed by the conversion of DAG to TAG by the action of PDAT and DGAT [\(Kennedy, 1961](#page-10-28); [Shockey et al., 2016](#page-11-0)). Finally, TAG is surrounded by phospholipids and proteins, with steroleosins, oleosinsand, and caleosins etc., to form an oil body ([Li-Beisson et al., 2013\)](#page-10-2). Expression patterns of key genes involved in TAG biosynthesis were analyzed, and a majority of them increased with cottonseed oil accumulation [\(Fig. 4\)](#page-5-0). The de novo of TAG formation occurs in the ER via the Kennedy pathway. GPDH catalyzes dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) to produce G3P in the cytosol, which is an important step to provide G3P pools. GPATs catalyzes G3P to produce LPA. Nine isoforms of GPATs exist, and eight were detected in cotton A previous study indicated that the isoform GPAT9 catalyzes 'sn-1' region specificity for acyl-CoA to produce acylglycerol precursors ([Singer et al., 2016](#page-10-29)). A high expression level of GPAT9 is essential for TAG biosynthesis, and GPAT9 is involved in storage lipid biosynthesis in A. thaliana [\(Shockey](#page-11-0) [et al., 2016;](#page-11-0) [Singer et al., 2016](#page-10-29)). GPAT9 was more abundant than the other GPAT isoforms in cottonseed, and its gene expression level was elevated with seed development, showing the same profiling as other TAG synthesis genes [\(Fig. 4](#page-5-0)). PDATs and DGATs were two categories of diacylglycerol acyltransferase for the last step of TAG biosynthesis of converting DAG to TAG [\(Dahlqvist et al., 2000](#page-10-30); [Lung and Weselake,](#page-11-32) [2006\)](#page-11-32). It has been confirmed that DGAT1, DGAT2, and DGAT3 are responsible for TAG production in peanut ([Saha et al., 2006](#page-11-33)). Meanwhile, the contribution of DGAT3 to the total triglyceride expression pool in cottonseed was suggested ([Hovav et al., 2015](#page-10-16)). Another conversion of DAG to TAG is performed by an acyl-CoA independent pathway catalyzed by PDAT ([Hernández et al., 2012](#page-10-4)). PDAT2 has been shown to be highly expressed in developing seeds, but could not be detected in leaves in C. Sativa ([Abdullah et al., 2016](#page-10-20)), indicating that the expression is exclusive to seeds. Our result showed that DGAT3 and PDAT2 were the two most highly expressed isoforms in DGAT and PDAT gene families, respectively. PDAT2 was more abundant than DGAT3 in cottonseed; the expression level of PDAT2 was temporally paralleled to the oil accumulation in developing cottonseed [\(Fig. 4\)](#page-5-0), suggesting that PDAT2 might play more important roles than DGAT3 for the conversion of DAG to TAG in cottonseed. Our result agreed with those of previous studies in sesame [\(Wang et al., 2014\)](#page-11-14), C. sativa ([Abdullah et al., 2016](#page-10-20)), and strand-specific transcriptome in Upland cotton [\(Ma et al., 2016](#page-11-34)), but differed from the single-end RNA-Seq data in cottonseed ([Hovav](#page-10-16) [et al., 2015](#page-10-16)). To reveal the underlying molecular mechanism, the function of PDAT2 and DGAT3 needed to be confirmed by cloning and transgenic analyses. Then, the gene network of oil accumulation in developing cottonseed was proposed, which will help us to decode gene expression profiles in cottonseed oil accumulation ([Fig. 5\)](#page-6-0).

Furthermore, TFs have been proved to regulate the transcription of multiple genes in FA biosynthesis simultaneously ([Shen et al., 2010](#page-11-2); [To](#page-10-6) [et al., 2012;](#page-10-6) [Roscoe et al., 2015;](#page-11-3) [Zhang et al., 2015](#page-11-17)). In the present study, many oil-metabolism-related TFs were significantly up-regulated at 20 DPA compared to at 10 DPA (Supplementary Table S10), corresponding to the primary stage for rapid oil accumulation [\(Fig. 1](#page-2-0)). bZIP67 and GCN5 are two TFs regulating FAD3 expression ([Mendes](#page-11-35) [et al., 2013;](#page-11-35) [Wang et al., 2016](#page-11-36)), but they showed different expression patterns in developing cottonseed. WRI1 is a kernel TF regulating FA biosynthesis [\(To et al., 2012](#page-10-6); [Grimberg et al., 2015\)](#page-10-7). Other than LEC1, overexpression of ZmWRI1 specifically increased oil content in seed without otherwise affecting the phenotype ([Shen et al., 2010\)](#page-11-2). In the present experiment, WRI1 was more abundantly expressed than other oil-related TFs, and gene expression of WRI1 increased with oil accumulation. WRI1 is known to be up-regulated by LEC1, LEC2, FUS3, and ABI3 [\(Baud and Lepiniec, 2010](#page-10-31)). FUS3 and ABI3 showed similar expression profiles as WRI1 in developing cottonseed, which supports the idea that FUS3 and ABI3 are the direct target of WRI1. Expression analysis also performed on the directed targets oil-related genes of WRI1, such as BCCP2, PDHC, and KASI [\(Baud and Lepiniec, 2010](#page-10-31)), all of them displaying a coordinated temporal pattern with WRI1. NF-YB is a category of the NF-Y kind TF family ([Siefers et al., 2009](#page-11-6)), which plays a role in controlling carbon flow into fatty acids ([Deng et al., 2015](#page-10-8)). The gene expression level of NF-YB6 increased tremendously in oil rapid accumulation stages, and increased about 340-fold and 380-fold at 20 DPA and 30 DPA compared with at 10 DPA (Supplementary Table S10). The expression patterns of WRI1 and NF-YB6 in developing cottonseed were verified by qRT-PCR [\(Fig. 6\)](#page-6-1). Taken together, WRI1 and NF-YB6 were highly expressed and gene expression profiles were in accordance with oil accumulation, and they were expected elite TFs in the regulatory network of oil accumulation in cottonseed.

Generally, UFA content was consistent with the expression level of genes for desaturation. Compared with other oil crops, cottonseed contains relatively high levels of C16:0, which makes up about 26% of the seed oil. KASII was the key gene to convert C16:0 to C18:0. Downregulation of KASII increases the content of C16:0 in Arabidopsis ([Pidkowich et al., 2007\)](#page-11-8) and Upland cotton ([Liu et al., 2017](#page-11-9)). In our observation, gene expression levels of KASII were concordant with the relative content of C16:0 in cotton, oleaster, and rapeseed, respectively ([Fig. 7\)](#page-7-1). Cottonseed oil contains a high content of PUFA, mainly of C18:2 ([Cherry, 1983;](#page-10-0) [Hovav et al., 2015\)](#page-10-16). SAD catalyzes the desaturation of C18:0 to produce C18:1, and SAD is supposed to be responsible for the production of C18:0 and C18:1 in Brassica and Arabidopsis ([Knutzon et al., 1992;](#page-10-32) [Pidkowich et al., 2007](#page-11-8)). RNA silencing of the ghSAD-1 gene substantially increased C18:0 content, from the normal level of 2% to 3%, up to as high as 40% in cottonseed [\(Liu et al., 2002](#page-11-7)). GhSAD genes were abundantly expressed at 20 DPA and 30 DPA, paralleled to the temporal C18:1 content in developing seeds. FADs arw an important category for FA desaturation, with several genes providing the evidence for FA desaturation by mutants of A. thaliana, such as fad2, fad3, fad4, fad5, fad6, fad7, and fad8, of which fad3, fad7, and fad8 encode omega-3 fatty acid desaturase ([Ohlrogge and Browse, 1995\)](#page-11-31). In Upland cotton, FAD4 and FAD5 were not detected. FAD2 and FAD6 catalyze C18:1 to C18:2, and FAD3, FAD7, and FAD8 catalyze C18:2 to yield C18:3, principally ([Ohlrogge and Browse, 1995;](#page-11-31) [Li-Beisson et al.,](#page-10-2) [2013\)](#page-10-2). The function of FAD2 was fully discussed recently [\(Dar et al.,](#page-10-33) [2017\)](#page-10-33); it acts as an elite in oil accumulation and stress tolerance in plants. There are nine FAD2 genes in cotton, while only FAD2-1 is specifically expressed in seeds [\(Liu et al., 1999](#page-10-34)). Down-regulation of ghFAD2-1 greatly increases C18:1 content, from 15% up to 77% in Upland cotton ([Liu et al., 2002\)](#page-11-7). Compared with in leaf, four of the five OeuFAD2 genes exhibit repressed expression in oleaster fruit. The only actively expressed OeuFAD2 gene may be responsible for the production of low amounts of C18:2 in oleaster oil ([Unver et al., 2017](#page-11-26)). In our observation, two homolog genes of GhFAD2-1 (Gh\_A13G1850 and Gh\_D13G2238) were characteristically abundant in cottonseed ([Fig. 8](#page-7-0)), consistent with the high content of C18:2 in cottonseed oil (more than 55%). Interestingly, GhFAD3 (At2g29980), FAD7 (At3g11170), and FAD8 (At5g05580) genes were barely expressed in developing cottonseed, also in accordance with the minor level of C18:3 in cottonseed oil ([Fig. 1](#page-2-0)C). However, FAD3 was abundantly expressed in Perilla frutescens seeds, in which C18:3 accumulated more than 50% of total fatty acid contents ([Chung et al., 1999\)](#page-10-35), while FAD8 was highly expressed in tree peony seeds, accounting for the high content of C18:3 ([Li et al., 2015b](#page-10-25)). These results suggest that the regulatory pathways in the FAs biosynthesis are conserved, and gene expression levels were correlated positively with FA components in plant seeds.

In the present study, three key stages in developing embryos were selected for transcriptome analysis of oil accumulation in Upland cotton, and expression profiles of genes involved in FA biosynthesis were interpreted. Our results support the idea that FA biosynthesis and related pathways are conserved in plant seeds, and reveal the specific gene expression profiles in cotton crop in comparison with other oil crops. Overall, the special gene expression network of oil accumulation responds the high content of polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) in cottonseed oil.
