#### **SUMMARY**

*Brassica napus* is a recent allopolyploid derived from the hybridization of *B. rapa* ( $A_rA_r$ ) and *B. oleracea* ( $C_oC_o$ ). Because of the high sequence similarity between the  $A_n$  and  $C_n$  subgenomes, it is difficult to provide an accurate landscape of the whole transcriptome of *B. napus*. To overcome this problem, we applied a single-molecule long-read isoform sequencing (Iso-Seq) technique that can produce long reads to explore the complex transcriptome of *B. napus* at the isoform level. From the Iso-Seq data, we obtained 147,698 nonredundant isoforms, capturing 37,403 annotated genes. A total of 18.1% (14,934/82,367) of the multi-exonic genes showed alternative splicing (AS). In addition, we identified 549 long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), the majority of which displayed tissue-specific expression profiles, and detected 7,742 annotated genes that possessed isoforms containing alternative polyadenylation (APA) sites. Moreover, 31,591 AS events located in open reading frame (ORF) lead to potential protein isoforms by in-frame or frameshift changes in the ORF. Illumina RNA-seq of five tissues that were pooled for Iso-Seq was also performed and showed that 69% of the total AS events were tissue-specific. Our data provides abundant transcriptome resources for the transcript isoform catalog of *B. napus*, which will facilitate genome reannotation, strengthen our understanding of the *B. napus* transcriptome and be applied

for further functional genomic research.

**Key words:** *Brassica napus*, polyploidy, single molecule long-read isoform sequencing (Iso-Seq), RNA-Seq, alternative splicing, lncRNAs, alternative polyadenylation

## Introduction

The allotetraploid oilseed rape (*Brassica napus* L.;  $A_nA_nC_nC_n$ ) was derived from the hybridization of *B. rapa* ( $A_rA_r$ ) and *B. oleracea* ( $C_oC_o$ ) nearly 10,000 years ago (Chalhoub *et al.*, 2014). *B. napus*, known as one of the most important oil-producing crops and used mainly as a food resource and biofuel, is an ideal model to study the phenomenon of polyploidy (Banuelos *et al.*, 2010). The genomes of *B. napus* ('Darmor-bzh') and its progenitors were released (Wang *et al.*, 2011, Chalhoub *et al.*, 2014, Liu *et al.*, 2014) as well as 31,526 homoeologous genes in syntenic regions between *B. napus*  $A_n$  and  $C_n$  subgenomes (Chalhoub *et al.*, 2014). However, the transcriptome of *B. napus* has not been fully elucidated due to high heterozygosity and the limitations of sequencing technologies to accurately unveil the transcriptome, specifically in the context of alternative splicing (AS) and alternative polyadenylation (APA).

AS and APA are two important post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms that can enhance the diversity of the transcriptome and, ultimately, the proteome (Nilsen and Graveley, 2010, Elkon *et al.*, 2013, Reddy *et al.*, 2013). Specifically, AS can generate more than one protein from a precursor messenger RNA (pre-mRNA) using different splicing sites (Nilsen and Graveley, 2010, Baralle and Baralle, 2017). Moreover, AS is prevalent in most eukaryotes, and more than 95% and 60% of multi-exonic genes are alternatively spliced in humans and plants, respectively (Pan *et al.*, 2008, Reddy *et al.*, 2013). In addition, several studies have also shown that abiotic stresses, such as heat stress, could induce or significantly impact the regulation of AS in plants (Cheng *et al.*, 2015, Keller *et al.*, 2017). For example, in rice, the gene *OsHSFA2d* encodes two main splice variant proteins that are in an inactive spliced form under normal conditions; however, the gene is alternatively spliced into a transcriptionally active form under heat stress (Cheng *et al.*, 2015). In tomato, more than 76% of the genes in pollen undergo AS in the form of intron retention (IR) or

exon skipping (ES) in plants under heat stress compared with the genes undergoing AS in untreated control plants (Keller *et al.*, 2017). Similarly, APA can also generate transcript variants with different 3' ends (Shen *et al.*, 2011, Elkon *et al.*, 2013). Furthermore, approximately 70% of genes in *Arabidopsis* and 48% of expressed genes in rice possessed multiple polyadenylation sites (Wu *et al.*, 2011, Elkon *et al.*, 2013). Although AS and APA are important post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, they have not been fully explored in *B. napus* thus far.

Over the last decade, expression sequence tag sites (ESTs) and high-throughput technology, for instance, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) using short reads, have been extensively applied to identify AS events at the whole-transcriptomic scale (Marquez *et al.*, 2012, Shen *et al.*, 2014b, Yang *et al.*, 2015). However, it is challenging to construct accurate full-length splicing isoforms from ESTs and RNA-seq through short-read sequence assembly, which especially limits the accuracy of a gene model annotation. To overcome the abovementioned problem of short-read sequencing, isoform sequencing (Iso-Seq) technology has recently been applied in humans and plants (Sharon *et al.*, 2013, Minoche *et al.*, 2015, Abdel-Ghany *et al.*, 2016, Wang *et al.*, 2016, Minio *et al.*, 2018, Zhang *et al.*, 2019). Iso-Seq can provide full-length splice variants and identify other post-transcriptional regulatory events such as AS and APA because of its relatively long reads (Abdel-Ghany *et al.*, 2016, Wang *et al.*, 2016, Li *et al.*, 2017, Wang *et al.*, 2017). Moreover, Iso-Seq has the advantage of distinguishing highly similar sequences between subgenomes in polyploid organisms using relatively accurate full-length reads without assembly.

We applied the PacBio Iso-Seq technique to uncover the complex and comprehensive transcriptome of the allotetraploid *B. napus* using mixed RNA samples from five different tissues. We identified 222,061 AS events, 558 unannotated genes, 549 long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) and 7,742 APA genes from genome-wide full-length transcripts, which could help to improve the current reference annotation. To discover the divergence of AS variants among different tissues, we also sequenced the tissues used for Iso-Seq and heat-shocked callus tissues on an Illumina platform. The results indicated that only a small proportion of AS events were common among all tissues; thus, the majority of AS events were tissue specific. In addition, heat stress induced the most divergence in the AS pattern when the AS patterns from all other tissues were compared with

that of the callus in parallel.

At present, all *B. napus* genes are annotated with a single transcript and lack annotation of AS, APA and other isoforms generated by post-transcriptional mechanisms, which can be found by high-throughput sequencing, especially the Iso-Seq technique. Hence, our data provides valuable resource for exploring the landscape of post-transcriptional phenomena in the complex *B. napus* polyploid system, update gene annotation and drive further research into gene regulatory mechanisms in this organism.

### RESULTS

## Full-length transcriptome sequencing analysis

In this study, we applied the Iso-Seq approach for transcriptomic analysis of *B. napus* (Fig. 1a), which has a polyploid genome of greater complexity than that of other Brassicaceae such as *Arabidopsis thaliana* (Wang *et al.*, 2018). To develop a comprehensive catalog of transcript isoforms, high-quality RNA was extracted from five tissues (root, leaf, bud, silique and callus) of *B. napus* cultivar 'ZS11'. These abovementioned tissues were sampled at different developmental stages and then pooled for construction of Iso-Seq size-fractionated libraries (0-1, 1-2, 2-3 and > 3 kb). After quality control, 31 cells of the PacBio RS II platform generated a total of 1,161,468 reads of inserts (ROIs), including 257,973 in the 0-1 kb library, 341,092 in the 1-2 kb library, 416,398 in the 2-3 kb library, and 146,005 in the >3 kb library (Table 1; Fig. S1). Of these ROIs, 64,293 (22.8%) were classified as non-full-length transcripts, and 838,583 (72.2%) were classified as full-length non-chimeric transcripts based on the presence of 5' primers, 3' primers and poly(A) tails (Fig. S2).

Because of the high sequence similarity between the  $A_n$  and  $C_n$  subgenomes in *B. napus*, only 47% of the full-length reads were uniquely mapped to the reference genome by GMAP (Fig. 2a) (Wu and Watanabe, 2005, Chalhoub *et al.*, 2014). Furthermore, to assign the 412,548 reads that were mapped to multiple gene loci to a certain location of the genome, we used the phasing method (Wang *et al.*, 2018) (Fig. 3a, 3b). As a result, 75% of the reads that were mapped to both homoeologous genes were assigned to the  $A_n$  or  $C_n$  subgenome. The sequences located in certain

gene loci were then collapsed into 147,698 nonredundant consensus isoforms, including 39,276 gene models. The mean length of our Iso-Seq transcripts was 2,487 bp, which was longer than the length of the reference annotations (Fig. 2b). We also compared our full-length isoform data with 101,040 previously reported cDNA sequences that were downloaded from Ensembl Plants Database (ftp://ftp.ensemblgenomes.org/pub/plants/release-37). Among these sequences, 26,346 matched with 63,714 PacBio isoforms (BLASTN; e-value  $\leq$  1e-10, coverage  $\geq$  0.8, identity  $\geq$  0.9) (Fig. S3b). The length of our full-length transcripts (mean 2,554 bp) was much longer than that of the Ensembl Plants sequences (mean 1,190 bp; Fig. S3a). Comparatively, the correction by Long-Read DBG Error Correction (LoRDEC) per-nucleotide error also decreased from 3.17% (0.43% insertions, 1.58% deletions and 1.16% mismatches) to 1.50% (0.26% insertions, 0.27% deletions and 0.97% mismatches) (Fig. S4a). Moreover, the corrected isoforms had a higher Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) completeness (83%) than the uncorrected isoforms (66.5%). We compared the nonredundant 147,698 isoforms against the annotated gene set from the previously sequenced 'Darmor-bzh' genotype (Chalhoub et al., 2014). We found 142,476 isoforms covering 37,403 gene loci, 31,392 of which were multi-exonic genes. As regard to remaining 5,222 isoforms that did not map to annotated genes, we found 4,947 of them could matched to the annotated genes in closely related species, Arabidopsis thaliana, B, rapa and B. oleracea. The result indicated that these transcripts may be unique to the 'ZS11' cultivar and not be presented in the reference genome. These Iso-Seq full-length isoforms that were directly produced from sequencing without assembly are valuable resources for optimizing gene models.

#### Splice junctions (SJs) and AS modes

Iso-Seq technology yields long reads without the aid of assembly and provides superior evidence for identifying AS variants. First, we explored the characterization of SJs and identified a total of 1,378,584 SJs from 39,276 gene models. In above identified SJs, we identified 6,795 of them was caused by reverse-transcription artifacts. With regard to the splicing motif, majority (93.74 %) are the canonical GT-AG, followed by GC-AG with only 1.55 % (Fig. S12a). In addition, the AT-AC

splicing motif presented features suggestive of U12 splicing, including the conserved U12 5 ' junction and branch point sequences (Turunen et al., 2013) (Fig. S13). We found that 15.45 % of SJs were unannotated compared with the SJs from the previous annotation. Moreover, we found that 20,230 annotated multi-exonic genes possessed alternative isoforms; among them, 5,761 genes produced more than five isoforms (Fig. 4b). On average, the Iso-Seq data showed 3.81 isoforms per gene. Here, the transcripts with a class\_code of "=" were defined as "known transcripts", whereas all others (such as "j", "u", "e", "x", and "o") were defined as "unannotated isoforms" using Cuffcompare (Trapnell *et al.*, 2012). Therefore, in total, we detected 128,967 unannotated isoforms. For example, a gene that is annotated to possess a single transcript was found to generate 14 splice isoforms from the Iso-Seq data (Fig. S5). We also detected a total of 222,061 AS events from 15,068 annotated gene loci using the AStalavista tool (Foissac and Sammeth, 2007) without transcript assembly.

The GO enrichment analysis for AS genes showed that their functions were associated with nucleotide binding, ATP binding, catalytic activity and other functions (Fig. 4c). A total of 146,056 (65.8%) of these AS events contained multiple AS types, suggesting that a large amount of complex AS types could be identified using Iso-Seq data. The remaining AS events were classified into four major types: 34,105 IR, 22,011 alternative acceptor (AA), 17,531 alternative donor (AD) and 2,358 ES events (Fig. 4a). Among these four main types of AS events, IR events were the most prominent AS type, followed by AA, AD, and ES, which was consistent with findings of previously reported studies in other plant species (Li *et al.*, 2017, Wang *et al.*, 2017). To verify the AS events detected above, we designed primers for the AS regions of these genes (Table S2) and performed reverse transcription (RT)-PCR. From our results, the size of the gel band fragments was consistent with the splice isoforms identified from the Iso-Seq data (Fig. 5).

Furthermore, we added the analysis of the contribution of the different sub-genomes to AS events based on the 31,526 homoeologous gene pairs reported previously (Chalhoub *et al.*, 2014) and phasing results. 102, 486 AS events occurred among the homoeologous genes from the  $A_n$  subgenome, which were significantly greater than the 82,273 AS events among the genes from the  $C_n$  subgenome (Paired t-test, *P*<0.01), indicating that AS events were more prevalent among genes

of the  $A_n$  subgenome. Our findings indicated that homoeologous genes in  $A_n$  subgenome contributed greater to AS events from the numbers of them when compared with  $C_n$  subgenome.

## Protein domains change caused by AS

Considering the high possibility of assembled errors from Illumina short reads, we used the PacBio data to generate Iso-Seq isoforms and predict ORFs and protein domains. The results showed that 158,731 AS events were located in ORFs and 23.4% of them caused frameshifts generating potentially new protein isoforms. Moreover, the analysis of AS events located in ORFs revealed that the majority of the changed nucleotides were between 1 and 200 bp (Fig. 6a). Furthermore, among these ORF located AS of which 31,591 caused domain changes by in frameshift or in-frame change, 15,638 gained additional domains, 12,745 lost conserved protein domains, and the remaining events presented more complex patterns (Fig. 6b). For example, AS event from the gene "BnaC03g66780D", alter the domain structure (Fig. 6c). To reveal whether the changes in the domains of these protein isoforms have biological significance, further functional studies are required.

#### The identification of unannotated genes and fusion genes

Here, we used the procedure of Transcriptome Analysis Pipeline for Isoform Sequencing (TAPIS) (Abdel-Ghany *et al.*, 2016) for the identification of unannotated genes. From the PacBio Iso-Seq data, we found a total of 558 candidate unannotated genes; these genes had no overlap with any annotated regions. Moreover, these unannotated genes were uniformly distributed in all chromosomes (Fig. 7h). To detect homologs of these genes in other organisms, we used BLAST analyses (see EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES section). From the TBLASTX results, we detected all 558 candidate unannotated gene clusters with significant matches against a collection of plant protein-coding gene cDNAs, and 449 of them were detected in the BLASTX search against Swiss-Prot proteins. Collectively, our results highlighted 449 unannotated genes, which were likely protein-coding genes in *B. napus*. We randomly selected several unannotated genes to verify with RT-PCR (Fig. S6).

Moreover, we also identified the 23 fusion genes (based on alignment analysis) that were located in at least two different annotated gene loci (see EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES section). Twenty of the fusion events occurred inter-chromosomally, and the remaining three events occurred intra-chromosomally. Our results showed that fusion events were more likely to take place inter-chromosomally than intra-chromosomally (Fig. 7h). RT-PCR was performed to validate fusion genes (Fig.S7).

## **LncRNA** identification

Currently, the information about lncRNAs in *B. napus* is insufficient; however, lncRNAs are an important component of a transcriptomic studies. Here, we used PLEK (Li et al., 2014) to build the classification models to identify the coding ability of the isoforms. First, we eliminated the isoforms with ORFs exceeding 100 amino acids. After that, a total of 782 candidate lncRNAs were generated. Furthermore, we screened them using BLASTX for homology with proteins from the UniProt database and then eliminated 229 isoforms. After filtering the isoforms with lengths less than 350 nucleotides, we used BLASTN to scan the remaining 549 transcripts with a high-confidence set of known lncRNAs that were downloaded from the Gramene database. The results revealed that 20 of 549 lncRNAs corresponded to previous lncRNAs (designated known lncRNAs), and the others were designated as "unannotated lncRNAs". The mean length of the unannotated lncRNAs was 1.7 kb (range, 0.4-4.9 kb) (Fig. 7a). Our data showed that 55% of IncRNAs were located in the intergenic region according to reference annotations (Fig. 7b). Besides, 43.7% of lncRNAs and 5.9% of non-lncRNAs were single exonic, which indicated that IncRNAs were more likely to be single exons than non-lnRNAs (Fig. 7c). In addition, the majority of lncRNAs presented a tissue-specific expression pattern from their expression profiling in our data (Fig. 7d). Randomly selected lncRNAs were verified with RT-PCR, which showed that they were reliable (Fig. S8).

Furthermore, we also explored the contribution of the different sub-genomes to lncRNAs, the homoeologous genes in two subgenomes generated 54 and 53 lncRNAs respectively and it showed that the two subgenomes contributed equally (Paired t-test, P > 0.05).

## **APA** analysis

Polyadenylation is an important transcriptional modification in mRNAs in most eukaryotic transcripts (Elkon et al., 2013, Bentley, 2014). APA can increase the transcriptome variety and regulate gene expression by producing transcripts that differ at the 3' end (Shen *et al.*, 2011, Sherstnev et al., 2012, Fu et al., 2016). Investigation of the 3' ends of transcripts using Iso-Seq allowed us to detect polyadenylation sites in *B. napus*. In order to exclude the variation from different software, we applied two programs TAPIS (Abdel-Ghany et al., 2016) and PRAPI (Gao et al., 2018) to identify APA events and we only retained results which can be identified by both of them (Fig. S9). In our study, we detected 32,052 poly(A) sites from 18,612 annotated genes from the Iso-Seq data. Among the 7,742 annotated genes with APA sites, 595 had at least five poly(A) sites (Fig. 7e). On average, 1.72 poly(A) sites per gene were found. Furthermore, we also analyzed the nucleotide composition in the flanking region of all poly(A) sites. There was a clear nucleotide bias for the enrichment of uracil (U) upstream and adenine (A) downstream of poly(A) sites. To verify this finding, we investigated the motifs of 50 nucleotides upstream of predominant sites in all isoforms with the aid of MEME analysis. Furthermore, we identified two conserved over-represented polyadenylation signals, i.e., AAUAAA and UGUA, that were found upstream of poly(A) cleavage sites (Fig. 7g). These conserved motifs were similar to those in previously reported studies on other plants (Abdel-Ghany et al., 2016, Wang et al., 2017). We randomly selected several APA events to validate with 3' rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) (Fig. S10).

In addition, 13,812 and 14,184 poly(A) sites were profiled from homoeologous gene pairs respectively. 3,299 and 3,522 APA genes were characterized among the two subgenomes homoeologous genes. Moreover, the homoeologous genes from  $A_n$  subgenome contributed less to the generation of the polyA sites than that from  $C_n$  subgenome (Paired t-test, P < 0.02).

## Identification of tissue-specific and differential AS variants

To investigate tissue-specific and differential AS variants, we also performed RNA-seq of at least

two biological replicates of the same tissues that were used for PacBio Iso-Seq. To investigate the influence of stresses on AS, we treated callus with heat shock and used these tissues for RNA-seq. In total, 1.23 billion paired-end reads were generated with a length of 150 bp. The Spearman's correlation of biological replicates for all tissue samples was > 0.9 (Fig. S11). Short reads were mapped to the *B. napus* reference genome. Collectively, our data highlighted various kinds of splice motifs, such as GT/AG, GC/AG, AT/AC and other noncanonical sites. In all of the above motifs, GT-AG was the most common splice motif (approximately 97%) in all tissues, followed by GC-AG, AT-AC, and other minor types, which was consistent with our Iso-Seq results (Fig. S12b).

For the quantification of the isoforms from PacBio Iso-Seq, we used RNA-seq data to evaluate isoform expression levels. Only isoforms/genes with FPKM  $\geq 0.01$  were analyzed to reduce transcription noise based on gene coverage saturation analysis. Our results showed that the expression profiles of root and callus tissues were assigned to one cluster, and the other three tissues (leaf, bud, and silique) were assigned to another cluster (Fig. 8a).

Furthermore, we used Illumina RNA-seq data to explore the characterization of AS among different tissues in *B. napus*. A total of 74,106 AS events from 27,336 multi-exonic annotated genes were detected in the five tissues (callus, root, leaf, bud, and silique) (Fig. 8c). 9,048 of 35,286 AS events detected from illumina platform were covered with AS detected from PacBio data by only considering the AS from known loci and were detected by both platforms to ensure a fair comparison. Moreover, the results highlighted that AS genes not only contained a greater number of exons, but also had higher expression values than non-AS genes (Fig. 8d, 8e). In all tissues, IR was dominant and accounted for approximately 50% of the AS events, and the proportions of the four main types of AS were similar in different tissues (Fig. 8b). Among the 74,106 AS events identified in the five different tissues, only 6% were shared in all tissues; however, 69% were tissue specific (Fig. 8f).

We chose the callus, which is undifferentiated tissue, as a reference to detect differential splicing events between different tissues. Furthermore, AS events from the callus were compared with those from other tissues in a pairwise manner using rMATS (Shen *et al.*, 2014a) (Fig. 8g). To

explore the impact of temperature stress on AS, we added the RNA-seq data of heat shock-treated callus (HS-callus) for analysis. Interestingly, most differential AS events were identified in the HS-callus, indicating that environmental factors caused greater variation in AS events than tissue differentiation (Fig. 7g). Nevertheless, root tissues generated the least differential splicing events, which was consistent with the similarity of the expression profile between the root and callus tissues. Furthermore, GO terms enriched for genes undergoing HS-callus-specific AS events were related to the membrane coat, protein targeting, transcription factor activity, localization, the response to temperature stimulus and the positive regulation of cellular processes (Fig. S14). In addition, we performer RT-PCR to validate differential splicing events (Fig. S15).

#### **DISCUSSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS**

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology for comprehensive transcriptome analysis has been extensively applied in many eukaryotic species over the last decade. However, it is challenging to study the transcriptome of polyploids with complex genomes. Short-read sequencing also has a limited scope in providing accurate transcript assembly. The recently developed Iso-Seq technology can produce full-length isoforms, which enables the feasible determination of the whole transcriptome at the isoform level (Abdel-Ghany et al., 2016). In this study, we combined Iso-Seq and Illumina sequencing technologies to provide a global survey of the transcriptome of the allopolyploid *B. napus*. We generated a comprehensive full-length transcript database in *B*. *napus* by pooling RNA from five tissues with four cDNA libraries with size ranges from <1 kb to > 3 kb using the PacBio Iso-Seq approach. Recently, it has reported that template switching take place in a sequential process, which allowed the reverse transcriptase (RT) to switch templates and continue copying the template-switching oligo (TSO) sequence (Wulf et al., 2019). Moreover, the presence of a 5' cap can enhance template switching efficiency, which cause cDNAs from uncapped RNA can also be tagged with 5' adapter sequence. Thus, truncated cDNAs missing part of the original transcripts 5' end sequence can also acquire 5' adapter sequence, which may be misconstrued as full-length transcripts. Nevertheless, a modified Iso-Seq method can partly avoid above mentioned template switching by applying enrichment of full-length cDNA before addition

of PacBio sequencing adapter to improve resolution of transcript ends (Cartolano et al., 2016). In addition, because of the high sequence similarity of the homoeologous genes between the two subgenomes, we applied a phasing method to distinguish the locations of the reads in the reference genome. Although there are some methods or tools that have been developed based on single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) phasing, such as IDP-ASE and HapCUT, to study allele-specific expression and isoforms (Bansal and Bafna, 2008, Deonovic *et al.*, 2017), they are rarely applied to discover full-length isoforms. Here, we selected the phase program embedded in SAMTOOLS, which was recently applied in polyploid cotton (Wang *et al.*, 2018). From our results, almost 75% of reads mapped to homoeologous gene pairs were distributed at relatively accurate locations, which indicates that this method can be relatively effective in distinguishing the location of full-length transcripts especially for the polyploid species with complex genome.

The present *B. napus* annotation includes only a single transcript for each gene, with no information of AS isoforms. From our study, we detected a total of 128,967 unannotated isoforms 86% of were generated from known genes as well as 558 unannotated genes, which can significantly supplement our current genome annotation at the isoform level.. With regard to 23 fusion genes we detected, a part of inter-chromosomal fusion genes may be chimeric cDNAs that were artifactually generated through template-switching driven by short stretches of homology at RNA ends. In addition, we also identified a total of 549 high-confidence lncRNAs, 20 of which were similar to the known lncRNAs. From our results, the majority of lncRNAs were tissue specific and preferentially contained a single exon compared with non-lncRNAs.

Full-length isoform data were also used for the investigation of APA sites in *B. napus* at the whole-genome level. The results showed that 7.7% of annotated genes produced different isoforms with APA sites . There was also evident nucleotide bias of poly(A) sites with the enrichment of uracil (U) upstream and adenine (A) downstream. Based on the abovementioned results, the APA analysis helps us to broaden the knowledge of post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms.

For the AS characterization, we identified a total of 222,061 AS events, and 66% of them contained complex AS events. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that Iso-Seq full-length reads have their own advantage in uncovering the complex isoforms or AS events

without the aid of assembly. Hence, the Iso-Seq approach is essential for isoform detection and transcriptional surveys.

By facilitating the phasing results which distinguish the isoforms from homoeologous genes, we could explore the different contributions of homoeologous genes to transcriptome from the aspects of AS, lncRNA and APA. Our results demonstrate that homoeologous genes in  $A_n$  subgenome contributed greater to AS but less to APA. From our data and study, both post-transcriptional mechanisms AS and APA contributed to the difference in isoforms from homoeologous genes, which may facilitate the diversity of subgenomes' transcriptome and their functional genomic necessary. However, we could not exclude the possibility that much deeper sequencing may identify other isoforms for these homoeologous genes.

To discover whether AS could contribute to the complexity of protein function, we compared the domains of isoforms from same AS event. The results showed that 23.4 % of AS that are located in ORF caused in frameshift by generating new AS isoforms. Moreover, among these AS that are located in ORF, 31,591 induced domain changes by causing in frameshift or in-frame change. Furthermore, we also identified the AS variants in five different tissues based on Illumina-Seq data. When compared distribution of splice motifs from two platform, the percentage of GT-AG from PacBio was little lower than that based on short reads suggesting that PacBio isoforms maintain its complex and original structures especially non-canonical splice motif which made percentage of GT-AG from PacBio lower. Moreover, interestingly, the results revealed that the ratio of four main AS types was similar among different tissues. Among different tissues, only 6% of AS events were common, whereas 69% were tissue specific. This finding suggested that AS may play a role in the regulation of some tissue-specific functions. In addition, the callus under heat stress generated the most differential AS events, which indicated that abiotic stress created higher diversity in terms of AS variants than tissue differentiation and that AS may help plants adapt to unfavorable environmental conditions.

Taken together, we expect that our resources will open avenues in polyploid transcriptome analysis and facilitate future studies to characterize post-transcriptional regulation mechanisms.

### **EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES**

#### **Plant materials**

We used an Asian semi-winter oilseed rape cultivar 'ZS11', which showed a highly genomic collinearity with reference winter-type cultivar 'Darmor-bzh' (Sun *et al.*, 2017) for this study. *B. napus* cultivar 'ZS11' was grown at the Oil Crops Research Institute, Wuhan, China, during the normal growing season. Three biological replicates of leaves and roots were collected at the trefoil stage. Two replicates of buds and three replicates of silique with a length of 1-2 cm were collected at the full-bloom stage (8 days after blooming). For callus regeneration, a hypocotyl explant was transferred to culture medium in the dark and then grown for 3-4 weeks. In addition, half of the callus samples were heat-shocked at 38 °C for 3 h (Apuya and Zimmerman). Then, all samples were quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C.

## **RNA** extraction

Total RNA was extracted by grinding tissue in TRIzol reagent (Tiangen Biotech Co. Ltd, Beijing, China) on dry ice and processed following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. To determine RNA quality, samples were assessed using a NanoDrop microspectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). RNA samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) of  $\geq 8$  were used for the construction of libraries for PacBio sequencing.

### Library preparation and Iso-seq

Total RNA from the five tissues including root, leaf, bud, silique and callus was pooled together in equal amounts. The mixed RNA samples were reverse transcribed into cDNA using the SMARTer® PCR cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara). For PCR amplification of cDNA, a total of 10 to 12 cycles were carried out using the KAPA HiFi PCR Kit. Then, the PCR product was separated into cDNA fraction lengths of 0-1 kb, 1-2 kb, 2-3 kb and >3 kb by agarose gel electrophoresis. These four libraries were constructed with the SMRTbell Template Prep Kit 1.0 (part 100-259-100). After that, all libraries were subsequently sequenced on the PacBio RS II platform

with P6C4 polymerase enzyme and chemistry. Specifically, the 0-1 kb library was sequenced using five cells, the greater than 3 kb library was sequenced using six cells, and the other two libraries were sequenced using ten cells.

#### **Pipeline for Iso-Seq analysis**

The PacBio Iso-Seq raw reads were filtered into circular consensus sequences (CCS) and non-CCS subreads using smrtanalysis 2.3.0 (http://www.pacb.com/products-and-services/analytical-software/smrt-analysis/). After quality evaluation, CCS were processed to generate the ROIs with a minimum full pass of 1 and minimum accuracy of 85. These ROIs were categorized into full-length, non-full-length and chimeric reads using pbtranscript.py from smrtanalysis 2.3.0 based on the presence and location of the 5' primer, 3' primer, and poly(A) tail. Only full-length non-chimeric ROIs were applied for download. All full-length reads were aligned to the *B. napus* reference genome using GMAP software with the parameter of -n 2 (Wu and Watanabe, 2005, Chalhoub et al., 2014). Because of the high sequence similarity between the coexisting  $A_n$  and  $C_n$  subgenomes of *B. napus*, the phase program embedded in SAMTOOLS (v.1.5) (Li et al., 2009) was introduced to distinguish these homoeologous reads based on homoeologous SNP mapping with the reference genome (Wang et al., 2018). After the phasing method was applied, every full-length read was adjusted to a relatively suitable region in the reference genome. Finally, all full-length reads were collapsed by Python script (collapse isoforms by sam.py) in the ToFU a package (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/cDNA primer/) with -min-coverage of 0.95 and -min-identity of 0.95. The alignments of collapsed transcripts in SAM format created using GMAP were transformed to GFF3 format. Then, the collapsed transcripts were corrected by using Illumina data with LoRDEC (Salmela and Rivals, 2014). Non-canonical splicing junctions caused by reverse-transcription artifacts were detected by applying the SQUANTI tool (Tardaguila et al., 2018).

#### Illumina RNA-Seq library construction

The quality of RNA from the three biological replicates of each tissue except the bud (two biological replicates) was assessed using a NanoDrop microspectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). RNA samples with OD260/280  $\geq$ 1.8, OD260/230  $\geq$ 1.8, and 28S/18S  $\geq$ 1.0 were selected for the construction of the libraries. Then, libraries were constructed by 10–11 cycles of PCR amplification, and cDNAs from all samples were sequenced on the HiSeq 4000 PE150 platform at Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI), Wuhan, China.

## Illumina data analysis

We used the NGSQCToolkit v2.3 software (http://www.nipgr.res.in/ngsqctoolkit.html) (Patel and Jain, 2012) to remove the pair-end reads containing more than 5% Ns or 20% of bases whose quality score was less than 20. The high-quality reads were aligned to the reference genome using TopHat2.1.1 (Trapnell et al., 2009) with minimum and maximum intron lengths set to 20 bp and 20 kb, respectively. The differential splicing analysis were performed through program rMATS (Shen et al., 2014a) (http://rnaseq-mats.sourceforge.net/index.html) by pairwise comparison aligned.bam files from callus and those from other tissues with default settings. Differentially spliced events were defined as the FDR was less than 0.05. The examined events included skipped exon (SE), alternative acceptor (AA), alternative donor (AD) and mutually exclusive exons (MXE), and retained intron (RI). The quantification of genes and isoforms was performed using cufflinks version 2.2.1 (Trapnell et al., 2012) based on the GTF annotation file generated by PacBio sequencing and reference file with uniquely mapping short reads. To reduce the transcription noise, saturation analysis was applied. The R package "corrplot" was used to evaluate and visualize the expression profile of the 5,984 B. napus transcription factors between different tissues with hierarchical clusters. The assembled transcripts from the *de novo* assembly tool Trinity (Haas et al., 2013) were mapped to the reference genome using GMAP (Wu and Watanabe, 2005) and filtered with > 85% alignment coverage and > 90% alignment identity.

### **Characterization of AS events**

To identify the AS events, we employed the AStalavista algorithm (Foissac and Sammeth, 2007) based on the assembled GTF file from the PacBio Iso-Seq and Illumina data. Four major types of AS events were classified by AS codes: IR (0, 1^2-), AA (1-, 2-), AD (1^, 2^) and ES (0, 1-2^). The in-house Perl script was used for statistical analysis.

## Identification of the AS induced domain changes from Iso-Seq data

First, we predicted the longest ORF for each PacBio isoform using EMBOSS. Then, we computed the length of changed nucleotides if the AS events located in ORF regions by comparing the alternative introns from two isoforms in a single AS event. For IR and ES, it was the length of the retained intron and skipped exon. For AA and AD, the length corresponded to the altered nucleotides that were present in the exon of one isoform, but included in the intron of the other one. We classified a string of nucleotides changes as in frameshift if the changed nucleotides located in ORF regions between AS variants are not multiples of three base pairs. In other word, the else nucleotides changes were in-frame change. Afterwards, we searched for conserved protein domains against the Pfam database version 31 by HMMER v3.1b2 (http://hmmer.janelia.org/) from predicted ORFs (e-value <0.01)(Finn *et al.*, 2016). Last, we compared conserved protein domains of AS flanking regions between splicing variants from single AS event to identify the changes in the domain from Iso-Seq data.

## LncRNA identification from PacBio data

PLEK was used to build a model using the 206,719 known high-confidence lncRNAs and microRNA precursors downloaded from Gramene release 52. All PacBio-generated isoforms were predicted based on the model, and ORFs were predicted with EMBOSS. Then, transcripts encoding ORFs longer than 100 amino acids or a nucleotide sequence of less than 350 bp were filtered. The remaining transcripts were further screened against protein sequences using UniProt (ftp://ftp.uniprot.org/pub/databases/uniprot/) with BLASTX (e-value, 1e-10). Finally, BLASTN was used to find the homolog of previously identified lncRNAs with the criteria: e-value  $\leq$  1e-10, min-identity = 90% and min-coverage = 85%.

### APA and unannotated gene analysis from PacBio data

APA sites and unannotated genes were identified using two softwares TAPIS (Abdel-Ghany et al., 2016) and PRAPI (Gao et al., 2018) and only consistent results detected from both of them were retained for further analysis. To identify the poly(A) sequence signals, we performed a MEME-Chip analysis based on the sequence of 50 nucleotides upstream of the poly(A) sites with the options "meme-chip -time 300 -order -db -oc. -rna 1 db/RNA/Ray2013 rbp All Species.dna encoded.meme -meme-mod zoops -meme-minw 6 -meme-maxw 6 -meme-nmotifs 3 -dreme-e 0.05 -centrimo-score 5.0 -centrimo-ethresh 10.0" from the MEME suite website. The cDNA sequences from Ensembl Plants, release 37 (ftp://ftp.ensemblgenomes.org/pub/plants/release-37) and protein sequences from UniProt were used to find the homologs of the unannotated genes using TBLASTX and BLASTX analysis with the criterion :-e-value  $\leq$  1e-6.

### Fusion transcript identification from PacBio data

Here, we applied a Python program (fusion\_finder.py) in the PBTRANSCRIPT-TOFU package (http://github.com/PacificBiosciences/cDNA\_primer/) to identify fusion transcripts. The full-length transcripts were mapped to  $\geq 2$  loci in the reference genome with at least 10 kb distance. Then, each mapped locus was aligned with at least 10% of the transcript. The combined alignment coverage of fusion transcripts was at least 99%.

## **RT-PCR** validation

Total RNA was isolated from the same samples as described above for Iso-Seq using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg total RNA in 20 µl solution using the PrimeScript<sup>TM</sup> RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa Co., LTD, Beijing, China). PRIMER PREMIER software (v.5.0) was applied to design the primers spanning the splicing events. Ex Taq MasterMix (CWBIO, CW0690H) was used for PCR amplification. PCR products were visualized in agarose gel stained with SuperRed/GelRed (Biosharp BS354B). To

validate APA events, we used 3'RACE (TIANDZ, CAT#:101104-10) strategy for cDNA synthesis and PCR amplification. PCR was carried out using cDNA, gene-specific forward primer and adaptor primer. The primers of the validation were listed in Table S2.
