- 18 **Short title:** Arabidopsis bidirectional TSSs and antisense transcripts
- 19

20 **One sentence summary:** We report the existence of bidirectionally transcribed loci producing 21 exosome-sensitive RNAs in Arabidopsis in which the produced RNAs are sources of small 22 interfering RNAs, and exosome-sensitive transcripts antisense to genes encoding regulatory 23 proteins, in particular transcription factors.

#### 31 **ABSTRACT**

32 In animals, RNA polymerase II initiates transcription bidirectionally from gene promoters to 33 produce pre-mRNAs on the forward strand and promoter upstream transcripts (PROMPTs) on 34 the reverse strand. PROMPTs are degraded by the nuclear exosome. Previous studies based 35 on nascent RNA approaches concluded that Arabidopsis does not produce PROMPTs*.* Here, 36 we used steady-state RNA sequencing in mutants defective in nuclear RNA decay including the 37 exosome to reassess the existence of Arabidopsis PROMPTs. While they are rare, we identified 38 ~100 cases of exosome-sensitive PROMPTs in Arabidopsis. Such PROMPTs are sources of 39 small interfering RNAs in exosome-deficient mutants, perhaps explaining why plants have 40 evolved mechanisms to suppress PROMPTs. In addition, we found ~200 transcription start sites 41 within 3'-UTR-encoding regions that produce long, unspliced, and exosome-sensitive antisense 42 RNAs. The previously characterized non-coding RNA that regulates expression of the key seed 43 dormancy regulator, *DELAY OF GERMINATION 1*, is a typical representative of this class of 44 RNAs. Transcription factor genes are overrepresented among loci with exosome-sensitive 45 antisense RNAs, suggesting a potential for widespread control of gene expression via this class 46 of ncRNAs. Lastly, we assess the use of alternative promoters in Arabidopsis and compare the 47 accuracy of existing TSS annotations.

#### 49 **INTRODUCTION**

50 The vast majority of promoters of mammalian protein-coding genes initiates transcription by 51 RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) on both strands, producing a sense pre-mRNA transcript and a 52 shorter, unstable RNA on the reverse strand, denominated promoter upstream transcript 53 (PROMPT) or upstream antisense RNA (uaRNA) (Core et al., 2008; Seila et al., 2008; Preker et 54 al., 2008). Transcription of PROMPTs and pre-mRNAs initiates in opposite directions from two 55 separate core promoters, where PROMPT and pre-mRNA TSSs are located at each edge of the 56 nucleosome depleted region (NDR) (Ntini et al., 2013; Andersson et al., 2014a, 2014b). Unlike 57 pre-mRNAs, PROMPTs are rapidly degraded by the nuclear exosome (Preker et al., 2008), a 58 central player in RNA degradation. The core exosome is a highly conserved 9-subunit complex 59 consisting of a central, hexameric barrel composed of ribosomal RNA-processing proteins 60 (RRPs) and the mRNA TRANSPORT REGULATOR 3 (MTR3) (as a trimer of RRP41-RRP42, 61 RRP45-RRP46 and RRP43-MTR3 heterodimers), and a 3-subunit lid containing the proteins 62 RRP4, RRP40, and CSL4 (CEP1 SYNTHETIC LETHAL protein 4). 3'-5' exonucleases associate 63 with the core exosome, and the resulting exosome machinery constitutes the main 3'-5' 64 exonuclease activity both in the nucleus and in the cytoplasm (Chlebowski et al., 2013; Kilchert 65 et al., 2016). The exosome sensitivity of PROMPTs correlates with the frequency of occurrence 66 of specific DNA sequence elements downstream of the PROMPT transcription start sites (TSS): 67 compared to the coding strand, the PROMPT region is enriched in TSS-proximal 68 polyadenylation (poly(A)) sites and depleted of splice donor sites (Almada et al., 2013; Core et 69 al., 2014; Ntini et al., 2013).

70

71 The pattern of bidirectional transcription initiation is not exclusive to promoters of coding 72 genes, but appears to be generic for RNAPII initiation in metazoans: it is also detectable at long 73 noncoding RNA (lncRNA) promoters (Andersson et al., 2014b) and in particular at active 74 enhancers (Kim et al., 2010). We and others have previously shown that enhancer regions can 75 be accurately predicted as sites of bidirectional initiation of unstable transcripts on the basis of 76 either RNA-seq (Wu et al., 2014), sequencing of capped RNA 5'-ends (Andersson et al., 77 2014a), or nascent RNA approaches (Core et al., 2014).

78

79 The similarity of transcription initiation patterns between different RNA classes has 80 spurred the hypothesis that transcription by RNAPII is subject to the same rules irrespective of 81 the RNA that is produced, so that bidirectionally transcribed NDRs constitute a generic 82 transcription initiation block, and the fate and function of RNAs produced are determined after 83 transcription initiation (Chen et al., 2017; Andersson et al., 2015b, also reviewed by Andersson 84 and Sandelin, 2020). It is currently an open question whether this framework is shared between 85 eukaryotes. The majority of enhancers and a substantial fraction of gene promoters were 86 recently shown to be divergently transcribed in *Drosophila melanogaster* S2 cells (Rennie et al., 87 2018) and larvae (Meers et al., 2018). In yeast species, the number of enhancers is small due to 88 their compact genomes. Promoter bidirectionality was observed in budding yeast 89 (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) (reviewed by Jensen et al., 2013), and in at least a subset of 90 promoters in fission yeast (*Schizosaccharomyces pombe*) (Shetty et al., 2017; Thodberg et al., 91 2018).

92

93 In flowering plants, only a handful of studies on a limited number of species has 94 investigated this question in depth, using approaches in which nascent RNA is either labeled 95 and affinity-purified from isolated nuclei (Global Run-On sequencing, GRO-seq (Core et al., 96 2008) and GRO-cap), or isolated as RNAPII-associated RNA by immunoprecipitation (Native 97 Elongating Transcript sequencing, NET-seq). Analysis of Arabidopsis (*Arabidopsis thaliana*)

98 seedlings by GRO-seq provided little support for divergent transcription from promoters of 99 protein-coding genes (Hetzel et al., 2016). Likewise, analysis of average GRO-seq and NET-100 seq signals around TSSs also led Zhu et al. to conclude that such promoters are unidirectional 101 in Arabidopsis (Zhu et al., 2018). One study in maize (*Zea mays*) also found little evidence for 102 bidirectional transcription using GRO-seq (Erhard et al., 2015). However, a precision nuclear 103 run-on sequencing (PRO-seq) analysis of cassava (*Manihot esculenta*) and a re-analysis of the 104 maize GRO-seq data generated by Erhard et al. showed many instances of clear bidirectional 105 transcription at intergenic loci that may be candidate enhancers (Lozano et al., 2018). In 106 addition, the same study identified some cases of bidirectional transcription from promoters of 107 protein-coding genes in cassava (Lozano et al., 2018). Although both GRO-seq and NET-seq 108 data clearly identify RNA populations distinct from the steady-state pool, several concerns may 109 be raised on negative findings based on these techniques. First, GRO-seq can have limited 110 power to detect weakly transcribed loci such as PROMPTs if the sequencing depth is low 111 (Andersson et al., 2015a), and indeed, many of the published plant GRO-seq studies suffer from 112 a lack of sample replicates. Second, RNA species may escape detection if their degradation is 113 rapid and occurs co-transcriptionally, even if the techniques successfully capture many other 114 species of nascent RNA. For these reasons, it is not completely clear whether plant promoters 115 are truly unidirectional, in particular because of conflicting conclusions reached in maize (Erhard 116 et al., 2015; Lozano et al., 2018). The extent of bidirectional transcription in genomic regions 117 other than gene promoters is similarly a question worthy of further investigation.

118

119 Although nascent RNA techniques also support bidirectional transcription at gene 120 promoters in mammals (Andersson et al., 2015a), one of the most instrumental techniques in 121 uncovering this aspect of RNAPII function has been the analysis of steady-state RNA in 122 reference cells compared to cells in which components of the nuclear RNA exosome machinery 123 were depleted (Preker et al., 2008; Ntini et al., 2013). In Arabidopsis, loss of function mutants of 124 the core exosome subunits *RRP4* and *RRP41* are not viable. In contrast, null alleles of the third 125 lid subunit *CSL4* displays surprisingly mild, if any, growth phenotypes, and few molecular 126 phenotypes (Chekanova et al., 2007). These findings initially inspired Chekanova et al. to 127 complete a thorough genome-wide survey of RNA exosome substrates in Arabidopsis using 128 inducible RNA interference lines targeting *RRP4* and *RRP41* in combination with whole-genome 129 tiling arrays (Chekanova et al., 2007). This pioneering study did notice that, upon exosome 130 depletion, a group of polyadenylated RNA species referred to as upstream non-coding 131 transcripts (UNTs) exhibited a conspicuous accumulation around the 5'-ends of a subset of 132 protein-coding genes. However, the poorly resolved TSS locations available at the time made it 133 unclear to what extent UNTs generally overlap with 5'-ends of coding transcripts. Indeed, 134 whereas PROMPTs in mammals were originally also found using comparative hybridization to 135 whole-genome tiling arrays and appeared to originate from both forward and reverse strands, 136 the identification of exosome-sensitive UNTs in Arabidopsis has not been followed up by more 137 powerful RNA sequencing analyses. Thus, a comparative Arabidopsis study using steady-state 138 RNA-seq and RNA 5'-tag sequencing approaches on wild type and mutants defective in nuclear 139 RNA decay components would be a useful complement to nascent RNA studies, not only 140 because of the potential limitations of these techniques, but also because of the original 141 identification of UNTs upon exosome knockdown. Moreover, because of the much higher 142 sensitivity and precision of modern-day sequencing approaches compared to tiling arrays used 143 earlier (Chekanova et al., 2007), such approaches may also inform on other classes of cryptic 144 transcripts in Arabidopsis*,* and on the nuclear RNA degradation pathways, including the 145 exosome, involved in their rapid turnover.

148 Exosomal RNA decay in vivo depends crucially on adaptor proteins of the DEAD-box 149 RNA helicase family that funnel RNA substrates to the exosome (Lykke-Andersen et al., 2009). 150 Plants encode at least three such adaptors: similar to yeast and mammals, SUPERKILLER 2 151 (SKI2) is required for exosomal degradation of cytoplasmic RNA substrates, including 152 endonucleolytic mRNA cleavage fragments (Branscheid et al., 2015). However, in contrast to *S.*  153 *cerevisiae* and mammals, two different nuclear DEAD-box helicase adaptors are encoded in 154 plant genomes: MTR4 localizes to the nucleolus and has specific functions in ribosomal RNA 155 processing (Lange et al., 2011), while HUA ENHANCER 2 (HEN2) localizes to the nucleoplasm 156 and is required for the degradation of a wide range of RNA substrates, including many non-157 coding RNAs (Lange et al., 2014). In addition to the RNA exosome, less well-characterized 158 nuclear 5'-3' exonucleolytic pathways exist. These pathways may involve the nuclear 5'-3' 159 exonucleases EXORIBONUCLEASE 2 (XRN2) and XRN3. XRN2 has documented functions in 160 ribosomal RNA cleavage (Zakrzewska-Placzek et al., 2010), while XRN3 has more identified 161 nuclear substrates, including RNA polymerase II-associated RNA and cleavage products by 162 DICER-LIKE enzymes, the former giving rise to defects in transcription termination in *xrn3* 163 mutants (Krzyszton et al., 2018). In addition, the heptameric nuclear LIKE Smith (Lsm) protein 164 complex, Lsm2-8, a well-established pre-mRNA splicing factor (Bouveret et al., 2000; Tharun et 165 al., 2000), also plays roles in nuclear RNA decapping and, ultimately, decay in Arabidopsis, 166 presumably via 5'-3' exonucleolysis (Golisz et al., 2013). In yeast, mammals, and plants, Lsm2- 167 8 shares six of its subunits, Lsm2 to Lsm7, with the cytoplasmic decapping activator Lsm1-7, 168 such that Lsm8 is the only subunit specific to the nuclear Lsm complex (Perea-Resa et al., 169 2012).

170

171 In this study, we used a null allele in *hen2* , a hypomorphic loss-of-function mutant in the core 172 exosome subunit *RRP4*, and an *lsm8* null allele for transcriptome-wide studies. We applied 173 transcriptome-wide sequencing of capped 5' ends of RNAs (CAGE, Takahashi et al., 2012), 174 small RNA-seq, and RNA-seq using RNA isolated from mutant and wild-type seedlings to 175 identify transcripts subject to preferential nuclear degradation. In agreement with the results of 176 nascent RNA analyses, we found that the majority of promoters of coding genes only have 177 CAGE signal on the sense strand, even when the exosome complex or its associated helicase 178 system was inactivated. However, unambiguous exceptions to these observations exist: we 179 found 96 mRNA TSSs with robust PROMPT-like configurations that produce exosome-sensitive 180 transcripts, supported by CAGE and RNA-seq, and, in some cases, by earlier GRO-seq 181 datasets. Interestingly, our small RNA-seq data showed that regions with detectable PROMPTs 182 in exosome mutants became a source of 21-22 nt small interfering RNAs, pointing to the 183 potential danger of widespread PROMPT production in plants. We also found 113 non-coding 184 regions featuring bidirectional transcription of exosome-sensitive RNAs reminiscent of active 185 enhancers in vertebrates. While exosome mutants did not reveal widespread bidirectional 186 transcription initiation as in mammalian genomes, a striking feature of the transcriptional output 187 in Arabidopsis not present to the same degree in mammalian systems was a frequent 188 occurrence of exosome-sensitive RNAs transcribed antisense to genes. Such transcripts 189 commonly initiated in 3'-UTR regions of cognate genes, were ~1,000 nt long, typically 190 unspliced, and, in many cases, occurred in genes encoding transcription factors (TFs). In 191 addition, we show that the set of active TSSs in wild type plants presented here has higher 192 accuracy than current TSS annotations such as TAIR10 (Berardini et al., 2015) and, in 193 particular, ARAPORT11 (Cheng et al., 2017). We also demonstrate the prevalence of 194 alternative TSSs in the wild type transcriptome. Our data, therefore, constitute an essential 195 resource for gene regulation and RNA degradation research in Arabidopsis.

#### 197 **RESULTS**

#### 198 **Generation of a Comprehensive Map of Accurate Arabidopsis Transcription Start Sites**

199 Investigations of bidirectional transcription are crucially dependent on highly accurate 200 measurements of TSS locations and activity. As a baseline for further analysis, we prepared 201 CAGE libraries from RNA purified from 14 d-old Arabidopsis Col-0 wild-type seedlings in 202 biological triplicates (**Figure 1A**). CAGE reads were mapped to the TAIR10 genome with an 203 average of 20.8 million (83.4% of total) uniquely mapped reads per replicate. As described 204 previously, gene TSSs in complex genomes, including Arabidopsis (Tokizawa et al., 2017), are 205 often locally dispersed, a phenomenon often referred to as 'broad promoters' (Carninci et al., 206 2006). It is therefore helpful to cluster CAGE tags with closely spaced 5'-ends on the same 207 strand into CAGE tag clusters (TCs). We created tag clusters across all libraries and calculated 208 the normalized expression of each library as tags per million mapped reads (TPM). Because a 209 TC may be composed of multiple adjacent TSSs, we will use the 'TC' term for CAGE initiation 210 events rather than just 'TSS', which we will use for annotated 5'-ends and data from other 211 methods. **Figure 1B** shows an example of CAGE tags identifying the annotated TSS for the 212 gene encoding *TRANSMEMBRANE PROTEIN 97* (At2g32380).

213

214 While CAGE mapping is independent of gene annotation, it is often useful for 215 downstream analysis to assess the overlap of TCs and gene annotation. As in (Thodberg et al., 216 2018), we devised a hierarchical annotation scheme (**Figure 1C**) based on gene models from 217 either TAIR10 or ARAPORT11 and counted the number of wild type CAGE TCs located in each 218 type of annotated region (**Figure 1D, left**). As expected, the vast majority of TCs fell into 219 annotated promoter regions (+/– 100 bp around the 5'-ends of gene models from ARAPORT11 220 or TAIR10), although ARAPORT11 promoters accounted for a smaller fraction of TCs than 221 TAIR10. All other categories accounted for only a minor fraction of TCs when using TAIR10, but 222 a much larger fraction of TCs mapped to 5'-UTR regions when using ARAPORT11. As 223 observed previously in human, mouse and *S. pombe* (Boyd et al., 2018; Bornholdt et al., 2017; 224 Thodberg et al., 2018), CAGE TCs falling into either promoter or 5'-UTR regions were 225 substantially more highly expressed than other categories (**Figure 1D, right**).

226

227 For the TAIR10 annotation, the CAGE signal peaked sharply at annotated TSSs. In 228 contrast, for ARAPORT11, the distribution of CAGE signal around annotated TSSs was much 229 broader (**Figure 1E**), pointing to possible inaccuracies in TSS annotation in ARAPORT11. 230 Indeed, the CAGE TCs that were differentially categorized in ARAPORT11 *vs.* TAIR10 and fell

231 within the annotation category 'promoter' in TAIR10 were almost exclusively annotated as 5'- 232 UTR region in ARAPORT11, while CAGE tags falling into the 'proximal' region in TAIR10 (–100 233 to –400 relative to annotated TSS) were frequently annotated as 'promoter' in ARAPORT11 234 (**Figure 1F**; all categories are defined as in **Figure 1C**, based on respective gene models). 235 These observations indicate that TAIR10 TSSs are in general agreement with CAGE TCs, while 236 ARAPORT11 TSSs are, on average, located upstream of CAGE TCs/TAIR10 TSSs.

# 238 **The TSS Annotation in TAIR10 is Substantially More Accurate than in ARAPORT11**

239 Since TSS accuracy is a crucial feature of any genome annotation, we decided to 240 assess rigorously which of the TSS annotations (TAIR10 and ARAPORT11) was more accurate. 241 To this end, we designed a series of quality control measures, and also considered two

242 previously published genome-wide TSS datasets in our analyses: paired-end analysis of 243 transcription sequencing (PEAT-seq) of 7 d-old seedling roots (Morton et al., 2014), and parallel 244 analysis of RNA 5'-ends (nanoPARE) of flower buds (Schon et al., 2018) (see **Supplemental**  245 **Table 1** for details about tissues and growth conditions of the different large-scale experiments 246 used). The majority (62%) of TCs/TSSs was supported by at least two methods, with 247 nanoPARE having the highest fraction of called TCs/TSSs without support from the other 248 methods (25%), compared to CAGE (12%) and PEAT (1%) (**Figure 2A**, see Methods). The 249 substantial overlap is noteworthy, given that the tissues analyzed differ between studies: whole 250 seedlings (CAGE), roots (PEAT-seq) and flower buds (nanoPARE). Notably, the number of 251 TSSs defined by PEAT-seq was much lower than in the other approaches and 95% were 252 supported by CAGE TCs (**Figure 2A, B**). Closer inspection showed that PEAT-seq TSSs had 253 an overall higher expression (as measured by CAGE tags, see Methods) than CAGE TCs. This 254 indicates that the two methods likely detected the same initiation events, although CAGE was 255 more sensitive and therefore uncovered TSSs of more weakly expressed genes (**Figure 2B**).

256

257 We reasoned that the most accurate TSS annotation set should be best at recapitulating 258 known promoter biology in terms of accessible DNA, sequence characteristics, and data from 259 nascent RNA assays. To make the analyses comparable between sets and focus on TSSs 260 expressed in our samples, we only assessed TCs/TSSs that had a CAGE expression ≥ 1 TPM 261 in at least two replicates in the +/–100 bp region around TCs peaks or annotated TSSs 262 (depending on analysis, see below). First, we assessed core promoter patterns by constructing 263 sequence logos (Schneider and Stephens, 1990) from DNA sequences around annotated TSSs 264 from TAIR10 and ARAPORT11, TC peaks from CAGE, and TSS sets from PEAT-seq and 265 nanoPARE (**Figure 2C**). Sequence logos constructed based on CAGE TCs resulted in the 266 expected patterns observed in mammals (Carninci et al., 2006) and Arabidopsis (Yamamoto et 267 al., 2009; Morton et al., 2014): a strong pyrimidine-purine (PyPu) signal at positions –1/+1 268 relative to the TC peak, corresponding to the central part of the initiator (Inr) motif, and a TA-rich 269 pattern at positions –35 to –27, corresponding to the TATA-box (**Figure 2C**). TSSs defined by 270 PEAT-seq and nanoPARE showed a highly similar pattern, whereas TSSs defined by TAIR10- 271 annotated TSSs had weaker TATA-box and pyrimidine-purine dinucleotide patterns. 272 Remarkably, both signatures were almost absent around ARAPORT11-annotated TSSs, 273 strongly indicating that ARAPORT11 poorly captures these central elements of promoter 274 biology. Second, we assessed the average signal of nascent RNA approaches relative to 275 TCs/TSSs. Three datasets were used: GRO-seq from two different laboratories (Hetzel et al., 276 2016; Zhu et al., 2018) (from 6 d-old seedlings and inflorescence tissues, respectively; 277 **Supplemental Table 1**) and 5' GRO-cap from one (Hetzel et al., 2016). We observed a 278 consistent shift of GRO-seq and GRO-cap signal maxima in accordance with the results 279 discussed above: TCs/TSSs defined by CAGE, PEAT-seq, and nanoPARE yielded highly 280 similar locations of GRO-seq maxima 35-40 bp downstream of the TSS (**Figure 2D-F**). For 281 TAIR10-annotated TSSs, we observed a slight 3'-shift in the GRO-cap maximum, and this shift 282 became substantial for ARAPORT11-annotated TSSs (**Figure 2D**). The same trend was 283 observed using GRO-seq signal from two different laboratories (**Figure 2E, F**). Third, to get a 284 measure of nucleosome occupancy of genomic DNA around TSSs defined by the different 285 annotations, we analyzed micrococcal nuclease-seq (MNase-seq) data from young Arabidopsis 286 flower buds (Zhang et al., 2015)*;* **Supplemental Table 1**)*.* MNase-seq showed similar peaks 287 and amplitudes when centered on TSSs defined by CAGE, PEAT-seq, and nanoPARE: In all of 288 these cases, the first peak corresponding to the center of the +1 nucleosome was located on 289 average 105 bp downstream from the TSS (+105 bp, **Figure 2G**). As above, we observed a 290 small 3'-shift when centering on TAIR10-annotated TSSs (+119 bp), and a substantially shifted 291 peak maximum and lower amplitude when using ARAPORT11-annotated TSSs (+145 bp, 292 **Figure 2G**). Taken together, these results indicate that CAGE, PEAT-seq, and nanoPARE are 293 the most accurate TSS sets, closely followed by TAIR10-annotated TSSs. In contrast, 294 ARAPORT11 TSSs are misplaced by 128 bp upstream on average, possibly due to the 295 overestimation of transcript lengths, as suggested previously (Schon et al., 2018). Because 296 TSSs annotated in TAIR10 were in better agreement with TSS definition by CAGE, PEAT-seq, 297 and nanoPARE than those annotated in ARAPORT11, we used the TAIR10 annotation in all 298 subsequent analyses.

# 300 **Most Arabidopsis Genes Expressed in Unchallenged Wild Type Seedlings Only Use One** 301 **Promoter**

302 The use of alternative promoters or TSSs is an important process for generating RNA 303 isoforms in complex genomes (Davuluri et al., 2008; Valen et al., 2009; Pal et al., 2011). We 304 define alternative TSSs as transcription initiation events that are distant from each other but 305 within the same gene. Therefore, local dispersions of TSSs at a single promoter (so-called 306 'broad TSS distributions', Carninci et al., 2006) are not considered alternative TSSs in this 307 analysis. Alternative promoters can generate mRNAs lacking one or more coding exons or 308 upstream open reading frames and may have an impact on protein abundance and function. To 309 assess the extent of alternative promoter usage, we counted the number of wild type TCs 310 overlapping each TAIR10-annotated gene, only retaining those that contributed ≥ 10% of the 311 total CAGE expression across the gene in order to filter out minor events, as in (Thodberg et al., 312 2019). Our analysis showed that the vast majority of genes only used one TC (90%, **Figure 3A**), 313 located in the annotated promoter (**Figure 3B**), although we also found a substantial number of 314 genes with multiple TCs. For instance, 1,632 genes (9% of all expressed genes) had two TCs, 315 each contributing at least 10% to the total expression of those individual genes (**Figure 3A**). 316 When two TCs or more were observed, they mostly occurred either in the annotated promoter 317 or in 5'-UTRs (**Figure 3B**). The few TCs occurring within protein-coding exons were typically not 318 the most highly expressed TCs within the gene. A full list of genes and corresponding 319 coordinates of TCs has been assembled in **Supplemental Data Set 1,** and two illustrative 320 examples are shown in **Figure 3C-D.** In **Figure 3C**, the most used TC coincides with an 321 annotated shorter protein-coding transcript, while **Figure 3D** illustrates a minor TC occurring at

322 the very end of the gene, which is unlikely to yield a protein-coding RNA. These results indicate 323 that in unchallenged wild type Arabidopsis seedlings, the use of alternative promoters, resulting 324 in mRNAs encoding different proteins, is uncommon. In contrast, several studies have 325 documented the widespread occurrence and functional importance of alternative transcription 326 initiation sites in different tissues or upon perception of environmental change, most notably light 327 quality and availability (Yamamoto et al., 2009; Ushijima et al., 2017; Kurihara et al., 2018).

#### 329 **Analysis of Bidirectional TSS Activity at Promoters of Protein-Coding Genes**

330 We next focused on the critical problem of promoter directionality. To comprehensively 331 map transcripts rapidly degraded by the nuclear exosome, we prepared triplicate CAGE and 332 ribosomal-RNA-depleted total RNA-seq libraries from *hen2-4* and *rrp4-2* mutant seedlings. 333 *hen2-4* is a null allele caused by an exonic insertion of a T-DNA (Lange et al., 2014), while the 334 hypomorphic loss-of-function *rrp4-2* allele encodes a RRP4 protein containing a mutation in a 335 conserved residue adjacent to the N-Terminal Domain (NTD) that forms the interface of the core 336 complex with the RRP6 exonuclease (Hématy et al., 2016). Principal component analysis (PCA) 337 on CAGE TC level showed that wild type (as analyzed in **Figures 1 and 2**), *hen2-4,* and *rrp4-2* 338 samples were all separable by the first principal component; *rrp4-2* was the most dissimilar from 339 wild type (**Supplemental Figure 1A**).

340

341 Our first objective was to use the data to assess the occurrence of PROMPT-like, 342 exosome-sensitive transcripts in Arabidopsis. In vertebrates, TSSs of protein-coding genes tend 343 to be located at the edges of DNase I hypersensitive sites (DHSs), marking open chromatin 344 regions, close to the +1 and –1 nucleosomes. We therefore analyzed the overlap between 1) 345 the DHS regions identified in leaves or flowers (closed buds) of 14 d-old plants (Zhang et al., 346 2012) (see **Supplemental Table 1**) and 2) our CAGE data: 89.7% of CAGE tags from wild type 347 fell within identified DHS regions, which echoes results from HeLa cells (Andersson et al., 348 2014b). This observation thus justifies the use of our CAGE data together with the DHS-seq 349 data of Zhang et al. (2012).

350

351 We first selected DHSs that overlapped with annotated TSSs of mRNAs. To distinguish 352 sense from antisense CAGE signal, DHS regions were attributed the same strand as the mRNA 353 TSS they overlapped with. We then constructed a metaplot of the average CAGE TPM signal 354 using the DNase I maxima (DHS summits, DHSSs) as anchor points. This analysis showed that 355 the sense (mRNA) CAGE signal was on average 25-fold higher than the antisense signal, 356 regardless of whether nuclear exosomal RNA decay systems were functional (**Figure 4A**). 357 Specifically, we did not detect a high average enrichment of upstream signal on the reverse 358 strand in exosome mutants, as would have been expected if exosome-sensitive PROMPTs 359 were prevalent (**Figure 4A**). These observations suggest that divergent transcription resulting in 360 exosome-sensitive PROMPTs at promoters of protein-coding genes is not widespread in 361 Arabidopsis, thus agreeing with previous studies (Hetzel et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2018). To 362 ensure that these results were not specific to the CAGE technique, we also plotted RNA-seq 363 reads from wild type and *rrp4-2* mutant seedlings. The outcome was similar, albeit with an 364 expected shift downstream of TSSs due to the inherent characteristics of RNA-seq data, which 365 tend to miss RNA fragments at the 5' and 3' edges of transcripts (**Figure 4B**).

## 367 **Inactivation of Nuclear Decapping does not Promote Detection of PROMPTs**

368 Two different scenarios may explain the overall low CAGE and RNA-seq signal in 369 exosome mutants upstream of mRNA TSSs on the reverse strand: 1) there is on average little 370 transcription initiation in these regions (in agreement with the lack of GRO-seq signal (Hetzel et 371 al., 2016)), or 2) transcription of PROMPTs may occur, but Arabidopsis may employ redundant 372 RNA decay systems for their degradation, precluding their detection by simple inactivation of the 373 nuclear exosome. To explore the latter possibility, we extended the RNA-seq experiments to 374 additional mutants defective in nuclear decapping (and hence 5'-3' exonucleolysis), or both 375 nuclear decapping and exosome activities. To this end, we used the *lsm8-2* mutant (Perea-376 Resa et al., 2012; Golisz et al., 2013) and constructed a *rrp4-2 lsm8-2* double mutant in which 377 both RRP4 and LSM8 functions were missing. RNA-seq metaplots of both *lsm8-2* single and 378 *rrp4-2 lsm8-2* double mutants were highly similar to the wild type (**Figure 4B**), strongly 379 indicating that LSM8 is not part of a redundant pathway for general PROMPT degradation.

#### 381 **Clear PROMPTs Can be Detected at Individual Genes**

382 Despite the low average signal upstream of genes on the reverse strand observed in 383 metaplots, visual genome-browser inspection allowed us to detect cases of exosome-sensitive 384 transcripts with the characteristics of mammalian PROMPTs. To identify such cases 385 systematically, we analyzed the same TSS-overlapping DHSs as above and required CAGE 386 expression on the reverse strand upstream of mRNA TSSs in *rrp4-2* to be two-fold higher than 387 in wild type, and statistically significant (log2 fold change 1, FDR 0.05). Despite these 388 conservative selection criteria, we identified 96 unique regions with typical PROMPT/pre-mRNA 389 configuration as described in mammals (see examples in **Figure** 4C and **Supplemental Figure**  390 **1D**; **Supplemental Data Set 1**). Similar to mRNA TSSs, PROMPT TCs showed a clear 391 enrichment of TATA around position –30 and of PyPu at position +/–1 (**Figure 4D**). These 392 sequence signatures are consistent with previously proposed models where PROMPT TSSs 393 have separate core promoters (reviewed by Andersson and Sandelin, 2020).

395 We next asked to what degree previously published nascent transcription data 396 supported these clear PROMPT examples, and defined PROMPT regions with evidence of 397 transcription as regions with 2-fold more signal than the genome-wide average (see Methods). 398 Using this criterion, about half of the identified PROMPTs were supported by nascent 399 transcription data (on average 52%, depending on the nascent RNA dataset, see 400 **Supplemental Figure 1B**) (Hetzel et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2018). Given the high thresholds we 401 employed for CAGE data, these results indicate that many PROMPTs did indeed escape 402 detection by available nascent transcriptome data. However, we cannot exclude the possibility 403 that the different tissues analyzed might impact the lower PROMPT detection rate in nascent 404 RNA data (14 d-old seedlings in our study, 6 d-old seedlings (Hetzel et al., 2016) or 405 inflorescences (Liu et al., 2018)). The detection rate of PROMPT regions by PEAT and 406 nanoPARE datasets were low: 1% of PROMPTs were detected as a TSS by PEAT-seq, and 407 22% were detected by nanoPARE, in both cases allowing a window of +/–100 bp of the CAGE 408 TC peaks to be interrogated for signal). These low rates of detection likely reflect the necessity 409 of using RNA decay mutants to detect PROMPTs by steady-state sequencing methods, but the 410 different tissues used may also have an impact (See **Supplemental Table 1**).

411

412 Taken together, our data indicate that transcription from most promoters of protein-413 coding genes appears to be unidirectional, in agreement with previous conclusions. However, 414 clear exceptions exist in which highly exosome-sensitive transcripts are initiated at locations 415 similar to that of the much more widespread PROMPTs in vertebrates. It is therefore possible 416 that initiation of transcription by RNAPII is fundamentally similar in plants and vertebrates, but 417 that plants have evolved mechanisms in addition to selective nuclear RNA decay to ensure a 418 directional promoter output.

## 420 **Properties of PROMPTs and Their Associated Genes**

421 We next sought answers to three questions to characterize the identified PROMPT-422 producing regions better. First, since the set of PROMPT-associated genes was small, is 423 PROMPT production linked to a specific biological function of the associated coding genes? 424 Gene ontology (GO) term analysis showed no significant over-representation of GO terms, 425 suggesting that there is no apparent functional link among PROMPT-associated genes. Second, 426 since enrichment of poly(A) sites and depletion of 5'-splice sites is pronounced in mammalian 427 PROMPT regions compared to mRNAs (Ntini et al., 2013; Almada et al., 2013), do the identified 428 Arabidopsis PROMPT regions share this pattern? In contrast to mammals, we only observed a 429 small enrichment of predicted poly(A) sites (AWTAA consensus) 200 bp or more downstream of 430 PROMPT regions and no difference in the occurrence of predicted 5'-splice sites (see Methods) 431 (**Supplemental Figure 1C**). By contrast, we did observe a significant enrichment of predicted 432 3'-splice sites (Kolmogorov-Smirnoff test, D = 0.33, *P* = 0.0009) (**Supplemental Figure 1C**), 433 although the underlying mechanistic cause of this enrichment remains unclear. The modest 434 enrichment in poly(A) sites may be due in part to the fact that plants use more diverse, and less 435 well characterized, poly(A) sites than mammals (Li and Du, 2014). Third, is there any 436 relationship between PROMPT-producing regions and the UNT-producing genes identified by 437 (Chekanova et al., 2007)? None of the 96 genes with exosome-sensitive PROMPT-like 438 production belonged to the set of UNT-producing genes. Thus, antisense PROMPT production 439 and sense UNT production are not linked. We note, nonetheless, that UNTs were clearly 440 detected in our experiments as regions with higher RNA-seq signal in *rrp4-2* downstream of 441 TSSs than in wild type (**Figure 4E).**

#### 443 **PROMPTs are Sources of small interfering RNAs in Arabidopsis**

444 The inability to detect widespread PROMPT production by either nascent RNA 445 techniques or steady-state RNA sequencing methods even when using mutants deficient in 446 nuclear RNA decay suggests that plants, in contrast to animals, may have evolved mechanisms 447 to suppress PROMPT transcription or expression. A key difference in RNA metabolism between 448 plants and mammals is that plants encode canonical RNA-dependent RNA Polymerases 449 (RdRPs) whose activity may route single-stranded RNAs towards RNAi pathways. RdRPs 450 produce double-stranded RNAs that are converted into short interfering RNAs (siRNAs) by 451 DICER-LIKE (DCL) enzymes. siRNAs may then guide RNA-Induced Silencing Complexes 452 (RISCs) to silence complementary nucleic acids, a process that may include DNA methylation in 453 plants via the RNA-directed DNA methylation pathway (Law and Jacobsen, 2010). Thus, 454 PROMPTs may be substrates for the production of illicit siRNAs that may interfere with proper 455 control of gene expression. To test this possibility, we prepared small RNA libraries from flowers 456 (which express high levels of *RdRPs*) and leaves, using wild type and *rrp4-2* mutants. For the 457 flower libraries, we also included the *hen2-5* knockout mutant (Lange et al., 2014). Global 458 analysis of small RNAs from these libraries showed that, at a genome-wide scale, small RNAs 459 were no more abundant in regions upstream of mRNA TSSs (potential PROMPT-producing 460 regions) in *rrp4-2* and *hen2-5* than in wild type (**Figure 5A**, **Supplemental Figure 2A**, **B**). A 461 scenario in which PROMPTs are generally produced, but turned over rapidly by RNAi, is 462 therefore unlikely. However, the 96 observed PROMPT regions did produce more small RNAs

463 in exosome mutants than in wild type. First, these loci, indicated with red dots in **Supplemental** 464 **Figure 2A**, **B**, tended to fall above the diagonal in the scatterplots showing small RNA read 465 counts in exosome mutant vs. wild type. Second, regions producing detectable PROMPT TCs in 466 exosome mutants displayed substantially higher small RNA read counts than those that did not 467 (**Figure 5A**).

468

469 We performed two additional analyses to support the notion that the small RNAs 470 mapping to PROMPT-producing regions were bona fide siRNAs, and not merely degradation 471 fragments that may over-accumulate as a consequence of lost exosome function. We reasoned 472 that if simple RNA degradation defects were the basis of small RNA accumulation in exosome 473 mutants, small RNA production should be proportional to the expression level of the longer RNA 474 precursor, and would be equally likely to take place in PROMPT and mRNA regions. We 475 therefore calculated the relative small RNA densities in PROMPT and mRNA regions, using the 476 expression levels from CAGE TCs in our seedling libraries to normalize small RNA read counts. 477 We also split small RNA counts according to size, since Arabidopsis DCLs produce diagnostic 478 size classes of siRNAs: DCL4 produces 21-nt siRNAs, DCL2 produces 22-nt siRNAs, and DCL3 479 produces 24-nt siRNAs (Xie et al., 2004; Dunoyer et al., 2005; Xie et al., 2005). Regions with 480 detectable PROMPTs in *rrp4-2* young flowers had markedly higher relative levels, particularly of 481 21- and 22-nt small RNAs, than wild type, while the corresponding mRNA regions did not 482 (**Figure 5B)**. Similar results, although clear only for 21-nt siRNAs, were observed in leaves 483 (**Supplemental Figure 2C**). Thus, small RNAs accumulate specifically in PROMPT regions and 484 display a size bias typical of bona fide siRNAs produced by DCL4 and, to some extent, DCL2. 485 This is important because DCL4/DCL2 are most likely to generate siRNAs from substrates 486 whose ultimate source are RNAPII transcripts, as 24-nt siRNAs produced by DCL3 are more 487 tightly linked to RNA Polymerase IV transcripts (Law and Jacobsen, 2010). A typical example of 488 siRNA generation in exosome mutants in regions with detectable PROMPTs is shown in **Figure**  489 **5C.** This example also shows that siRNAs accumulate on both forward and reverse strands, 490 consistent with the involvement of an RdRP-DCL pathway rather than mere degradation. We 491 conclude that PROMPTs indeed give rise to illicit siRNA production when allowed to accumulate 492 in exosome mutants.

#### 494 **Evidence for Bidirectional TSS Activity at Intronic and Intergenic Space**

495 We next examined the possibility of bidirectional transcription at intronic or intergenic 496 loci, as this was shown to be a powerful predictor of enhancer regions and enhancer activity in 497 vertebrates and insects (Andersson et al., 2014a; Rennie et al., 2018). Visual inspection also 498 revealed such cases in Arabidopsis (examples in **Figure 6**). To identify such regions 499 systematically, we used the same approach as in (Thodberg and Sandelin, 2019; Thodberg et 500 al., 2019) to locate genomic regions with a balanced bidirectional CAGE signal. A total of 113 501 bidirectional clusters were identified, of which 78 were intronic, and the remaining 35 were 502 intergenic (**Supplemental Data Set 1**). On average, such regions were more highly expressed 503 in *rrp4-2* or *hen2-4* compared to wild type and exhibited clear average bidirectional CAGE 504 transcription in all mutants except *lsm8-2*, both in CAGE and RNA-seq data (**Figure 7A**). A 505 similar but less pronounced trend was observed in 5' GRO-cap data (**Figure 7B**). RNAPII ChIP-506 seq signal from 10 d-old seedlings (Cortijo et al., 2017) was also enriched at the edges of 507 NDRs; in intronic regions, these were highly balanced while there was a surprising minus-strand 508 bias in intergenic regions (**Figure 7C**).

509

510 To further characterize these bidirectionally transcribed loci, we assessed their 511 chromatin state using published data. The majority of bidirectional CAGE sites overlapped with 512 a DHS (**Figure 7D**) and were centered on accessible DNA, as measured by DNase and MNase-

513 seq (**Figure 7E**). Histones adjacent to the NDRs were enriched for H3K27ac (from 12 d-old 514 seedlings) (Chen et al., 2017) and to a lesser degree, H3K4me1 and H3K4me3 (from 3-week 515 old plants and 4-week old leaves, respectively) (Wang et al., 2015; van Dijk et al., 2010) (**Figure**  516 **7F**) (see **Supplemental Table 1** for details of tissues). These patterns should be assessed with 517 the caveat that the ChIP data for histone modifications and RNAPII were not taken from the 518 same tissues. We note, however, that the patterns observed were highly reminiscent of those 519 observed in mammals, with the exception of H3K4me1, which is highly enriched around 520 enhancer regions in mammals. This may be explained by the association of H3K4me1 with 521 active transcription elongation in Arabidopsis (Nielsen et al., 2019), but not in mammals. 522 Overall, our analysis indicates that some DNase I hypersensitive intergenic and intronic loci in 523 Arabidopsis feature bidirectional transcription initiation at NDR edges. The majority of these loci 524 produce exosome-sensitive RNAs and have enhancer-associated chromatin marks. While such 525 patterns are predictive of enhancer activity in vertebrates, their possible enhancer activity 526 remains to be tested in Arabidopsis.

#### 528 **Identification of Sets of mRNAs Sensitive to Depletion of** *RRP4* **and** *HEN2*

529 We reasoned that while PROMPTs and potential enhancer RNAs (eRNAs) were rarely 530 detected, even when depleting two distinct nuclear RNA degradation systems, a systematic 531 comparison of CAGE and RNA-seq data from exosome mutants and wild type might identify as 532 yet unknown transcripts in other regions of the genome. Because we wanted to find exosome 533 substrates, we focused on transcripts that were more abundant in either mutant. We first 534 identified CAGE TCs whose expression was significantly higher in *hen2-4* or *rrp4-2* compared to 535 wild type (log2 fold change 1, FDR 0.05, by the limma method (Ritchie et al., 2015), see 536 **Supplementary Data S2**). This resulted in a total of 1747 up-regulated TCs in both mutants 537 combined. All up-regulated CAGE TCs showed the expected nucleotide distribution around 538 TSSs with enriched TATA and PyPu dinucleotide patterns at –30 and +1 bp, respectively 539 (**Supplemental Figure 3A**), making it likely that they represent genuine TSSs. TCs up-540 regulated in *hen2-4* were less numerous, and 92% of them were also significantly changed in 541 *rrp4-2* compared to wild type (Fisher's exact test, *P* = 0) (**Figure 8A**). The few TCs (N=73) 542 significantly up-regulated only in *hen2-4,* but not *rrp4-2* (**Figure 8B**), may represent transcripts 543 whose exosome targeting requires HEN2, yet their exosomal decay still functions in the 544 hypomorphic *rrp4-2* mutant. Alternatively, HEN2 may have as-yet undescribed functions 545 independent of the exosome. This latter scenario is supported by the fact that only 2 of the 73 546 transcripts were found in the set of 848 transcripts up-regulated upon inducible RNAi 547 knockdown of RRP4 (Chekanova et al., 2007).

548 We next classified all TCs up-regulated in either *rrp4-2* or *hen2-4* based on their overlap 549 with the TAIR10 reference annotation (as in **Figure 1C**) (**Figure 8C**). This produced two

550 surprising outcomes. First, a large number (N=479) of TCs corresponding to sense mRNA TSSs 551 were up-regulated in the *rrp4-2* mutant. Some (N=112) were also up-regulated in *hen2-4*, but 552 the majority (N=367) was only up-regulated in *rrp4-2*. Only 19 TCs were up-regulated 553 specifically in *hen2-4* (**Figure 8D, E**). Their up-regulation might arise either as a direct 554 consequence of reduced exosome activity or indirectly due to transcriptional induction caused 555 by the *rrp4-2* mutation. In the first scenario of transcriptional up-regulation, one might expect the 556 mRNAs to encode functionally related proteins, but we found no significant enrichment of GO 557 terms in the group of genes associated with the up-regulated TCs. A more direct involvement of 558 the exosome in the decay of these two groups of mRNAs would be of interest: The group of 131 559 mRNAs up-regulated in *hen2-4* may be appreciably affected by nuclear mRNA quality control, 560 akin to the Poly(A)-tail exosome targeting (PAXT) pathway described in mammals (Meola et al., 561 2016), while the up-regulation of the larger group of 367 mRNAs specifically in *rrp4-2* may be 562 explained by a substantial contribution of the exosome to their cytoplasmic decay. Notably, the 563 average expression of all of these moderately exosome-sensitive TCs of mRNAs was higher 564 than most other categories, perhaps suggesting that the decay of highly expressed mRNAs 565 tends to implicate the exosome (**Supplemental Figure 3B**).

566

567 The second surprising outcome was related to a group of 525 CAGE TCs that were up-568 regulated in *rrp4-2* relative to wild type. The majority of these TCs (*N*=371) were also up-569 regulated in *hen2-4*. These TCs were neither PROMPTs nor bidirectionally transcribed 570 intergenic or intronic loci analyzed above, but were located on the reverse strand within gene 571 bodies, thus representing antisense RNAs (**Figure 8C**, red highlight). To annotate these 572 antisense TCs more accurately, we used the same hierarchical procedure as above, but 573 inverted the strand information in the annotation (**Figure 8F**, top panel). Exosome-sensitive 574 antisense TCs tended to overlap with coding exons (CDS) and 3'-UTRs of mRNAs (**Figure 8F**, 575 bottom panel). Following normalization of the number of TCs to the genome-wide lengths of 576 CDSs and 3'-UTRs, up-regulated antisense CAGE TCs were most enriched within 3'-UTRs 577 (**Figure 8G**). More generally, antisense TCs had similar average expression and fold change 578 across annotation categories, which in turn tended to be similar to that of other exosome-579 sensitive TCs (**Supplementary Figure 3C**). The antisense CAGE TCs may represent genuine 580 initiation events or partially degraded, recapped RNAs (Affymetrix ENCODE Transcriptome 581 Project and Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory ENCODE Transcriptome Project, 2009). We 582 reasoned that if antisense TCs represent genuine TSSs, they should have a similar distribution 583 of core promoter elements as other TCs. Indeed, the great majority of antisense TCs, 584 regardless of overlap category, displayed a strong Initiator (Inr) as well as a TATA motif (**Figure**  585 **8H**), suggesting that the exosome-sensitive, antisense TCs represent bona fide TSSs.

586

587 To verify the existence of these antisense RNAs, and to investigate possible redundancy 588 in their degradation between exosome-dependent and LSM8-dependent pathways, we analyzed 589 RNA-seq reads mapping to genes on the antisense strand, comparing mutants with wild type. 590 This confirmed the existence of the class of antisense RNAs identified by CAGE, but yielded 591 slightly higher numbers of differentially expressed transcripts than the CAGE-based analysis: 592 656 genes were up-regulated (log2 fold change 1, FDR ≤ 0.05, see Methods) in either *rrp4-2,*  593 *lsm8-2* or *rrp4-2 lsm8-2* mutants compared to wild type on the antisense strand (**Supplemental**  594 **Data Set 2**). For the remaining transcripts, 52% were up-regulated in both *rrp4-2* and *rrp4-2*  595 *lsm8-2* mutants, while relatively large sets were significantly up-regulated only in *rrp4-2* single 596 mutant (30%) or the *rrp4-2 lsm8-2* double mutant (17%) (**Figure 8I**). Plotting the log2 fold-597 change of *rrp4-2 vs.* wt against the log2 fold-change of *rrp4-2 lsm8-2* compared to wild type 598 revealed that genes found to be differentially expressed in the *rrp4-2 lsm8-2* mutant had the 599 same trend in *rrp4-2* single mutants (**Figure 8J**). Thus, the RNA-seq data strongly corroborated 600 the existence of exosome-sensitive antisense RNAs. Further, we found no clear cases of 601 redundancy between LSM8-dependent and exosome-dependent pathways involved in their 602 degradation.

# 604 **Characterization of Exosome-Sensitive Antisense RNAs Initiating Towards 3'-ends of** 605 **Genes**

606 Antisense RNAs initiating within 3'-regions of genes encoding developmental regulators 607 can play essential roles in developmental transitions in plants, including germination and 608 flowering (e.g. (Fedak et al., 2016)). Given this, and based on the unexpected prevalence of 609 exosome-sensitive TSSs in 3'-regions of genes, we characterized this set of TSSs and their 610 cognate RNAs in more detail. A total of 354 CAGE TCs antisense to an annotated 3'-UTR 611 region were found across all samples using the same expression cutoffs as above, of which 197 612 (56%) were up-regulated in at least one of the mutants compared to wild type (**Figure 8D**). 613 Interestingly, genes with an up-regulated CAGE TC antisense to their 3'-UTR showed a weakly 614 significant enrichment in 'sequence-specific DNA binding' GO term (GO:0043565, FDR = 0.02, 615 8.7% of genes), indicating that genes encoding transcription factors were more prone to have 616 antisense transcription initiation within their 3'-UTRs. Examples of exosome-sensitive antisense 617 transcripts and TCs are shown in **Figure 9A**.

618

619 CAGE TCs antisense to 3'-UTRs showed no specific bias in terms of location within the 620 3'-UTR, but were substantially more likely to occur in longer 3'-UTRs (**Supplemental Figure**  621 **4A, B**, a summary of statistical tests is available in **Supplemental Table 3**). Since the 622 Arabidopsis genome is compact, we hypothesized that exosome-sensitive antisense TCs might 623 be PROMPTs from closely located downstream genes. However, this phenomenon appears to 624 be rare: Only 16 instances were identified where the maximal distance between a 3'-UTR 625 antisense TC and its closest downstream mRNA TSS was 300 bp, and this number only 626 increased to 39 instances when a maximal distance of 500 bp was allowed (**Supplemental**  627 **Figure 4C**). More generally, there was no relationship between the intergenic distance between 628 genes (3' end of gene to next 5' or 3' gene end downstream) and whether the 3'-UTRs had 629 exosome-sensitive antisense CAGE TCs (**Supplemental Figure 4D**).

630

631 Since we had both CAGE and RNA-seq data, we analyzed RNA-seq read density across 632 sense and antisense strands for the 481 genes that had at least one differentially up-regulated 633 antisense CAGE TC in *rrp4-2* or *hen2-4* (the set is larger than above, because antisense CAGE 634 TCs within other regions than 3'-UTRs were included). Antisense RNA-seq reads were 635 uniformly distributed across the gene body in the exosome mutants except at the 5'- and 3'- 636 ends, while the antisense signal was on average 14.5-fold lower than that of the sense strand 637 (**Figure 9B**, right panel). Conversely, CAGE antisense reads resided in the last *~* 20% of the 638 gene (**Figure 9B**, left panel), consistent with their dominant 3'-UTR overlap observed above. 639 Last, we leveraged the RNA-seq data to investigate the length and splicing status of up-640 regulated antisense transcripts in *rrp4-2*. To assess this in as unbiased a way as possible, we 641 used strand-aware de novo transcript assembly for RNA-seq reads (see Methods and 642 **Supplemental Data Set 3**) in *rrp4-2*, and then plotted the length of novel antisense transcripts 643 (**Figure 9C**) and their number of introns (**Figure 9D**). The length of antisense transcripts had a 644 large variance, but their most common length was ~1,000 bp, and the vast majority (91.3%) 645 were unspliced. An important caveat of this analysis is that de novo transcript assembly is only 646 possible if sufficient RNA-seq reads are present. This means that transcript length and splicing 647 status are measured only for more highly expressed transcripts, and length estimates may be 648 conservative. Taken together, our analysis revealed that a substantial number of Arabidopsis 649 genes exhibit antisense transcription of unspliced, exosome-sensitive antisense RNAs, a 650 property that is more common in genes encoding transcription factors.

# 652 **The** *DOG1/asDOG1* **Locus Exemplifies a Complex TSS Organization with a Functional** 653 **Antisense lncRNA**

654 As a final example of the utility of our data, we analyzed the *DELAY OF GERMINATION*  655 *1* (*DOG1*) locus in detail. The DOG1 protein is an essential developmental regulator that 656 controls seed dormancy such that *dog1* loss-of-function mutants exhibit uncontrolled seed 657 germination, even during fruit development, while *DOG1* over-expressors cannot break seed 658 dormancy (Fedak et al., 2016). Importantly, *DOG1* expression is regulated in cis by a lncRNA, 659 *asDOG1*, initiated at an alternative 3'-UTR in exon 2 (Fedak et al., 2016). Overlaying our RNA-660 seq and CAGE data shows the overall complexity and exosome effects at this locus (**Figure**  661 **10**). First, *DOG1* has two alternative sense strand TSSs based on CAGE data: the first 662 corresponds roughly to TAIR10 annotation, while the second is located within exon 2 and 663 produces an uncharacterized transcript. Interestingly, both CAGE and RNA-seq shows that 664 these transcripts have a degree of exosome sensitivity, which is uncommon for mRNAs. 665 Second, we identified an antisense CAGE TC very close to the previously annotated TSS of 666 *asDOG1*: both CAGE and RNA-seq data shows that *asDOG1* is highly exosome-sensitive 667 (notably, TAIR10 annotation suggests that this TSS lies within the coding region of exon 2, while 668 (Fedak et al., 2016) showed that this region has a dual function as a 3'-UTR for a shorter mRNA 669 isoform). Consistent with our results above, de novo RNA-seq isoform reconstruction indicated 670 that *asDOG1* was not spliced and extended until the annotated TSS of *DOG1* (estimated length 671 of 1,223 bp), agreeing with the RACE experiments from the original study (Fedak et al., 2016). 672 Thus, the well-established cis-regulator of gene expression *asDOG1* is a prominent example of 673 the set of exosome-sensitive antisense RNAs that we identified here. It is therefore plausible 674 that more of these lncRNAs, or the act of their transcription, have regulatory potential. This

- 675 hypothesis is particularly appealing because of the enrichment of genes encoding transcription
- 676 factors in the group of genes giving rise to exosome-sensitive antisense transcripts.

#### 679 **DISCUSSION**

#### 680 **Divergent Transcription at Genes is Uncommon, but not Absent in Arabidopsis**

681 The initial aim of our study was to characterize the extent of bidirectional transcription at 682 promoters of protein-coding genes as well as in intergenic and intronic regions in Arabidopsis, 683 given their frequent occurrence in vertebrates. We did this by using two complementary steady-684 state RNA methods (CAGE and RNA-seq), where critical nuclear RNA degradation pathways 685 were rendered non-functional. Our results largely agree with previous efforts using nascent RNA 686 methods (Hetzel et al., 2016): PROMPTs are rare in Arabidopsis, but do exist. Using 687 conservative thresholds, we identified nearly 100 PROMPTs that are clear exosome substrates. 688 Notably, such cases were not reported previously using nascent RNA methods, even though 689 about half had nascent RNA support.

690

691 It is unclear why so few genes feature distinctive PROMPTs in *rrp4-2* or *hen2-4* mutants: 692 there was no discernible difference in terms of sequence motif predictions previously associated 693 with exosome sensitivity (Ntini et al., 2013; Almada et al., 2013), and no apparent shared role 694 among genes with PROMPTs. Principally, the lack of detected PROMPTs may be either due to 695 1) the absence of transcription initiation on the reverse strand upstream of Arabidopsis mRNA 696 TSSs, or 2) the efficient degradation of PROMPTs by redundant RNA degradation systems, so 697 that transcripts remain undetectable even after the loss of exosome activity. The lack of signal in 698 nascent RNA assays together with our failure to detect widespread occurrence of PROMPTs 699 using double mutants is consistent with the first hypothesis. Although our results combined with 700 previous nascent RNA studies can most easily be interpreted as evidence for the general 701 absence of PROMPT transcription in Arabidopsis, we cannot completely rule out the possibility 702 that PROMPT transcription is widespread, but coupled with redundant nuclear RNA decay 703 pathways that include exosome- and LSM8-independent mechanisms. In particular, we note 704 that nuclear decapping pathways are relatively poorly understood, and it is formally possible that 705 LSM8-independent mechanisms of nuclear decapping exist that allow 5'-3' decay of PROMPTs 706 in the *rrp4-2/lsm8-2* mutant background.

707

708 We also found evidence of bidirectional transcription at intergenic NDRs producing 709 exosome-sensitive transcripts, which share many of the same signatures as mammalian 710 enhancer regions, including chromatin states, with the caveat that tissues were not always fully 711 matched between experiments. It remains to be seen whether these enhancer-like regions bind 712 transcription factors and have a role in regulating distal gene transcription initiation.

## 714 **Reasons for PROMPT Scarcity in Plants**

715 Why Arabidopsis promoters, unlike those of vertebrates, generally initiate predominantly 716 in only one direction, and what might have favored the evolution of promoter directionality are 717 important questions with ties to fundamental aspects of transcription and RNA metabolism. A 718 genetic study in yeast demonstrated that chromatin-based mechanisms can enforce promoter 719 directionality: the histone chaperone, Chromatin Assembly Factor I (CAF-I), which facilitates 720 replication-coupled nucleosome assembly (Smith and Stillman, 1989), also restricts divergent 721 transcription, while histone exchange promoted by acetylation of Lysine 56 on histone 3 722 (H3K56) promotes divergent transcription (Marquardt et al., 2014). Similar or other 723 independently evolved chromatin-based mechanisms may act in plants to enforce promoter 724 directionality. Insights into such mechanisms could be rewarding for an understanding of 725 RNAPII transcription in plants, and the data presented here should allow the design of genetic 726 screens, akin to those used by (Marquardt et al., 2014) towards the isolation of missing factors.

727 One reason for the repression of PROMPT initiation in Arabidopsis may lie in the high 728 gene density of its genome, whereby PROMPTs may interfere with closely located upstream 729 gene transcription. However, the fact that PROMPTs are also rare in maize that has a less 730 gene-dense and larger genome than many vertebrates (Erhard et al., 2015; Lozano et al., 2018) 731 does not support this hypothesis and suggests that PROMPT suppression may be more general 732 in plants. What might drive suppression of upstream transcripts specifically in plants within the 733 eukaryotic kingdom? Our analysis of small RNAs in exosome mutants provides a plausible 734 explanation. We observed clear accumulation of 21- and 22-nt siRNAs in the regions that 735 produce detectable PROMPTs in exosome mutants, demonstrating that PROMPTs, if allowed to 736 accumulate, are efficiently converted into siRNAs. We did not examine whether these siRNAs 737 cause aberrant gene repression, but note that the current understanding of RNA directed DNA 738 methylation (RdDM) in plants allows for the possibility that PROMPT-derived siRNAs may 739 induce aberrant DNA methylation patterns and unwanted transcriptional repression, perhaps 740 over the course of generations. Although RdDM-loci are associated with 24-nt siRNA production 741 via a RNA Polymerase IV-RDR2-DCL3 module (Singh et al., 2019), loci with initial bursts of 21- 742 22-nt siRNAs, generally used for post-transcriptional gene regulation, may switch to epigenetic 743 silencing via 24-nt guided RdDM (McCue et al., 2015; Marí-Ordóñez et al., 2013). Thus, 744 stringent transcriptional control of endogenous genes may be jeopardized by PROMPT 745 production in plants, because of their tendency to be converted into siRNAs by RdRPs and DCL 746 enzymes. In contrast to plants, mammals and insects do not encode RdRPs, perhaps explaining

- 747 why PROMPT degradation solely via the nuclear exosome is sufficient to protect against 748 additional potentially deleterious effects of these long noncoding RNAs in these species.

# 750 **A Large Class of Exosome-Sensitive Antisense RNAs Initiating in 3' Regions of Protein-**751 **Coding Genes**

752 The large number of antisense TCs residing within the 3'-UTRs of mRNAs was an 753 unexpected finding, and differs from earlier studies showing peaks of RNAPII or GRO-seq 754 signal at or downstream of 3' ends on the sense strand, e.g. (Zhu et al., 2018). These TSSs 755 define the initiation of long, exosome-sensitive unspliced antisense RNAs, which often cover 756 large parts of the cognate mRNA. The function of these transcripts, if any, is unclear. However, 757 it is interesting to note a clear over-representation in genes encoding transcription factors: 758 antisense transcripts may be repressing the mRNA by RNA-RNA hybridization, or antisense 759 transcription may repress the transcription of the cognate mRNAs by steric effects such as 760 RNAPII collision, or clearing of DNA-binding proteins and chromatin states. Indeed, there is 761 already evidence for regulation of developmental and stress response genes by antisense 762 RNAs (Kindgren et al., 2018; Henriques et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2010), including the 763 *DOG1*/*asDOG1* example in **Figure 10**, which is similar to the class of RNAs we show here to be 764 prevalent. This class of exosome-sensitive antisense RNAs is, therefore, an obvious focus for 765 future functional delineation of noncoding RNA and noncoding transcription in plants.

## 789 **Growth conditions**

790 For the CAGE samples, wild type Col-0, *hen2-4*, and *rrp4-2* seeds were surface-791 sterilized first by incubation in 70% EtOH (2 min) followed by 1.5% sodium hypochlorite and 792 0.05% Tween-20 (10 min), and rinsed three times with sterile ddH2O. Seeds were stratified in 793 complete darkness at 4ºC for 72 h, then germinated on full-strength Murashige & Skoog (MS) 794 medium, pH 5.7 and supplemented with 1% sucrose and 0.8% agar in sterile conditions in petri dishes under 16 h L/8 h D photoperiod (130 μmol photons m−2 s−1 795 at 21ºC, Philips Master TL-D 796 36W/840 and 18W/840 bulbs). Intact 12 d-old seedlings were transferred to 8 mL of 1% liquid 797 MS medium, pH 5.7, supplemented with 1% sucrose, in 6 well-plates (Nunc) and allowed to 798 acclimate for 48 h with mild agitation (130 rpm), under the same light and temperature 799 conditions as above. For the RNA-seq samples, seeds for wild type Col-0, *lsm8-2*, *rrp4-2,* and 800 the *rrp4-2 lsm8-2* double mutant were subjected to the same sterilization, stratification, and 801 growth conditions as described for the CAGE samples. For the small RNA-seq samples (wild 802 type Col-0, *hen2-5* and *rrp4-2),* seeds were surface-sterilized as above and spotted directly on 803 soil (SW Horto Plugg/Såjord; SW Horto AB, 27650 Hammenhög, Sweden), then grown in Percival chambers 16 h L/8 h D photoperiod (130 μmol photons m−2 s−1 804 , Philips Master TL-D 805 36W/840 and 18W/840 bulbs) and temperature cycles (21ºC during the day, 18ºC at night) for 3 806 weeks for leaf production, or 6 weeks for flower production.

#### 808 **Total RNA Extraction**

809 For the CAGE and RNA-seq samples, total RNA was extracted for 3 biological 810 replicates, each formed of a pool of 10 complete and intact 14 d-old seedlings. For the small 811 RNA-seq samples, total RNA was recovered from a pool of 2-3 leaves or young flower buds 812 across 3 different repeats of 3 independently grown replicates, for a total of 9 small RNA 813 samples. Collected plant material was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and finely ground before 814 adding 1 mL of TRI-Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) per 100 mg of ground tissue and vortexed directly. 815 Phase separation was achieved by adding 200 µL of chloroform, vigorous shaking, and 816 centrifugation at 4ºC (10 min at 15,000 rpm). The aqueous phase was transferred to a fresh 817 tube and one volume (400 µL) of isopropanol was added to precipitate RNA at room 818 temperature (30 min). The total RNA was pelleted after centrifugation at 4ºC (15 min, 15,000 819 rpm) and rinsed three times with 70% ethanol prior to solubilization in sterile ddH2O. To remove 820 contaminants and obtain higher quality RNA material, polysaccharide precipitation was carried 821 out as described by (Asif et al., 2000). Total RNA was assessed for concentration and purity 822 using NanoDrop ND-1000 (Thermo Scientific) and its integrity was checked using a Bioanalyzer 823 2100 with High Sensitivity RNA chip (RNA 6000 Pico) (Agilent).

## 825 **Preparation of CAGE Libraries, and Filtering and Mapping of CAGE Sequence Reads**

826 CAGE libraries were prepared as in (Takahashi et al., 2012) with a starting material of 5 827 µg of total RNA. The National High-Throughput DNA Sequencing Centre of the University of 828 Copenhagen performed the sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform. As recommended 829 by Illumina, 30% of Phi-X spike-ins were added to each sequencing lane to balance the low 830 complexity of the 5' ends of the CAGE libraries. FASTX Toolkit v0.0.13 831 (http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx\_toolkit) was used to remove the linker sequences, retain the 832 first 25 nt from the 5' end, and filter out sequences that fell below a Phred score of Q30 in 50% 833 of the remaining bases. The clean reads were mapped to the TAIR10 reference genome 834 (Ensembl release 26) with Bowtie v1.1.2 (Langmead et al., 2009) using the parameters -t best - 835 strata -v -k 10 -y -p 6 -phred33-quals -chunksmbs 512 -e 120 -q -un. Uniquely mapped CAGE 836 tags 5' ends were counted at each genomic position to obtain a set of CAGE TSSs, typically 837 referred to as 'CTSSs' in the CAGE literature. CTSS coordinates were offset by 1 bp to account 838 for G-addition bias (Carninci et al., 2006).

839

#### 840 **Quantification and Clustering of CAGE TCs**

841 Most analyses were performed with the R package *CAGEfightR* v1.2 (Thodberg et al., 842 2019) in the Bioconductor environment. We kept only CTSSs with at least one count in at least 843 three libraries (the smallest sample group size). CTSSs were normalized into tags-per-million 844 (TPM) to mitigate differences in sequencing depths. A pan-experiment set of CAGE Tag 845 Clusters (TCs) was established by computing the sum of TPM-values over all libraries at each 846 base pair, followed by neighbor-clustering of CTSSs on the same strand (within a distance of 20 847 bp). CAGE TCs detected in the pan-experiment where then quantified individually for each 848 sample (**Supplemental Data Set 4**). Only TCs supported in at least two libraries with a pan-849 experiment minimum of 1 TPM were considered for further analysis (**Supplemental Data Set**  850 **1**). TC peaks were defined as the position with maximum signal within the TC.

851

#### 852 **Annotation of CAGE TCs**

853 TAIR10 annotation was recovered from the TxDb.Athaliana.BioMart.plantsmart28 854 Bioconductor package. ARAPORT11 GFF3 annotation (June 2016) was obtained from 855 www.araport.org and converted into a TxDb object (Lawrence et al., 2013) using the 856 makeTxDbFromGFF function from the R package *GenomicFeatures*. Using TC peaks as proxy 857 (see above), annotation of CAGE TCs was conducted against a hierarchical annotation built 858 from TAIR10 and ARAPORT11 individually (see **Figure 1**). The promoter region was defined as 859 the 200 bp window centered on the annotated gene TSS. The promoter-proximal region extends 860 400 bp upstream of the annotated TSS. The same regions, but on the opposite strand, were 861 referred to as the reverse region. The antisense category covered the whole gene body but on 862 the opposite strand. The 5'-UTRs, 3'-UTRs, and CDS regions were defined as in TAIR10 and 863 ARAPORT11. The exonic category corresponds non-protein-coding exons, for instance of long 864 non-coding RNAs. In case of multiple overlaps, a CAGE TC peak was annotated using the 865 highest-priority category of the overlapping annotations in the annotation hierarchy.

866

#### 867 **Alternative TSS Analysis**

868 CAGE TCs attributed to a TAIR10 annotated gene and contributing at least 10% of the 869 total TPM gene expression across the gene, were selected as a basis for assessing the 870 landscape of alternative TSSs in unchallenged wild type seedlings.

871

#### 872 **Recall of TSSs/TCs Across Datasets**

873 TCs and TSSs from CAGE, PEAT-seq (Morton et al., 2014), and nanoPARE (Schon et 874 al., 2018) datasets were merged. Overlapping regions were collapsed to obtain a non-redundant 875 set of TSSs. The number of recalled TCs/TSSs from the non-redundant set was computed for 876 each dataset and plotted as a Venn diagram (**Figure 2A**).

877

#### 878 **Average Meta-Profiles**

879 Meta plots/profiles show the average signal from a given experiment across all genes of 880 interest, where the length of all genes is normalized to the same length. All meta plots were 881 computed on normalized read counts using the R package *TeMPO* 882 (https://github.com/MalteThodberg/TeMPO) with trimmed signal (0.01% to 0.99% percentiles). 883

#### 884 **Quantification and Comparison of TSSs Across Datasets**

885 TSSs from ARAPORT11, TAIR10, nanoPARE (Schon et al., 2018) and PEAT-seq 886 (Morton et al., 2014) were extended by 100 bp in both directions and quantified using the CAGE 887 wild type TPM signal. To account for the large differences in expression as observed in **Figure**  888 **2B**, TSSs were filtered to have at least 1 CAGE TPM in at least 2 samples and were 889 subsequently used as anchors for comparison of nascent RNA signals, MNase signal, and 890 sequence patterns.

## 892 **Preparation of RNA-seq Libraries, and Mapping and Quantification of RNA-seq Reads**

893 Total RNA was purified from complete and intact 14 d-old seedlings and polysaccharide 894 precipitation was used as described above for the CAGE samples. The resulting 895 polysaccharide-free total RNA was sequenced by Novogene (Hong-Kong) as stranded Illumina 896 paired-end 150 bp reads. Library preparation included an early ribosomal RNA depletion (Ribo-897 zero); in addition, the fragment size selection limit was lowered to 200 nt to allow for the capture 898 of hypothetical PROMPT transcripts. Adapters were removed from the raw reads using 899 Cutadapt v2.3 (Martin, 2011). Mapping to the TAIR10 reference genome (Ensembl release 26) 900 was done with STAR v2.7.0 (Dobin and Gingeras, 2015) using default parameters, only 901 considering unique mappers and concordant pairs. The matrix of counts antisense to TAIR10

- 902 annotated genes was generated with featureCounts v1.6.3 (Liao et al., 2014) with parameters 903 O --minoverlap 3 --largestOverlap -B -p -s 1 (**Supplemental Data Set 5**).
- 904

#### 905 **Open-Chromatin Data and DHS Annotation**

906 DHS regions, DNase-Seq and MNase-Seq were downloaded from PlantDHS.org (Zhang 907 et al., 2016) (see **Supplemental Table 1** for details). Position of the highest DNase signal in 908 flower tissue was used to identify the DHS summit (DHSS) in each DHS region. DHSSs were 909 extended 400 bp on both directions and annotated based on TAIR10, using the hierarchical 910 strategy as defined in **Figure 1C**.

911

#### 912 **Poly(A), 5' and 3' Splice Sites at mRNA & PROMPT TSSs**

913 We used the Bioconductor package *seqPattern* to scan for to scan for AWTAA as well 914 as 5' and 3' splice sites. For AWTAA, the consensus sequence was used. Position frequency 915 matrices from (Brown et al., 1996) were used with a matching score threshold of 80% for the 5' 916 and 3' splice sites.

917

#### 918 **Differential Expression Analysis, Overlap and GO-Term Enrichment**

919 We used the *edgeR* (Robinson et al., 2010) and *limma* (Ritchie et al., 2015) R packages 920 to conduct differential expression analysis. Counts were normalized using the weighted trimmed 921 mean of M-values (TMM) method (Robinson and Oshlack, 2010) and transformed using the 922 voom method (Law et al., 2014) to model the mean-variance relationship prior to linear 923 modeling as recommended in (Liu et al., 2015). Resulting *P*-values were corrected for multiple 924 testing by the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Statistical significance of overlap between 925 differentially expressed gene sets was assessed by Fisher's exact test using the 926 *testGeneOverlap* function in the R package *GeneOverlap* (**Figure 7A**). For gene set enrichment 927 analyses, we used the R package *gProfileR* (Reimand et al., 2007) with the complete set of 928 detected genes as background.

## 930 **Statistical Tests**

931 For **Supplemental Figure 4**, t-tests were conducted on log-transformed genomic 932 distances using the t.test methods in R with settings alternative = 'greater' and paired = FALSE. 933 Wilcoxon tests on the untransformed distances were also conducted with the same parameters 934 as detailed above. The complete detail on statistical tests is available in **Supplemental Table 3**. 935

#### 936 **Nascent RNA Sequencing and Support of PROMPTs**

937 Nascent RNA sequencing libraries from (Hetzel et al., 2016) were re-processed as in the 938 original article. Briefly, adapter sequences were removed from the 3'-end of reads and mapping 939 to the TAIR10 reference was conducted with STAR (v2.4.2a) (Dobin and Gingeras, 2015). Only 940 uniquely-mapped reads were retained and the genome-wide signal was normalized per million 941 mapped reads. For support of PROMPTs by nascent RNA sequencing, we calculated the 942 average signal of 5' GRO-cap, as well as GRO-seq from two labs (Hetzel et al., 2016; Liu et al., 943 2018) (see **Supplementary Table S1** for details of libraries and tissues), across the genome-944 wide PROMPT regions, defined as the –400 bp antisense stretches from TAIR10 annotated 945 TSSs. Support of exosome-sensitive PROMPT regions by nascent sequencings was considered 946 upon showing 2-fold the average genome-wide signal.

947

#### 948 **Small RNA-seq Libraries and Analysis**

Libraries were constructed from 1 µg of purified RNA using the NEBNext® 949 Multiplex 950 Small RNA Library Prep Set (New England Biolabs) following manufacturer's instructions. 951 Sequencing was done on an Illumina NextSeq platform with 75 bp single-end reads. Adapters 952 were removed from raw reads with Cutadapt v2.3 and filtered for a minimum and maximum 953 length of 19 and 25 bp, respectively. Clean reads were mapped to the TAIR10 reference 954 genome with STAR v2.7.0f. To distinguish between small RNA read lengths, alignments from 955 SAM output were separated into 21, 22, 23 and 24 nt, using the CIGAR matching length code. 956 Raw read counts were normalized to the total number of clean reads.

## 958 **Coverage at Potential PROMPT Regions**

959 Potential PROMPT regions were defined as the 500 bp stretches upstream of annotated 960 TSSs, not overlapping any other annotated features, independently of strand. mRNA regions to 961 compare with were defined as the 500 bp downstream annotated TSSs. Small RNA-seq 962 coverage was computed over potential PROMPT and mRNA regions. To normalize small RNA 963 coverage to the RNA expression of respective regions, we divided the small RNA-seq coverage 964 values either by the CAGE PROMPT TC expression, or by the sum of CAGE TCs annotated as 965 'promoters' depending on the type of region.

966

#### 967 **de novo Reconstruction of Antisense Transcripts**

968 To assemble de novo transcripts from RNA-seq data, we used Cufflinks v2.2.1 with 969 default parameters (Roberts et al., 2011), with the TAIR10 general feature format (GFF) 970 annotation provided as a guide (option -g). Cuffcompare was applied to compare the set of de 971 novo transcripts across genotypes. de novo transcripts overlapping a reference exon or intron 972 but on the opposite strand (codes s and x, respectively), and associated with an RNA-seq up-973 regulated gene on the reverse strand were selected for assessing the length and splicing of 974 exosome-sensitive transcripts.
