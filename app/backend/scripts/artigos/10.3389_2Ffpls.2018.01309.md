#### 32 Introduction

33 Extensive morphological diversity is exhibited by inflorescences across grass species, family 34 Poaceae. Since the inflorescences bear grain, understanding the genetic and molecular bases for 35 this variation can accelerate the generation of higher yielding crops through breeding or 36 precision engineering. Grasses encompass the world's important cereal crops; e.g. maize, 37 sorghum, and millets in the subfamily Panicoideae, wheat, oats, and barley in subfamily 38 Pooideae, and rice in subfamily Oryzoideae (Soreng et al., 2017). Many genes that regulate 39 aspects of inflorescence architecture in grasses have been discovered and characterized, largely 40 in maize and rice (Bommert and Whipple, 2017; Zhang and Yuan, 2014). In many cases gene 41 function is conserved across species (Bommert and Whipple, 2017; Whipple et al., 2010), 42 whereas in others, it varies between species or clades (Bommert and Whipple, 2017; McSteen, 43 2006; Whipple, 2017). Relatively little is known about how these genes interact in the larger 44 context of a developmental network to control inflorescence form and how these networks are 45 rewired across species.

46 All inflorescence structures are ultimately derived from a group of pluripotent cells called 47 the inflorescence meristem (IM). The IM is indeterminate and transitions from the shoot apical 48 meristem (SAM) during the shift from vegetative to reproductive growth (Bartlett and 49 Thompson, 2014; Kyozuka et al., 2014). In grasses, the terminal reproductive structure is the 50 spikelet, which bears one or more flowers that produce the seeds. The different routes that the IM 51 takes to form a determinate spikelet meristem (SM) largely determine the morphology of the 52 mature inflorescence (Kyozuka et al., 2014; Whipple, 2017). For example, the IM can produce 53 SMs directly on its flanks, as in wheat, or it can produce few to many indeterminate branch 54 meristems (BMs), which is typical for most grasses. Like the IM, BMs can produce SMs 55 directly, as in finger millet, or can initiate higher order branches, as in rice. The IM and BM may 56 ultimately convert to a terminal SM or simply cease development. Eventually, SMs initiate 57 sterile bracts (glumes) and floral meristems (FMs), which produce lateral organs that 58 differentiate into floral structures including lemma, palea, anthers and ovary (Thomas et al. 1988; 59 Chapman 1997).

In species of the Andropogoneae tribe, which includes maize and sorghum, spikelets are
borne in pairs where two SMs arise from a spikelet pair meristem (SPM). Paired spikelets also
arose independently in some other closely related groups. In the "bristle clade" of grasses, which

63 includes *Setaria* species and other members of the subtribe Cenchrinae (see Doust and Kellogg, 64 2002 and references therein). BMs can alternatively differentiate into sterile branches called 65 bristles. While spikelets are not paired in these species, morphological analyses suggest that 66 spikelets may be paired with bristles (Doust and Kellogg, 2002). Recent work in S. viridis showed that BMs poised to form bristles first initiate a SM identity program before the homeotic 67 68 shift to bristle formation. This developmental switch is dependent on proper spatiotemporal 69 synthesis of growth-promoting brassinosteroids (BRs) during SM development (Yang et al., 70 2017).

71 Despite this variation in inflorescence morphology among grasses, the core underlying 72 developmental processes are shared, and often leverage common regulatory modules. Pathways 73 that regulate IM size (Somssich et al., 2016), SM identity (Bommert and Whipple, 2017), and 74 flower development (Hirano et al., 2014), for instance, are largely conserved across those grass 75 species studied. However, these processes can vary by the spatiotemporal expression of certain 76 factors, functional divergence of gene family members, and/or co-option of novel factors all 77 together. For example, the RAMOSA1 (RA1) transcription factor (TF) is expressed at the base of 78 SPMs in Andropogoneae species and acts to suppress branching by conferring determinacy on 79 the SPM. The timing and degree of RA1 expression dictates inflorescence branching patterns; 80 i.e. during maize tassel development *ral* is induced early and suppresses higher order branching 81 whereas in sorghum and *Miscanthus*, delayed *ral* expression leads to highly branched 82 inflorescences (Vollbrecht et al., 2005). By leveraging comparative transcriptome analyses 83 across grasses, we can gain invaluable insight into the core components that regulate these 84 processes, and what variations are associated with species-specific morphologies.

85 Transcriptome studies of early inflorescence development have been reported for a few 86 important cereal crops, including maize (Eveland et al., 2014), rice (Furutani et al., 2006; Harrop 87 et al., 2016), barley (Digel et al., 2015) and wheat (Feng et al., 2017), leading to systematic 88 discovery of underlying regulatory modules. To our knowledge, maize is the only panicoid grass 89 with existing transcriptome data across early inflorescence development (Eveland et al., 2014). 90 Given the variation in architectures among important cereals in the Panicoideae, comparative 91 expression maps from additional species will help determine conserved and species-specific 92 components. Here, we present a comprehensive developmental and transcriptomics analysis of 93 inflorescence development in *S. viridis*, a model panicoid grass with a rapid life cycle, sequenced

94 genome, and emerging genetics and genomics toolkit (Bennetzen et al., 2012; Brutnell et al., 95 2010; Doust et al., 2009; Huang et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2017). We profiled six sequential stages 96 of inflorescence development that captured key events from the reproductive transition to floral 97 organ differentiation and identified stage-specific co-expression signatures. Our detailed characterization of S. viridis inflorescence development at both morphological and molecular 98 99 levels provides an invaluable resource for the community as a foundation for gene discovery and 100 a comparative platform for studying diverse architectures in agronomically important cereal 101 crops.

#### 103 Materials and Methods

### 104 **Plant growth conditions**

105 The reference *S. viridis* genotype A10.1 was used for this study. A10.1 seeds from two-year old 106 stocks were used to ensure full loss of dormancy and promote synchronized germination. Seeds 107 were planted in MetroMix 360 soil (Sun Gro Horticulture company) and grown in a controlled 108 growth chamber with the following conditions: temperature of  $31^{\circ}C/23^{\circ}C$  (day/night), light 109 intensity of 200 µmol/sq.meter/sec for 12 hours (6am-6pm) and 50% relative humidity, at the 110 Donald Danforth Plant Science Center's Integrated Plant Growth Facility. Plants were fertilized 111 with Jack's 15-16-17 (Hummert International) twice a week.

#### 127 RNA extraction, RNA-seq library construction, sequencing and analysis

128 Inflorescences were hand-dissected into fresh 100% acetone on dry ice from S. viridis seedlings 129 at 10, 12, 14, 15, 16 and 18 DAS. Depending on the representative size at a given stage, 10-30 130 individual inflorescence primordia were pooled per biological replicate; three to four biological 131 replicates were collected for each stage. All sampling was performed within a two-hour window 132 in the morning to control for circadian effects. Acetone was removed and samples were flash-133 frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into a fine powder using 3mm tungsten-carbide beads 134 (Qiagen) in a Tissue Lyser-II. Total RNA was isolated using the PicoPure RNA Isolation Kit 135 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with in-column DNase I treatment following manufacturer's 136 protocols. RNA-seq libraries were generated from 1µg total RNA using the NEBNext Ultra 137 Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England BioLabs Inc.) and size-selected 138 for 200 bp inserts. Libraries were quantified on a bioanalyzer (Agilent 2100) using a DNA 1000 139 chip to confirm the insert size and quantified again on a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Life 140 Technologies) to ensure different libraries were equally loaded, and then sequenced using 141 standard Illumina protocols (Illumina, Inc.) for either paired-end (PE) or single-end (SE) 142 sequencing at 100bp on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform at the University of Illinois at Urbana-143 Champaign W.M. Keck Center.

144

145 Sequenced reads were quality-checked using fastQC with Phred scores all above 30. For read

146 mapping and transcript quantification, we used *Salmon* (v0.8.1) (Patro et al., 2017) in a quasi-

147 mapping mode for indexing reference transcripts from the *S. viridis* primary transcript file

148 (Sviridis\_311\_v1; Phytozome v12.1, (phytozome.jgi.doe.gov) (Table S1). Transcripts were

149 quantified using the option –numBootstraps 30. Parameters not specified were run as default.

150 Salmon outputs were imported into R with the Bioconductor package tximport (v1.0.3). Raw

151 counts and Transcripts per Million (TPM) were extracted for 35,214 S. viridis primary transcripts

152 (Table S2). All subsequent analyses were conducted using TPM (Soneson et al., 2015).

#### 154 Gene co-expression analyses

155 To reduce noise from genes with ubiquitously low expression or that do not change expression 156 during development, we applied a set of criteria to filter genes that: (1) showed more than two-157 fold change in expression between at least two of the stages, and (2) collectively had an 158 expression value of at least 1 TPM across all stages. This resulted in 11,425 dynamically 159 expressed genes during inflorescence development (Table S3). To determine the optimal number 160 of clusters to use for fuzzy c-means (FCM) cluster analysis, we used two functions in the R 161 Bioconductor package, *Mfuzz* (Kumar and E Futschik, 2007): 1) Dmin, which calculates the 162 minimum centroid distance for a range of cluster numbers and 2) cselection, which reports the 163 cluster number where empty clusters are detected in the repeated soft clustering. Cluster number 164 was chosen for FCM analysis based on the centroids of each cluster being well separated based 165 on Dmin and there were no empty clusters based on cselection. FCM was performed using 166 *Mfuzz*. The 11,425 genes were clustered into groups based on their standardized mean expression 167 profiles (for each transcript, average expression is 0 and the standard deviation across 6 stages is 168 1). Each gene was assigned to the cluster with its highest membership score. Heatmaps and 169 hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distances and complete linkage clusters were generated 170 using MeV (Saeed et al., 2003) (http://mev.tm4.org/, version 4.8).

## 193 Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

194 The R Bioconductor package wgcna was used to perform WGCNA on the same set of 11,425 195 genes used in FCM analysis (Langfelder and Horvath, 2008). A matrix of all genes with their 196 TPM values across 23 samples (including individual biological replicates) was used as input. An 197 adjacency matrix was generated to determine similarity between genes (i.e. correlation for every 198 gene pair across the 23 samples) and transformed through a soft-thresholding procedure using 199 the function "pickSoftThreshold", where a soft power of 18 was chosen for module detection. A 200 topological overlap measure (TOM) was then calculated from the adjacency matrix to estimate 201 network interconnectedness. The dissimilarity of TOM (1-TOM) was used as the input for 202 average linkage hierarchical clustering to identify co-expression modules. Module eigengenes 203 (MEs), the first principle component of a given module, was used as a representative gene 204 expression profile for that module. Modules were further merged based on their MEs (using cutHeight = 0.25) and the module membership (MM) for each gene indicated the degree of 205 206 similarity between its expression profile and each ME. The entire network including WGCNA-207 calculated weights for each edge between genes (nodes) is available on NCBI GEO. Gene-to-208 gene connections were filtered if the weight of interaction was less than 0.02. Sub-networks were 209 visualized using Cytoscape v3.4.10 (http://www.cytoscape.org).

### 211 **Results and Discussion**

**1. Morphological characterization of inflorescence development in** *S. viridis.* 

213 To characterize the developmental progression of the *S. viridis* inflorescence, we used scanning

- electron microscopy (SEM) to examine sequential stages from floral transition to floral organ
- 215 development. At 8 days after sowing (DAS), the SAM had not yet transitioned, but had emerged

216 from surrounding leaf primordia (Figure 1A). By 10 DAS, the SAM had transitioned to the IM 217 and BMs were initiated but barely visible (Figure 1B). By 11 DAS, primary BMs had initiated at 218 the base of the developing inflorescence and continued to form in a helical pattern at 12 DAS 219 (Figure 1C and 1D). From 12 to 14 DAS, secondary and tertiary BMs developed sequentially in 220 a distichous pattern (Figure 1D-F). By 15 DAS, the IM had ceased to produce new BMs and 221 those at the inflorescence tip were the first to differentiate into SMs or bristles (Figure 1G and 222 1J). At 16 DAS, differentiation of SMs and bristles continued basipetally and the two developing 223 structures became morphologically distinguishable. SMs then initiated glumes and FMs, while 224 bristles started to elongate and form an indented ring below the meristem tip (Figure 1H and 1K), 225 which often later detached (Doust and Kellogg, 2002; Yang et al., 2017). By 18 DAS, floral 226 organs such as lemma, palea, and anther primordia formed in the spikelets, and bristles further 227 elongated and produced prickle hairs (Figure 1I and 1L) (Doust and Kellogg, 2002).

228

229 **2.** Transcriptome profiling and stage-specific expression of transcription factor families.

230 To establish a dynamic transcriptome map of S. viridis inflorescence development, we used 231 RNA-seq to link global changes in gene expression with developmental transitions. Based on our 232 detailed morphological characterization, we selected six stages that captured key events in 233 inflorescence development for transcriptome profiling by RNA-seq: the initiation of the IM (10 234 DAS), primary and secondary (12 DAS) and higher order (14 DAS) branching events, transition 235 to SMs (15 DAS), differentiation of spikelets and bristles (16 DAS), and development of floral 236 organs (18 DAS) (Figure 1). For each of these six stages, inflorescence primordia were hand-237 dissected and RNA-seq libraries prepared for three to four biological replicated as described in 238 the Methods (Table S1). Transcript abundance was quantified in TPM at each of the six stages; 239 these analyses showed strong correlations among biological replicates and dynamic patterns of 240 gene expression across developmental stages (Figures S1 and S2; Table S2). Based on our 241 filtering criteria described in the Methods, all analyses in this manuscript were performed using a 242 subset of 11,425 dynamically expressed genes (Table S3).

243

244 We explored developmental dynamics of transcription factor (TF) families across the six stages.

245 Spatiotemporal expression and combinatorial action of TF family members fine-tune

246 developmental decisions, and knowledge of their individual expression profiles can provide

247 insight into underlying regulatory mechanisms. We annotated several TF families in S. viridis 248 based on annotations of functional TF domains from various sources. Specifically, we focused 249 on families with multiple members previously implicated in plant development and plotted 250 relative expression of family members across the six stages: MADS-box, TCP, SBP (Figure 2), 251 AP2/ERF, bHLH, C2H2 Zinc, bZIP, LOB and NAC families (Figure S3; see abbreviations in 252 Box 1). Individual TF family members showed dynamic expression profiles during early 253 inflorescence development in S. viridis (Figures 2, S3 and S4; Table S4). These included 254 orthologs of previously characterized developmental regulators from maize and rice, as well as 255 many uncharacterized family members with potential functions in inflorescence development 256 based on their co-expression patterns. For example, we identified a set of 21 candidate TFs that 257 were highly expressed and showed large fold changes in expression that peaked at specific stages 258 of inflorescence development (Figure S4 and Table S4).

In addition, we performed a detailed phylogenetic analysis for the MIKC type MADSbox family based on these annotations to determine homology between the *S. viridis* genes and those in maize, rice and/or Arabidopsis (Figure S5). This phylogenetic framework enables comparative analyses among MADS-box family members that have been shown in multiple systems to work in a combinatorial manner to regulate development (Bartlett, 2017; Theißen et al., 2016).

### 266 **3. Modules of co-expressed genes during** *S. viridis* inflorescence development.

To define signatures of gene expression across this developmental trajectory, we used an FCM clustering approach (Kumar and E Futschik, 2007) and our filtered set of 11,425 dynamically expressed genes. Based on simulations and selection criteria as described in the Methods, we determined 25 as an optimal number of clusters to use for the FCM analyses (Figure S6A). FCM was used to assign a membership score for each gene to each cluster, and based on the highest score for each gene, 25 clusters with unique expression profiles were defined ranging from 290-608 genes per cluster (Figure S6B and Table S3).

The mean relative expression value across the six developmental stages was determined for each of the 25 clusters and hierarchical clustering on these values revealed four groups (Figure 3A), each representing a distinct developmental expression pattern: (1) floral transition and IM identity, (2) BM initiation and determinacy, (3) SM differentiation and FM initiation, and

278 (4) floral organ development. We first located orthologs of known developmental genes;

- 279 expression profiles of representative genes within each group were comparable to those of their
- 280 orthologs in maize and rice, consistent with their predicted roles in development (Figure 3B and
- Table S3). In the following four subsections, we describe expression of these and other key
- 282 developmental regulators during *S. viridis* inflorescence development compared to other grasses
- and highlight some key findings from each of the major expression groups. For simplicity, we
- listed full names and abbreviations of genes discussed along with their abbreviations in Table 1.
- 285

### **3.1. Group 1: Floral transition and inflorescence meristem (IM) identity.**

287 Genes in Group 1 (including clusters 8, 12, 15, 19 and 25) showed highest expression at 10 DAS 288 as the IM was initiated and decreased at later stages of development (Figures 3A and S6; Table 289 S3). We inferred that genes involved in regulating the transition to reproductive growth, IM 290 identity, and/or primary branch initiation would be included in these clusters. For example, 291 SvTfl1 (Sevir.8G033800), an ortholog of TERMINAL FLOWER1, a general repressor of 292 flowering in many species including maize, rice and Arabidopsis (Danilevskaya et al., 2010; 293 Hanano and Goto, 2011; Nakagawa et al., 2002), was grouped in cluster 19 (Figure 3B). Three 294 other TFL1-like homologs clustered in Group 1; Sevir.7G334800, a closely-related paralog of 295 SvTfl1, Sevir.7G097700 and Sevir.1G183200. The latter gene, which we named SvTfl1-like, was 296 expressed markedly higher than the other two at 10-12 DAS in cluster 15 (Table S3). Co-297 expressed in cluster 15 was SvLg2 (Sevir.5G394700), the ortholog of liguleless 2 (lg2) from 298 maize, which encodes a bZIP TF responsible for early establishment of lateral organ boundary 299 positioning (Harper and Freeling, 1996; Walsh et al., 1998). Loss-of-function lg2 mutants in 300 maize produce few to no tassel branches. Consistent with its expression profile in S. viridis 301 (Figure S4), *lg2* in maize has been implicated in the transition from vegetative to reproductive 302 growth (Walsh and Freeling, 1999).

In rice, *TAWAWA1 (TAW1)* encodes a nuclear protein belonging to the *ALOG* (*ARABIDOPSIS LSH1 and ORYZA G1*) gene family that is highly expressed in the IM and BMs early in inflorescence development, and decreases during the phase change to SM identity (Yoshida et al., 2013). *TAW1* promotes indeterminate IM and BM activity by suppressing acquisition of SM identity, and thus is a major regulator of inflorescence architecture (Yoshida et al., 2013). The *S. viridis* ortholog, *SvTaw1* (Sevir.9G221800), was found in cluster 19 (Figure

- 309 3A-B), which shows a very similar expression profile to cluster 15, consistent with *TAW1*
- 310 expression in rice. Five other *ALOG* genes were found in Group 1 and shared similar expression
- 311 profiles (Table S3); Sevir.7G17600 and Sevir.1G244200, which we named *SvTaw1-like 1* and
- 312 SvTaw1-like 2, respectively, shared highest homology with SvTaw1 and were co-expressed in
- 313 cluster 15 (Figure 4). Functional studies of other *ALOG* genes in various species indicate that
- they generally share a role in developmental phase changes and organ identity, and their
- 315 spatiotemporal expression patterns determine their specificity (Cho and Zambryski, 2011;
- Takeda et al., 2011; Yoshida et al., 2009). Notably, three genes belonging to a subfamily of
- 317 MADS-box TFs called SHORT VEGETATIVE PHASE (SVP), were also co-expressed in Group 1
- 318 (Figures 3B and 4; Table S3). In rice, homologs of these *SVP* genes (*OsMADS47*, *OsMADS55*,
- and OsMADS22) suppress FM initiation and were positively regulated by TAW1 to promote
- 320 indeterminacy (Kyozuka et al., 2014; Yoshida et al., 2013).

# 322 **3.2.** Group 2: Branch meristem (BM) initiation and determinacy.

- 323 Genes in Group 2 (clusters 3, 5, 9, and 13) tended to show highest expression at 12 and 14 DAS,
- decreasing by 15 DAS and into later stages (Figure 3A and S6). This expression pattern
- 325 coincides with the initiation, maintenance and patterning of BMs, which occurs mostly between
- 326 10 and 14 DAS, and is largely completed by 15 DAS. Among genes in this group were those
- 327 with putative roles in axillary meristem initiation and maintenance. For example, *SvMoc1*
- 328 (Sevir.4G229000), a GRAS family protein homologous to MONOCULM 1 (MOC1) from rice,
- 329 was found in cluster 3. This expression pattern is consistent with a possible role for *SvMoc1* in
- axillary meristem initiation, analogous to how it controls tiller outgrowth in rice (Li et al., 2003).
- 331 The rice WUSCHEL (WUS) ortholog, TILLERS ABSENT 1 (TAB1/OsWUS), was also shown to
- 332 play a role in initiating axillary meristems (Tanaka et al., 2015). Consistent with this, the *S*.
- 333 viridis ortholog, SvWus (Sevir.3G028500), was co-expressed in cluster 3 (Table S3). Also co-
- expressed with these developmental regulators was SvLfy, the S. viridis ortholog of zea
- 335 floricaula1/2/ABERRANT PANICLE ORGANIZATION 2 (APO2)/LEAFY (SvLfy
- 336 (Sevir.7G234000)). These genes play universal roles in regulation of meristem determinacy, but
- 337 with some species-specific differences; *LFY* and *zfl1/2* promote meristem determinacy and FM
- identity in Arabidopsis and maize, respectively (Bomblies et al., 2003; Schultz and Haughn,

1991), but *APO2* promotes meristem indeterminacy as well as BM initiation in rice (IkedaKawakatsu et al., 2012; Kyozuka et al., 1998).

341 Among the SBP TFs annotated in developing S. viridis inflorescences (Figure 2), three 342 were associated with Group 2. SvUb3 (Sevir.6G212600), the ortholog of unbranched 3 (ub3) 343 from maize and WEALTHY FARMERS PANICLE (WFP) from rice, was expressed in cluster 3. 344 The function of this gene is generally conserved in both species in controlling partitioning of 345 cells between lateral organ formation and meristem maintenance (Chuck et al., 2014; Jiao et al., 346 2010; Li et al., 2016; Miura et al., 2010). In maize, *ub3* shares redundant roles with *tassel sheath* 347 4 (tsh4), another SPB TF that functions also to suppress formation of leaves in the inflorescence (Chuck et al., 2010, 2014). SvTsh4 (Sevir.2G265300) was also found in Group 2, and was co-348 349 expressed in cluster 13 with SvTsh1 (Sevir.3G136200), which encodes a GATA TF that 350 functions in the same pathway (Wang et al., 2009; Whipple et al., 2010) (Figures 2 and 3A). 351 Initiation of lateral organs involves the formation of boundaries, regions of decreased cell 352 division that separate the growing organ primordia from meristematic signals to promote proper 353 organ identity. Genes implicated in setting up lateral organ boundaries were identified in Group 354 2. SvLg1 (Sevir.3G023500), the third SBP TF in this group and orthologous to *liguleless 1 (lg1)* from maize (Lewis et al., 2014; Moreno et al., 1997) and OsLG1 from rice (Lee et al., 2007), was 355 356 found in cluster 3 (Figures 2 and 3B). In maize, *lg1* is classically known for its role in setting up 357 the proximal-distal boundary that separates leaf blade from sheath and, along with  $lg_2$ , is 358 required for ligule and auricle development, thus controlling leaf angle. Recently, *lg1* was also 359 shown to regulate inflorescence architecture by controlling tassel branch number in maize (Lewis 360 et al., 2014) and branch angle in rice (Ishii et al., 2013; Zhu et al., 2013). Consistent with our 361 data from S. viridis, lg2 was proposed to act upstream of lg1 in maize, with lg2 expression 362 initiated earlier in development to specify boundary position (Harper and Freeling, 1996; Lewis 363 and Hake, 2016).

Also co-expressed with *lg1* in cluster 3 was *SvRa2* (Sevir.5G116100), ortholog of the maize *ramosa2* gene, which encodes a *LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARY* (*LOB*) domain TF that is expressed transiently in positions where axillary meristems will form on the inflorescence (Bortiri et al., 2006; Moreno et al., 1997) (Figure 3B and S3). In maize, *ra2* and *lg1* were shown to act in parallel pathways to control branch number and angle during early inflorescence development (Bai et al., 2012). The *S. viridis* ortholog of maize *barren stalk fastigiate 1* (*baf1*),

370 *SvBaf1* (Sevir.4G025200), was also co-expressed in cluster 3 (Figure 4; Table S3). In maize,

371 *baf1* encodes an AT-hook protein that is involved in demarcating the boundary region of axillary

- 372 meristems and plays a role in their initiation (Gallavotti et al., 2011).

374 The positioning of axillary meristems is strongly influenced by transport and function of 375 the phytohormone auxin. As auxin flows in and out of emerging primordia, localized auxin 376 response maxima arise at places of lateral meristem initiation, and this mechanism is generally 377 conserved in maize inflorescence development (Barazesh and McSteen, 2008; Eveland et al., 378 2014; Gallavotti, 2013). Therefore, we would expect suites of auxin-related genes to be 379 dynamically expressed between 12 and 15 DAS in S. viridis while branching occurs; i.e. in 380 Groups 2 and 3. We annotated genes in these groups based on homology to those implicated in 381 synthesis, transport, signaling and/or response of auxin, and plotted their expression profiles 382 (Figure S7 and Table S5). The expression of SvVt2 (Sevir.5G116300), the ortholog of maize 383 vanishing tassel 2 (vt2), was initiated early in development and associated with cluster 13, while 384 expression of SvBif2 (Sevir.3G410700), the ortholog of barren inflorescence 2 (bif2), peaked 385 slightly later and was in cluster 3. *Vt2* encodes a tryptophan aminotransferase that regulates an early step of auxin biosynthesis (Phillips et al., 2011), and *bif2* encodes a PINOID 386 387 serine/threonine kinase that regulates auxin transport (McSteen et al., 2007) (Figure S7). 388 Other key genes implicated in auxin-mediated lateral branching in maize and rice 389 inflorescence development were expressed slightly later and in Group 3 (Figures 3A and S7). For 390 example, genes in cluster 22 increased expression between 10 and 14 DAS, but expression was 391 stable after 15 DAS unlike Group 2 clusters that decreased expression between 15 and 18 DAS 392 (Figures 3A and S6). These included *S. viridis* orthologs of maize *sparse inflorescence 1 (spi1*; 393 SvSpi1; Sevir.5G251100), encoding a YUCCA-like gene involved in a late step of auxin 394 biosynthesis (Gallavotti et al., 2008), and barren stalk 1 (ba1)/LAX PANICLE 1 (LAX1) (SvBa1; 395 Sevir.5G374100), a bHLH protein involved in auxin signaling (Gallavotti et al., 2004; Komatsu 396 et al., 2003a) (Figure S7). In maize, functional BA1 is required for creation of auxin maxima at 397 the meristem anlagen to promote lateral meristem initiation. Our expression data from S. viridis 398 are consistent with expression and genetics analyses in maize that show *ba1* acts downstream of 399 *baf1* (Gallavotti et al., 2011).

#### 401 **3.3.** Group 3: Spikelet meristem (SM) differentiation and floral meristem (FM) initiation.

402 Clusters 10, 11, 18, and 22 in Group 3 have similar expression signatures that show a progressive

403 increase during early stages of development, peaking around 16 DAS, and decreasing slightly by

404 18 DAS (Figure 3A and S6). Among these clusters were a number of genes related to axillary

405 meristem identity and determinacy. For example, *SvBd1* (Sevir.2G437800), a marker of SM

406 identity in *S. viridis* (Yang et al., 2017), was found in cluster 18 (Figure 3B and Table S3).

407 Orthologs of this AP2/ERF TF across grass species, e.g. branched silkless 1 (bd1) in maize,

408 FRIZZY PANICLE (FZP) in rice, and MORE SPIKELETS 1 (MOS1) in Brachypodium

409 *distachyon,* share a conserved function in specifying SM identity (Chuck et al., 2002; Derbyshire

410 and Byrne, 2013; Komatsu et al., 2003b).

411 Genes in the maize RAMOSA pathway regulate meristem determinacy prior to SM 412 identity (Vollbrecht et al., 2005). Among these genes, ra2 is widely conserved across grasses and 413 its expression comes on early during axillary meristem initiation. Consistent with genetics and 414 expression data from maize (Bortiri et al., 2006; Satoh-Nagasawa et al., 2006), the S. viridis 415 orthologs of ral (SvRal; Sevir.2G209800) and ramosa 3 (ra3) (SvRa3; Sevir.2G407500), which 416 encodes a trehalose-phosphate phosphatase (Satoh-Nagasawa et al., 2006), were expressed after 417 initiation of SvRa2 and were found in clusters 10 and 11, respectively, their expression peaking 418 at 15-16 DAS (Figure 3A-B).

419 Genetic interactions among genes controlling meristem determinacy and meristem size 420 pathways in maize indicated these processes interface generally (Bommert and Whipple, 2017). 421 Interestingly, many genes orthologous to known players in meristem size pathways were also co-422 expressed in Group 3. For example, SvFea4 (Sevir.4G119100), the ortholog of fasciated ear 4 423 (fea4) from maize and PERIANTHIA (PAN) from Arabidopsis encoding a bZIP TF that 424 negatively regulates meristem size (Maier et al., 2009; Pautler et al., 2015), was found in cluster 425 22 (Figure 3A and S3). Regulatory components of the CLAVATA-WUSCHEL negative 426 feedback signaling pathway central to meristem maintenance (Somssich et al., 2016) were also 427 co-expressed in cluster 22. Among these were SvTd1 (Sevir.4G294000) and SvFon2 428 (Sevir.8G183800), orthologs of maize thick tassel dwarf 1 (td1)/CLAVATA1, encoding a leucine-429 rich repeat receptor-like kinase (Bommert et al., 2005), and rice FLORAL ORGAN NUMBER 430 2/CLV3, encoding a CLV3/ESR related peptide (Chu et al., 2006; Goad et al., 2017), respectively

431 (Figure S2 and Table S3). Both *td1/CLV1* and *FON2/CLV3* negatively control meristem size.

432 Certain Class I KNOX homeodomain TFs also function in pathways to maintain 433 meristem cell identity and size. Orthologs of the maize knotted 1 (kn1) and rough sheath 1 (rs1) 434 genes, SvKn1 (Sevir.9G107600) and SvRs1 (Sevir.2G029800), respectively, both homologs of 435 the Arabidopsis BREVIPEDICELLUS (BP) gene, were co-expressed in cluster 11 (Figure 3A and 436 S6). In maize, *kn1* is a key marker of meristem maintenance (Bolduc et al., 2012; Kerstetter et 437 al., 1997), and the localized expression of *rs1* predicted and subtended the initiation of axillary 438 meristems on the flanks of the IM (Schneeberger et al., 1995). Interestingly, the S. viridis 439 homologs of the BP interaction partner, BRAHMA (BRM), and a closely related gene, SPLAYED 440 (SYD), both encoding SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling ATPases in Arabidopsis (Wagner and 441 Meyerowitz, 2002; Zhao et al., 2015), Sevir.2G237500 and Sevir.4G112300, respectively, were 442 also found cluster 11 (Table S3). In Arabidopsis, BRM upregulates CUC genes (Kwon et al., 443 2006), which are essential players in lateral meristem establishment and proper boundary 444 formation; SvCuc2 (Sevir.4G124400) and SvCuc3 (Sevir.6G213600) were co-expressed in 445 cluster 22. In addition, two class II TCP TFs orthologous to branch angle defective 1 (bad1)/ 446 wavy auricle blade 1 (wab1) (Bai et al., 2012; Lewis et al., 2014) and teosinte branched 1 (tb1) 447 (Doebley et al., 1997) were co-expressed in cluster 22 (SvBad1 (Sevir.2G210100) and SvTb1 448 (Sevir.9G122200), respectively). Consistent with their expression profiles during S. viridis 449 inflorescence development (Figure 2), their localized expression overlaps in axillary meristems 450 of young maize tassels and both genes contribute to the regulation of branch outgrowth (Bai et 451 al., 2012).

### 484 **4. Co-expression network analysis using WGCNA.**

485 To extend our co-expression analyses, we also performed a Weighted Gene Co-expression

486 Network Analysis (WGCNA) (Langfelder and Horvath, 2008). We used WGCNA to

487 independently define modules of co-expressed genes and to construct a co-expression network

488 for early *S. viridis* inflorescence development (Table S6). WGCNA produced 14 co-expression

- 489 modules based on parameters detailed in the Methods, which were highly consistent with our
- 490 FCM results; i.e. each WGCNA module largely corresponded to the sum of several FCM clusters
- 491 (Figure S8). In addition to grouping genes into co-expression modules, WGCNA was used to
- 492 determine the interconnectedness of genes within and between modules. A strength value (i.e.
- 493 weight) was assigned to the connection (edge) between each gene (node) and every other gene in

494 the network. An integrated co-expression network was generated after filtering gene-to-gene 495 connections with very low connectivity (weight > 0.02; available in NCBI GEO).

496 To define sub-networks related to specific developmental processes and potentially 497 identify new regulatory factors, we filtered the co-expression network for genes with strong 498 connections to known genes of interest within a module. For example, we explored a co-499 expression module that included SvLg1, SvBaf1, and SvBif2, which are known players in 500 boundary formation and axillary meristem initiation ("Plum" module; Figure S8 and Table S6). 501 To reduce complexity in visualization and interpretation of this module, we included only genes 502 with strong interactions (edges between genes having a weight > 0.2) to a set of ten predicted 503 developmental genes (Figure 5 and Table S7). One sub-network was defined that included strong 504 co-expression "interactions" among SvDfl1 (Sevir.2G302300), a homolog of maize delayed 505 flower 1 (Muszynski et al., 2006), SvVt2 and a GH3 IAA-amido synthase (Sevir.9G364900; 506 Figure S7), homologous to Arabidopsis YADOKARI 1 (YDK1) and predicted to regulate auxin 507 levels (Staswick et al., 2005), via a common set of genes. In another sub-network, SvPla1 508 (Sevir.9G259300), a cytochrome P450 homologous to Arabidopsis PLASTOCHRON1 (Miyoshi 509 et al., 2004; Sun et al., 2017), was identified as a hub gene directly or indirectly connected to 510 SvBaf1, SvLg1, and SvBif2, consistent with its conserved role in lateral organ initiation (Figure 5; 511 Table S7). Within these subnetworks, some developmental regulators showed high connectivity 512 to many other genes, while others showed relatively low connectivity (Figure 5).

#### 514 **5.** *S. viridis* as a comparative model for panicoid grass inflorescence development.

515 Our transcriptomics analysis in *S. viridis* identified homologs of known developmental regulators 516 as well as uncharacterized genes with putative roles in panicoid grass inflorescence development. 517 Based on spatiotemporal expression profiles, we showed that genes controlling transitions in 518 meristem types during inflorescence development are largely conserved between S. viridis and 519 maize (Figure 4). Since the diversity of inflorescence architectures found across grasses are 520 largely determined by subtle regulatory variation on common developmental processes, it is not 521 surprising that suites of genes have conserved functions across grasses, and often analogous 522 functions in more distantly related species. For example, *tsh1/NL1* genes share conserved 523 functions in boundary establishment and bract suppression in maize and rice (Wang et al., 2009; 524 Whipple et al., 2010), whereas the close Arabidopsis homolog, HANABA TARANU, functions in

floral organ development (Zhao et al., 2004). However, in some cases orthologous genes can

- 526 take on context-specific functional roles. The rice ortholog of *baf1*, *DEPRESSED PALEA 1*
- 527 (*DP1*), regulates palea formation and floral organ number (Jin et al., 2011), while its close
- 528 homolog in Arabidopsis, *AHL22*, functions in the floral transition (Yun et al., 2012).

529 We anticipate that species-specific expression differences underlie the variation in 530 inflorescence form in *Setaria spp.* compared to maize and other closely related grasses. While we 531 are beginning to elucidate some of the genes responsible for these morphological differences 532 through mutant screens and experimental analyses (Yang et al., 2017), identifying subtle 533 variation in gene regulation at the network level will require parallel transcriptomics resources 534 from other species. In addition, using co-expression analyses to prioritize novel candidate genes 535 as having previously undefined functions in development will also be most powerful when 536 evaluated in the context of comparable datasets. Thus, a detailed knowledgebase of how the 537 expression of individual genes shift during development and how co-expressed gene modules are 538 rewired across grass species, is invaluable to understanding the genetic basis for morphological 539 diversity in grass inflorescence architecture.

540 The apparent similarity between maize and *S. viridis* at the transcriptome level supports 541 its use as a functional model for panicoid cereals, especially when paired with rapid advances in 542 gene editing and transformation technologies (Zhu et al., 2017). Our transcriptomics analyses 543 provide a platform for gene discovery in *S. viridis* inflorescence development and a comparative 544 model for studying diverse architectures of agronomically important cereal crops. With respect 545 to the former, it is anticipated that these data will help uncover novel expression patterns 546 associated with the unique features of S. viridis inflorescence morphology, which can be 547 translated to other millets (Huang et al., 2016), including subsistence crops in many developing 548 countries that remain largely untapped for genetic improvement.

**Figure 1.** Key developmental stages of *S. viridis* inflorescence development by SEM. (A) At 8 DAS, the *S. viridis* SAM (white open circle) had not yet transitioned to reproductive development. (B) By 10 DAS, the SAM had transitioned to an indeterminate IM (white dot) and primary BMs (yellow arrow) were initiated. As development proceeds through (C) 11 DAS, (D) 12 DAS, (E) 13 DAS and (F) 14 DAS stages, primary, secondary and higher order branching was progressively initiated (white dot = IM; yellow arrow = primary BM; white arrow = secondary BM; red arrow = tertiary BM). (G and J) At 15 DAS, SMs (red asterisk) had begun to differentiate and by 16 DAS (H and K), were distinguishable from developing bristles (white asterisk) and initiated glume primordia (lg = lower glume; ug = upper glume). (I and L) At 18 DAS, bristles were elongated and developed prickle hairs (ph) and SMs further differentiated floral organs (ul = upper lemma; p = palea; ap = anther primordium; o = ovary). Developmental stages that were used in for RNAseq-based transcript profiling are labeled.

**Figure 4.** Orthologs of genes known to regulate inflorescence architecture in maize and rice showed expected expression patterns during *S. viridis* inflorescence development and transitions between meristem types. The schematic diagrams sequential developmental transitions captured by the RNA-seq data and shows relative expression of genes predicted to control these transitions. For example, consistent with the initiation of BMs from the IM between 10-14 DAS, positive regulators of lateral meristem formation (e.g. *SvBif2* and *SvUb3*) showed increased expression between 10-14 DAS, and negative regulators of axillary meristem formation (e.g. *SvTd1* and *SvFea4*) increased later at 14-16 DAS. Genes displayed in the heat map are divided by group and developmental processes can transition across groups. Light-to-dark color indicates low-to-high relative expression.
