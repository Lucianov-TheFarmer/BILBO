The maternal-to-zygotic transition (MZT) is an essential developmental turning point in both plants and animals. In plants, the timing of MZT and parental contributions to the zygotic transcriptome remain unclear. Here, by overcoming technical limitations, we characterize the Arabidopsis egg cell, zygote, and embryo transcriptomes across multiple stages. Using these datasets, we demonstrate that MZT occurs during zygote development and is a two-step interrelated process of rapid maternal transcript degradation followed by large-scale de novo transcription. Parental contributions to the zygotic transcriptome are stage-dependent: the spherical zygote is characterized by a maternally dominated transcriptome, whereas the elongated zygote transcriptome shows equal parental contributions. Our results show that plant MZT is similar to that in animals, showing a typical two-step process, and that zygotic genome activation is required for zygote elongation and division, indicating that de novo transcripts are essential for the establishment of zygote polarity and embryogenesis promotion.

In higher plants, fertilization bridges the gametophytic and sporophytic generations. During the progression between the two generations, genetic control of development undergoes a maternal-to-zygotic transition (MZT), representing the transition from maternal genome control to zygotic genome control. During this process, complex combinations and interactions between maternal and paternal genomes occur, which are critical for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying zygote development and promotion of embryogenesis. In animals, zygote development and early embryogenesis depend on the genetic information deposited previously in the egg cell, referred to as maternal control, and zygotic genome activation (ZGA) occurs after several rounds of embryonic cell division; e.g., one cell cycle in mouse and eight cycles in *Drosophila* [\(Schier, 2007; Lee](#page-12-0) [et al., 2014\)](#page-12-0). Moreover, the MZT in animals has been shown to consist of two overlapping processes, the clearance of maternal factors and the *de novo* transcription of the zygotic genome [\(Tadros and Lipshitz, 2009](#page-12-1)).

In flowering plants, knowledge of the MZT remains fragmented. Because of technical limitations, particularly the challenges of egg cell and zygote isolation, complete transcriptome analysis covering all stages of early embryogenesis has not been achieved. Therefore, questions related to the component events of MZT, timing of ZGA, and parental contributions to the zygotic transcriptome have not been fully elucidated, and available findings are often conflicting ([Autran et al., 2011; Del Toro-De Leo´](#page-11-0) n et al., [2014; Nodine and Bartel, 2012; Vielle-Calzada et al., 2000; Weijers](#page-11-0) [et al., 2001\)](#page-11-0). This has been attributed to the contamination of transcriptomes by maternal tissues ([Schon and Nodine, 2017](#page-12-2)) or to the effects of hybridization between different accessions on gene expression ([Baroux et al., 2013; Del Toro-De Leo´](#page-12-3) n et al., 2016).

In this study, we clarified this process with a careful investigation that overcame technical limitations to realize a complete deep transcriptome analysis of early embryogenesis. Because the MZT not only implies transcriptional activity of the zygotic genome but also that the fate of the embryo is controlled by the zygotic genome, we also evaluated the role of *de novo* transcripts in zygote development and early embryogenesis. The results revealed that the plant MZT starts with global clearance of maternal transcripts, followed by two waves of ZGA during zygote development. We confirmed that the ZGA is required for zygote development and the initiation of embryogenesis; that is, zygotic control indeed occurs at the zygote stage. We also revealed two-phase ZGA, a maternal-dominant transcriptome phase in the early zygote and an equal-parental-contribution phase in the elongated zygote. Furthermore, we showed that the maternal-dominant transcriptome in the early zygote is due to carry-over of maternal transcripts from the egg cell, not to primary transcription of the maternal genome. In addition, we provide reliable datasets, including all the transcripts from egg cells, spherical zygotes, elongated zygotes, one-cell embryos, and 32-cell embryos, respectively, as well as all the parental-of-origin transcripts at each of zygote stages, to facilitate further studies.

Transcriptome Reconstruction Occurs in Zygotes The UHC analysis and PCA revealed clear reconstruction of the transcriptome between egg cells and Zy14, and also between Zy14 and Zy24 ([Figures 2A](#page-3-0) and 2B), as evidenced by the good separation of stage-correlated transcriptomes. To further characterize the transcriptome changes at a system level, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) ([Langfelder](#page-12-7) [and Horvath, 2008, 2012](#page-12-7)) was performed, from which we identified 14 distinct modules of co-expressed genes from the transcriptome datasets ([Figure 2](#page-3-0)C). Notably, seven representative co-expression modules were highly correlated with their corresponding developmental stage (*r* > 0.7, p < 0.01; [Figure 2](#page-3-0)D), that is, genes in these modules displayed a developmental stage-specific expression pattern ([Figure 2E](#page-3-0)). For example, egg cell-enriched module M1 was composed of 1,967 genes with a common expression pattern that showed the highest expression levels in egg cells. Importantly, we identified three modules, with particular enrichment in Zy14 (M4 and M5) and Zy24 (M9), respectively. These results indicate that transcriptome reconstruction in zygotes results in distinct expression modules shortly after fertilization. Therefore, the components of the egg cell transcriptome change substantially after fertilization, with two notable reconstructions at the Zy14 and Zy24 stages.

MZT Starts with Maternal Transcript Deletion In animals, MZT comprises two overlapping processes: the clearance of maternal transcripts and ZGA. Although MZT research has been extended to various plants for decades, such studies have focused on ZGA, and it remains unclear whether maternal transcript deletion occurs in plants during early embryogenesis. Given that transcriptome reconstruction occurs shortly after fertilization, we investigated whether plant MZT undergoes a conserved maternal transcript deletion process, as in animals. Pairwise comparisons between EC and zygotes were performed to define downregulated genes. We observed significant downregulation of 953 genes (fold change [FC] % 0.5, false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.01) in Zy14, and 2,956 downregulated genes in Zy24 ([Figures 3A](#page-5-0) and 3B; [Table S2](#page-11-1)). These genes were clustered into four groups based on their abundance change after fertilization [\(Figures 3](#page-5-0)C and 3D). Hundreds of genes in groups Ⅰ and Ⅱ (n = 929) exhibited the highest levels in EC, which were significantly downregulated in zygotes, and continually decreased in embryos. Of these genes, 377 were not detected after fertilization (FPKM % 1; [Table S3\)](#page-11-1), suggesting that these transcripts were carried over from EC and degraded during zygote development. Furthermore, several known EC marker-genes, such as *EC1*, were among the top-ranking genes in this group, supporting the grouping and their disappearance in zygotes. These results reveal that global clearance of maternal transcripts occurs shortly after fertilization, and the deletion process appears to be conserved in both animals and plants during the MZT.

In particular, 1,676 transcripts were upregulated in Zy24 compared with Zy14 [\(Figure 4](#page-6-0)B), suggesting that two major activation waves occurred after fertilization. In total, we observed significant upregulation of 4,436 genes in zygotes compared to egg cells. ZGA Is Required for Zygote Growth and Division To functionally confirm the occurrence of the MZT, we specifically blocked *de novo* transcription in zygotes with transcriptional inhibitor a-amanitin using an *in vitro* microculture system [\(Figure 5](#page-8-0)A). Zygotes treated at 8 HAP failed to elongate and were unable to establish polarity. Moreover, these zygotes never divided (n = 139). By contrast, control zygotes (98%, n = 127) displayed normal elongation, nuclear migration, and subsequent asymmetric cell division [\(Figures 5B](#page-8-0) and 5D). Next, we treated zygotes at 14 HAP, in which substantial *de novo* transcripts had generated ([Figure 4C](#page-6-0)), most of which (91%, n = 131) continued to elongate and divide symmetrically after treatment [\(Figures 5C](#page-8-0) and 5E). These observations suggest that *de novo* transcripts are required for zygote polarity establishment and asymmetric division, and that maternal transcripts deposited in egg cells appear to be insufficient to support these developmental processes.

Parental Contributions Are Stage Dependent To investigate the parental contributions to *de novo* transcription of zygotes, the transcriptomes of 14 HAP and 24 HAP hybrid zygotes derived from the reciprocal crossing of Col-0 and Ler were generated, which were highly correlated with isogenic zygotes at the corresponding stages [\(Figure S4\)](#page-11-1). Moreover, 66.1% of informative reads were of maternal origin in Zy14. However, nearly equal reads from maternal and paternal genomes were observed in Zy24 ([Figures 6](#page-9-0)A and [S5](#page-11-1)A; [Table S5\)](#page-11-1). These results show that the paternal contributions to the zygotic transcriptome display a stage-dependent pattern; the first phase is characterized by the maternally dominated transcriptome, whereas the second phase is characterized by equivalent contributions by the parental genomes.

To explain the change in parental contributions in Zy24, the relative parental contributions to the expression of each gene were examined in zygotes. Consistent with the maternally dominated Zy14 transcriptome, thousands of genes displaying a maternally biased expression pattern (FC [maternal O paternal] > 4, FDR < 0.05) were found in Zy14. However, maternally biased transcripts sharply decreased in Zy24 ([Figures 6B](#page-9-0), 6C, [S5B](#page-11-1), and S5C; [Table S6](#page-11-1)). Next, we analyzed the parental expression of all maternally expressed genes (MEGs) identified in Zy14 to follow their dynamics in Zy24 (n = 448). Most MEGs (n = 404) in Zy14 were converted into biparental expression in Zy24 ([Figure 6](#page-9-0)D). Quantification of the parental contributions to the expression of these genes revealed that 69.6% of MEGs showed a decrease in maternal level and 93.6% showed an increase in paternal level in Zy24 ([Figure 6](#page-9-0)E). Therefore, the transition from maternally dominated to equiparentally contributed transcriptome in Zy24 mainly resulted from maternal transcript degradation and increased paternal expression.

To explore whether EC-delivered transcripts or the prioritization of maternal transcription is the major mechanism driving the maternally dominated transcriptome in Zy14, we examined the expression pattern of MEGs in all cell types. Most MEGs (1,200 of 1,258) were co-expressed in EC and Zy14 ([Figure S6](#page-11-1)A). The expression profiles of these MEGs were categorized by Short time-Series expression miner (STEM), enabling the identification of distinct temporal expression profiles [\(Figure S6B](#page-11-1)). Many MEGs (n = 341) displayed higher levels in EC and lower levels in zygotes [\(Figure S6C](#page-11-1)), which was further confirmed by clustering the expression data of these MEGs ([Figure S6D](#page-11-1)). Furthermore, both down- and upregulated genes in Zy14 with SNPs were selected for parental contribution analysis. The population of downregulated genes was maternally dominant [\(Figures 7](#page-10-0)A–7C; [Table S7\)](#page-11-1). However, the upregulated genes generated by *de novo* zygotic transcription showed nearly equal parental contributions [\(Figures 7D](#page-10-0)–7F; [Table S7\)](#page-11-1). This was supported by the GFP reporter line analysis of four representative *de novo* genes [\(Figures 7](#page-10-0)G and 7H). These results reveal that maternally dominant transcripts in Zy14 are caused by the carry-over of transcripts deposited in EC.

The MZT is a critical point of genetic control during early embryogenesis. The timing of the transition and the parental contributions to the transcriptome are major questions regarding the MZT. The various analyses of the model plant *Arabidopsis* performed to date have yielded inconsistent results [\(Autran et al.,](#page-11-0) [2011; Nodine and Bartel, 2012; Del Toro-De Leo´](#page-11-0) n et al., 2014). In this work, we isolated five cell types (including egg cells and zygotes) at two developmental stages. These time-course transcriptomes have not been analyzed previously in *Arabidopsis*.

Our results revealed large-scale *de novo* transcripts as early as Zy14, indicating that spherical zygotes have at this time point the ability to transcribe at the genome-wide scale, which was further confirmed by the GFP reporter line analysis. More detailed UHC analysis, PCA, and WGCNA across all cell types confirmed significant transcriptome reconstruction between egg cells and Zy14, and between Zy14 and Zy24, indicating that the MZT initiated in the zygote stage. Interestingly, in maize and rice the ZGA occurs shortly after fertilization [\(Anderson et al.,](#page-11-2) [2017; Chen et al., 2017](#page-11-2)), suggesting that the process is similar in eudicots and monocots.

<span id="page-11-1"></span>Our findings suggest that the time course of embryonic development governed by maternal factors in plants differs significantly from that in animals, in which maternal factors can support several cell cycles of early embryo development; e.g., one cell cycle in mouse and eight cycles in *Drosophila*, and ZGA is initiated considerably later [\(Schier, 2007; Lee et al., 2014\)](#page-12-0). Hence, parental contributions to the zygotic transcriptome display a developmental stage-dependent characteristic. As mentioned above, *Arabidopsis* zygotic development comprises two typical phases: that from the fertilized egg cell to the spherical zygote stage, and subsequently from the spherical stage to the elongated zygote stage. Interestingly, the stage-dependent characteristics of the zygote transcriptome are coupled with the two phases of zygote development. Recent research on algal zygotes supports that the zygote undergoes a two-phase process of polarization: the first phase comprising zygote elongation according to a maternally pre-determined direction, and the second phase dependent on zygotic transcription instead of solely on maternal factors ([Bogaert et al., 2017\)](#page-12-11). Thus, the two phases of zygote development and two models of underlying genetic control may be a conserved process during the MZT in different plant species.

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions *DAZ3::GFP* reporter lines were generated in Columbia-0 (Col-0) and Landsberg erecta (Ler) backgrounds. The plants were cultured in a greenhouse at 22 ± 1C with a 16/8 h light/dark cycle.

Mature ovules were collected from the emasculated flower buds of the *DD45::GFP* line for egg-cell isolation. To isolate zygotes and early embryos, we performed hand emasculation and pollination using the *DD45::GFP* marker line. The *Arabidopsis* ovules in the upper one-third of siliques after 14, 24, 36, and 80 HAP were collected individually under a fluorescence microscope to isolate 14 and 24 HAP zygotes and 1- and 32-cell embryos, respectively. The isolation of egg cells and 14 HAP zygotes was performed according to our previous protocol with some modifications ([Yu et al., 2016\)](#page-12-12). Egg cells and 14 HAP zygotes were directly dissected manually from ovules in 13% mannitol solution under an inverse fluorescence microscope. The 24 HAP zygotes, 1-cell embryos, and 32-cell embryos were isolated using a protocol for *Arabidopsis* embryo isolation with minor modifications ([Zhou et al., 2019\)](#page-12-6). Ovules at different stages after pollination were collected by treatment for 15 min with 0.1% cellulase R-10 and 0.08% Macrozyme R-10 (Yakult Pharmaceutical Ind. Co. Ltd., Japan) and dissolved in 80 mM sorbitol in 10% glycerol. Next, the seeds were extensively washed in the same solution without cellulase before embryo microdissection. Egg cells, zygotes, and early embryos were extensively washed in the same solution after isolation, collected in 10 mL of Lysis Buffer (Life Technologies, USA), and stored in liquid nitrogen for mRNA extraction.

cDNA Preparation and Library Construction for Sequencing mRNA was isolated from all samples (18–25 cells or embryos per sample) using the Dynabeads mRNA DIRECTTM Micro Kit (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA synthesis and amplification were performed using a SMARTer Ultra Low RNA Kit for Illumina Sequencing (Clontech, USA). cDNA was amplified for 15–18 cycles of LD PCR. The amplified cDNA was purified using an Agencourt AMPure Purification Kit (Beckman Coulter, USA). Purified cDNA was measured using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) and quantified using a Qubit Fluorometer according to the manufacturer's instructions.

RNA-Seq libraries were prepared using a NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, E7370, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten, generating about 6 Gb of paired-end 150 bp reads for each library, which are available in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus under accession number GSE121003.

RNA-Seq Data Analysis Reads that contained adaptors or paired-end reads of low quality were filtered using Cutadapt version 1.15 ([Martin, 2011\)](#page-12-14) and an inhouse script. Next, reads were mapped to the genome (Col-0 genome reference, TAIR10) using Bowtie 2 ([Langmead and Salzberg,](#page-12-18) [2012](#page-12-18)). Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM using RSEM [\(Li and Dewey, 2011\)](#page-12-13). Genes with an FPKM value of R 1.0 in at least one cell type were selected for analysis of their expression differential. DESeq2 ([Love et al., 2014\)](#page-12-16) was used to identify differentially expressed genes. Hierarchical clustering of the gene expression data was carried out using the Agnes function in the Cluster package. The Hclust function was used for hierarchical clustering analysis according to the value of the Pearson correlation coefficient between different samples.

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis A weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed to identify co-expression networks [\(Langfelder and](#page-12-7) [Horvath, 2008, 2012\)](#page-12-7) using the blockwiseModules function in the WGCNA package. For all of the input genes, a pairwise correlation matrix was first created. This matrix was transformed into a weighted network adjacency matrix. A power of 12 was set as the softthresholding power according to the scale-free topology criterion. Next, an average linkage hierarchical clustering tree was constructed using topological overlap-based dissimilarity for each pair of genes. The Dynamic Hybrid Tree Cut algorithm was used to cut the hierarchal clustering tree and define modules. A minimum module size of 30 and a height cut of 0.25 (corresponding to the correlation of 0.75) were used to merge modules. To identify modules significantly associated with each developmental stage, the Pearson correlation (*R*) and Student's asymptotic *P*-value (*P*) between each module and developmental stage were calculated.

Allele-Specific Transcriptome Analysis To overcome the mapping bias of the reads generated from hybrid zygotes, the reads were mapped to the Col-0 genome using a modified *in silico* simulation method ([Panousis et al., 2014](#page-12-19)). First, SNPs between Col-0 and Ler were downloaded from the TAIR ([https://www.arabidopsis.org/\)](https://www.arabidopsis.org/). Deduced Ler genome and transcript sequences were generated according to known SNPs. Next, the allelic read-mapping bias was estimated by simulating RNA-Seq reads from the transcripts with and without SNPs. We simulated reads covering all of the annotated transcribed regions using a sequencing error rate of 1%. For each haplotype that originated from Col-0 or Ler, we simulated an equal 503 coverage depth. The total coverage depth was 1003. The simulated reads were mapped to the Col-0 reference genome with BWA ([Li, 2013](#page-12-20)). A total of 150,693 SNPs was covered by the simulated reads, of which 18,944 sites were identified as having significant mapping bias (more reads from Col-0 than Ler; *P* < 0.05, binominal distribution test). SNPs with significant mapping bias were excluded from further analysis, and the remaining SNPs were used as markers for enumeration of allele-specific reads. To further reduce the influence of mapping bias, the number of reads carrying different alleles in the simulation data was calculated, and the ratio of the two allele counts was used as the null hypothesis for a binomial distribution test to evaluate allele-specific expression using the real sequencing data. SNPs covering transcribed regions of annotated genes were used to evaluate the paternal contribution to the transcriptome according to the following procedure. (1) Filtering out the SNP sites with < 95% Col-0- and Ler-specific bases. (2) Transcripts that had R 10 SNP-overlapping reads in the forward or reverse cross between Col-0 and Ler were used for further analysis. (3) If the same gene covered multiple SNPs, all SNPs were combined to quantify the parental contribution. (4) Differences in Col-0 and Ler allele expression were assessed by the binomial distribution test, and the *P*-value was adjusted using the FDR method. (5) Maternally and paternally enriched transcripts were screened in each sample according to: FC [maternal O paternal] > 4 or < 0.25; FDR < 0.05.

GO Analysis The GO terms for the genes were downloaded from the TAIR [\(https://www.arabidopsis.org/\)](https://www.arabidopsis.org/). Mapping of genes to GO terms was performed using an in-house Perl script. Enrichment *P*-values were calculated using the hypergeometric test as implemented in the R phyper function and were adjusted using the FDR method.

Time-Series Gene Expression Analysis Gene expression over time from egg cells to 32-cell embryos was analyzed using the STEM [\(Ernst and Bar-Joseph, 2006\)](#page-12-15). Expression profiles with a correlation > 0.8 were clustered together, and the maximum number of model profiles was set to 10. The *P*-values derived from STEM analysis were adjusted using the FDR method (FDR < 0.05).

Ovule Culture and Inhibitor Treatment Ovule culture was performed according to a previous protocol with some modifications [\(Kimata et al., 2016](#page-12-21)). Hand-pollinated flowers from the *DD45::GFP* line were dissected under a stereomicroscope to collect ovules for *in vitro* culture. Ovules in the upper one third of siliques were isolated and transferred into Nitsch medium, which contains 5% (wt/vol) trehalose dihydrate (J&K Scientific, China), 0.05% (wt/vol) MES-KOH (pH 5.8), and 13 Gamborg's Vitamin Solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). To block transcription in young zygotes, 100 mM a-amanitin (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the medium at the beginning of culture.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time Reverse Transcription-PCR Total RNA was extracted from ovules using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). After treatment with RNase-free DNase I (Promega, USA), cDNA was synthesized using an M-MLV First-Strand Kit (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. *TIP41* (AT4G34270), *UBC* (AT5G25760), and *PDF2* (AT1G13320) were selected as reference genes for reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) according to a previous report ([Czechowski et al., 2005](#page-12-22)). RT-qPCR was performed using FastStart Essential DNA Green Master Mix (Roche, Switzerland) and a CFX Connect-Real-Time System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA).

Vector Construction and Transformation To generate GFP reporter lines, gene promoter sequences were amplified and cloned into the vector pART 27 upstream of *GFP*. The constructs were transferred into *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* strain GV3101 for transformation according to a protocol published previously [\(Zhang et al., 2006](#page-12-23)).

Image Acquisition Egg cells, zygotes, and early embryos were observed and photographed using a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany). The fluorescence intensity of zygotes was measured using LAS-X software (Leica, Germany).
