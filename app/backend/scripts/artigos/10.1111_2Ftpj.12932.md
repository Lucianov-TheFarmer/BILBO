#### SUMMARY

The plant vascular system serves as a conduit for delivery of both nutrients and signaling molecules to various distantly located organs. The anucleate sieve tube system of the angiosperm phloem delivers sugars and amino acids to developing organs, and has recently been shown to contain a unique population of RNA and proteins. Grafting studies have established that a number of these macromolecules are capable of moving long distances between tissues, thus providing support for operation of a phloem-mediated inter-organ communication network. Currently, our knowledge of the roles played by such phloem-borne macromolecules is in its infancy. Here, we show that, in tomato, translocation of a phloem-mobile cyclophilin, SlCyp1, from a wild-type scion into a mutant rootstock results in restoration of vascular development and lateral root initiation. This process occurs through reactivation of auxin response pathways and reprogramming of the root transcriptome. Moreover, we show that long-distance trafficking of SlCyp1 is associated with regulation of the shoot-to-root ratio in response to changing light intensities, by modulating root growth. We conclude that long-distance trafficking of SlCyp1 acts as a rheostat to control the shoot-to-root ratio, by mediating root development to integrate photosynthesis and light intensity with requirements for access to water and mineral nutrients.

Keywords: auxin, cyclophilin, diageotropica, phloem, root development, shoot-to-root ratio, Solanum lycopersicum.

# INTRODUCTION

The plant vascular system provides the major route for inter-organ communication and coordination. While the xylem mostly conducts water and minerals, the phloem sieve-tube system delivers photoassimilates from source leaves to developing sink organs (Spanner, 1971; Turgeon and Wolf, 2009), as well as information molecules, including hormones, proteins and RNA transcripts (Giavalisco et al., 2006; Lough and Lucas, 2006; Omid et al., 2007; Lin et al., 2009). Numerous proteins identified in phloem sap collected from Brassica napus and pumpkin (Cucurbita maxima) are involved in signal transduction, RNA binding and defense responses (Ruiz-Medrano et al., 1999; Giavalisco et al., 2006; Lin et al., 2009). While these findings are consistent with the notion that the phloem serves as a conduit for inter-organ signaling (Lough and Lucas, 2006), little is known about the role of most phloem-borne macromolecules. The best characterized long-distance trafficking protein is FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT), whose phloem translocation from source leaves to the shoot apex has been shown to control the onset of flowering in Arabidopsis (Corbesier et al., 2007; Jaeger and Wigge, 2007), rice (Oryza sativa) (Tamaki et al., 2007) and pumpkin (Lin et al., 2007). FT has therefore been proposed to function as a component of the plant florigenic signaling pathway.

Cyclophilins are proteins that are present in phloem exudates analyzed from a number of plant species (Schobert et al., 1998; Lin et al., 2009; Rodriguez-Medina et al., 2011). Cyclophilins are peptidyl-prolyl cis/trans isomerases that are known to be involved in protein folding, and were first isolated as targets of the immunosuppressive drug cyclosporin A (Handschumacher et al., 1984). These proteins constitute a large family in plants, with over 29 genes characterized in Arabidopsis (Romano et al., 2004). Transcriptional studies have established that various cyclophilin isoforms are present in various plant organs, including leaf, flower buds, roots and stems (Romano et al., 2004). Accumulated data indicate that, in addition to their role in protein folding, cyclophilins also function in mRNA processing, protein degradation and signal transduction. Thus, it has been postulated that they play a crucial role during both development and the stress response (Romano et al., 2005).

One cyclophilin detected in phloem exudates of various plants is cyclophilin 1 (Cyp1) (Schobert et al., 1998; Gottschalk et al., 2008; Lin et al., 2009; Rodriguez-Medina et al., 2011). Insight into the role of Cyp1 in plant development has come from studies that mapped the diageotropica (dgt) mutation to the SlCyp1 gene (Solyc01g111170.2.1) in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) (Oh et al., 2006) and the PpCyp1 gene moss (Physcomitrella patens) (Lavy et al., 2012). Although not impaired in auxin synthesis (Fujino et al., 1988), dgt (SlCyp1) tomato mutants are auxin-insensitive (Kelly and Bradford, 1986), a finding that correlates with down-regulation of several Aux/IAA transcripts (Balbi and Lomax, 2003). As a result of their lack of auxin response, these mutants exhibit a pleiotropic phenotype, including limited secondary growth, aberrant xylem development, and a lack of lateral roots (Zobel, 1973). An earlier study indicated that basipetal polar auxin transport is identical in dgt and control plants (Daniel et al., 1989); however, more recent research demonstrated altered PIN-FORMED auxin efflux carrier in dgt mutants (Ivanchenko et al., 2015).

Here, we provide experimental evidence for the role that SlCyp1 proteins play in shoot-to-root long-distance signaling. Our results show that trafficking of SlCyp1 from a wild-type scion to a dgt mutant rootstock results in major changes in the root transcriptome and rescue of the dgt mutant auxin response capacity, leading to restoration of lateral root development and xylem vessel formation. Also, we show that trafficking of SlCyp1 from scion to rootstock is up-regulated by elevated light intensities, a finding that correlates with a specific increase in root growth and a reduction in shoot-to-root ratio. Collectively, our findings provide experimental support for the notion that SlCyp1 is a key player in the signal transduction pathway that is required for maintaining a physiologically balanced shoot-to-root ratio under the prevailing environmental conditions.

# RESULTS

# A phloem SlCyp1-derived signal restores dgt root development

In order to explore the possible long-distance functions of SlCyp1, reciprocal grafting experiments between wild-type (VFN8) and dgt mutant tomato plants were performed. The dgt rootstocks grafted onto dgt scions were less developed and lacked lateral roots (Figure 1a) compared with control (VFN8) rootstocks grafted onto VFN8 scions (Figure 1b). This resulted in a higher shoot-to-root ratio for dgt compared with VFN8 homografted plants (Figure S1). By contrast, dgt rootstocks grafted onto VFN8 scions developed lateral roots and exhibited restoration of root density (Figure 1c), resulting in shoot-to-root ratio values similar to those for control VFN8 homografted plants (Figure S1). Interestingly, the root system of VFN8 rootstocks grafted onto dgt scions (Figure 1d) was less developed than that of homografted VFN8 rootstocks (Figures 1b and S1). These experiments indicate that a SlCyp1-derived signal moves from the control scion into the dgt rootstock where it restores root development. Conversely, absence of SlCyp1 in the shoot (scion) resulted in inhibition of root growth.

Anatomical studies performed on the heterografted rootstocks revealed a clear influence of the SlCyp1-derived long-distance signal. Only narrow secondary xylem vessels were observed in dgt rootstocks grafted onto dgt scions (Figure 1e,i), in marked contrast to the large vessels present in the VFN8 vasculature (Figure 1f,j). Strikingly, restoration of normal secondary xylem vessel development was observed in the stems and roots of dgt rootstocks grafted onto VFN8 scions (Figure 1g,k). Newly developed xylem vessels, located adjacent to the vascular cambium, resembled those of control (VFN8) plants. Timecourse studies established that the anatomical changes in dgt rootstocks grafted onto VFN8 scions were observed between 10 and 20 days after grafting (Figures S2 and S3). It should be noted that the vascular bundles of VFN8 rootstocks grafted onto dgt scions were smaller compared with homografted VFN8 scions, albeit with similar xylem morphology (Figures 1h versus 1f). Taken together, these findings show that a SlCyp1-mediated long-distance signal, trafficking from the scion into the rootstock, alters developmental processes, including vascular development and lateral root growth.

## SlCyp1 protein serves as the long-distance signal

Based on the abundance of Cyp1 in phloem exudates of various plants (Schobert et al., 1998; Lin et al., 2009; Rodriguez-Medina et al., 2011), we determined whether the SlCyp1 protein serves as the phloem-mobile signal. Immunological testing using anti-AtCYP18–3/ROC1 antiserum, revealed an absence of signal when reacted with proteins extracted from root or stem segments taken from homografted dgt plants (Figure 4a,b). This result was further supported by immunolocalization tests showing that SlCyp1 signal was absent from homografted dgt rootstocks (Figure 4c) but present in the phloem and surrounding cambial cells of homografted VFN8 rootstocks (Figure 4d), with a similar signal present in dgt rootstocks grafted onto VFN8 scions (Figure 4e); no signal was detected in the pre-immune control (Figure 4f).

To ascertain whether SlCyp1 transcripts may also be phloem-mobile, we generated transgenic tomato plants expressing 69His-tagged SlCyp1 under the control of the companion cell-specific AtSuc2 promoter. Reciprocal grafting experiments between transgenic pSuc2:69His-SlCyp1 (TG) and dgt plants were performed to test whether the 69His-SlCyp1 transcript crosses the graft union. RT–PCR analysis, using primers specific for the 69His sequence, detected signal only in TG/TG rootstock tissues (Figure S4). These results are in agreement with absence of SlCyp1 from the list of rescued genes in the dgt rootstock grafted onto the VFN8 scion. We therefore propose that the SlCyp1 protein, but not the transcript, serves as the long-distance signal responsible for restoration of the mutant phenotype.

Previous studies established that, in control tomato plants, SlCyp1 is expressed in leaf vascular tissue and developing lateral root primordia (Ivanchenko et al., 2006, 2015; Oh et al., 2006). Consistent with this finding, SlCyp1 transcripts were detected in both source leaves and young developing roots of the VFN8 tomato line (Figure S5). Western blot assays performed on total protein extracted from these same tissues indicated that the level of SlCyp1 was comparable in source leaves and roots (Figure S5). The observed expression of SlCyp1 in both leaf and roots suggests that trafficked SlCyp1 may serve as a quantitative signal that is added to locally expressed SlCyp1 in the root to enhance lateral root development.

## Phloem delivery of SlCyp1 integrates photosynthesis with root growth

This study established that long-distance movement of SlCyp1 rescues root development in dgt mutants. We speculated that SlCyp1 trafficking from the shoot may play a quantitative role in optimizing resource allocation between the shoot and root systems when photosynthesizing leaves are subjected to changing external inputs. Light intensity is an environmental cue that is perceived primarily by the shoot, and has been shown to distinctly regulate root growth (Nagel et al., 2006). To further investigate the effect of light intensity on SlCyp1 trafficking and accumulation in rootstocks, plants were subjected to a series of photosynthetic photon flux densities (PPFDs). As an internal control for the effect of PPFD on phloem protein trafficking, we created grafts in which the scions were transgenic tomato plants expressing GFP driven by the AtSuc2 promoter. Levels of SlCyp1 in the grafted rootstocks were normalized to the level of phloem-delivered GFP to verify the specific effect of light intensity on the long-distance movement of SlCyp1. Levels of SlCyp1 in dgt rootstocks grafted onto pSuc2:GFP scions were elevated when plants were exposed to a higher PPFD in a growth chamber (Figure S6) or in a greenhouse (Figure 5a), a finding that correlated with SlCyp1 levels detected in the source leaves (Figure 5c). Moreover, a higher PPFD caused a significant reduction in the shoot-to-root ratio only when SlCyp1-expressing VFN8 plants were used as scions (Figures 5b and S6). Conversely, when the scions were dgt mutants that do not express SlCyp1, the shoot-to-root ratio was not significantly altered by the PPFD (Figures 5b and S6). These experiments established that both accumulation of SlCyp1 in source leaves and its long-distance trafficking are stimulated by PPFD, and these findings are tightly correlated with the regulation of root growth and concomitant adjustment of the shoot-to-root ratio.

#### DISCUSSION

Reciprocal grafting experiments between VFN8 wild-type and dgt mutant tomato plants established that the presence of SlCyp1 in the scion results in restoration of wildtype root and xylem development in dgt mutant rootstocks, demonstrating trafficking of a signal from the scion to the stock. As dgt is an SlCyp1 mutation in the VFN8 isogenic line, and complementation with the SlCyp1 coding region restores the dgt mutant phenotype (Oh et al., 2006), we tested the hypothesis that the long-distance signal is the SlCyp1 protein. In contrast to a recent report (Ivanchenko et al., 2015), immunological experiments confirmed that SlCyp1 moves long-distance via the phloem from the VFN8 scion to its presumed target sites in the dgt rootstock.

Alteration in secondary xylem development as well as lateral root formation may be explained by up-regulation of auxin response pathways (Reinhardt et al., 2003; Dharmasiri et al., 2005; Petrasek and Friml, 2009; Zhao, 2010). Numerous studies have established the role of the local auxin concentration on cambial patterning (Uggla et al., 1996), xylem development (Moyle et al., 2002) and lateral root formation (Kepinski and Leyser, 2005; Fukaki et al., 2007). The dgt mutant has been shown to be impaired in the auxin response, resulting in formation of undifferentiated cells instead of lateral root primordia (Ivanchenko et al., 2006, 2015). Our results show that lateral root differentiation requires a second longdistance signal, in addition to auxin, to promote the auxin response.

This family of plant-specific transcription factors controls multiple developmental processes, specifically lateral root formation (Xie et al., 2001; He et al., 2005; Willemsen et al., 2008), xylem development (Yamaguchi et al., 2008; Zhao et al., 2008) and the auxin response (Xie et al., 2001; He et al., 2005). Interestingly, the only NAC domain gene whose expression was elevated in dgt roots grafted onto VFN8 scions was the tomato ortholog of Arabidopsis Nac1, which was found to transduce the auxin signal downstream of TIR1, and thereby promote lateral root development (Xie et al., 2001). The results of our transcriptome analysis suggest a potential involvement of NAC domain transcription factors in the auxin response and root development. These transcription factors appear to act downstream of the SlCyp1-mediated long-distance signal.

Another significant finding from our study is that SlCyp1 long-distance trafficking is promoted under increasing light intensity. This result correlated with an increase in root biomass and a concomitant reduction in the shoot-to-root ratio. It was previously shown that exposing the shoot to increasing PPFD causes rapid upregulation of root growth, while shoot growth remains constant (Walter and Nagel, 2006). However, the factors linking root growth with the light intensity perceived by the shoot remained unknown. Our studies demonstrate that long-distance shoot-to-root trafficking of SlCyp1 is a key component in the inter-organ communication pathway that is required to adjust the root response to light intensity in order to optimize the shoot-to-root ratio for the prevailing environmental conditions.

The molecular mechanism by which SlCyp1 acts as a signaling agent in modulation of the auxin response in distant organs remains to be elucidated. A recent study suggested that cyclophilins facilitate auxin-mediated Aux/IAA protein degradation (Kang et al., 2013). As cyclophilins play a role in protein folding, it may well be that the long-distance trafficking signal includes an additional noncell-autonomous protein whose folding/refolding is SlCyp1-dependent. At this stage, we cannot exclude the possibility that an additional molecule also traffic long distance to restore lateral root development.

Collectively, our findings establish that elevated light intensities enhance both the accumulation of SlCyp1 in source leaves and its long-distance trafficking from the shoot to the root, where it induces major transcriptional changes in a manner that appears to involve increasing auxin-response capacity. These processes lead to changes in xylem and lateral root development that result in enhanced root growth. The increase in root growth causes a reduction in the shoot-to-root ratio, an important response to changing environmental conditions. Thus, our results indicate that SlCyp1 level and entry into the phloem in source leaves, like FT, are regulated by light to enable transport through the phloem to sink tissues. Whereas FT targets the apical meristem to activate the floral induction program (Corbesier et al., 2007; Jaeger and Wigge, 2007; Lin et al., 2007; Tamaki et al., 2007), SlCyp1 targets distant tissues of the root, where it induces major root architectural changes that are necessary to orchestrate and coordinate growth at the whole-plant level. Unlike FT, transcription of SlCyp1 occurs both in source leaves and in young roots where lateral roots develop (Ivanchenko et al., 2006). We observed that, in the early stages of tomato growth, VFN8 rootstocks grafted onto dgt scions underwent development that was close to normal (Figure 1d,h,l). This is consistent with a role for local SlCyp1 in root development (Figure 6), and suggests that a dynamic balance exists between locally synthesized and phloem-delivered SlCyp1. Future studies aimed at examining the balance of local and phloem-delivered SlCyp1 will provide further insights into this aspect of shoot-to-root homeostasis.

## EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

## Plant material, growth conditions and grafting

Tomato dgt mutants and their respective control (VFN8) were obtained from the Tomato Genetics Resource Center at the University of California at Davis. Control (M82–1–8) transgenic tomato plants expressing pDR5:39VENUSnls were kindly provided by Naomi Ori (The Robert H. Smith Institute of Plant Sciences and Genetics in Agriculture, Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Israel). For mRNA trafficking experiments, the 69His-tagged SlCyp1 gene was cloned downstream of the AtSuc2 promoter, and transgenic tomato plants (M82–1–8) were produced using conventional Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (Barg et al., 1997). For grafting experiments, plants were grown in an environmentally controlled growth chamber with temperatures of 25 2/18 2°C (day/night, respectively), under a 12 h photoperiod with a light intensity of 180 lmol m<sup>2</sup> sec<sup>1</sup> . In the greenhouse experiments, plant were grown at temperatures of 25 2/18 2°C (day/night, respectively), and exposed to natural light. Grafting was performed 2–3 weeks after sowing, 5 mm beneath the cotyledons, and the grafts were supported using plastic grafting tubes. Grafted plants were covered with a transparent plastic bag, immediately after grafting, for 7 days. Samples were collected 3 weeks after grafting. The data presented are based on at least five independent grafting experiments. Light intensities in the growth chamber were controlled by a combination of standard fluorescent and LED lights (Solo 503, 12 mm system, D–Led Ltd, [http://](http://www.d-led.net) [www.d-led.net\)](http://www.d-led.net), to provide a PPFD of either 120 or 280 lmol m<sup>2</sup> sec<sup>1</sup> . Plants were subjected to the various PPFDs for a period of 10 days, starting 7 days after grafting. The results from this experimental series are based on three independent experiments with 4–6 biological replicates in each of the treatments. Additional light intensity experiments were performed in a greenhouse. Plants were grafted in the growth chamber and allowed to recover for 7 days, after which they were transferred to a temperature-controlled greenhouse. Two shade levels were provided by white netting to give PPFDs of 600 (without netting), 300 and 150 lmol m<sup>2</sup> sec<sup>1</sup> , measured at the level of young source leaves. Plants were subjected to the indicated PPFDs for 8 days, after which samples were collected. Results are based on three independent experiments with 4–6 biological replicates for each treatment.

## Auxin response assay

Stem segments were excised from the various rootstocks, 1.5 cm below the graft union. These sections were cut into short pieces and incubated in MES/sucrose solution (10 mM MES buffer, 1% w/ v sucrose, pH 6.0) for 2 h to deplete endogenous auxin. Segments were then transferred to 100 mM sodium potassium citrate buffer (pH 4.6) containing 0.1 mM IAA or a similar solution without IAA. Samples were gently shaken for 2 h at room temperature. After incubation, samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at 80°C.

## RNA isolation and RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from 500 mg stem and root tissue from grafted rootstocks using Tri-reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, [http://](http://www.sigmaaldrich.com) [www.sigmaaldrich.com\)](http://www.sigmaaldrich.com), according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was prepared from the same amounts of RNA (1 lg) per sample pre-treated with 1 U lg<sup>1</sup> of RQ1 DNAse (Promega, <http://www.promega.com>), using a Verso cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Scientific, [http://www.thermoscientific.com\)](http://www.thermoscientific.com). A 2 ll aliquot of cDNA was used for PCR amplification. Real-time RT–PCR was performed using 0.5 ll of 2.5 pmol/ll of each primer (see Table S4), 4 ll cDNA and 5 ll ABsoluteTM Blue QPCR SYBR Green ROX mix (Thermo Scientific). The PCR conditions were as follows: 95°C for 10 sec, 59°C for 15 sec and 70°C for 25 sec, repeated 45 times. The obtained cycle temperature (CT) values were analyzed using Rotor-Gene 6000 series software (Qiagen, [www.qiagen.com\)](www.qiagen.com) by averaging the two independently calculated normalized expression values of the duplicates. The calculated numerical values were divided by the values obtained for the housekeeping tubulin gene in each respective sample.

## Protein extraction and Western blot analyses

Samples were ground in liquid nitrogen, suspended in 500 ll of 49 protein sample buffer (40% glycerol, 8% SDS, 4% b–mercaptoethanol, pH 6.8) and centrifuged at 10 000 g (4°C), and the supernatant was collected for protein analysis. Protein content was determined using a Bradford protein assay kit (Bio– Rad, [http://www3.bio-rad.com\)](http://www3.bio-rad.com). Protein extracts were further separated by 15% acrylamide SDS–PAGE and electroblotted. Blots were blocked and incubated with rabbit polyclonal antibodies (1:1000 dilution) directed against Arabidopsis thaliana cyclophilin AtCYP18–3 (a gift from Charles Gasser at the Department of Molecular and Cellular Biology, University of California at Davis) in milk/PBS/Tween (5% skim milk, 0.1% Tween in PBS). Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat antirabbit antibody (Sigma-Aldrich) was used as the secondary antibody (1:50 000 dilution). Detection was performed by film exposure after ECL reaction using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Thermo Scientific). GFP detection was performed in the same manner, using a monoclonal anti-GFP antibody (Covance Inc., [www.covance.com\)](www.covance.com). Expression of Rubisco was determined by Ponceau staining of the large subunit fragment (approximately 55 kDa). For the light-mediated protein trafficking assay, the abundance of SlCyp1 relative to GFP or Rubisco was determined based on the band intensity as assessed using GelPro software (Media Cybernetics Inc., [http://www.mediacy.](http://www.mediacy.com) [com\)](http://www.mediacy.com).

### Immunolocalization

SlCyp1 immunolocalization was performed as previously described (Paciorek et al., 2006), with the following modifications. Stem samples (25 mm below the grafting point) were collected from the various rootstocks and trimmed. The cut sections were fixed in a paraformaldehyde solution (4% paraformaldehyde, 5% acetic acid in 19 PBS), infiltrated and embedded in wax (90% poly (ethylene glycol), 10% 1–hexadecanol), and then sectioned (15 lm thick) using a cooled microtome (Leica RM2245, [http://www.leica](http://www.leicabiosystems.com/)[biosystems.com/\)](http://www.leicabiosystems.com/). Sections were transferred to microscope slides for incubation with 1:100 rabbit polyclonal antibodies directed against A. thaliana cyclophilin AtCYP18–3 diluted in 19PBS containing 2% BSA. Fluorescein isothiocyanate-conjugated goat antirabbit (Sigma-Aldrich) was used as the secondary antibody (dilution 1:50).

# Light microscopy

Stem or root samples were fixed in an FAA solution (10% formaldehyde, 5% acetic acid, 85% ethanol by volume), subjected to vacuum infiltration for 30 min, and then dehydrated at room temperature in a graded ethanol series (30 min each at 50, 70, 90, 95 and 100%). #### RNA-seq analysis

For RNA-seq analysis, plants were germinated in soil in a growth chamber. Plants were transferred to a hydroponic system either 2 weeks (VFN8) or 3 weeks (dgt) after sowing. Hydroponic growth conditions were as described by Golan et al. (2013). Plants were grafted 3 days after transfer to hydroponics. In order to assess the transcriptional changes occurring early in dgt rootstock recovery phenotype restoration, samples were collected 10 days after grafting, at which point lateral roots had started to emerge in dgt rootstocks grafted onto VFN8 scions. RNA sequencing was performed using an Illumina GA II sequencing system [\(http://www.illumina.com/](http://www.illumina.com/)). A total of 18 libraries were constructed, comprising six biological replicates from each root system: VFN8/VFN8, VFN8/dgt and dgt/dgt (Zhong et al., 2011). The differential gene expression analysis compared three graft pairs: 'VFN8/VFN8 versus dgt/dgt', 'VFN8/dgt versus dgt/dgt' and 'VFN8/dgt versus VFN8/VFN8'. A false discovery rate value results from testing the null hypothesis that the mean gene expression level is equal in the two compared grafts. The graftrescued gene list consists of genes that were differentially expressed in 'dgt/dgt versus VFN8/dgt' and 'dgt/dgt versus VFN8/ VFN8', but not differentially expressed in 'VFN8/dgt versus VFN8/ VFN8' (false discovery rate < 0.05), yielding a set of 2584 'rescued' genes. Gene ontology and gene set enrichment analyses are described in Methods S1.
