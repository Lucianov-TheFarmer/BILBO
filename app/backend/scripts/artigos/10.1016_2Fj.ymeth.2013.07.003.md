# abstract

The advent of high-throughput sequencing has led to an explosion of studies into the diversity, expression, processing, and lifespan of RNAs. Recently, three different high-throughput sequencing-based methods have been developed to specifically study RNAs that are in the process of being degraded. All three methods—genome-wide mapping of uncapped and cleaved transcripts (GMUCT), parallel analysis of RNA ends (PARE), and degradome sequencing—take advantage of the fact that Illumina sequencing libraries use T4 RNA ligase 1 to ligate an adapter to the 5<sup>0</sup> end of RNAs that have a free 5<sup>0</sup> -monophosphate. This condition for T4 RNA ligase 1 substrates means that mature mRNAs are not substrates of the enzyme because they have a 5<sup>0</sup> -cap moiety. As a result, these sequencing libraries are specifically made up of clones of decapped or degrading mRNAs resulting from 5<sup>0</sup> -to-3<sup>0</sup> or nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) and the 3<sup>0</sup> fragment of cleaved microRNA (miRNA) and small interfering RNA (siRNA) target RNAs. Here, we present a massively streamlined protocol for GMUCT that takes 2–3 days, can be initiated with as little as 5 lg of starting total RNA, and involves only one gel size-selection step. We show that the resulting datasets are similar to those produced using the previous GMUCT and PARE protocols. In total, our results suggest that this method will be the preferable approach for future studies of RNA degradation intermediates and small RNA-mediated cleavage in eukaryotic transcriptomes.

### 1. Introduction

Genomic studies of gene expression have primarily focused on analyses of total RNA abundance and regulation of RNA expression at the level of transcription. The advent of high-throughput sequencing has led to an explosion of genomic techniques for studying very specific populations of RNAs, allowing for a greater understanding of how gene expression can be affected globally by the rate of transcription, alternative splicing, RNA silencing, RNA stability, translation, and chromatin modifications.

Recently, three different high-throughput sequencing based methods (genome-wide mapping of uncapped and cleaved transcripts (GMUCT), parallel analysis of RNA ends (PARE), and degradome sequencing) have been developed to specifically study the degradation of mRNAs [\[1–3\].](#page-9-0) The pathways that degrade mRNAs are highly conserved in eukaryotes. Most turnover of normal, functional mRNAs occurs by 5<sup>0</sup> -to-3<sup>0</sup> degradation, 3<sup>0</sup> -to-5<sup>0</sup> decay, or RNA

silencing [\[4–8\].](#page-9-0) Aberrant mRNAs are degraded by one of three pathways—nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD), non-stop decay (NSD), and no-go decay (NGD) [\[9–12\]](#page-9-0). Before discussing the high-throughput sequencing methods, we will briefly consider each of the decay pathways in turn.

50 -to-3<sup>0</sup> and 3<sup>0</sup> -to-5<sup>0</sup> decay of normal mRNAs both involve exoribonucleases, RNases that chew the ends of RNAs processively. Normal mRNAs are stabilized and protected from degradation by a 5<sup>0</sup> -m7GpppN cap and a 3<sup>0</sup> -poly-A tail. Most methods of mRNA degradation begin with the removal of one or the other of these protective elements. For instance, 5<sup>0</sup> -to-3<sup>0</sup> decay begins with deadenylation of the poly-A tail in processing bodies (p-bodies) [\[4,9,13\].](#page-9-0) Then, the 5<sup>0</sup> -cap is also removed in p-bodies by DCP2 with the help of DCP1 and VCS, yielding an mRNA with a 5<sup>0</sup> -monophosphate [\[9,14,15\]](#page-9-0). The 5<sup>0</sup> -to-3<sup>0</sup> RNases, XRN family members, then degrade the deadenylated and decapped mRNA by processively removing one nucleotide at a time from the 5<sup>0</sup> end. Each intermediate product in the degradation of the mRNA by XRN RNases is an RNA with a 5<sup>0</sup> -monophosphate [\[4,9,16\]](#page-9-0). By contrast, 3<sup>0</sup> -to-5<sup>0</sup> decay requires deadenylation but does not require decapping. After deadenylation, most 3<sup>0</sup> -to-5<sup>0</sup> mRNA decay is done by the exosome, a multisubunit protein complex that has 3<sup>0</sup> -to-5<sup>0</sup> ribonuclease activity that

removes one nucleotide at a time from the 3<sup>0</sup> end [\[4,5,9,17\]](#page-9-0). The final step in this process is the hydrolysis of the 5<sup>0</sup> -cap moiety by DcpS [\[4,5\]](#page-9-0).

The turnover of functional mRNAs can be initiated by endoribonucleases as well as exoribonucleases [\[5–9\].](#page-9-0) The most well-studied pathway of endoribonuclease-mediated degradation of normal mRNAs is RNA silencing. The endoribonucleases involved in RNA silencing are Argonaute (AGO) family proteins. In RNA silencing, small RNAs (smRNAs) including microRNAs (miRNAs) and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) can initiate AGO-mediated cleavage of target RNAs with high complementarity through specific base pairing interactions [\[5–8\]](#page-9-0). The 3<sup>0</sup> product of this cleavage reaction has a resulting monophosphate on its 5<sup>0</sup> end, while the 5<sup>0</sup> intermediate lacks a poly-A tail [\[18\]](#page-9-0). Thus, both cleavage products are susceptible to further degradation by either 5<sup>0</sup> -to-3<sup>0</sup> or 3<sup>0</sup> -to-5<sup>0</sup> decay, respectively [\[5–8\].](#page-9-0)

All three mechanisms that act on aberrant mRNAs recognize specific mRNAs as abnormal during the process of translation and then elicit their degradation. NMD-targeted mRNAs include those that have early termination codons resulting in a truncated open reading frame, upstream open reading frames (uORFs), a mutated poly-A site preventing proper polyadenylation, mRNAs subject to alternative polyadenylation due to the presence of multiple poly-A sites, introns in the 3<sup>0</sup> -UTR, and very long 3<sup>0</sup> -UTRs, though not all mRNAs with lengthy 3<sup>0</sup> -UTRs are equally susceptible to NMD [\[9,10,12,19\].](#page-9-0) NMD occurs by first decapping the mRNA, which is done by the mRNA decapping complex as described above for 5<sup>0</sup> -to-3<sup>0</sup> degradation of normal mRNAs. The decapped mRNA is then degraded by members of the XRN family of 5<sup>0</sup> -to-3<sup>0</sup> exoribonucleases, as for 5<sup>0</sup> -to-3<sup>0</sup> decay of normal mRNAs, or becomes a target of RNA-dependent RNA polymerases, which convert them into double-stranded RNA [\[9,10,12,19\]](#page-9-0). The double-stranded RNA is then subject to Dicer-like endoribonucleases that cleave it into 21–24 nucleotide (nt) small-interfering RNAs that can go on to silence cis targets with perfect complementarity [\[9\]](#page-9-0).

Non-stop decay (NSD) and no-go decay (NGD) also recognize aberrant mRNAs but act on very different classes of mRNAs than NMD [\[5,7,11,20,21\].](#page-9-0) For instance, NSD acts on mRNAs for whom translation continues through the poly-A tail due to the absence of a stop codon [\[5,11,21\]](#page-9-0). Translation through the poly-A tail results in the addition of a string of lysines to the polypeptide because the codon AAA encodes a lysine, and the ribosome is not released from the 3<sup>0</sup> end of the mRNA due to the lack of a stop codon. The string of lysines initiates NSD in eukaryotes by stimulating the degradation of the nascent polypeptide by the proteasome, endonucleolytic cleavage of the mRNA near the ribosome, and the degradation of the mRNA by exosomes without the removal of the 50 -cap. NGD recognizes ribosomes that stall in the process of translation elongation, and, like NSD, initiates the degradation of the polypeptide, endonucleolytic cleavage of the mRNA near the stalled ribosome, and 3<sup>0</sup> -to-5<sup>0</sup> decay of the mRNA by the exosome. Like NSD, the 5<sup>0</sup> -cap is not removed during NGD [\[11,20\]](#page-9-0).

In total, the GMUCT, PARE, and degradome approaches are useful for studying 5<sup>0</sup> -to-3<sup>0</sup> decay, small RNA-mediated target cleavage, and NMD, but not 3<sup>0</sup> -to-5<sup>0</sup> decay, NSD, or NGD because a key enzyme (T4 RNA ligase 1) used to construct the library of molecules to be sequenced requires an available 5<sup>0</sup> -monophosphate on every RNA to be cloned. This requirement also means that mature, functional mRNAs are not substrates of these high-throughput methods because they have a 5<sup>0</sup> -cap. As a result, these sequencing libraries are specifically made up of clones of decapped or degrading mRNAs from 5<sup>0</sup> -to-3<sup>0</sup> exoribonucleases, the 3<sup>0</sup> fragment of cleaved miRNA and siRNA targets, and NMD decaying mRNAs.

Here, we present a streamlined method for GMUCT that we call GMUCT 2.0. The new method reduces the time necessary to make a GMUCT library from 5–6 to 2–3 days and decreases the amount of starting total RNA from 50 lg to 5 lg [\[1,22\].](#page-9-0) We use this protocol to make libraries of degrading and cleaved RNAs isolated from plant tissue and human cell lines. We also compare the data generated from this improved, more time-efficient method to data obtained using the original GMUCT [\[1\]](#page-9-0), PARE [\[3\]](#page-9-0), and degradome sequencing [\[2\]](#page-9-0) approaches to show that the data produced is similar. Thus, we have significantly improved a methodology that will be widely useful for future studies of mRNA turnover in all eukaryotic organisms.

#### 2.2. Day 1

#### 2.2.1. Starting RNA sample

The GMUCT 2.0 protocol begins with at least 5 lg of isolated total RNA. We have made GMUCT libraries from 5, 20, and 30 lg of total RNA. All worked well, but 30 lg gave us the largest amount of final product at the end of the procedure (data not shown). We typically purify our RNA using the Qiagen miRNeasy Mini Kit because this kit allows for the purification of the total collection of RNAs larger than 18 nucleotides (nt). For studies focused on the cleavage of miRNA or siRNA targets, it is ideal to make smRNA sequencing (smRNA-seq) libraries from the same RNA samples. This allows one to focus solely on target RNAs of miRNAs or siRNAs that are actually present in the same sample.

To verify that the starting total RNA is of high quality, the concentration, purity, and degradation should be examined using Agilent's BioAnalyzer or a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and then running an agarose gel. If using a BioAnalyzer, the intactness/degradation of the RNA is reported as the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) score. RIN scores range from 1 to 10, with 10 being completely intact RNA. Therefore, the RIN score of your RNA should be as close to 10 as possible. If using a Nandodrop, the 260/280 and 260/230 ratios should be close to 1.8 and 2.0, respectively, to show that there is limited contamination of the samples. Then a small amount of each RNA sample (200–250 ng) should be run on a 1.0% agarose gel to verify that the RNA is not degraded. The ribosomal RNA (rRNA) bands of degraded RNA are less prominent and the intensity of larger rRNA bands is reduced compared to that of smaller ones.

## 2.2.2. Poly-A selection using Dynabeads mRNA DIRECT kit

The directions that follow are for purifying polyadenylated RNA using the Dynabeads mRNA DIRECT kit. Many other companies sell poly-A selection kits, and these are also likely to work. If an alternative kit is chosen, skip steps 1–21 here, and pick back up with step 22, the precipitation step. The Dynabeads method requires the use of a magnetic stand that holds 1.5 ml tubes. If you do not already have a magnetic stand, you will need to purchase one (e.g. the DynaMag™-2 magnet from Invitrogen, catalog number 12321D). For all steps involving separation using the magnetic stand, first do a very brief centrifugation of your 1.5 ml tubes prior to placing them on the stand to make sure that all of the beads are on the bottom of the tube and not on the lid or sides. All buffers used in steps 1–21 of this section come with the Dynabeads kit, but the instructions are not the same as those provided with it. It is also worth noting that all tubes used in this entire GMUCT 2.0 protocol should be sterile and nuclease-free.

- 1. For each sample, add 5–30 lg of starting total RNA to a 1.5 ml tube.
- 2. Add 300 ll Lysis/Binding Buffer to the RNA samples.
- 3. Heat the RNA Samples: 70 C for 3 min (min), ice for 3 min.
- 4. Prepare beads:
  - a. Vortex the bottle of beads until fully resuspended.
  - b. Add 150 ll of resuspended beads to new 1.5 ml tubes. Vortex the stock bottle of beads between every sample because the beads settle quickly.
  - c. Place the tubes with beads on the magnetic stand. In this and all later steps that involve separating the beads from the supernatant, wait until the supernatant is clear and the beads have formed a cluster on the side of the tube near the magnet before going on (usually 10 seconds (s)–2 min). Then remove and discard the supernatant without disturbing the beads except when directed in steps 11 and 21 to transfer the supernatant to a new tube.
  - d. Wash beads with 300 ll lysis binding buffer. Be sure to fully resuspend the beads in the buffer for this and all following washes before putting the tubes on the magnetic stand. Remove and discard the supernatant.
- 5. Add RNA samples to the beads.
- 6. Incubate with continuous mixing on a mechanical rotator for 5 min at room temperature.
- 7. Place samples on the magnetic stand, remove and discard supernatant.
- 8. Wash beads 2 with 600 ll Washing Buffer A. Remove and discard supernatant after each wash step.
- 9. Wash beads 1 with 300 ll Washing Buffer B. Remove and discard supernatant after the wash step.
- 10. Elute by adding 50 ll 10 mM Tris–HCl, gently mixing by tapping the tube until the beads are completely resuspended, and heat the samples at 70 C for 2 min.

- 15. Add the 350 ll sample from step 12 to the beads.
- 16. Rotate at RT for 5 min.

#### 2.2.4. Poly-A selection again to separate 5<sup>0</sup> -ligated RNA from unligated adapter

Purify the 5<sup>0</sup> -ligated RNA away from any unligated RA5 adapter by performing a second poly-A selection. To do this, repeat the steps described in Section [2.2.2](#page-2-0) with the exception that the RNA pellet from the final EtOH precipitation step is resuspended in 7.74 ll of DEPC water. This step is very important. We tried making libraries with and without this second poly-A selection and found that skipping it results in a huge proportion of adapter-adapter products (clones where the 5<sup>0</sup> -adapter is directly fused to the 3<sup>0</sup> -adapter without an insert in between) in the final library. While these products can largely be removed at the size selection in step Section [2.4,](#page-5-0) their presence reduces the reverse transcription and PCR amplification of good clones. This is because the random hexamer primer used in the reverse transcription step (Section 2.3.1) can bind to a free 5<sup>0</sup> -adapter just like it can to inserts ligated to 5<sup>0</sup> -adapters. Because adapter-adapter products are the smallest sequences in a library and PCR (step Section [2.3.2](#page-5-0)) preferentially amplifies smaller products, the PCR will increase the proportion of adapter-adapter products in the library, resulting in a proliferation of what will become useless sequence reads over good reads.

#### 3. Computational analysis

Using this updated protocol, we constructed 2 replicate GMUCT libraries using RNA from mixed stage flower buds of the Arabidopsis thaliana accession Columbia (Col-0) and three human cell lines—the cervical cancer cell line HeLa, human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cell line, and the human chronic myelogenous leukemia cell line K562. After receiving the Illumina sequencing data, the reads were trimmed to remove the adapter sequences, the abundance of each unique read was determined, and unique reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis and human genomes, respectively. The vast majority of the inserts were 50 nt or longer (data not shown). Because (1) the GMUCT protocol clones only pieces of RNA that have a free 5<sup>0</sup> -monosphosphate, which are produced as a result of many of the degradation processes in the cell, and (2) the exact position of the 3<sup>0</sup> end is not important because it is randomly determined based on where the random hexamer primer binds to the RNA, the important information from each cloned insert is the genomic location of the nucleotide at the 5<sup>0</sup> end of each read. As a result, all mapped sequencing reads that start with the same 5<sup>0</sup> nucleotide are summed together to calculate the number of cleavage/degradation events that occurred at that position of the RNA molecule in the tissue or cell line of interest.

Using this information, we plotted the summed distribution of GMUCT 2.0 reads across all protein-coding transcripts for the libraries from the Arabidopsis flower buds and the three human cell lines to obtain an idea of the frequency of decay products in different parts of mRNAs [\(Fig. 3](#page-6-0)A and D). In all libraries of both species, there were significantly more reads at the 3<sup>0</sup> end of mRNAs compared to the 5<sup>0</sup> end. This is similar to the result we obtained by reanalyzing the Arabidopsis flower bud data obtained using the original GMUCT [\[1\]](#page-9-0) and PARE [\[3\]](#page-9-0) protocols [\(Fig. 3B](#page-6-0) and C, respectively). This same distribution of mRNA degradation products has previously been reported for degradome sequencing libraries [\[2\].](#page-9-0) In total, these results indicate that the highly improved GMUCT 2.0 protocol captures the expected collection of turnover events in protein-coding mRNAs of both plants and animals.

We also found that the total abundance of GMUCT 2.0 reads varied greatly across all protein-coding transcripts. However, in general the abundance of GMUCT reads for a transcript was positively correlated with the overall abundance of that mRNA calculated using mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq) data [\(Fig. 4\)](#page-6-0). This result reveals that increased levels of mRNA degradation events will be interrogated for RNAs that are more abundant and vice versa. These results are consistent with what was previously observed using the degradome sequencing approach [\[2\],](#page-9-0) and suggest that the number of normal molecules decaying 5<sup>0</sup> -to-3<sup>0</sup> and targeted by NMD for a given protein-coding transcript are proportional to its abundance.

One of the major uses of GMUCT and other sequencing methods that specifically clone degrading mRNAs is for validating and identifying miRNA and siRNA cleavage sites on target mRNAs. We looked at the average abundance of GMUCT reads with 5<sup>0</sup> ends at miRNA target cleavage sites compared to the 100 bp up- or downstream for both Arabidopsis and humans. In both of the GMUCT 2.0 datasets for Arabidopsis flower buds there was a clear peak of read 5<sup>0</sup> ends precisely at the predicted miRNA cleavage sites ([Fig. 5](#page-7-0)A). Similar results were obtained when reanalyzing the original Arabidopsis GMUCT [\[1\]](#page-9-0) and PARE datasets [\[3\]](#page-9-0) ([Fig. 5](#page-7-0)B and C, respectively). Conversely, none of the libraries made from the human cell lines showed a peak in abundance at predicted miRNA cleavage sites [\(Fig. 5D](#page-7-0)). This result was as expected because while miR-NA-directed target cleavage and translational inhibition are known to occur in both plants and animals, cleavage is more common in plants and translational inhibition in animals. This difference in silencing mechanism has been attributed to the disparity in complementarity between target RNAs and miRNAs in plants versus animals [\[6\]](#page-9-0). In total, these results also validate the usefulness of data obtained using the GMUCT 2.0 protocol for future studies of smRNA-directed cleavage events.

#### 4. Conclusions

The development of high-throughput sequencing and the increased interest in understanding the different levels of gene expression regulation has resulted in many new genomic approaches for analyzing very specific populations of mRNAs. GMUCT, PARE, and degradome sequencing were all developed to study mRNA degradation resulting in free 5<sup>0</sup> -monophosphates on decaying mRNAs, including 5<sup>0</sup> -to-3<sup>0</sup> exoribonucleolytic decay, RNA silencing directed target RNA cleavage, and non-sense mediated decay of aberrant mRNAs.

Here, we present an improved GMUCT protocol that produces results similar to the longer original method and to the two other high-throughput sequencing-based methods (PARE and degradome sequencing) for studying mRNA degradation but requires less time and less starting total RNA. Additionally, the distribution of sequencing reads across transcripts, the correlation between GMUCT reads and the steady state abundance of mRNAs measured by mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq) libraries, and miRNA target cleavage in plants are similar for the different methods. Thus, our improved methodology is more time efficient, and still captures the desired information.

As expected and in contrast to the analysis of plant GMUCT data, analyzing the average abundance of GMUCT products at human miRNA target sites did not provide obvious evidence of widespread cleavage. These results are consistent with most previous data concerning animal miRNA silencing mechanisms. It would be of interest, however, to further parse the GMUCT data from the human cell lines to test the hypothesis that it is the level of complementarity between a miRNA and its target that determines whether this smRNA will induce the cleavage of its target. Overall, <span id="page-9-0"></span>the shorter time investment, increased simplicity, and lower required input of starting total RNA needed for the improved GMUCT method will likely lead to the increased use of this approach for future studies of mRNA degradation in all eukaryotic organisms.
