## **Abstract**

High temperature (HT) is becoming an increasingly serious factor in limiting crop production with global climate change. During hot seasons, owing to prevailing HT, cultivated tomatoes are prone to exhibiting stigma exsertion, which hampers pollination and causes fruit set failure. However, the underlying regulatory mechanisms of the HT-induced stigma exsertion remain largely unknown. Here, we demonstrate that stigma exsertion induced by HT in cultivated tomato is caused by more seriously shortened stamens than pistils, which is different from the stigma exsertion observed in wild tomato species. Under the HT condition, the different responses of pectin, sugar, expansin and cyclin cause cell wall remodeling and differentially localized cell division and selective cell enlargement, which further determine the lengths of stamens and pistils. In addition, auxin and jasmonate (JA) are implicated in regulating cell division and cell expansion in stamens and pistils, and exogenous JA instead of auxin treatment can effectively rescue tomato stigma exsertion through regulating the JA/COI1 signaling pathway. Our findings provide a better understanding of stigma exsertions under the HT condition in tomato, and uncover a new function of JA in improving plant abiotic stress tolerance.

#### **KEYWORDS**

Tomato, high temperature, stamen, pistil, stigma exsertion, auxin, jasmonate

Stigma exsertion is commonly observed in cultivated tomatoes under elevated temperature conditions, which severely hampers pollination and fruit set.

We demonstrate that high temperature (HT)-induced stigma exsertion in cultivated tomato is caused by more shortened stamens than pistils.

The difference of stamen and pistil length changes under HT is attributed to the different responses of pectin, sugar, expansin and cyclin, which causes cell wall remodeling and differentially localized cell division and selective cell enlargement under the HT condition.

The JA/COI1 signaling pathway participates in regulating HT-induced stigma exsertion.

# **1 INTRODUCTION**

As sessile organisms, plants have evolved new mating behaviors to respond to a wide range of environmental challenges including drought, salinity, cold, and heat stress (Chaiwanon *et al*. 2016). The most common change of new mating behaviors is the conservation from outcrossed-pollination to self-pollination, which contributes to plant reproductive success (Hermann & Kuhlemeier 2011). The pollination success depends on the adaptations in floral morphology (Smyth 2005). For cross-pollinating (allogamous) species, the stigma is exserted beyond its own anther and receives pollen from adjacent plants, while in self-pollinating (autogamous) species, the stigma is recessed within its own anther (Chen *et al*. 2007). The evolution of autogamy is tightly coupled with the position of the

stigmatic surface of the pistil relative to anthers, which can be well illustrated by the evolution of cultivated tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*) (Moyle 2008). Like *Arabidopsis thaliana,* tomato flowers consist of four whorls of organs: sepals, petals, stamens, and pistils. However, tomato plants produce the anther cone involved in the specific form of pollination (Glover, Bunnewell & Martin 2004). Most wild tomato species are allogamous with exserted stigmas in flowers. However, the cultivated tomato is autogamous and bears flowers with flush or inserted stigmas (Spooner, Peralta & Knapp 2005; Chen *et al*. 2007).

Precisely coordinated growth of stamens and pistils determines the fertility of plants. Unfortunately, multiple environmental stresses particularly high temperature (HT) can markedly transform the position of stigma relative to anthers, and thus resulting in the so-called stigma exsertion that is common in cultivated tomato and rice (Lohar *et al*. 1998; Saeed *et al*. 2007; Yan *et al*. 2009; Rang *et al*. 2011; Ramesh *et al*. 2017). With global warming, plant HT injury has become a serious problem nowadays (Lobell & Field 2007; Mohammed & Tarpley 2009). Although the cultivated tomato is widely adapted to diverse climatic conditions, HT can readily cause severe morphological defects during flowering, and a common feature of which is stigma exsertion (Peet, Sato & Gardner 1998). Grown at even moderately elevated temperature, tomato plants manifest high rate of stigma exsertion during anthesis, which substantially hinders self-pollination and further reduces the fruit productivity and quality (Fernandez-Munoz & Cuartero 1991; Sato *et al*. 2006; Giorno *et al*. 2013). Comparison of different genotypes of cultivated tomato plants exposed to HT found that the rate of stigma exsertion ranged between 25.0 to 55%, indicating that this trait was partially genetically determined (Saeed *et al*.

2007). Recently, we identified five miRNA-target cleavage cascades implicated in responding to heat stress in stamens and pistils (Pan *et al*. 2017). However, the molecular and physiological mechanisms of stigma exsertion caused by HT remain poorly understood.

Plant hormones act as chemical messengers in the regulation of a range of physiological and developmental processes, as well as in response to environmental stimulus. Increased evidences have shown that auxin and jasmonate (JA) play essential roles in regulating the filament development in *Arabidopsis* (Cecchetti *et al*. 2008; Cheng *et al*. 2009; Song *et al*. 2013). In the auxin biosynthesis-defective *yuc2 yuc6* double mutant, stamen development is halted (Cheng, Dai & Zhao 2006). Additionally, auxin-efflux carriers PIN family genes function in regulating intracellular auxin homeostasis and metabolism and building floral morphology (Wisniewska *et al*. 2006; Mravec *et al*. 2009; Pattison & Catala 2012). Jasmonate is synthesized via a series of JA-biosynthetic enzymes, such as fatty acid desaturase (FAD), allene oxide cyclase (AOC), lipoxygenase (LOX), and eventually catalyzed by jasmonate resistant 1 (JAR1) into bioactive form jasmonoyl-isoleucine (JA-Ile) (Song *et al*. 2013; Yuan & Zhang 2015). Mutations in genes encoding the JA-biosynthetic enzymes or direct downstream transcription factors MYB21/24 could result in defects of the filament development (Wasternack *et al*. 2013). Few studies have evaluated the role of phytohormones on filament and pistil development under environmental stresses. In *Arabidopsis* and barley*,* endogenous auxin levels are decreased specifically in the developing anthers under the HT condition, leading to shorter filament (Sakata *et al*. 2010). However, the IAA content is significantly increased in late-stage anthers in cotton (*Gossypium hirsutum*) under the HT condition (Min *et al*. 2014). These results suggest that endogenous hormones exhibit different responses to HT stress in different plant species. Hence, it would be interesting to see whether and how the phytohormones participate in regulating tomato stamen and pistil development under HT condition.

Here, we synchronously explore the development of stamens and pistils under the HT condition in cultivated tomato. The objectives of this study are to (i) investigate the inherent distinctions on gene expression, cell morphology, and physiological metabolism between stamens and pistils in response to HT stress; (ii) explore the connection between phytohormone signal pathways and HT-induced stigma exsertion. Our findings could contribute to the revealing of the incongruous growth of stamens and pistils under environmental stresses and the understanding of the morphogenesis of stigma exsertion caused by HT stress in tomato.

# **2 MATERIALS AND METHODS**

# **2.1 Plant materials and growth conditions**

Tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*) cv. 'Micro-Tom' and six other varieties were employed in this study, including 'Earlinorth', 'Hotset', 'Ailsa Craig', 'NC HS-1' from the Tomato Genetics Resource Center, University of California, Davis (CA, USA), a heat-tolerant cultivar 'CLN2413R' from Asian Vegetable Research and Development Center, and 'T431' from Northeast Agricultural University (Harbin, China).

The tomato plants were grown in controlled chambers with 16-hour (h) light (300 μmol photons·m −2 ·s −1 ) at 25 ± 1°C and 8 h dark at 20 ± 1°C, both with a relative humidity of 60-70%. The treatments were performed using 5- to 6-week old plants with the first flower fully opened. The plants were exposed to 33 ± 1°C/28 ± 1°C and 35 ± 1°C/30 ± 1°C (16 h day/8 h night) for 12 d as modestly elevated temperature (MET) and HT treatments, respectively. The control plants (CK) were kept under normal conditions.

As previously described, flower buds with lengths of 6.0-6.5 mm represent the binucleate pollen stage in tomato cultivar 'Micro-Tom' (Pan *et al*. 2017). In order to exclude the impact of different developmental stages, stamens and pistils stripped from flower buds of 6.0-6.5 mm in length were simultaneously sampled at 2 d and 12 d under HT and control conditions, respectively. Two biological replicates were collected for each sample. For each biological replicate, 15 independent 'Micro-Tom' plants were used. Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -70 °C till use.

#### **2.2 RNA-Seq library preparation and sequencing**

Total RNA was isolated using the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). The integrity and quality of the total RNA were determined by a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and electrophoresis. Strand-specific RNA-Seq libraries were prepared from total RNA using the protocol described in Zhong *et al*. (2011) and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 system with the single-end mode according to the manufacturer's instructions (Illumina). Raw sequence reads have been deposited into the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession SRP150550.

#### **2.3 RNA-Seq data analysis**

Raw RNA-Seq reads were first processed to trim adapter sequences and low-quality sequences using Trimmomatic (Bolger, Lohse & Usadel 2014), and trimmed reads shorter than 40 bp were discarded. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) reads were identified by aligning the reads to an rRNA database (Quast *et al*. 2013) and excluded. The final cleaned reads were mapped to the tomato genome using HISAT allowing up to two mismatches (Kim, Langmead & Salzberg 2015). Based on the alignments, raw counts were derived for tomato genes and subsequently normalized to fragments per kilobase exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM). To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), raw counts were analyzed using the edgeR package (Robinson *et al*. 2010). A very stringent cutoff, adjusted *p*-value < 0.05 and the absolute value of log<sup>2</sup> ratio ≥ 1, was used for identifying DEGs. The MapMan tool (Thimm *et al*. 2004) was used to analyze functional enrichment of typical GO categories for differentially expressed genes and subsequently visualized based on the functional annotated file Slyc\_ITAG2.3 (https://MapMan.gabipd.org/MapManstore).

# **2.4 qRT-PCR analysis**

Total RNA was isolated from the stamens and pistils of flower buds at the binucleate pollen stage with or without HT treatment at 2 d, 6 d and 12 d using the Plant RNA Kit (OMEGA) with three biological replicates. First-strand cDNA was generated using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit according to the manufacturer's protocol (TaKaRa, Japan). qRT-PCR reactions were performed in a Bio-Rad CFX96 (Bio-Rad, USA) using SYBR® Green Realtime PCR Master Mix (TaKaRa, Japan) following the method described in our previous study (Pan *et al*. 2017). Gene-specific primers were designed

according to the reference gene sequences (Additional file Table S1). The relative gene expression levels were normalized using the reference gene *SlUbi3* (GenBank accession number X58253) (Wu *et al*. 2014) and calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method (Livak & Schmittgen 2001). The data were obtained from three biologically independent experiments and analyzed with SPSS (version 20, IBM Corp, USA).

#### **2.5 Pectin content determination**

The cell wall compositions of stamens and pistils were extracted according to the previous report (Zhong & Lauchli 1993) with minor modifications. Briefly, stamens and pistils were ground into powder using liquid nitrogen and homogenized in 5 mL 75% ethanol, centrifuged at 17000 g for 10 min after incubating on ice for 20 min, then repeated with acetone, methanol:chloroform (1:1, v/v), and methanol, respectively. Afterward, freeze-dried cell wall materials were stored at 4 ºC for further use.

The pectin fraction was extracted three times by boiling 5 mg above freeze-dried cell wall materials with 1 mL water at 100 ºC for 1 h. After centrifugation at 17 000 g for 10 min, the supernatants containing pectin were collected in a 5 mL tube and stored at 4 ºC until use (Yang *et al*. 2008). Uronic acid content was determined as described (Blumenkrantz & Asboehan 1973) with minor modifications. 200 μL pectin samples were incubated with concentrated sulfuric acid containing 0.0125 M sodium tetraborate (Sigma-Aldrich) at 100 ºC for 5 min. After cooling to room temperature, 20 μL of 0.15% (w/v) M-hydro-diphenyl (Sigma-Aldrich) were added and pipetted thoroughly before incubating at room temperature for 20 min, and then the absorbance was measured

at 520 nm. The galacturonic acid (GalA, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as a calibration standard and the pectin content was expressed as GalA equivalents. Three independent biological replicates were performed for each sample.

To determine sugar contents in pectin, 200 μL pectin samples were incubated with 1 mL concentrated sulfuric acid containing 10 μL 80% (v/v) phenol (Sigma-Aldrich) at 100 ºC for 15 min. After cooling to room temperature, the absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically at 520 nm using glucose (Sigma-Aldrich) as a standard (Dubois *et al*. 1956). Three independent biological replicates were performed for each sample.

#### **2.6 Assays of total soluble sucrose, glucose and fructose content in stamens and pistils**

Sucrose, glucose and fructose contents in stamens and pistils were determined using the protocol described in the previous report (Wilson *et al*. 1981) with minor modifications. Briefly, 200 mg fresh stamens and pistils were ground into a homogenate in 5 mL of 80% (v/v) ethanol, followed by incubation for 30 min at 80 ºC and centrifugation at 12,000 g for 20 min, then repeated with 80% (v/v) ethanol. The supernatant was collected and evaporated in a water bath, then resuspended with 3 mL ddH2O. After filtered through a 0.45-μm microfiltration membrane, the sucrose, glucose and fructose contents in supernatants were analyzed on Waters Alliance equipment coupled with a NH2 column (PSS831115, S5NH2 COL 25 cm × 4.6 mm) using the HPLC method. Four independent biological replicates were performed for each sample.

#### **2.7 Quantification of endogenous IAA, JA and JA-Ile levels**

Phytohormone extraction and analysis from tomato stamens and pistils were performed using protocols described in previous reports (Pan, Welti & Wang 2008; Fu *et al*. 2012) with minor modifications. Briefly, fresh stamens and pistils were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen with mortar and pestle. For each sample, 100 mg powder was homogenized in 1 mL of ethyl acetate with internal standards d2-IAA (Sigma-Aldrich), d5-JA (QCC), and d6-JA-Ile (CDN Isotopes) in 1.5 mL snap-cap vials, then agitated for 10 min and centrifuged at 13,000 g for 20 min at 4 ºC. The supernatant was collected and dried with N<sup>2</sup> gas, then resuspended in 0.2 mL of 60% (v/v) methanol and centrifuged at 13,000 g for 10 min at 4 ºC. After filtered through a 0.22-μm nylon membrane, the supernatant was analyzed using a HPLC-mass spectrometry on an Agilent 1290 infinity HPLC system coupled with an Agilent 6460 Triple Quad liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry device (Agilent Technologies, Germany). The Agilent Zorbax XDB C18 column (150 mm×2.1 mm, 3.5 μm) was used to perform the HPLC analysis as described in Chen *et al*. (2017). Four independent biological replicates were performed for each sample.

# **2.8 Exogenous hormone treatments**

For JA and MeJA treatments, the flower buds were sprayed with 10-6 M, 10-5 M, 10-4 M JA and MeJA with or without 10-4 M MG132 solutions with 0.01% (v/v) aqueous Triton X-100 (Seo *et al*. 2011). The control plants were treated only with aqueous solution of 0.01% (v/v) Triton X-100. For IAA and NPA treatments, the flower buds were sprayed with IAA at different concentrations including 10-6 M, 10-5 M, and 10-4 M, or NPA solutions (10-5 M) with 0.01% (v/v) aqueous Silwet L-77 (Krizek 2009).

The aqueous solution containing 0.01% Silwet L-77 was sprayed as the control.

The solutions were sprayed on flower buds in the morning of 0, 2, 4, and 6 days after exposing to the HT condition. The lengths of stamens and pistils at the binucleate pollen stage treated with hormone solutions were measured at 2-day intervals until 12 days after the temperature upshift. The stamens and pistils at the binucleate pollen stage treated with 10-5 M JA solution for longitudinal section were sampled at 12 d after exposure to the HT condition. Each series of experiments was carried out with 15 independent tomato seedlings with 20 mL of solutions in triplicate.

#### **2.9 Histochemical localization of GUS activity**

The *proDR5:GUS* construct and transgenic tomato lines at the 'Micro-Tom' background were generated by leaf disc method (McCormick *et al*. 1986). Histochemical GUS staining was performed as described in the previous report (Jefferson, Kavanagh & Bevan 1987). Flower buds harvested from the transgenic lines were stripped of sepals and petals, then incubated in the GUS staining buffer containing 0.2 mM X-Gluc (5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolylb-glucuronic acid), 0.15 M NaH2PO<sup>4</sup> (pH 7), 2 mM K3Fe(CN)6, 2 mM K4Fe(CN)6, and 0.05% (v/v) Triton X-100 in the dark at 37 ºC for 16 h. Tissues were washed several times to extract chlorophyll using a gradient ethanol series. GUS expression pattern was photographed under a Nikon Eclipse 90i microscope.

#### **2.10 Light microscopy**

Semi-thin section analysis was performed to determine the cell size of longitudinal section for stamens and styles at the binucleate pollen stage. Briefly, the specimens were fixed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde (v/v) and washed thrice with 0.1 M phosphate buffer, then soaked in 1% osmic acid (v/v) for 1 to 2 h. Specimens were washed again using 0.1 M phosphate buffer as above, and dehydrated through a gradient ethanol series. Ultimately, the specimens were embedded and polymerized in Spurr's resin (Huang *et al*. 2009). Semi-thin sections (2 μm) were stained with 1% methylene blue and then photographed under a Nikon Eclipse 90i microscope. Images are representative of at least five observed samples.

## **2.11 Identification of** *jai1-1* **homozygotes**

The homozygous *jasmonic acid-insensitive1-1* (*jai1-1*) mutants in the tomato 'Micro-Tom' background were screened from self-crossed progenies of *Jai1/jai1-1* heterozygotes by triple primer method according to the previous reports (Li *et al*. 2004; Dobritzsch *et al*. 2015). The upstream primer (5'-GTGGAGACGATATGTTGAGACTAA-3') combined with the middle primer (5'-CCATGGAGTCCATCACCTAACAGT-3') amplified a 525-bp PCR product corresponding to the wild-type allele, whereas the upstream primer combined with the downstream primer (5'-GTGGTCAGATCAGAGCCCTCTATT-3') amplified a 777-bp fragment that is unique for *jai1-1.*  For *jai1-1* homozygotes, the PCR products contained only the 777-bp fragment.

#### **2.12 Statistical analysis.**

The data in graphs represent the results of multiple independently repeated experiments (*n* ≥ 3). The statistical significance of experimental data was assessed by the SPSS version 20 (IBM Corp., USA) using the *t*-test or Tukey test.

**3 RESULTS**

#### **3.1 HT stress causes stigma exsertion in different genotypes of cultivated tomato**

To investigate the relationship between stigma exsertion and HT stress, cultivated tomato 'Micro-Tom' was employed in this study. Under the normal condition, at anthesis, all the stigmas were recessed within its own anthers (Figure 1a). However, under the elevated temperature conditions (33°C/28 ± 1°C for MET and 35 ± 1°C/30 ± 1°C for HT) for 12 days, the stigmas were visibly protruded from the anther cone at the both binucleate and mature pollen stages, and this phenomenon is known as 'stigma exsertion' in horticulture (Figure 1a). The proportion of protruding stigmas (65.2%) were significantly higher under HT than that under MET (23.5%) (Figure 1b), indicating that the stigma exsertion rate had a strong positive correlation with elevated temperatures.

To further prove that the stigma exsertion phenotype under high temperature is common in cultivated tomatoes, six other genotypes of cultivated tomatoes were examined under MET treatments. Mostly of them exhibited high percentages of stigma exsertion ranging from 29.0% to 62.0% (Figure S1), except the heat tolerant genotype CLN2413R. These data were consistent with the previous studies in tomato (Saeed *et al.* 2007; Kartikeya *et al.* 2012).

To characterize the morphology for stamens and pistils under HT, the length of stamens and pistils at the binucleate pollen stage was measured at sequential time points after tomato plants subjected to the HT treatment (Figure 1c). Under the normal condition, the stamen lengths at the binucleate pollen stage were closely coincident with pistil lengths (Figure 1c). However, the pistil lengths were significant longer than stamen lengths after exposure to HT for 12 d, although dramatically decreases in the lengths were observed in both stamens and pistils (Figure 1c). Apparently, stigma exsertion caused by HT is resulted from the more seriously shortened stamens than the pistils, which is different from the stigma exsertion observed in wild tomato species that is caused by excessive elongation of pistils (Chen *et al.* 2007). These findings indicate that tomato stamens are more susceptible to the HT stress than pistils, which contributes to HT-induced stigma exsertion. It is worth noting that both stamens and pistils exhibited a slight fast elongation at 2 d after the HT treatment compared with the normal condition (Figure 1c).

## **3.2 Comparative transcriptome profiles between HT-treated stamens and pistils**

To systemically elucidate the different responses to high temperature in stamens and pistils, RNA-Seq was employed to analyze the transcriptome patterns in stamens and pistils at the binucleate pollen stage under normal and HT conditions. In total, 181,631,300 cleaned reads from 16 samples were generated, and approximately 90% of the reads were mapped to the tomato reference genome. The summary of the read information is shown in Supplemental Additional file Table S2.

The HT and CK treatments were compared at 2 d and 12 d, respectively, for stamens and pistils. A total of 4,779 genes were differentially expressed, among which 4,423 were in stamens, 824 were in pistils, and 467 were common in both stamens and pistils (Figure 2a, Tables S3 and S4). The significantly greater number of HT-responsive genes in stamens than that in pistils further supported the conjecture that stamens are more vulnerable than pistils to HT stress.

The number of HT-responsive genes increased from 2,069 at 2 d after the HT treatment to 3,517 at 12 d in stamens (Figure 2a), of which, 1,123 were commonly identified in both time points. A total of 887 and 967 genes were up-regulated, whereas 1,182 and 2,550 were down-regulated at 2 d and 12 d, respectively (Figure 2b). However, in pistils, the numbers of HT-responsive genes were 435 at 2 d and 445 at 12 d (Figure 2b). Among these genes, 84 and 193 were up-regulated, and 351 and 252 were down-regulated at 2 d and 12 d, respectively (Figure 2b). Together, these data showed that both shortand long-term HT treatments trigger dramatically stress responses in tomato reproductive organs, particularly in stamens.

#### **3.3 Functional characterization of HT-inducible genes in stamens and pistils**

The expression profiles of HT-responsive genes were subjected to cluster analysis using MapMan (Thimm *et al.* 2004). The 4,423 DEGs in stamens and 824 in pistils were assigned to eight and seven clusters, respectively (Figure S2, Additional file Table S5). Clusters 1-3 in stamens and pistils contained the most down-regulated genes, and Clusters 4-6 in stamens and Clusters 4-5 in pistils grouped the most up-regulated genes (Figure S2).

Subsequently, these clusters were analyzed for over-representation of gene ontology (GO) categories (Gene Ontology Consortium 2015). Interestingly, most of the clusters showed enrichment for particular GO categories. For example, clusters 1 containing genes that exhibited substantially decreased expression under HT in both stamens and pistils, were greatly enriched with the pectin catabolic process, cell wall organization or modification, and glucose import. Additionally, regulation of pH and cellulose biosynthetic process were significantly enriched in cluster 1 of stamens. Clusters 2 and 3 in stamens and pistils grouped moderate down-regulated genes, and in these clusters multiple metabolic and transport processes including cell morphogenesis, and glycolytic process were over-represented in stamens, while only polysaccharide catabolic and macromolecule metabolic processes in pistils. Genes in clusters 4-6 in stamens and clusters 4-5 in pistils are those required for carbohydrate transmembrane transport, water transport, and polysaccharide metabolism. Together, these results reveal that cell-wall structural components were severely disturbed by the HT stress, especially in stamens.

DEGs were then annotated to the reference pathways and grouped in ''BINs'' using the MapMan software. Remarkably, genes participating in cell wall degradation, pectin esterases and ethylene signaling were significantly responsive to HT in both stamens and pistils (Figure S3). However, many BINs including cell wall, signaling and transport were particularly enrichment in stamens. By contrast, only one BIN "misc.oxidases" was specifically enriched in pistils (Figure S3). Interestingly, genes involved in cell wall modification were mainly grouped in cluster 1, which appeared to be down-regulated under the HT condition (Figures S2 and S3).

#### **3.4 HT differentially affects carbohydrate metabolism in cell wall of stamens and pistils**

Given the results that pectin metabolism relevant MapMan Bins were strongly enriched (Figures S2 and S3), the detailed changes of the expression profiles of pectin metabolic genes were further characterized based on RNA-Seq results, as well as using qRT-PCR. In stamens, a total of 61 genes encoding enzymes involved in the disintegration of pectins, namely pectinmethylesterases (PME), pectin acetyl esterases, pectate lyases (PL), and polygalacturonases (PG), showed significant changes in transcript abundance at 2 d or 12 d of HT treatments (Figure 3a, Additional file Table S6). Among these genes, 39 including pectate lyase *LAT59*, *SlPGA4*, PME genes *SlVGD1* and *SlPPME1*, and PME inhibitor *SlVGDH1* were significantly down-regulated at 12 d, whereas only 15 genes encoding PME, PL, and PG were up-regulated (Figure 3a, Figure S4a). However, in pistils, only 26 genes including pectate lyase *AT59-Like, SlVGD1,* and *SlVGDH1*, participating in modification and degradation of pectin were HT-responsive (Figure 3a, Figure S4a). These results suggest pectin metabolism was induced by HT in both stamens and pistils, but more intricate in stamens.

Pectin contents (as reported by uronic acid) (Xiong *et al.* 2015) in stamens and pistils were assessed under CK and HT treatments. In stamens, the pectin content was increased under the HT treatment; particularly, it was significantly higher at 2 d (*p* < 0.01, *t*-test) than that under the normal temperature (Figure 3b). In contrast, the pectin content in pistil was significantly decreased (*p* < 0.01, *t*-test) at 12 d after the HT treatment (Figure 3b), although it exhibited a slight but non-significant increase at 2 d after the HT treatment. The total sugar contents in pectins under CK and HT conditions were also examined. In stamens, the total sugar content in pectins was gradually reduced under HT, especially at 6 d (*p* < 0.05, *t*-test) and 12 d (*p* < 0.01, *t*-test) (Figure S4b). As expected, in pistils, the total sugar content was only moderately reduced (*p* < 0.05, *t*-test) at 12 d under HT, and exhibited no difference compare to CK at 2 d and 6 d (Figure S4b).

In addition, genes involved in carbohydrate (CHO) metabolism were significantly enriched in stamens and pistils under HT at 12 d (Figure S3). In stamens, 17 DEGs encoding invertases (INV), sucrose synthase, and hexokinase associated with sucrose metabolism pathways (German *et al.* 2002; Ruan 2014) were significantly regulated by the HT (Figure 4a-c). qRT-PCR results revealed that cell wall invertase genes *SlCWINV2* and *SlCWINV4* were significantly reduced by the HT stress in stamens, while sucrose synthase gene *SlSUS4* and vacuolar invertase gene *SlVIN2* showed a significantly increase under the HT condition (Figure 4c). However, in pistils, only two genes, *SlCWINV2* and *SlCWINV4*, were significantly downregulated under the HT treatment,consistent with their responses to HT in stamens (Figure 4b, c).

Invertases and sucrose synthases have been demonstrated to be crucial in catalyzing sucrose cleavage into fructose and glucose (Koch 2004). The contents of total sucrose, glucose, and fructose in stamens and pistils at the binucleate pollen stage were assessed under CK and HT conditions, respectively. In stamens, the sucrose content showed significant decrease under the HT treatment at 2 d (*p* < 0.05, *t*-test) than that under the normal temperature, whereas significant increase was observed at 6 d and 12 d after the HT treatment (*p* < 0.01, *t*-test) (Figure 4d). However, the sucrose level in pistils treated with HT only increased markedly at 12 d (*p* < 0.01, *t*-test) (Figure 4d). Furthermore, the glucose and fructose contents were significantly increased in stamens but were prominently reduced in pistils at 2 d and 6 d by the HT treatment. However, at 12 d, the glucose contents showed significant reduction in both stamens and pistils, whereas no obvious changes were found in the fructose content in either stamens or pistils (Figure 4d).

Taken together, all the above results indicate that cell wall pectin and sugar metabolisms discrepantly respond to HT between stamens and pistils, contributing to transforming the wall properties which tightly determine the cell shape (Cosgrove 2005).

#### **3.5 Characterization of cell morphology of stamens and pistils under HT**

Semi-thin section was utilized to characterize the changes of cell sizes of stamens and styles at the binucleate pollen stage (Figure 5a). A time-course analysis showed that the cell sizes (mean area) of stamens were significantly increased at 2 d (*p* < 0.05, *t*-test) and at 12 d (*p* < 0.01, *t*-test) after the HT treatment compared with CK (Figure 5a). In styles, an obvious cell elongation was only observed from 0 d to 2 d (*p* < 0.05, *t*-test), whereas no significant change was detected at 12 d by HT (Figure 5a). However, the total cell number was significantly reduced in both stamens (*p* < 0.01) and styles (*p* < 0.05) at 12 d after the HT treatment, whereas no significant change was observed at 2 d (Figure 5a). These findings indicate that shortened stamens and pistils were consequence of mainly the significantly reduced cell numbers, although the cells were enlarged in both stamens and styles to a certain degree.

The essential functions of cell cycle genes and expansins in control of spatial cell division and expansion have been extensively characterized in other plants (Inze & Veylder 2006; Cosgrove 2015). Here, a subset of genes involved in cell division and cell expansion were identified and characterized by RNA-Seq and qRT-PCR. In stamens, a total of 10 cell cycle-related genes were substantially regulated by HT (Figure 5b). Among them, seven cyclins including *SlCycA1;1*, *SlCycB2;5* and *SlSDS* were significantly downregulated at 12 d after exposure to HT (Figure 5b, Additional file Table S7), which was further proved by qRT-PCR (Figure 5c). Furthermore, seven expansins were identified to be responsive to HT, of which five α-expansins, *SlEXPA4*, *SlEXPA7*, *SlEXPA11*, *SlEXPA13*, and *SlEXPA15*, were significantly upregulated at 2 d and 12 d after exposure to HT (Figure 5c, Additional file Table S7). By contrast, only *SlCycU4;1* significantly downregulated by HT was identified in the HT-treated pistils at 2 d and 12 d (Figure 5c, Additional file Table S7). In addition, two α-expansins *SlEXPA13* and *SlEXPA15* were significantly upregulated, and one β-expansin *SlEXPB2* was significantly downregulated in pistils (Figure 5c, Additional file Table S7). Together, these data reveal that cell cycle genes coupled with expansins co-regulated the cell morphology of stamens and pistils under the HT condition, whereas the changes of stamen and pistil lengths are mainly regulated by cell cycle genes which determine the cell number.

## **3.6 HT alters hormone metabolism and signaling differentially in stamens and pistils**

Our RNA-Seq data showed that the expression of large amount of genes related to multiple phytohormone metabolism and signaling pathways was altered in stamens and pistils under HT (Figure 6a, Additional file Table S8). In stamens, genes involved in the auxin metabolism and signaling constituted the largest group, whereas jasmonate, ethylene, and abscisic acid signaling pathways were also markedly affected by HT (Figure 6a), which is consistent with the results in cotton (Min *et al.* 2014). However, in pistils, mainly the ethylene signaling-related genes were altered by HT, followed by genes involved in the auxin and JA metabolisms (Figure 6a, Additional file Table S8). These results indicated that a sophisticated phytohormone cross-talk might be involved in HT responses in stamens and pistils.

Intriguingly, the ethylene signaling was only enriched at 2 d after the HT treatment rather than 12 d in both stamens and pistils (Figure S3), implicating ethylene might function as a short-term regulator of HT response, which is beyond the scope of this study. Auxin metabolism and signaling was significantly different between stamens and pistils under the HT. In stamens, *ToFZY2* (homolog of *YUC2*) encoding key enzyme in the auxin biosynthesis was always significantly downregulated by the HT treatment, whereas *SlAMI1*, a homologous gene of *AtAMI1*, which convert IAM (indole-3-acetamide) to IAA (Mano *et al.* 2010), was significantly upregulated at 6 d and 12 d (Figure 6b). In addition, two auxin efflux genes *SlPIN-LIKES 1* (homolog of *PIN-LIKES 1*) and *SlPIN5* were significantly reduced by HT, whereas *SlPIN-LIKES 2* (homolog of *PIN-LIKES 2*) exhibited an increased expression level under the HT condition (Figure 6b). However, in pistils, genes involved in the IAA signaling pathway exhibited a quite different response to HT. The expression level of *ToFZY2* was substantially increased under HT at 6 d and 12 d, and the *SlAMI1* expression peaked at 2 d (Figure 6b). Among the above three auxin efflux genes, *SlPIN-LIKES* 1 and *SlPIN-LIKES 2* displayed expression patterns similar to those in stamens, but *SlPIN5* was enhanced in response to HT, which is different from its decreased expression in stamens (Figure 6b).

Considering the prominently expression changes of genes involved in the auxin metabolism when treated with HT, the content of endogenous IAA was measured by HPLC-mass spectrometry. In stamens, the endogenous IAA content increased weakly at 2 d under HT, but evidently decreased by approximately 40% at 12 d after the HT treatment compared with the normal condition (Figure 6c). By contrast, in pistils, HT led to a considerable increase in the IAA levels, significantly at 2 d (*p* < 0.05) (Figure 6c). A recombinant 'Micro-Tom' line that expressed a synthetic auxin response element

(*DR5*-*GUS*) was generated to further characterize the temporal and spatial effect of HT on the IAA content (Figure 6d). The *DR5*-*GUS* signals were uniformly distributed at the surface of tomato stamen, but disappeared locally at 12 d after the HT treatment, which is consistent with the content analysis (Figure 6c). In pistils, the *DR5*-*GUS* signals were mainly distributed in ovaries and stigmas without obvious changes under HT (Figure 6d).

Importantly, a subset of DEGs encoding key JA biosynthetic enzymes were identified under the HT condition, of which *SlLOX3/4* and *SlAOC4* showed substantially increased expression, while *SlFAD2* and *SlFAD3* were dramatically downregulated (Figure 6e), indicating JA biosynthesis is readily and significantly affected by HT in stamens. In contrast, discrepant responsive profiles of JA biosynthesis-related genes were detected in pistils under the HT condition. The expression levels of *SlFAD2* and *SlFAD3* tended to be slightly but non-significantly reduced, whereas expression levels of *SlLOX3/4* and *SlJAR1* were significantly increased after the HT treatment (Figure 6e).

In stamens, the changes of endogenous JA content responding to HT were tightly consistent with that of JA-Ile; both were significantly decreased (*p* < 0.01, t-test) under HT (Figure 6f). In pistils, the content of JA was significantly decreased (*p* < 0.01, *t*-test) by the HT treatment, whereas the JA-Ile had no significant change at 2 d after the HT treatment (Figure 6f). At 12 d after the HT treatment, neither JA nor JA-Ile contents exhibited significant changes (Figure 6f).

Together, these data suggest that higher levels of IAA, JA, and JA-Ile in pistils compared with stamens may play an important role in maintaining pistil normal growth even under the HT condition. The different metabolism and signaling responses of IAA and JA pathways between pistils and stamens under the HT stress could be tightly associated with the growth difference between stamen and pistil, which result in stigma exsertion under the HT condition.

#### **3.7 Exogenous JA instead of IAA can effectively rescues the HT induced-stigma exsertion**

To further verify the role of auxin and JA in stigma-exsertion development under the HT condition, exogenous IAA and JA solutions with different concentrations were applied to the flower buds of 'Micro-Tom' plants with two-day intervals until 6 days after the temperature upshift. Surprisingly, we found that exogenous application of JA rather than auxin can reverse stigma exsertion caused by the HT treatment.

At anthesis, control stamens and pistils grew to 5.66 ± 0.10 mm and 5.65 ± 0.08 mm in length, respectively, whereas stamens and pistils exposed to HT were only 4.94 ± 0.09 mm and 5.12 ± 0.06 mm in length with a higher rate of stigma-exsertion (Figure S5a). Unexpectedly, exogenous auxin application couldn't restore the lengths of stamens and pistils, irrespective of the concentrations (10-5 or 10-6 M IAA) used (Figure S5a). This result is quite different from the previous reports in *Arabidopsis* and barley, in which auxin application can restore anther length caused by high temperatures in a dose-dependent manner (Sakata *et al.* 2010). It's worth pointing out that increasing the IAA concentration to 10-4 M caused the flower bud abortion under the HT condition (data not shown). Accordingly, high stigma exsertion rates (61-65%) were observed under the HT condition with and

without exogenous auxin application (Figure S5a). However, 2-Naphthoic acid (NPA, inhibitor of auxin transport) severely suppressed the lengths of stamens and pistils (Figure S5a) under the HT condition, although the stigma exsertion was restored by the NPA treatment to a certain degree (Figure S5a). These findings suggest that auxin is indispensable in the regulation of stamen and pistil development in tomato, but it is not the main regulator of the HT-induced stigma exsertion.

By contrast, exogenous JA or MeJA spray suppressed the HT-caused length reduction of stamens and pistils and finally reduced the stigma exsertion rate in a dose-dependent manner. Analysis of JA-treated plants showed that 10-5 M JA specifically restored the shortened stamens and pistils caused by HT to nearly normal lengths, and 10-4 M JA application also reversed the length of stamens and pistils, although to a less extent (Figure 7a), whereas 10-6 M JAexhibited no significant effects on length of stamens and pistils. More importantly, the stigma exsertion rates of HT-treated plants with either 10-5 M JA or 10-4 M JA were significantly reduced from 72% to 27% and 22%, respectively (Figure 7a). Similar results were obtained by spraying with different doses of MeJA (the methyl ester of jasmonate) (Figure S5b). The application of 10-5 M or 10-4 M MeJA effectively attenuated the severity of the HT injury on pistil and stamen growth.

To examine whether the rescue of stigma exsertion by exogenous JA application is associated with the regulation of cell morphology, longitudinal sections of stamens and styles treated with and without JA under HT for 12 d were generated. A significantly decrease (*p* < 0.05, *t*-test) in cell size of stamens treated with JA was observed under the HT condition, but no significant change was found in styles (Figure 7b). However, under the HT condition, the total cell numbers were prominently increased in both stamens and styles treated with JA in comparison with those without the JA treatment (Figure 7b). These results indicate that JA reverses stigma exsertion through restoring cell development under the HT condition.

## **3.8 JA/COI1 signaling pathway participates in the HT-induced stigma exsertion**

In the JA/COI1 signaling pathway in plants, JA-Ile is perceived by jasmonate receptor1 (COI1), which recruits the jasmonate zim-domain proteins (JAZs) for ubiquitin-dependent degradation by the 26S proteasome (Katsir *et al.* 2008)*.* Here, the 26S proteasome inhibitor MG132 was used to further verify whether the JA/COI1 signaling pathway is implicated in reversing stigma exsertion caused by the HT treatment. Under the HT condition, 10-5 M JA-mediated restoration of stamen and pistil growth was completely counteracted by the addition of 10-4 M MG132 (Figure S5c). Accordingly, a high proportion of stigma exsertion (67%) was detected in plants treated with 10-5 M JA+10-4 M MG132 solution (Figure S5c), implicating that the 26S proteasome may also participate in the HT-induced stigma exsertion.

Furthermore, a mutant impaired in the tomato ortholog of COI1 in the 'Micro-Tom' background named *jasmonate-insensitive1-1* (*jai1-1*) was used to elucidate the function of the JA/COI1 signaling pathway in regulation of stigma exsertion under the HT condition. Under the HT condition, the length changes of stamens caused by HT in *jai1-1* homozygotes was consistent with that of wild-type plants which had a low JA level. However, in contrast to wild-type plants (5.05 ± 0.13 mm), HT induced more severe impairment on pistil lengths in *jai1-1* homozygous plants (4.87 ± 0.13 mm) (Figure 7c)*.* Consequently, under the HT condition, *jai1-1* homozygotes showed a significantly low stigma exsertion rate (33%) compared with that of the wild-type plants (*p* < 0.05, *t*-test) (Figure 7c).

#### **4 DISCUSSION**

Stamen-pistil synchrony seems to be a prerequisite for successful mating in flowering plants (Cheng *et al.* 2009). Unfortunately, the elaborate operation is readily annihilated by environmental stresses, particularly thermal hurt under global warming, and results in stigma exsertion in some plant species including tomato and rice (Giorno *et al.* 2013). Stigma exsertion induced by high temperature is prevalent in cultivated tomato species and is considered to adjust plant behavior against abiotic stresses, resulting in substantial yield loss in hot seasons (Dane *et al.* 1991). However, the regulatory mechanism of HT-induced stigma exsertion remains poorly understood. Here, RNA-Seq analysis combined with physiological and biochemical approaches were used to globally identify genes and pathways implicated in the HT-induced stigma exsertion, providing new insights into stigma exsertion under a global-warming scenario (Figure 8).

HT stress during anthesis triggered stigma exsertion in different tomato cultivars (Figure 1b, Figure S1), consistent with previous reports (Dane, Hunter & Chambliss 1991; Lohar & Peat 1998; Saeed, Bhatti & Khan 2007; Kartikeya *et al.* 2012). Moreover, the stigma exsertion rate was strongly positively correlated with environmental temperature (Figure 1b). Studies have suggested that HT stress can differently affect male and female organ development in diverse crops (Hedhly, Hormaza & Herrero 2009; Djanaguiraman *et al.* 2017). In our study, HT stress resulted in significantly reduced lengths of both stamens and pistils, in particular to stamens, thus generating the protruding stigma (Figure 1c), different from the stigma exsertion in wild tomato species which is caused by excessive elongation of pistils (Chen *et al.* 2007). At the cellular level, longitudinal sections uncovered that the total cell number in stamens treated with HT for 12 d were significantly reduced by 36%, whereas only 12% in styles

(Figure 5a), and enlarged cells were exclusively found in stamens (Figure 5a). In general, cell morphology closely coincided with organ shape and size (Tsukaya 2003). In addition, the pollen viability and fruit set rate were significantly affected (Figure S6). These findings indicate that stamen is more susceptible than pistil to the HT stress in cultivated tomatoes, and the differences in cell morphology between stamens and pistils under HT may be tightly associated with the phenotype of stigma exsertion.

It is well documented that plant morphogenesis depends on localized cell division and cell enlargement processes (Cosgrove 2005; Gonzalez, Vanhaeren & Inze 2012). Normally, cell remodeling is closely related to the primary cell wall which can be considered as a polymeric network of hydrophilic matrix mainly composed of pectins, celluloses, and hemicelluloses (Carpita & Gibeaut 1993). Pectins, the fundamental polysaccharides in the plant primary cell wall, perform significant functions in the determination of cell wall porosity and extensibility, and further regulate cell and organ growth during plant development (Atmodjo *et al.* 2013; Xiong *et al.* 2015). Furthermore, pectins also play crucial roles in modulating cell wall structure in response to various stresses including heat, drought, cold and salt (Gall *et al.* 2015). In this study, GO and KEGG pathway analyses on HT-responsive genes showed that a majority of significantly down-regulated genes in stamens and pistils were specifically enriched with those involved in the pectin catabolic process, cell wall organization or modification and glucose metabolism (Figures S2 and S3). qRT-PCR analysis further confirmed that numerous key enzymes involved in pectin modification and degradation such as *SlVGD1, SlPPME1*, *SlVGDH1*, *SlPGA4* and *LAT59* were significantly suppressed by HT in stamens, while fewer genes including *AT59-Like*, *SlVGD1*, *SlVGDH1* showed significantly reduced expression in pistils (Figure S4a).

Due to the sophisticated networks regulating the biosynthesis and degradation of pectins in plants, the exact correlation between genes involved in pectin metabolism and the pectin content has not been clearly elucidated (Pelloux, Rusterucci & Mellerowicz 2007; Mohnen 2008; Atmodjo, Hao & Mohnen 2013). Here, we found that the pectin content was considerably increased in both stamens and pistils under HT for 2 d, corresponding to the enlarged cell size (Figures 3b and 5a), suggesting that increased pectin content enhances cell wall extensibility, which is associated with cell enlargement (Xiong *et al.* 2015). However, the significantly decreased pectin content in pistils after the HT treatment for 12 d (Figure 3b) restrained stress-induced cell enlargement (Gall *et al.* 2015), which is further supported by the unchanged cell size in pistils (Figure 5a).

The essential roles of cell wall invertases (CWINs) and vacuolar invertases (VINs), which hydrolyze sucrose into glucose and fructose, and sucrose synthases, which degrade sucrose into UDP-glucose and fructose, in plant development have been highlighted (Ruan 2014). Notably, VINs double the osmotic contribution of sucrose and are considered to be vital in regulating cell expansion through their osmotic effects (Ruan *et al.* 2010). The different responses of CWIN and VIN genes have been observed among diverse crops under environmental stresses (Ji *et al.* 2005). Here, *SlCWINV2* and *SlCWINV4* showed significantly decreased expression in both stamens and pistils subjected to the HT treatment (Figure 4b, c). However, the expression of *SlSUS4* and *SlVIN2* was increased exclusively in stamens (Figure 4a, c), which is speculated to be tightly coupled with the accumulation of glucose and fructose and further induce cell expansion in stamens (Figures 4d and 5a).

Spatial and temporal expression patterns of cell division and expansion genes determine morphogenesis through regulating cell number and cell size (Somerville *et al.* 2004). Expansins are key endogenous regulators of plant cell enlargement (Cosgrove 2005), and can induce stress relaxation and extension of plant cell walls by weakening the non-covalent binding between wall polysaccharides in a pH-dependent manner, thus permitting turgor-driven cell expansion (McQueen-Mason & Cosgrove 1994; Tenhaken 2015). Moreover, cellular water uptake is prerequisite in response to the turgor relaxation (Cosgrove 2005). Under abiotic stress conditions, expansin genes are generally transcriptionally upregulated (Tenhaken 2015). Interestingly, our data showed that the GO term of 'regulation of pH' was significantly enriched in the cluster 1 and cluster 2 of stamens rather than pistils (Figure S2). However, the GO term of 'water transport' was strongly enriched in both stamens (cluster 5) and pistils (cluster 4) (Figure S2). Together with the results that the expression of five expansin genes (*SlEXPA4*, *SlEXPA7*, *SlEXPA11*, *SlEXPA13*, and *SlEXPA15*) was significantly increased in stamens under HT, but not other than *SlEXPA13* and *SlEXPA15* in pistils (Figure 5c), we suggest that expansin-mediated 'acid induced cell wall loosening' (Cosgrove 2005) predominantly happened in stamens instead of pistils under the high temperature condition. The longitudinal section of stamens and styles showed larger cells induced by HT at 12 d in stamens not pistils (Figure 5a), further supporting our speculation.

Cyclins coupled with cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) function as an ancient molecular module that drives and regulates cell cycle progression governing cell division and growth (Umeda, Shimotohno & Yamaguchi 2005; Inze & Veylder 2006). In this study, ten cyclins were identified to be HT responsive in stamens, while only one in pistils (Figure 5b). Intriguingly, most of them were

markedly up-regulated at 2 d after the HT treatment, while down-regulated at 12 d (Figure 5b), precisely matching the change of cell numbers and lengths of stamens and pistils (Figure 1c). These findings indicate that cyclins perform crucial functions in determining the lengths of stamens and pistils under the HT condition through regulating cell numbers. In conclusion, under the HT condition, the discrepant co-regulation of pectin, sugar, expansin, and cyclin in stamens and pistils contributes to the determination of the blueprint of cell shape and number through regulating cell expansion and division.

Numerous studies have reported that phytohormones, particularly auxin and JA, play essential roles in regulating the filament elongation in *Arabidopsis* (Cecchetti *et al.* 2008; Cheng *et al.* 2009; Song *et al.* 2011; Chae *et al.* 2012; Saito *et al.* 2015). However, the exact molecular bases of phytohormone in control of stigma exsertion have not been studied. Auxin plays an indispensable role in the induction of cell division by controlling cell-cycle progression (Perrot-Rechenmann, 2010). Researches on cultured cells and leaf explants have revealed that low and high auxin concentrations facilitate cell expansion and cell division, respectively (Bhalerao *&* Bennett 2003). In this study, we revealed that two key auxin biosynthetic genes (*ToFZY2* and *SlAMI1*) were actively involved in the HT response in stamens and pistils with different patterns, which resulted in low auxin concentration in stamens and a relatively higher auxin content in pistils at 12 d after the HT treatment (Figure 6b, c). Considering the fact that only very few expansins and cyclins were involved in the responses to HT in pistils (Figure 5b), we suggest that a relatively high concentration of auxin in pistils treated with HT for 12 d plays a critical role in preserving expansin and cyclin expression levels. Surprisingly, different from the previous report in barley and *Arabidopsis* that exogenous auxin restored stamen lengths under HT (Sakata *et al*. 2010), in tomato the application of exogenous auxin could not reverse stamens and

pistils lengths and the stigma exsertion induced by HT irrespective of the auxin dose (Figure S5a). However, lengths of both stamens and pistils were severely suppressed in plants treated with 10-5 M NPA (Figure S5a). These results suggest that auxin is necessary in regulating the HT-induced growth inhibition both in stamens and pistils, but the different response was not mainly contributed to the stigma exsertion in tomato.

JA acts as an essential regulator of plant development and plant defense (Wasternack & Hause 2013), usually via altering cell cycle progression, cell expansion and synchronously triggering defense responses in *Arabidopsis* (Pauwels *et al.* 2008; Noir *et al.* 2013). The accumulation of endogenous JA may rapidly trigger the induction of plant defense responses against abiotic stresses and pathogens (Kazan *et al.* 2015). Here, we found that both JA and JA-Ile contents were decreased dramatically in stamens rather than pistils treated with HT by severely suppressing *SlFAD2* and *SlFAD3* transcript abundances regardless of strongly up-regulated *SlLOX3/4* and *SlAOC4* (Figure 6e and f), consistent with previous report in rice that heat stress suppresses the accumulation of endogenous JA within 12 h (Du, Liu & Xiong 2013). However, in *Arabidopsis,* the JA levels were significantly increased during heat stress for 6 h (Shannon *et al.* 2009). Exogenous application of JA has been found to be effective in mitigating cold-induced damage in *Arabidopsis* (Hu *et al.* 2013). Here, 10-4 M and 10-5 M JA treatment could effectively decrease stigma exsertion rates from 72% to nearly 25% under the HT condition, and restore stamen and pistil lengths in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 7a). Cytological observation indicated that JA treatment significantly increased cell number in both stamens and styles (Figure 7b), which also substantially weakened the cell enlargement induced by HT at 12 d in stamens, implicating that JA restores stigma exsertion through direct or indirect regulation of cell morphology. Our data

provides a new insight into the response mode of JA signaling to plant heat stress during floral organ development, and suggests that JA differentially regulate stress tolerance in a species- and organ-specific manner.

Previous studies have revealed that JA-Ile binding to its receptor COI1 induces the degradation of JAZ proteins and then release the repression of downstream transcription factors such as MYC2 (Kazan *et al.* 2013), which is dependent on activities of the SCFCOI1 ubiquitin ligase and the 26S proteasome (Thines *et al.* 2007). Here, we found that JA treatment reversed the HT-induced stigma exsertion in tomato through the JA/COI1 signaling pathway. First, the specific inhibitor of the 26S proteasome MG132 prevented JA treatment from rescuing the stigma exsertion and length reduction of stamens and pistils induced by HT (Figure S5c), implicating that the 26S proteasome participate in the jasmonate-mediated stigma exsertion restoration. Second, *jai1-1* mutant plants blocking the JA signaling exhibited stamens with almost identical lengths to the wild type plants but shorter pistils under the HT condition, resulting in a significant decrease of HT-induced stigma exsertion rate (Figure 7c). Under the HT condition, the difference in length of pistils between *jai1-1* mutant and wild-type plants is tightly associated with the fact that the JA signaling was blocked in *jai1-1* mutant, but not in wild-type plants (Figure 6f). These findings indicate that the JA/COI1 signaling pathway is a key hub in regulating stigma exsertion induced by HT, and the relatively higher concentration of JA in pistils even under the HT condition plays crucial role in protecting pistils against the HT injury, thus resulting in stigma exsertion in tomato.

In summary, our data revealed that HT-induced tomato stigma exsertion was resulted from shorter stamen lengths because of the high impressionability of stamens to high temperature. Under the high temperature condition, signaling of phytohormones including IAA and JA, and pectin and sugar exhibit differential responses between stamens and pistils, which further affect transcript abundances of expansins and cyclins, as well as the cell wall properties such as porosity and extensibility, leading to different cell size and number in stamens and pistils through regulating cell expansion and division (Figure 8). More importantly, the application of exogenous JA effectively reverses the tomato stigma exsertion caused by high temperatures (Figure 8). Overall, our results shed light on the regulatory mechanisms of stigma exsertion induced by HT and provide new insights into the function of JA in protecting plants from heat stress damages in particular under the global warming conditions.
