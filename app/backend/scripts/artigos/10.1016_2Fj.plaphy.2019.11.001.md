#### ABSTRACT

Tubers are vegetative reproduction organs formed from underground extensions of the plant stem. Potato tubers are harvested and stored for months. Storage under cold temperatures of 2–4 °C is advantageous for supressing sprouting and diseases. However, development of reducing sugars can occur with cold storage through a process called cold-induced sweetening (CIS). CIS is undesirable as it leads to darkened color with fry processing. The purpose of the current study was to find differences in biological responses in eight cultivars with variation in CIS resistance. Transcriptome sequencing was done on tubers before and after cold storage and three approaches were taken for gene expression analysis: 1. Gene expression correlated with end-point glucose after cold storage, 2. Gene expression correlated with increased glucose after cold storage (after-before), and 3. Differential gene expression before and after cold storage. Cultivars with high CIS resistance (low glucose after cold) storage. The cultivars with low CIS resistance (high glucose after cold) showed increased expression of genes involved in abiotic stress response, gene expression, protein turnover and the mitochondria. There was a small number of genes with similar expression patterns for all cultivars including genes involved in cell wall strengthening and phospholipases. It is proposed that the pattern of gene expression is related to clise.

# 1. Introduction

Potato tubers are vegetative reproductive organs that form as underground extensions of the stem. Tubers are dormant when harvested which involves internally regulated endodormancy, externally regulated ecodormancy and inter-tissue regulated paradormancy (Suttle, 2007). Regulation of the cell cycle is involved in dormancy and bud sprouting (Campbell, 2006). Under natural growing conditions tubers remain dormant underground until the endogenous program and environmental cues signal conditions suitable for dormancy release and bud sprouting. Key to the survival of the tuber over winter is the ability to remain dormant and withstand cold and freezing conditions. These conditions lead to decreased cell membrane fluidity and formation of ice crystals within plant cells causing irreversible damage (Takahashi et al., 2013). In plants, exposure to low temperatures triggers a process of cold acclimation leading to expression of genes that increase tolerance to freezing (Thomashow, 2010). Sucrose and raffinose accumulation with cold stress is a documented phenomenon (Levitt, 1958) and

Chilling and freezing stress along with high temperature, drought and salinity induce metabolic changes in plants including synthesis of amino acids, polyamines, polyols, and soluble sugars ([Krasensky and Jonak, 2012](#page-11-3)). The relationship between sugars and cold acclimation is complex. There is evidence for function in osmoprotection ([Singh et al., 2015](#page-12-4)), and sugars have also been found to have a role in maintaining membrane stability [\(Strauss and Hauser,](#page-12-5) [1986;](#page-12-5) [Dhaliwal et al., 2019\)](#page-11-4), protein stability [\(Rani and Venkatesu,](#page-12-6) [2018\)](#page-12-6), carbohydrate homeostasis ([Nägele and Heyer, 2013](#page-12-7)) and intracellular signaling [\(Tarkowski and Van den Ende, 2015\)](#page-12-8).

Harvest of potato tubers for human consumption requires storage for several months. Storage at low temperatures (2–4 °C) is advantageous to preventing diseases, shrinkage and sprouting ([Sowokinos,](#page-12-9) [2001\)](#page-12-9). However, cold storage triggers a cold acclimation response involving production of reducing sugars, fructose and glucose [\(Gould](#page-11-5) [et al., 1979](#page-11-5); [Coffin et al., 1987](#page-11-6)), which cause darkened color of fried potato products in a chemical process called the Maillard reaction ([Shallenberger et al., 1959\)](#page-12-10). The Maillard reaction occurs with high temperature of frying in the presence of asparagine and reducing sugars and also results in production of the neurotoxin and carcinogen acrylamide [\(Mottram et al., 2002](#page-12-11); [Stadler et al., 2002\)](#page-12-12). Accumulation of reducing sugars is a balance of starch and sugar metabolism in the potato tuber including pathways for starch synthesis, starch degradation, glycolysis, hexogenesis and mitochondrial respiration ([Sowokinos,](#page-12-9) [2001;](#page-12-9) [Zhang et al., 2017a\)](#page-13-0). CIS was affected by two enzymes involved in the conversion of starch to reducing sugars, vacuolar invertase and UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase ([Gupta and Sowokinos, 2003](#page-11-7); [McKenzie et al., 2005;](#page-12-13) [Xu et al., 2009;](#page-13-1) [Sowokinos et al., 2018](#page-12-14)). Respiratory enzymes [\(Barichello et al., 1990\)](#page-11-8), the amyloplast membrane ([O'Donoghue et al., 1995\)](#page-12-15), amylose content [\(Jansky and Fajardo, 2014\)](#page-11-9) and amylase genes ([Hou et al., 2017](#page-11-10)) were also found to be involved.

Variation in resistance to CIS is under genetic regulation ([Douches](#page-11-11) [and Freyre, 1994](#page-11-11)). Silencing of vacuolar invertase using RNAi ([Zhang](#page-13-2) [et al., 2008](#page-13-2); [Bhaskar et al., 2010;](#page-11-12) [Ye et al., 2010](#page-13-3); [Wu et al., 2011](#page-13-4); [Wiberley-Bradford et al., 2014;](#page-13-5) [Clasen et al., 2016](#page-11-13); [Wiberley-Bradford](#page-13-6) [et al., 2016](#page-13-6)) reduced CIS. Vacuolar invertase is post-translationally regulated by the vacuolar invertase inhibitor, which increased CIS resistance when it was induced [\(Liu et al., 2010;](#page-12-16) [Brummell et al., 2011](#page-11-14); [Lin et al., 2013](#page-11-15); [McKenzie et al., 2013](#page-12-17); [Lin et al., 2015](#page-11-16)). Overexpression of the apoplastic invertase inhibitor also increased CIS in transgenics ([Datir, 2011](#page-11-17)). Using association mapping, candidate genes invertase, sucrose synthase 3, sucrose phosphate synthase, ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase, sucrose transporter 1, and a putative sucrose sensor gene were associated with CIS resistance [\(Menendez et al., 2002](#page-12-18)). In another candidate gene association mapping study using historical phenotyping data, UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase and apoplastic invertase genes were significantly associated with cold-induced sweetening [\(Baldwin et al., 2011](#page-11-18)). Quantitative trait loci (QTL) for CIS resistance co-mapped with expression QTL for the Auxin-regulated protein (AuxRP) and Heat-shock protein 90 (Hsp90) genes [\(Sołtys-](#page-12-19)[Kalina et al., 2015\)](#page-12-19).

Cold-induced transcripts were cloned and sequenced from potato tubers and found to encode a small heat-shock protein and a potato homolog of the dehydrin/RAB group of dehydration stress- and abscisic acid-inducible proteins ([van Berkel et al., 1994\)](#page-12-20). Studies comparing gene expression five days after cold treatment in CIS resistant wild species *S. berthaultii* and CIS susceptible *S. tuberosum* cv E3 demonstrated that genes with functions in cell rescue, defense and virulence, metabolism, energy and protein fate, included in various processes of plant defense against abiotic stresses were differentially expressed ([Chen et al., 2012\)](#page-11-19). Proteomic analysis of CIS sensitive and tolerant cultivars showed differential protein expression for protease inhibitors, patatins, heat shock proteins, lipoxygenase, phospholipase A1 and leucine aminopeptidase ([Fischer et al., 2013\)](#page-11-20).

Several wild *Solanum* species with CIS resistance have been

identified [\(Hamernik et al., 2009](#page-11-21); [McCann et al., 2010](#page-12-21); [Chen et al.,](#page-11-19) [2012;](#page-11-19) [Ali and Jansky, 2015\)](#page-11-22) and are resources for potato germplasm enhancement. One of these, *S. brevicaule* (formerly *S. oplocense*), was introgressed into *S. tuberosum* and backcross hybrids were found to have Colorado potato beetle and CIS resistance ([Tai et al., 2015](#page-12-22); [Neilson](#page-12-23) [et al., 2017](#page-12-23)). The current study compares differences in gene expression in eight cultivars with variation in CIS resistance after exposure to cold temperatures in storage for three months. Tuber gene expression, correlated with cultivar differences in glucose, was examined. In addition, gene expression differences and similarities between cultivars before and after cold storage were also examined. Biological processes correlated with glucose and response to cold were analyzed in the context of chilling damage and stress responses in tubers.

#### **2. Materials and methods**

#### *2.1. Plant material*

The material used in the study were breeding lines and cultivars that have a range of CIS resistance described in a previous study [\(Neilson](#page-12-23) [et al., 2017\)](#page-12-23). Tubers from *S. brevicaule*-derived breeding lines 15329-03, 15323-04 and 15328-26 were obtained from the Benton-Ridge Substation of the Fredericton Research and Development Centre (formerly Potato Research Centre), Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada (AAFC), Benton, New Brunswick, Canada in 2010. Tubers from breeding line 575.04.01 and cultivars Doremi and Lady Claire were obtained from Comité Nord research station of Sipre, Bretteville du Grand Caux, France in 2010. Tubers from breeding line Crop 20 and cultivar Summer Delight were obtained from the Canterbury Agriculture and Science Centre of Plant & Food Research, Lincoln, New Zealand in 2011.15329-03, 15323-04 and 15328-26 were backcross lines derived from an intercross of *S. brevicaule* and *S. tuberosum.* Tubers were sampled at the Benton-Ridge, Bretteville du Grand Caux and Lincoln sites before storage at 4 °C within two weeks after harvest as indicated below. For the after storage samples, tubers were stored for three months at 4 °C and sampled.

For each treatment, tubers were washed and sampled using a 7 mm Harris Uni-core (Sigma-Aldrich, Oakville, Canada) as described in [Neilson et al. \(2017\)](#page-12-23). Briefly, each tuber was sampled once at either end and three times around the middle for a total of five cores per tuber in regions without eyes. For each replicate the cores from three tubers were pooled in 50 ml Falcon tubes. This results in 15 cores from three tubers per replicate. Three replicates were taken from each clone for a total of nine tubers per clone. Tuber cores were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and freeze-dried. Two 5/32" stainless steel balls (MP Biomedicals) were added to tubes containing tuber cores and the tuber cores were ground to a powder using Geno/Grinder (SPEX, Metuchen, U. S. A.) at 500(1×), for 2 min. Freeze-dried tuber powder was then stored at room temperature. The same tuber powder was used for both glucose determination and RNA extraction.

Tuber glucose was determined using with the Glucose (HK) assay kit from Sigma-Aldrich (product code: GAHK-20) with 100 mg freeze-dried and ground tuber tissue. Glucose data was averaged over the three replicates. ANOVA with general linear model was used to analyze differences between cultivars and treatments (before and after cold storage). Tukey's test was used for pairwise comparisons using SYSTAT 13 (Systat Software, Inc. San Jose, U. S. A.). To calculate glucose increase after cold storage the average of the before cold storage glucose was subtracted from the average of the after glucose levels for each cultivar.

# *2.2. RNA library prep and transcriptome sequencing*

RNA from eight clones 15329-03, 15323-04, 15328-26, 575.04.01, Crop 20, Summer Delight, Doremi, and Lady Claire was used for transcriptome sequencing. Libraries were generated using the TruSeq RNA kit (Illumina). Libraries were subjected to 100 bases of sequencing on a HiSeq 2000 (Illumina) instrument in paired-end mode. Sequencing was done in three lanes with eight samples per lane. Initial quality control of the data was performed using the software included with the sequencer. Output from the sequencer was aligned to the *S. tuberosum* reference genome v3\_2.1.10 [\(Potato Genome Sequencing Consortium, 2011\)](#page-12-24) using the TopHat software suite v. 2.0.9 ([Trapnell et al., 2012;](#page-12-25) [Kim](#page-11-23) [et al., 2013\)](#page-11-23) with the mode for "fr-unstranded" library types. The quality of the alignments was verified using the 'flagstat' tool from the SAMtools software suite v. 0.1.19 ([Li et al., 2009\)](#page-11-24). Reads were assembled into transcripts with CuffLinks v. 2.1.1 [\(Trapnell et al., 2012](#page-12-25)), using the same genome file as a reference, in addition to the *S. tuberosum* ITAG1.0 annotation file obtained from the *Sol Genomics Network* ([Tomato Genome Consortium, 2013](#page-12-26); [Fernandez-Pozo et al., 2015](#page-11-25)). Transcriptome assembly was performed with the 'multi read correct' and 'fragment bias correct' modes activated. Sequencing quality statistics are in Table S1 of Supplementary Materials. Finally, assembled transcripts from different replicates and treatments were merged into a single reference transcriptome for each variety using the 'CuffMerge' tool included in CuffLinks and quantified, and fragment per kilobase per million mapped reads (FPKM) calculated. Raw and processed transcriptome sequence data was submitted to the National Centre for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus (NCBI GEO) ([Barrett et al., 2012](#page-11-26)) accession number GSE138254. Pearson correlation analysis was done with FPKM for genes after cold storage (end-point glucose after cold storage) and glucose increase after cold storage (after minus before cold storage glucose) using the Multiexperiment Viewer 4.9.0 ([Howe et al., 2010\)](#page-11-27) Pavlidis Template Matching tool across the eight cultivars. The genes with correlation < −0.9 and > 0.9 were identified. Log2 ratio of after (A) over before (B) (log2A/B) cold storage gene expression was also calculated after adding 1 to all FPKM values to remove zeros. The glucose increase after cold storage was calculated as the glucose after cold storage subtracted by the glucose before cold storage (A-B). Correlation analysis of log2A/B fold change in gene expression with the A-B glucose was done across eight cultivars. The genes with correlation < −0.9 and > 0.9 and fold change > 1 and < - 1 in at least one cultivar were identified. Finally, CuffDiff was used to analyze gene expression in tubers after versus before storage at 4 °C for each of the cultivars. Genes with p ≤ 0.05 were identified for each treatment and each variety as differentially expressed ([Trapnell et al.,](#page-12-25) [2012\)](#page-12-25) (Supplementary Material Tables S1 and S4). Genes with log2 > 1 were classified as up-regulated after cold storage and those with log2 < −1 were classified as down-regulated after cold storage. The GO terms from the iTAG1.0 annotation files were used for functional analysis using topGO ([Alexa and Rahnenfuhrer, 2010](#page-11-28)) for the differentially expressed genes from CuffDiff analysis. Enriched GO terms were those where p ≤ 0.05 in the topGO Fisher's exact test.

#### *2.3. nCounter mRNA quantification*

Multiplex analysis of the expression of genes was examined using nCounter Digital Analyzer (Nanostring Technologies, Seattle, U. S. A.). Details of the nCounter multiplex gene expression analysis are described elsewhere ([Geiss et al., 2008;](#page-11-29) [Tai et al., 2009](#page-12-27)). An nCounter codeset was designed for 52 genes (Supplementary Table S3). These 52 genes that were differentially expressed in the transcriptome sequencing analysis were used for nCounter analysis. The nCounter codeset included five housekeeping genes: Cox1-B (X83206.1) [\(Quinones et al.,](#page-12-28) [1995\)](#page-12-28), EF-1-alpha (AB061263.1) ([Nakane et al., 2003\)](#page-12-29), cyclophilin (AF126551.1) [\(Drouin and Dover, 1990](#page-11-30)), actin (X55749) and tubulin (Z33382.1) [\(Nicot et al., 2005\)](#page-12-30). mRNA abundance of the three biological replicates for each clone was log2-transformed and averaged for each clone and each time point (before and after storage). One hundred ng of total RNA was used for each nCounter quantification reaction.

#### *2.4. Tuber sprouting and glucose analysis*

Tuber sprouting and glucose data collected from a storage trial of commercial varieties Atlantic, Green Mountain, Kennebec, Hindenburg, Russet Burbank and Shepody in 2009 were used for analysis. Tubers were collected from five-hill plots with three replicate plots for each variety. Storage at 7 °C was done to allow for development of tuber sprouting as storage at 4 °C did not permit sprouting. All tubers were harvested for each plot, bagged and stored at 7 °C for three months. Sprout length indices were scored for five randomly selected tubers from each bag as a group. Sprout indices were a visual score of 0–5 as follows: 0 = 0–0.1 mm, 1 = 0.1–5 mm, 2 = 6–50 mm, 3 = 51–100 mm, 4 = 101–150 mm, 5 = 151–200 mm ([ŚLiwka et al., 2008\)](#page-12-31). Glucose was analyzed using glucose test strips ([Misener et al., 1996](#page-12-32)) on the five tubers/bag and the data mmol/L glucose concentrations from five randomly selected tubers were averaged.

#### **3. Results**

## *3.1. Gene expression after cold storage correlated with CIS resistance*

The transcriptome sequence reads were analyzed and 34947 genes were identified (Supplementary Table S2). Gene expression was quantified as FPKM. The expression of 52 of the differentially regulated genes were selected for analysis using nCounter and compared to transcriptome sequencing gene expression results (Supplementary Table S3 and [Fig. 1\)](#page-2-0). Spearman rank correlation analysis indicated that the transcriptome sequencing gene expression analysis was correlated with results of the nCounter analysis, providing validation for the transcriptome sequencing results ([Fig. 1](#page-2-0)).

Resistance to CIS is quantified by the amount of reducing sugars produced after cold storage [\(Douches and Freyre, 1994;](#page-11-11) [Xiao et al.,](#page-13-7) [2018\)](#page-13-7). The results indicate that Lady Claire has the highest resistance to CIS and Summer Delight has the lowest [\(Fig. 2\)](#page-3-0). The average glucose for Lady Claire after cold storage was below 2 mg/g dry weight. #### *3.2. Fold change gene expression correlated with glucose increase after storage*

The glucose before cold storage varied between cultivars in addition to glucose after storage. It was noted that the cultivars varied not only in the glucose after cold storage, but also in the level of glucose increase after cold (glucose after storage minus glucose before storage) [\(Fig. 2](#page-3-0)). All cultivars showed increased glucose after cold storage, however the increased levels were not significant for Lady Claire. Some cultivars had very low levels of glucose before cold storage and responded to cold treatment with large increases (e.g. Crop 20 and 15323-04). These cultivars ended up with relatively lower glucose after cold compared to other clones but had strong responses to cold. It was also of interest to study genes that were correlated to glucose increases after cold rather than just the end-point glucose after cold. These were genes that were correlated with the intensity of glucose production in response to cold.

To analyze gene expression differences correlated with increases in glucose after cold storage the log2 fold change in gene expression after cold storage was correlated with glucose increase after cold storage (after minus before cold storage). Genes with positive and negative correlation of fold change in expression and glucose increase after cold storage were identified and presented in [Table 2.](#page-5-0) There were small numbers of genes in these lists which were not sufficient for finding significant GO enrichment. Instead, functional enrichment of the groups of positively and negatively correlated genes was examined using gene descriptions and the published literature.

The negatively correlated genes were those with expression that had the highest fold change in the cultivars with the lowest glucose increases after cold. There were seven genes, one of them had unknown function ([Table 2](#page-5-0)). Among the six remaining genes, there were two genes that had functions in M phase of the cell cycle and one gene was involved in auxin biosynthesis. There was one gene encoding an E3 ubiquitin (Ub) ligase complex F-box protein among the negatively correlated genes that had protein domains similar to those involved in degradation of cyclin cell cycle regulators. There was also a disease resistance gene and a hnRNP involved in RNA splicing in the list of negatively correlated genes.

The positively correlated genes were those with expression that had the highest fold change in cultivars with the highest glucose increases after cold. There were 23 genes and five were involved in gene expression including transcription, RNA processing and translation ([Table 2\)](#page-5-0). One gene in the positively correlated group encoded a mitochondrial protein. Three genes were involved with intracellular signaling and intracellular transport. Two genes had functions in cell wall and cytoskeleton modification. A F-box family protein and the 26S proteasome regulatory subunit were positively correlated as were proteins involved in post-translational protein modification – Ub thioesterase, Sumo activating enzyme 1b. A gene encoding lysosomal palmitoyl-protein thioesterase were among the positively correlated genes. The expression of the trehalose-6-phophate phosphatase gene was also positively correlated. Trehalose-6-phosphate is a signaling molecule that in concert with sucrose regulates carbohydrate metabolism and the phosphatase activity converts it to trehalose.

# *3.4. Tuber sprouting and glucose*

The functional analysis of the gene expression results indicated that expression of genes involved in DNA replication and cell division were associated with low glucose production in tubers. These results suggested that glucose levels may affect cell growth and proliferation, which is important in bud sprouting. Tuber sprouting was suppressed for all cultivars under storage at 4 °C. To study glucose and sprouting, tubers from six commercial varieties stored at 7 °C were used. A range of tuber dormancy release and sprouting was observed. The results show that tubers with high levels of glucose had lower levels of tuber sprouting ([Fig. 5](#page-9-0)). The range of sprout length in tubers with high glucose did not go above 5 mm (sprout index 1). In contrast tubers with low glucose had a wider range of sprouting from 0 to 100 mm. These results are supportive of a relationship between tuber glucose, cell division and sprouting.

# **4. Discussion**

Storage of potato tubers in the cold at low temperatures can provide suppression of tuber sprouting and storage diseases, but subjects tubers to chilling stress [\(Sowokinos, 2001](#page-12-9)). To better understand CIS, transcriptome sequencing was used to examine gene expression in tubers under cold storage in eight cultivars with variation in glucose levels under cold storage. Three approaches to gene expression analysis were taken: 1. Gene expression correlated with end-point glucose after cold storage, 2. Gene expression correlated with increased glucose after cold storage (after-before), and 3. Differential gene expression before and after cold storage. The biological processes identified in the transcriptome sequencing analysis were considered in the context of chilling stress which induces a cellular damage and cold acclimation response in tubers that involves production of glucose [\(Xin and Browse,](#page-13-11) [2000\)](#page-13-11). Chilling stress is very damaging to plant cells as lipid bilayers of cell membranes lose fluidity with decreasing temperature [\(Parkin et al.,](#page-12-40) [1989;](#page-12-40) [Mukhopadhyay and Roychoudhury, 2018](#page-12-41)). In addition, chilling-induced membrane damage causes loss of ion fluxes, water potential and osmotic stress. Cold acclimation is associated with increased accumulation of sucrose and reducing sugars in many plants ([Wanner and Junttila, 1999;](#page-12-3) [Klotke et al., 2004;](#page-11-2) [Schulze et al., 2012](#page-12-43); [Tarkowski and Van den Ende, 2015\)](#page-12-8). Soluble sugars provide cryoprotection through depressing freezing points and interacting with lipid bilayers to increase membrane stability [\(Strauss and Hauser, 1986](#page-12-5); [Valluru and Van den Ende, 2008;](#page-12-44) [Dhaliwal et al., 2019](#page-11-4)). Freezing temperatures also decrease relative humidity causing dehydration stress for plants, which can be prevented through production of soluble sugars that act as osmolytes to prevent loss of water and turgor pressure ([Xin and Browse, 2000](#page-13-11); [Krasensky and Jonak, 2012](#page-11-3); [Schulze et al.,](#page-12-43) [2012\)](#page-12-43). Hence, the production of sugars in response to cold is beneficial for plant survival in cold and freezing temperatures. Moreover, the variation in glucose production between cultivars may be due to genetic differences in cellular damage and cold acclimation responses.

In the current study, cultivars with genetic variation in CIS resistance were examined using transcriptome sequencing. The cultivars examined all produced glucose with cold treatment at 4 °C and one cultivar, Summer Delight, produced levels of glucose above the acceptable range. There is high interest in decreasing production of reducing sugars in response to chilling stress in potato. Potato invertases that control conversion of sucrose to glucose and fructose and the protein that inhibits invertase activity were demonstrated by others to be critical for CIS ([Bhaskar](#page-11-12) [et al., 2010](#page-11-12); [Liu et al., 2010;](#page-12-16) [Brummell et al., 2011;](#page-11-14) [Liu et al., 2011](#page-12-45); [Liu](#page-12-46) [et al., 2013a, 2013b;](#page-12-46) [McKenzie et al., 2013](#page-12-17); [Lin et al., 2015](#page-11-16)). The current study is supportive of these previous studies done by others in demonstrating a role for invertase inhibitor in CIS resistance. High expression of the Sotub12g031160 invertase inhibitor gene was found for Lady Claire, which had insignificant increase in glucose after cold and the lowest glucose levels after cold storage compared to the other cultivars. Conversely, the expression of this gene was not detectable in Summer Delight, which had the lowest CIS resistance. Suppression of the vacuolar invertase gene using RNAi decreased levels of reducing sugars under low temperatures in studies by others [\(Zhang et al., 2008](#page-13-2); [Bhaskar et al., 2010](#page-11-12); [Ye et al., 2010;](#page-13-3) [Wu et al., 2011](#page-13-4); [Wiberley-Bradford](#page-13-5) [et al., 2014](#page-13-5); [Clasen et al., 2016;](#page-11-13) [Wiberley-Bradford et al., 2016](#page-13-6)). Moreover, growth and development of RNAi plants was similar to wildtype plants suggesting that vacuolar invertase activity is dispensible for the cultivated potato, especially since tubers are picked up and stored under controlled conditions. In the wild, on the other hand, tubers would be left in the ground and production of glucose via vacuolar invertase provides an osmolyte to help maintain viability of tubers under freezing conditions in the field. The results of the current study demonstrate that natural variation in the expression of an invertase inhibitor gene was associated with CIS resistance in the potato cultivars studied. These findings also demonstrate that high expression of the invertase inhibitor gene can serve as a proxy trait for selecting natural variation in CIS resistance in breeding.

Transcriptome sequencing data was analyzed three ways in the current study and all of the analyses indicated that functions related to auxin, DNA replication and repair were associated with low glucose and CIS resistance in the cultivars studied. We propose that expression of genes involved in auxin, DNA replication and repair are a part of a response by tubers to chilling injury in cold storage. The results concur with studies published by others showing that QTL for CIS resistance co-mapped with expression QTL for the Auxin-regulated protein (AuxRP) [\(Sołtys-Kalina et al., 2015\)](#page-12-19). One explanation for the relationship between low glucose and DNA replication and repair is that it is related to DNA damage induced by chilling temperatures in plants [\(Nisa](#page-12-47) [et al., 2019\)](#page-12-47). DNA damage responses include progression of cells to S phase of the cell cycle to undergo DNA replication and repair [\(Hustedt](#page-11-45) [and Durocher, 2016\)](#page-11-45). Tuber wounding was also found to induce auxin and DNA synthesis [\(Neubauer et al., 2012](#page-12-48); [Lulai and Neubauer, 2014](#page-12-49); [Lulai et al., 2014](#page-12-50); [Lulai et al., 2016\)](#page-12-51). Wounding was associated with production of suberized phellum cells to repair damage. The results of the current study suggest that cultivars with low glucose after cold were undergoing DNA replication and repair, whereas, cultivars with high glucose did not. Genetic variation in DNA repair in response to stress was also documented in aspen trees and *Arabidopsis thaliana* [\(De Block](#page-11-46) [et al., 2005](#page-11-46); [Tai et al., 2010\)](#page-12-52).

Post-harvest tubers are dormant, which involves endodormancy, ecodormancy and paradormancy ([Suttle, 2007](#page-12-0)). Endodormant bud cells in the potato tuber were found to be arrested at the G1/S phase of the cell cycle ([Campbell et al., 1996](#page-11-47)). The increased expression of genes involved in DNA replication and repair after cold in low glucose cultivars is evidence for progression of the cell cycle and suggests that cells in the buds may have exited from endodormancy. The 4 °C storage conditions were not permissive for tuber sprouting and dormancy release was not assessed. However, tubers stored at 7 °C did show sprouting and examination of this data revealed that low glucose was associated with advanced sprouting compared to tubers with high glucose. These results supports an association of low glucose with progression of the cell cycle.

Glucose production is part of the cold acclimation response and can protect membranes, decrease freezing temperatures, reduce osmotic stress and loss of turgor pressure [\(Strauss and Hauser, 1986;](#page-12-5) [Valluru](#page-12-44) [and Van den Ende, 2008;](#page-12-44) [Dhaliwal et al., 2019](#page-11-4)). The study provided evidence that cultivars with high glucose were the ones showing increased protein turnover and regulation of gene expression with upregulation of ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, transcription factors, intracellular signaling, transcription, RNA processing and translation genes. Differential expression of genes with these functions were related to stress responses in other plants [\(Tai et al., 2007](#page-12-53); [Stone,](#page-12-54) [2014\)](#page-12-54). The results suggest that high glucose cultivars were more responsive to chilling stress and produced a chilling acclimation response that included increasing glucose. Summer Delight, which had the highest levels of glucose after cold storage, had increased expression of genes involved in abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis after cold storage. ABA is involved in regulation of cold acclimation [\(Mantyla et al., 1995\)](#page-12-55) and increase in Summer Delight indicates a stronger response to cold compared to other cultivars.

The genes with fold change positively correlated with increase in glucose after cold included peroxidase, which is associated with production of ROS in plants with chilling injuries [\(Moller, 2001;](#page-12-56) [Kawakami](#page-11-41) [et al., 2002](#page-11-41); [Mukhopadhyay and Roychoudhury, 2018](#page-12-41)). A sign of response to cellular damages was the expression of a gene involved in lysosomal degradation of acyl proteins [\(Verkruyse and Hofmann,](#page-12-35) [1996\)](#page-12-35). We propose that cultivars with low CIS resistance maintain dormancy and remain arrested in G1 of the cell cycle. The reason may be these cultivars undergo higher levels of membrane and other cellular damage under chilling temperatures or have a stronger response to chilling stress triggering a strong cold acclimation response including production of high levels of glucose. The gene expression data also provides evidence for mitochondrial biogenesis in cultivars with high glucose after cold with increased expression of a mitochondrial gene. The biogenesis may be due to increased requirement for respiration to produce energy to carry out cellular repair and cold acclimation responses. Tubers of CIS susceptible and resistant cultivars were both found to have a transient increase in respiration following low temperature exposure that decreased to basal levels [\(Zommick et al., 2014](#page-13-13)). However, the basal level of respiration of a CIS susceptible cultivar under low temperature was higher than the CIS resistant cultivars. The results of the current study provide further evidence of an association between CIS and mitochondrial biogenesis and respiration.

Differentially expressed genes for each of eight cultivars demonstrated that a small number had shared expression patterns across cultivars before and after cold storage. Expression of a hydroxyproline rich glycoprotein gene, which is a constituent of the structural cell wall glycoprotein extensin, was found increased in all of the cultivars examined after cold storage. This result concurs with observations that cold acclimation in plants induces changes to cell walls to increase strengthening [\(Weiser et al., 1990](#page-13-14)). Increased expression of erect panicle2, another gene involved in mechanical strengthening, was also found. Down-regulation of triacyglycerol lipase, phospholipase A1 and patatin was observed. Patatin is a storage protein but also has phospholipase activity ([Senda et al., 1996;](#page-12-57) [Hirschberg et al., 2001](#page-11-48)). Phospholipase A was found to be increased in the first week of cold acclimation in wheat but is followed by a decrease after longer periods of cold exposure ([Skinner, 2009\)](#page-12-58). Proteomic analysis of potato tubers showed that phospholipase A1 protein levels were higher in CIS tolerant cultivars and decreased after 12 weeks of cold storage at 4 °C ([Fischer et al., 2013](#page-11-20)). Phopholipase genes were also found to be differentially expressed during dormancy release and tuber sprouting ([Liu](#page-12-59) [et al., 2015](#page-12-59)). Phospholipase activity is involved in providing precursors for biosynthesis of lipid-based secondary metabolites including jasmonic acid and signaling molecules ([Munnik and Testerink, 2009](#page-12-60); [Schaller and Stintzi, 2009](#page-12-61)). Down-regulated genes also included an Sadenosylmethionine synthase gene that encodes an enzyme involved in ethylene and polyamine biosynthesis. Ethylene and polyamine production is low in dormant tubers, but increases as tubers sprout [\(Kaur-](#page-11-49)[Sawhney et al., 1982](#page-11-49); [Suttle, 2007](#page-12-0)). Ethylene was found to negatively regulate cold acclimation-induced freezing tolerance in *Medicago truncatula* seedlings [\(Zhao et al., 2014\)](#page-13-15) and may be related to low temperature conditions in storage. Down-regulation of the allene oxide synthase gene was also found. Allene oxide synthase functions to convert fatty acid hydroperoxides in the biosynthesis of jasmonic acid (JA), suggesting that JA is reduced with cold storage in tubers.

The genes showing down-regulation after cold storage were enriched for the term "L-phenylalanine catabolic process" for all cultivars except Summer Delight. All the genes annotated for this term were phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), which is a gateway enzyme involved in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. PAL is associated with defence in potato ([Joos and Hahlbrock, 1992](#page-11-50)). PAL is also involved in lignin biosynthesis [\(Rogers and Campbell, 2004\)](#page-12-62). The results indicate that potato tubers under cold storage may have decreased capacity to produce phenylpropanoids and defense responses. There was also evidence for-down regulation of a gene involved in sulfur assimilation in 15329-03, 15323-04, 15328-26, 575.04.01, and Doremi but not Crop 20, Summer Delight and Lady Claire. Sulfur is required for synthesis of amino acids methionine and cysteine, and the antioxidant glutathione. All of the cultivars also had decreased expression of S-adnosylmethionie synthase, which produces a precurosor for ethylene biosynthesis, S adenosine from Met and ATP [\(Iqbal et al., 2013](#page-11-51)). The results of the current study suggest a coordinated decrease in sulfur assimilation and ethylene biosynthesis in cold stored tubers.

# **5. Conclusion**

Gene expression analysis of similarities and differences in development, physiology and biochemistry between eight cultivars during cold storage has contributed to understanding biological processes underlying CIS resistance. The study demonstrated that genetic variation in chilling injury and cold acclimation response play a role in the production of glucose in tubers during cold storage and in CIS resistance.
