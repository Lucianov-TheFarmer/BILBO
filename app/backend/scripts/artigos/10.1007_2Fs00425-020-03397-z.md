## **Abstract**

## *Main conclusion* **A frst salt shock event alters transcriptional and physiological responses to a second event, being possible to identify 26 genes associated with long-term memory.**

**Abstract** Soil salinity signifcantly afects rice cultivation, resulting in large losses in growth and productivity. Studies report that a disturbing event can prepare the plant for a subsequent event through memory acquisition, involving physiological and molecular processes. Therefore, genes that provide altered responses in subsequent events defne a category known as "memory genes". In this work, the RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) technique was used to analyse the transcriptional profle of rice plants subjected to diferent salt shock events and to characterise genes associated with long-term memory. Plants subjected to recurrent salt shock showed diferences in stomatal conductance, chlorophyll index, electrolyte leakage, and the number of diferentially expressed genes (DEGs), and they had lower Na+/K+ ratios than plants that experienced only one stress event. Additionally, the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathways, and carbohydrate and amino acidassociated pathways were altered under all conditions. Memory genes can be classifed according to their responses during the frst event (+ or −) and the second shock event (+ or −), being possible to observe a larger number of transcripts for groups [+/−] and [−/+], genes characterised as "revised response." This is the frst long-term transcriptional memory study in rice plants under salt shock, providing new insights into the process of plant memory acquisition.

**Keywords** Memory genes · *Oryza sativa* L. · Recurrent stress · Revised response genes

## **Introduction**

Soil salinisation is one of the main environmental factors that negatively infuences productivity, which can substantially reduce food production, particularly for sensitive crops such as rice (Munns and Tester [2008](#page-15-0); Ismail and Horie [2017\)](#page-14-0). Approximately, 6.5% of the world's total area and about 45 million hectares of irrigated land are afected by excess salt, and this is expected to increase due to climate change and irrigation practices (Rengasamy [2010](#page-15-1)). Yield reductions of one-third can occur when rice is grown at an electrical conductivity (EC) of 2 dS/m<sup>2</sup> and halved at 4 dS/ m2 , and these are considered low and moderate salinity stresses, respectively. Above 8 dS/m<sup>2</sup> is considered a severe stress, and can be lethal to the plant (Lutts et al. [1995;](#page-14-1) Grattan et al. [2002](#page-14-2); Thitisaksakul et al. [2015\)](#page-15-2).

In experiments with high concentrations of NaCl, there are two ways of exposing the plants: (1) gradual application of NaCl, twice daily, until a fnal, predetermined salt

concentration is reached, also known as 'salt-adapting' (Baisakh et al. [2006\)](#page-13-0); (2) and salt shock, when plants are suddenly transferred from normal growth solution (without NaCl) into solution containing high concentrations of NaCl (Shavrukov [2013;](#page-15-3) Tani et al. [2018\)](#page-15-4), as is the case in this study. Adverse efects of saline stress have two main causes: frst, osmotic stress reduces root water absorption, which occurs within minutes to days, causing stomatal closure and inhibition of cell expansion; the second phase occurs over days or weeks, causing the accumulation of salts (ionic toxicity) that impair metabolic processes, causing premature senescence or even cell death (Munns and Tester [2008](#page-15-0); Gilroy et al. [2014](#page-14-3); Roy et al. [2014\)](#page-15-5). However in salt shock, hyperosmotic stress is the main component, due to large differences in osmotic pressure between solutes contained in the cell cytoplasm and solutes outside the cell cytoplasm (Munns [2002](#page-15-6); Shavrukov [2013](#page-15-3)). Cell plasmolysis and osmolyte leakage occur under osmotic stress, phenomena that do not appear under ionic stress, indicating that gene expression is diferent in response to stress and salt shock (Shavrukov [2013](#page-15-3)).

Plants respond to salt shock by dynamically changing their physiological behaviour and by modulating a complex gene network (Tani et al. [2018;](#page-15-4) Skorupa et al. [2019](#page-15-7)). After the stress has ceased, the plants redefne their metabolism; however, this recovery process may not be complete and there is retention of "information" about the disturbance suffered, a process called "priming". Stimuli from pathogens, herbivores, as well as chemicals and abiotic cues, can trigger the establishment of priming by acting as warning signals (Mauch-Mani et al. [2017](#page-14-4)). Several studies report that a frst stressful event (priming) can prepare the plant for a subsequent stressor, usually causing a faster or more intense response through memory acquisition (Liu et al. [2014](#page-14-5); Conrath et al. [2015;](#page-14-6) Crisp et al. [2016;](#page-14-7) Hilker et al. [2016](#page-14-8); Lämke and Bäurle [2017\)](#page-14-9). Bruce et al. ([2007](#page-13-1)) defned memory as a genetic or biochemical modifcation of a plant that occurs after exposure to stress, making the responses to subsequent stressful events diferent. The memory of the frst (priming) stress and the recovery of the information remembered after the second stressful event are necessary if there is a prolonged period of absence of stress between the two events (Hilker and Schmülling [2019\)](#page-14-10).

Studies show that plants undergoing dehydration/recovery cycles have a reduced rate of water loss after subsequent stress by controlling stomatal opening, a process called physiological memory (Ding et al. [2014](#page-14-11); Virlouvet and Fromm [2015\)](#page-15-8). Additionally, other processes such as relative water content (RWC), leaf mass per area ratio (LMA), maximum efciency of photosystem II (Fv/Fm ratio) and total amount of chlorophylls (Chl *a*+*b*) were also altered in plants subjected to recurrent dehydration stress (Fleta-Soriano and Munné-Bosch [2016](#page-14-12)). Regarding NaCl stress, Hu et al. ([2016\)](#page-14-13) demonstrated that ryegrass (*Lolium perenne*) plants submitted to the subsequent elevated salt concentration treatment showed lower electrolyte leakage and minimum H2O2 and O2 −.

Gene expression changes may occur, revealing the existence of memory genes, a concept called "transcriptional stress memory" (Ding et al. [2012](#page-14-14), [2014;](#page-14-11) Guedes et al. [2018](#page-14-15)). The criterion for classifying transcriptional memory is that the level of transcription of response genes in subsequent stresses (S2 and S3), after a frst stress recovery period (R1), should be signifcantly diferent from the transcription levels produced during the frst stress (S1), despite similar stress levels and durations (Ding et al. [2013](#page-14-16)). Therefore, memory genes have altered expression to subsequent stress, whereas non-memory genes respond similarly to each stress event (Ding et al. [2012](#page-14-14); Avramova [2015\)](#page-13-2).

Despite the growing number of studies related to transcriptional memory in rice plants (Goswami et al. [2010](#page-14-17); Auler et al. [2017a,](#page-13-3) [b](#page-13-4); Li et al. [2019](#page-14-18)), none of them show this process in response to subsequent exposure to salinity shock. Additionally, most experiments were restricted to time intervals of less than 1 week; however, there is evidence that this memory event lasts longer (Bruce et al. [2007](#page-13-1)). The present study analysed the transcriptional behaviour of rice plants subjected to recurrent saline shock at diferent developmental stages (vegetative and reproductive) to identify genes involved in memory at the transcriptional level. These genes were classifed as "long-term memory-related genes" because the time interval between a frst event and subsequent was longer than 1 week.

#### **Materials and methods**

#### **Plant material and salt shock**

Rice seeds, genotype BRS Ligeirinho (*Oryza sativa* L. ssp. *Indica*; seeds obtained from Empresa Brasileira de Pesquisa Agropecuária, Rio Grande do Sul, Brasil), were germinated in a paper roll in a BOD type growth chamber (Model 202, EletroLab, São Paulo, Brazil), with a photoperiod of 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness and a temperature of 25±2 °C, and they were kept there for 7 days. After this period, the seedlings were transferred to 8-L plastic pots, drilled at the base to ensure perfect percolation of water. As substrate, sand previously washed with water and 1% hydrochloric acid were used. The plants were kept in a greenhouse with 70% relative humidity, a temperature of 25±2 °C, and daily irrigation, alternated with water and nutrient solution of Hoagland and Arnon [\(1950](#page-14-19)) until reaching the V4 stage (collar formation on the forth leaf), when they were exposed to salt shock conditions.

At the V4 stage, the plants were separated into two groups. The frst group was irrigated with water and nutrient solution, while the second group was exposed to salt shock by adding the nutrient solution with 150 mM NaCl (17.10 dS m−1) for 48 h (frst shock event). After this period, the plants received normal irrigation, with alternating water and nutrient solution, until reaching the reproductive stage.

In the reproductive period, R0–R1 (panicle initiation), the plants were again separated into two groups: the frst group received water and nutrient solution, while the second group was exposed to salt shock by the addition of nutrient solution plus 150 mM of NaCl (second shock event) for 48 h. Therefore, the four conditions were the following: (I) control (C): plants received irrigation with water and nutrient solution throughout the experiment; (II) vegetative (SV), plants were exposed to saline shock only at the vegetative stage; (III) reproductive (SR) plants were exposed to saline shock only at the reproductive stage; and (IV) vegetative+productive (SV+R), plants were exposed to saline shock at the vegetative stage (frst shock event) and at the reproductive stage (recurrent event) (Fig. [1](#page-2-0)).

Samples for analysis of RNA-seq, real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), electrolyte leakage, and Na+ and K+ concentration were collected after 48 h of salt shock exposure in the reproductive stage. Stomatal conductance and chlorophyll index analyses were performed after 6 h, 24 h, and 48 h of salt shock in the reproductive stage. All analyses and collections were performed in the middle portions of young and expanded leaves.

#### **Na and K concentration**

The plant material was dried for at least 48 h in an oven at 80 °C and incubated in a 2:1 (v/v) solution of nitric acid (HNO3) and perchloric acid (HClO4) to extract the ions. Na+ and K+ concentrations were measured by a fame spectrophotometer (Micronal B361, Ribeirão Preto, Brazil) (mg g−1 DW). For this analysis, leaves and roots of the four conditions after salt shock for 48 h in the reproductive stage were used.

#### **Physiological parameters**

Stomatal conductance (gS) was measured for 30 s on the median portion of the abaxial face of the last expanded leaf using a steady-state leaf porometer (LI-1600, Li-Cor Bioscience, Lincoln, NE, USA). The chlorophyll index (Chl) was analysed using a Portable Leaf Monitoring Sensor (Dualex Scientifc+—ForceA). Two readings were taken on all plants of each condition for both parameters. The electrolyte leakage from the cells was measured by cutting three 1-cm<sup>2</sup> leaf discs, which were washed and incubated in 20 mL of deionised water for 24 h at room temperature. After this incubation, the leaf discs were autoclaved for 20 min. The conductivity of the solutions was measured using a conductivity meter (WTW GmbH, Weilheim, Germany) (Lutts et al. [1995\)](#page-14-1) and calculated using the following formula: EL=*C*1/*C*2×100, where *C*1 is the initial conductivity and *C*2 is the conductivity after autoclaving.

#### **RNA extraction and construction of RNA‑seq libraries**

Collected leaf tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using Trizol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and treated with DNase I (Invitrogen). RNA integrity and quality were confrmed with a NanoDrop ND-1000® spectrophotometer (GE Healthcare) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Libraries were prepared with the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation® V2 (Illumina) kit, and library quality was assessed on an Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer® (Agilent Technologies) using the Agilent DNA 1000® (Agilent) kit. Two replicates per treatment were paired-end (2×100 bp) sequenced in a HiSeq 2500® platform sequencer (Illumina). RNA-seq data for *O. sativa* cv. BRS Ligeirinho can be downloaded from NCBI BioProjects.

#### **Bioinformatics analysis**

For quality analysis of the reads, FastQC Ver. 0.11.7 software and later the Trimmomatic Ver. 0:35 program (Bolger et al. [2014](#page-13-5)) were used to remove adapters and poor-quality bases in each library. The reads were mapped against the *O. sativa* cv. Nipponbare (IRGSP build 1.0) from the MSU Rice Genome Annotation Project (RGAP 7) Database using STAR Ver. 2.7 software (Dobin et al. [2013\)](#page-14-20). HTSeq Ver. 0.11.1 software (Anders et al. [2015\)](#page-13-6) was used to count mapped reads.

Identifcation of diferentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed in the R Ver. 3.5.2 statistical environment with the edgeR Ver. 3.8 package (Robinson et al. [2010](#page-15-9)), obtained from the Bioconductor repository (Gentleman et al. [2004](#page-14-21)). The raw reads were transformed to counts per million (CPM), keeping the genes with CPM values>1, and then normalised by the trimmed mean of M values method (TMM), considering DEGs with false discovery rates (FDRs) of <0.05 by the edgeR test.

The DEGs were subsequently subjected to enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG metabolic pathways. The MSU Ver. 7 annotation fle and a custom Fisher exact test script in the R language were used. Enrichment analysis was conducted separately for up- and downregulated DEGs and was considered to be overrepresented by the FDR <0.05.

To identify genes related to transcriptional memory, the method described by Ding et al. [\(2013\)](#page-14-16) was used. Briefy, the genes responsive to salinity (SV\*Control) was determined, and this group was compared to genes diferentially expressed in SV+R versus SV (SV\*V+R). A simple classifcation was given to response types in plants that underwent salt shock in SV (+, −, or =) and another classifcation for plants that underwent in SV+R (+, −, or =), combining them into the following four classes: [+/+], [−/−], [+/−], and [−/+]. Two additional classes, [+/=] and [−/=], correspond to non-memory-related genes, and two other classes, [=/+] and [=/−], correspond to late-response genes. A list of the DEGs in each condition and genes related to transcriptional memory are in the Supplementary material S1.

Functional relationships and protein–protein interaction between DEGs of each condition were extracted from the STRING Ver. 10 database (Szklarczyk et al. [2015](#page-15-10)) for the *O. sativa* species. The interactions obtained from the STRING database were downloaded, and the networks were built in Cytoscape Ver. 3.7.1 (Shannon et al. [2003\)](#page-15-11) for the diferent conditions. The degree of interaction ("degree centrality") parameters, which measure the number of node interactions with other nodes, and the degree betweenness centrality (BC), measuring the number of shortest paths using a given node as intermediate, were computed for the nodes of each network by the NetworkAnalyzer Ver. 2.7 package (Assenov et al. [2008](#page-13-7)).

To describe numerically the DEGs profles and perform a relationship analysis, we created a bar chart representing the total number of DEGs by its regulation, up or down-regulated, using the ggplot2 package in R. Additionally, a Venn diagram of all DEGs was created using a Venn web tool [\(https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/\)](https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/); and a heatmap was created using the ComplexHeatmap package in R (10.18129/B9.bioc.ComplexHeatmap) with the following parameters: clustered and labels by memory genes.

#### **Reverse transcription and RT‑qPCR**

Reverse transcription was performed from 1 µg of total RNA using the iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR kit (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA). RT-qPCR was performed using BioRad CFX and the GoTaq® qPCR Master Mix kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) containing BRYT Green® Dye fuorophore. RT-qPCR was performed using the following parameters: 95 °C for 10 min; 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s; and 60 °C for 1 min, with melting curve insertion at 65–95 °C and an increase of 0.5 °C each cycle (Fig. S1). Relative quantitation of expression for each gene was obtained using the 2−∆∆Ct method (Livak and Schmittgen [2001](#page-14-22)), and the *UBQ10* and *UBC-E2* genes were used as reference genes (Moraes et al. [2015](#page-14-23); Auler et al. [2017b](#page-13-4)). The primers used in the RT-qPCR reactions are listed in Table S1.

#### **Experimental design and statistical analysis**

The experimental design was completely randomised (CRD), containing four treatments, represented by the salt shock conditions to which the plants were subjected for 48 h, with three replications each (six plants per replicate). For the stomatal conductance and chlorophyll content parameters, the design was completely randomised in a 4×3 factorial scheme, with four salt shock conditions and three times (6 h, 24 h and 48 h) totalling 12 treatments (readings on two leaves from each plant). For the Na+ and K+ content, the design was completely randomised in a 4×2 factorial scheme, with four salt shock conditions and two tissues (six plants per replicate).

The experimental units consisted of three pots with six plants in each. Data were submitted to analysis of variance (ANOVA) with *P*≤0.05, and means were compared by the Tukey test at 5% probability. Statistical analyses were performed using the R V. 3.5.2 software (R Core Team [2018\)](#page-15-12).

#### **Results**

To determine if the plants retained memory of the previous salt shock, BRS Ligeirinho rice plants were subjected to a high salt concentration solution (150 mM) at diferent stages of development.

#### **Na and K concentration**

Salt tolerance in plants is generally associated with low absorption and accumulation of Na+ compared to K+ ions. Plants develop various physiological and biochemical mechanisms in order to survive in soils with high salt concentration, including efcient sequestration of cytosolic Na<sup>+</sup> to vacuoles, accumulation of compatible solutes, greater cytosolic K+ retention, and efcient control of xylem ion loading and unloading (Gupta and Huang [2014](#page-14-24); Ismail and

Horie [2017](#page-14-0)). The genotype used in this study is considered sensitive to salinity; however, its mechanisms of ion absorption/exclusion are unknown. Therefore, the concentrations of Na+ and K+ ions were determined in leaves and roots of rice plants subjected to 48 h of salt shock at diferent developmental stages. According to the statistical analysis, it is possible to observe signifcant interactions between the conditions and the tissues analysed (*P*≤0.05) for both cations. For Na+, the highest concentration in the roots occurred in plants that underwent recurrent shock (SV+R), followed by plants that underwent saline shock only in the SR, while control and SV plants had the lowest concentrations. Regarding the leaf, there was no diference between the conditions. Comparing the two tissues, the control and SV conditions showed no diference between root and leaf, while SR and SV+R showed higher root accumulation (Fig. [2](#page-4-0)a).

For the K+ concentration in the roots, there was a decrease only in the SR, with values that were approximately fvefold smaller than the others conditions. In the leaves, there was a similar result to those found in the roots, with the lowest concentration in the SR (14.38 mg g–1 DW); however, there was also a decrease in SV and SV+R, with the highest value for control plants (20.31 mg g–1 DW). Comparing the two tissues, the leaves showed higher K+ accumulation under all conditions (Fig. [2b](#page-4-0)).

Initially, physiological and transcriptional responses of plants subjected to salt shock during diferent stages were characterised, with the following four conditions considered: control (no salt shock), plants that sufered salt shock only in vegetative (SV), plants that sufered salt shock only in reproductive (SR), and plants that underwent salt shock in these two stages (SV+R). Subsequently, we identifed genes related to long-term memory to salt shock.

Plant growth was reduced due to exposure to high salt concentration through several distinct processes, which were related to Na+ accumulation in toxic amounts. However, to overcome this adverse condition, plants have developed several mechanisms. Among these mechanisms are the exclusion of some of the Na+ from the soil, in which plants are able to accumulate other ions that will serve to maintain metabolism and osmotic adjustment, with K+ being the main one (Munns and Tester [2008](#page-15-0); Ali and Yun [2017](#page-13-8)). It was observed in the present work that plants that underwent recurrent salt shock, and only in the reproductive period, accumulated a greater amount of Na<sup>+</sup> in the roots in relation to the other conditions, while in the leaves there was no diference. However, it can be observed that plants of the SV+R condition presented higher K+ concentrations in the roots compared to the Reproductive condition, demonstrating that plants that underwent previous salt shock can maintain a lower Na+/K+ ratio than plants that underwent shock only in the Reproductive, indicating a physiological memory mechanism. In the leaves, there was also similar behaviour, with higher K+ content in plants that underwent recurrent salt shock in relation to the SR condition (Fig. [2\)](#page-4-0). Studies report that several salt tolerant rice cultivars maintain a low Na+/K+ ratio due to the maintenance of potassium content (Koyama et al. [2001](#page-14-25); Ren et al. [2005;](#page-15-13) Zhang et al. [2010](#page-15-14)). In our study, this response was presented by plants that underwent two salt shock events (recurrent salt shock), demonstrating a more efcient ionic adjustment. Similar results have been reported for *Lolium perene* plants submitted to various salt stress events, in which pre-trained plants maintained higher K+ concentrations in both roots and leaves, maintaining a lower Na+/K+ ratio (Hu et al. [2016\)](#page-14-13).

In the frst phase of salt shock, there is a reduction in the osmotic potential of the soil/nutrient solution, causing a rapid decrease in stomatal conductance (Maggio etal. [2007](#page-14-26); Munns and Tester [2008\)](#page-15-0). A reduction in stomatal conductance was observed only after 48 h of salt shock in SV+R and SR conditions, with no memory efect for this parameter (Fig. [3](#page-5-0)a). Due to the low Na+ content in the leaves of all conditions, this reduction was due to the osmotic efect of salt shock. Several studies report this efect of the osmotic component of salinity reducing the stomatal conductance (Jiang et al. [2006](#page-14-27); Moradi and Ismail [2007\)](#page-14-28).

Additionally, high salt concentrations cause premature leaf senescence, leading to the degradation of several molecules, such as chlorophyll and lipids, in addition to destabilising membranes (Thompson et al. [1998;](#page-15-15) James et al. [2002](#page-14-29); Munns and Tester [2008](#page-15-0)). These events are usually associated with high Na+ content in leaves; however, despite the low Na+ content in the plants in this study, there was a reduction in chlorophyll content (48 h only) and an increase in electrolyte leakage in plants that underwent reproductive salt shock, whereas plants that had recurrent shock showed similar responses to the other two conditions (Fig. [2](#page-4-0)b, c), showing a physiological memory efect as a consequence of a frst salt shock event. Hu et al. ([2016](#page-14-13)) reported that after repeated stress/recovery events, pre-trained ryegrass plants had lower electrolyte leakage values than untrained plants during the 8 days of saline stress, with the maximum diference achieved during 4 days of stress, indicating that pre-treatment strengthened cell membrane stability.

#### **Diferentially expressed genes and functional analysis**

In addition to physiological changes, salt shock responses in rice plants are coordinated by multiple genes, which also interact with other transduction pathway components (Shavrukov [2013;](#page-15-3) do Amaral et al. [2016](#page-14-30); Reddy et al. [2017](#page-15-16); Tani et al. [2018](#page-15-4); Skorupa et al. [2019\)](#page-15-7). Diferences in expression patterns were demonstrated in the three conditions, in which plants that received salt shock only in Vegetative presented a lower number of DEGs, with more than 78% being negatively regulated. It was possible to observe that plants that underwent a previous shock event presented a lower number of DEGs compared to plants that had shock only in the Reproductive, demonstrating a transcriptional memory efect (Figs. [4](#page-6-1), [5\)](#page-7-0). Similar results were reported by Guedes et al. [\(2018\)](#page-14-15), in which two cofee cultivars presented a higher number of DEGs in plants subjected to a drought stress cycle and a smaller number in plants subjected to three cycles.

Functional analysis of GOs terms demonstrated terms enriched for SV\*C and SR\*C conditions, and only for down-regulated genes. Among the terms of GOs stand out response to a biotic stimulus (GO: 0009607) for both conditions and catalytic activity (GO: 0003824), response to stress (GO: 0006950), and response to an abiotic stimulus (GO: 0009628) for SR\*C. Several studies report these terms of GOs involved in salinity tolerance in rice (Baldoni et al. [2016](#page-13-9); Wang et al. [2016;](#page-15-17) Suratanee et al. [2018](#page-15-18)), which may explain the sensitivity of this genotype to salt stress.

Regarding KEGG-enriched metabolic pathways, the mTOR signalling pathway appeared under the three conditions for both up- and down-regulated genes, demonstrating that it is one of the major pathways involved in salt shock responses (Fig. [6\)](#page-9-0). The mTOR pathway is conserved in

[2011;](#page-14-31) Xiong and Sheen [2012\)](#page-15-19). Several pathways related to amino acid and carbohydrate metabolism were also enriched under the three conditions, demonstrating that despite the stage of development or the number of shock events, these pathways play a key role in salt shock. Metabolic adaptation is crucial for tolerance in plants, and studies clearly demonstrate that protein and carbohydrate metabolism play very important roles in the response to excess NaCl (Rai [2002](#page-15-20); Zhang et al. [2017;](#page-15-21) Hildebrandt [2018\)](#page-14-32).

#### **Classifcation of memory genes**

After a general analysis of physiological processes and molecular responses in each condition, an analysis was performed to characterise memory genes, based on the methodology described by Ding et al. ([2013\)](#page-14-16), who characterised them into the following four groups: [+/+], [−/−], [+/−], and [−/+]. This author characterised transcriptional memory as genes that respond to a frst stress event and alter their expression in a subsequent stress, allowing the plant to fnetune its responses to recurrent stress. Only one gene was classifed into the group [+/+], which was the *zinc fnger family protein* (LOC\_Os06g04920) (Fig. [7a](#page-10-0)). The zinc fnger family protein (ZFP) comprises a large family of plant proteins that are primarily involved in abiotic stress tolerance, regulation of sodium and potassium homeostasis, elimination of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and regulation of osmotic potential (Shankar et al. [2013](#page-15-22); Zang et al. [2016\)](#page-15-23).

For the six genes in the [+/−] group, fve are not yet characterised (expressed proteins) genes, demonstrating the importance of further functional gene characterisation studies, mainly associated with transcriptional memory (Fig. [5\)](#page-7-0). Although not yet characterised, among these fve expressed proteins, four of them have been shown to be involved in abiotic stress in rice (Childs et al. [2011](#page-13-10); Shaik and Ramakrishna [2014](#page-15-24); Wilkins et al. [2016](#page-15-25)). The other gene belonging to this group, the *SPX domain containing protein* (LOC\_Os06g40120), is involved in the regulation of stress involving Pi (phosphate), as well as cold stress; however, little is known about its functions in salt shock or saline stress (Wang et al. [2009](#page-15-26), [2013\)](#page-15-27).

The most prominent group was genes that had low expression levels during the frst salt shock event but increased their expression in recurrent salt shock ([−/+]) (Fig. [5](#page-7-0)). In this group, four transcription factors were found, which are involved in abiotic stress responses (Abiri et al. [2017](#page-13-11); Sperotto et al. [2018\)](#page-15-28). Among them, two are from the AP2/ ERF superfamily (*AP2 domain containing protein*—LOC\_ Os08g36920 and *dehydration-responsive element-binding*  *protein*—LOC\_Os02g45450) and present the most contrasting expression values, indicating that they are strong candidates for metabolism remodelling after recurrent event. Analysing the PPI network for the SV\*C comparison, it was observed that *AP2 domain containing protein* (LOC\_Os08g36920) and *cytochrome 450*, two memory genes showed high degree values and were considered hubs, and therefore important for the memory acquisition process (Fig. [7a](#page-10-0)).

Also in this group were other abiotic stress-related genes, including three DUFs (LOC\_Os01g32770, LOC\_ Os04g4966,0 and LOC\_Os04g49670), a *stress-induced protein* (LOC\_Os03g56220), *a heavy metal transport and detoxifcation protein* (LOC\_Os04g57200), and a *MAPK2* (OsMSR) (Nischal et al. [2012](#page-15-29); Shankar et al. [2013;](#page-15-22) Shaar-Moshe et al. [2015](#page-15-30)). Analysing the PPI network for SV\*SV+R comparison (Fig. [7b](#page-10-0)), *OsMSRMK2* obtained a high value of betweenness centrality, having importance for connecting modules within the network. MAPK plays a crucial role in response to high salt concentrations in rice, and it is a key molecule in the signal transduction process (Kumar et al. [2013](#page-14-33)). Agrawal et al. ([2002\)](#page-13-12) demonstrated that *OsMSRMK2* had no constitutive expression in rice leaves; however, it was induced within 15 min by cutting wounds, signalling molecules, protein phosphatase inhibitors, ultraviolet irradiation, fungal elicitor, heavy metals, salt, sucrose and drought, suggesting an important role in defence/stress response processes.

These last two groups, [+/−] and [−/+], were classifed by Ding et al. ([2013\)](#page-14-16) as "revised response" genes because they "revise" their initial behaviour after recurrent stress. In the present study, genes related to long-term memory in rice plants subjected to salt shock were classifed mainly into these two groups, making it possible to highlight several genes involved in abiotic stress responses, transcription factors, and one MAPK. Genes of "revised response" can play a key role in cellular metabolism adjustment to suit the new conditions (Ding et al. [2014\)](#page-14-11).

#### **Conclusion**

From the parameters evaluated in the present work, it was possible to conclude that an initial salt shock event causes changes in physiological and transcriptional responses, such as Na+ and K+ concentration, stomatal conductance, electrolyte leakage, and diferent numbers of DEGs, in rice plants when they are subjected to a second event, through memory acquisition (Fig. [8](#page-13-13)). We identifed 26 genes associated with long-term memory, classifying them into three groups ([+/+], [+/−], and [−/+]) according to their expression patterns, where the importance of
