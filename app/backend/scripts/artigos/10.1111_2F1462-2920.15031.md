**Running title**: Fruit sugar content affects pathogenic interaction

**Originality-Significance Statement**: Host sugar in fruit modulates differential pathogen colonization and host response to fungal attack.

#### **Summary**

*Colletotrichum gloeosporioides* and *Penicillium expansum* cause postharvest diseases in tropical and deciduous fruit. During colonization, *C. gloeosporioides* and *P. expansum* secrete ammonia in hosts with low sugar content (LowSC) and gluconic acid in hosts with high sugar content (HighSC), respectively, as a mechanism to modulate enhanced pathogenicity. We studied the pathogens interactions with tomato lines of similar genetic background but differing in their sugar content. *C. gloeosporioides* showed enhanced colonization of the LowSC line with differential expression response of 15% of its genes including enhanced relative expression of glycosyl hydrolases, glucanase and MFS-transporter genes. Enhanced colonization of *P. expansum* occurred in the HighSC line, accompanied by an increase in carbohydrate metabolic processes mainly phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, and only 4% of differentially expressed genes. Gene response of the two host lines strongly differed depending on the sugar level. Limited colonization of HighSC line by *C. gloeosporioides* was accompanied by a marked alteration of gene expression compared the LowSC response to the same pathogen; while colonization by *P. expansum* resulted in a similar response of the two different hosts. We suggest that this differential pattern of fungal/host responses may be the basis for the differential of host range of both pathogens in nature.

#### **Introduction**

Postharvest pathogens may penetrate uripe host during fruit growth, harvest and storage but develop and show symptoms in mature fully ripe fruit. The lenght of the lag time needed by the infecting pathogen to activate their metabolic processes, colonize the ripening tissue and show symptom expression may change in different host pathogen/fruit interactions (Prusky et al., 2013). In some cases as for *Pencillium* attack, the intiation of fungal colonization and necrotrophic development occur very close to fungal wound penetration after harvest. In other cases, the stage of fungal germination and penetration is followed by a longer period of fungal interaction with its host, as observed in *Colletotrichum*, where a biotrophic/quiescent interactions proceed to the necrotrophic phase when the fruit is ripen after harvest (Prusky et al., 2013).

Studies on the modulation of host and pathogen metabolic changes that contribute to the activation of colonization in mature-ripe fruit has been the basis of many reserach work (Prusky et al., 2013). Early reports suggested that four factors modulate the activation of biotrophic/quiescent interactions into necrotrophic phase: (*a*) induced accessibility of disassembled cell wall substrates during fruit softening (Cantu et al., 2008) and ethylene induction (Giovannoni, 2001); (*b*) a decline in preformed antifungal compounds, such as polyphenols and other preformed fungitoxic substances and inducible phytoalexins(Prusky et al., 2013); (*c*) the decline of inducible host-defense responses (Beno-Moualem and Prusky, 2000); and (*d* ) the pH and carbon availability conditions in the host (Prusky et al., 2016) that contribute to pathogen induced colonization. pH modulation by the pathogen has been suggested as one of the key factor for modulation of colonization by the pathogen in ripe fruit (Prusky et al., 2013). In fruit, the initial host pH varies from less than 4 to over 6, but pathogens may also contribute to its increase, to as high as 8.0 or reduce it to as low as 3.2 (Prusky and Yakoby, 2003).

In recent years, a link between carbon availability and pH modulation of host tissue by postharvest pathogens has become evident. Study with several fungal pathogens indicated that host sugar content is a major factor that determines the fungal response, whether alkalinizing or acidifying the environment (Bi et al., 2016; Prusky et al., 2016). While excess of sugar content in the fruit contribute to environmental acidification, via oxidation of glucose to GLA by glucose oxidase 2 (*gox2*) in acidifying pathogens, it can enhance also acidification in pathogenic organisms that are classified as having an alkalizing lifestyle. In contrast, alkalization is dominant under conditions of carbon deprivation and is catalyzed by deamination of amino acid glutamate, by glutamate dehydrogenase 2 (Alkan et al., 2013a; Alkan et al., 2013b; Ment et al., 2015; Bi et al., 2016). Thus, the sugar level of fruits is emerging as an important determinant influencing postharvest host-pathogen interactions.

Induction of alkaline-expressed and/or acid-repressed fungal genes was found as a very efficient mechanism for activating pathogenicity of both the necrotrophic, *Penicillium* (Barad et al., 2016b) and hemibiotrophic, *Colletotrichum*, pathogens (Barad et al., 2017). In both cases, the highly conserved pH-sensor system, mediated by the zinc finger transcription factor PACC (Espeso and Arst, 2000; Miyara et al., 2008; Alkan et al., 2013a; Alkan et al., 2013b), regulate genes expressed under alkaline and acidic conditions thereby creating conditions for enhanced pathogenicity (Alkan et al., 2013b). In line with this, numerous PACC-regulated genes were shown to be required for full pathogenicity. This includes the pectate lyase *pelB* in *C. gloeosporioides* (Yakoby et al., 2000), polygalacturonases *pg1* and *pg5* in *Fusarium oxysporum* (Caracuel et al., 2003), endoglucanases in *Alternaria alternata* (Eshel et al., 2002), polygalacturonase (Prusky et al., 2004) and glucose oxidase *gox2* (Yakoby et al., 2000; Prusky and Yakoby, 2003; Vylkova et al., 2011; Prusky et al., 2013) in *Penicillium expansum*.

Fungal response to carbon availability is dependent on several signaling pathways, which are involved in carbon catabolite repression (CCR). CCR is a wellstudied mechanism that enables microorganisms to rewire their metabolism and adapt their physiology to changes of carbon availability in their environment. CCR in filamentous fungi is mediated by the zinc-finger transcription factor CreA, along with additional elements that play a crucial role in sensing sugar availability and transducing the signal to alter diverse cellular functions (Adnan et al., 2018). These elements include several hexokinases, cAMP, Protein Kinase (PKA), Ras proteins, G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), adenylate cyclase, RcoA and SnfA (Adnan et al., 2018). Some of these elements and specifically CreA showed to be involved in PACC/pH regulation of pathogenicity machinery (Bi et al., 2016; McCotter et al., 2016). Furthermore, these two transcription factors, CreA and PacC co-regulate the expression of genes that are required for pathogenicity in *P. expansum*, (Poussereau et al., 2001; McCotter et al., 2016; Tannous et al., 2018). Thus, an intimate crosstalk between CREA/carbon availability and PacC/pH regulation, play a significant role in the adaptive response of pathogens to the changes that occur in the chemical composition of their host tissue.

Sugar level in fruit is dynamically changing during ripening in the field as well as in storage. During ripening, most fruit will accumulate simple sugars like hexoses as result of macromolecules degradation (like starch or cell wall). Thus, postharvest pathogenic fungi are required to sense, modulate and adapt to their changing host / environment in order to survive and reproduce (Biswas et al., 2007). While fruit sugars modulate the expression and secretion of pathogenicity factors by the fungus (Bi et al., 2016), they also serve as carbon nutrients for pathogens thus enhancing their pathogenicity. Thus, one should expect that the higher sugar level in fruit would enhance fungal virulence in postharvest pathogens. However, this effect is not directly related to fruit susceptibility in some *Botrytis cinerea* host interactions (Cantu et al., 2009). Tomato NOR transcription factor was shown to be involved in ripening processes including accumulation of hexoses, carotenoid compounds and organic acids (Osorio et al., 2011). While, fully ripe NOR mutants, accumulates less sugars and they showed significantly increased fruit resistance to *B. cinerea* (Cantu et al., 2009). On the other hand, Cnr mutant fruits, which are very susceptible to *B. cinerea*, contain lower levels of sugars (Blanco-Ulate et al., 2010). Thus, while it is becoming clear that carbon source availability is an important factor that modulate pathogenic interactions, the specific role of carbon availability per se was not investigated thoroughly *in vivo.*

Fruit differs widely in their levels of soluble sugars—with sucrose, glucose and fructose representing more than 75–80% of total soluble solids (TSS) (Hulme, 1971; Prusky, 1996). Upon ripening, fruit metabolize polysaccharides to sugars to achieve as high as 25% TSS; in this case, the concentration of sucrose may reach up to 10–12% (Hulme, 1971; Prusky, 1996). The wide carbohydrate metabolic responses during ripening fruit led us to analyze the transcriptomic and metabolomic responses of tomato lines of similar genetic background with phenotypes sharply differing in their sugar content on the colonization abilities of *P. expansum*, a necrotrophic and *C. gloeosporioides*, an hemibiotrophic-postharvest pathogen. This approach enabled a global transcriptomic and metabolomic study of the contribution of plant genotypes differing in fruit sugar concentration on the differential pathogenicity of *C. gloeosporioides* and *P. expansum* contributing to the understanding of the host range of both pathogens in nature.

#### **Results and discussion**

*Analysis of TSS, pH, acidity and susceptibility of High and LowSC tomato fruit colonized by* C. gloeosporioides *and* P. expansum

Two tomato lines, with HighSC (12.33% TSS) and LowSC (7.33% TSS) fruit were compared. TSS levels measured for the HighSC and LowSC fruit were largely correlated with the glucose and fructose levels detected, which were 1.9 and 1.6-fold higher in the HighSC fruit, respectively (Table 1). Sucrose amount in both tomato fruit lines was 10 times lower than glucose, though still 2 fold higher in the HighSC as compared to LowSC fruit. Further analysis of mesocarp extracts showed similar pH values for the two lines (Table 1), which is in agreement with the total acids measured and with the observed relative abundance of citric, malic and glutamic acids, the major organic acids in ripe tomato fruit (Davies, 1966).

Comparison of the colonization patterns of *C. gloeosporioides* isolate Cg-14 on freshly harvested LowSC and HighSC fruit inoculated with a suspension of 5 x 103 spores showed 2.22-, 2.82- and 2.85-fold-enhanced colonization in the LowSC line compared the HighSC line after 4, 5 and 6 days, respectively (Fig. 1A,C). The pH of the HighSC fruit on day 6 postinoculation was not increased by *Colletotrichum* colonization, but that of the colonized LowSC fruit increased from pH 4.6 to 5.17 (Fig. 2A,B).

The colonization pattern of *P. expansum* was opposite to that of *C. gloeosporioides. P. expansum* inoculation with a spore suspension of 5 x 103 spores showed a colonized area of 260 mm<sup>2</sup> on the HighSC fruit compared to 220 mm<sup>2</sup> on the LowSC fruit (Fig. 1B, D). The differential pH change from the initial pH (∆pH) induced by *P. expansum* colonization in the LowSC line was minimal (Fig. 2C). However, colonization of the HighSC line induced a decrease of 0.4 pH units compared to noninoculated plants, with a pH of 4.3 (Fig. 2C, D).

Principal component analysis (PCA) of the polar metabolites detected for the tomato fruits of the Low and HighSC lines indicated distinct metabolic profiles of the inoculated fruits vs. the control (non-inoculated) fruits (Fig. 3A). The analysis included 100 metabolites identified against a library of standards, and their relative abundance in the different samples was determined (Supporting Table S1). PC1 (explaining 37.1% of the differences) separated between the inoculated fruits and the non-inoculated ones, while differences between the lines was represented by PC2 (explaining 19.3% of the differences), suggesting a greater effect on polar metabolites as result of fungal inoculation. The analyzed metabolites included mainly compounds related to primary metabolism, i.e., carbohydrates, organic acids, amino acids, fatty acids, nucleotides and their derivatives. Differences in abundance between the two (control) lines were detected only for very few metabolites. These include an increased abundance of sugars (glucose, fructose, sucrose, sorbitol and raffinose, Fig. 4) in the HighSC tomatoes. The concentration of the identified organic acids was not significantly different between the fruit of the two lines (Supporting Information Fig. S1) except for ascorbic acid level (Supporting Information Fig. S2) and aspartic acid level (Supporting Information Fig. S3) that were reduced in the HighSC fruit. In addition, the level of several amino acids (asparagine, alanine, glutamine, serine and aspartic acid) showed reduced concentration in the HighSC tomatoes (Supporting Information Fig. S3).

PCA of HighSC tomatoes inoculated with *C. gloeosporioides* and *P. expansum* showed relatively minor differences in metabolites, (Fig. 3A). In LowSC lines colonized with *C. gloeosporioides* and *P. expansum,* the metabolic pattern of the response was dependent on the specific pathogen interaction. The metabolic responses of the LowSC line inoculated with *C. gloeosporioides* were clustered closer to those of the colonized HighSC line. However, the response of the LowSC line infected with *P. expansum* differed from that of the same line infected with *C. gloeosporioides* (Fig. 3A). This suggests that modulation of host metabolites during LowSC infection is largely dependent on the pathogen, whereas the HighSC metabolites are similarly responsive, regardless of the pathogen infection strategy. Furthermore, the host sugar level may serve as a significant factor in modulating the differential mechanism of *Penicillium* attack. To further elucidate the differences and similarities between the pathogenicity mechanisms, we carried out a transcriptomic analysis for all four interactions and concentrate to decipher the pathogenic strategies in different host sugar-level interactions.

*Gene-expression profiling of* C. gloeosporioides *and* P. expansum *colonizing LowSC and HighSC tomato lines*

Gene-expression profiling of *C. gloeosporioides* and *P. expansum* colonizing HighSC and LowSC tomato fruit was conducted in three replicates each. Twelve libraries of single-end RNA sequences (deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive [SRA] under Bioproject accession no. PRJNA480740) were mapped to the reference genomes of *C. gloeosporioides* isolate Cg-14 (GEO accession number gca\_000446055\_1)(Alkan et al., 2015) and *P. expansum* (accession GCA\_000769735.1) using Bowtie2 software (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012).

The 12 libraries consisted of: (i) three replicates of HighSC tomato colonized by *C. gloeosporioides* and sampled 72 h postinoculation; (ii) three replicates of LowSC tomato colonized by *C. gloeosporioides* sampled 72 h postinoculation; (iii) three replicates of *P. expansum* colonized on HighSC tomato sampled 72 h postinoculation;

(iv) three replicates of LowSC tomato colonized by *P. expansum* sampled 72 h postinoculation. Heat-map analysis of *P. expansum* and *C. gloeosporioides* colonizing the LowSC and HighSC lines (Supporting Information Fig. S4) showed a series of upand downregulated genes that were differentially expressed according to the pathogen analyzed. The three replications of each treatment showed similar expression patterns (Supporting Information Fig. S4). The identified transcripts and their relative expression level are presented in Supporting Table S2 (for *C. gloeosporioides*) and Supporting Table S3 (for *P. expansum*).

Orthologues search between the two fungal transcriptomes identified 4,685 genes that were expressed for both fungi during inoculation of the tomato fruits. Of these genes, 486 genes of *C. gloeosporioides* and 138 genes of *P. expansum* were differentially expressed (Fig. 5). Most of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were upregulated when the two fungi inoculated the HighSC fruit. Interestingly *C. gloeosporioides* transcriptome was significantly altered in response to the nature of the host, and only a small fraction of these DEGs was shared with *P. expansum* transcriptome. The shared DEGs represent the core response (37 genes) of fungal pathogens to the sugar level of the host and they include mostly genes involved in amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism and lipid metabolism as well as several transporters / MFSs (Supporting Tables S4 and S5), thus we focused our analysis on these gene categories.

*Analysis of the differentially expressed* C. gloeosporioides *genes during enhanced colonization of LowSC vs. HighSC tomato lines*

Of the 1,496 transcripts differentially expressed during *Colletotrichum* colonization of LowSC and HighSC lines, 1,154 were upregulated during colonization of the HighSC line (Supporting Table S2). The other 342 genes were upregulated during colonization of the LowSC line, under conditions that contributed to enhanced *Colletotrichum* colonization.

Given that *Colletotrichum* showed enhanced colonization of the LowSC line, we concentrated on the most strongly upregulated genes on this line (Supporting Table S2) compared to the HighSC line, where the pathogen showed limited colonization. Among the genes that were upregulated while infecting the LowSC line were several glycosyl hydrolase (CAZy) genes from family 7 (12-fold; lcl|AMYD01003108.1\_mrna\_12480; locus CGLO\_13805; protein accession EQB47096.1), family 61 (11-fold; lcl|AMYD01001648.1\_mrna\_7429; locus CGLO\_08257; protein accession EQB52147.1), family 47 (4-fold; lcl|AMYD01001457.1\_mrna\_6485;locus CGLO\_07237; protein accession EQB53088.1), and family 71 (3.5-fold; lcl|AMYD01001992.1\_mrna\_8915; locus CGLO\_09889; protein accession EQB50655.1), which contributed to the pectolytic and maceration activity of the pathogen. Glucan 1,3-β-glucosidase (lcl|AMYD01003668.1\_mrna\_13882; locus CGLO\_15407; protein accession EQB45690.1) and exo-1,3-β-D-glucanase (lcl|AMYD01002948.1\_mrna\_12099; locus

CGLO\_13382; protein accession EQB47470.1), both contributing to cellulolytic activity, were far more strongly expressed during colonization of the LowSC vs. HighSC line. These enzymes participate in the hydrolysis of glucan, the main structural component of the host cell wall. Taken together, these enzymes may prepare the tissue for enhanced maceration by the pathogen.

Additional transcripts that were induced in the interaction with LowSC were eight genes from the major facilitator superfamily (MFS) of transporters; *mfs*1 is expressed only during the necrotrophic phase of the *Colletotrichum lindemuthianum*  infection cycle (Pereira et al., 2013). Upregulation of MFS genes encode proteins that facilitate the movement of small [solutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution) across [cell membranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane) in response to [chemiosmotic gradients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemiosmosis) (Quistgaard et al., 2016). Previous studies have indicated that these transporters are responsible for the uptake of glucose, mannose and fructose from the environment. MFS transporters are directly involved in balancing the interface between microorganisms and they induce the rapid export of ammonia and other toxic compounds. Given that the availability of monosaccharides to the fungus differ between HighSC and LowSC tomatoes lines, MFS may contribute to enhanced capturing of carbon sources during the necrotrophic development of *Colletotrichum* and *Botrytis* (Pereira et al., 2013; Vela-Corcía et al., 2019). Consequently, sugar availability in the host apoplast can determine the outcome of pathogenic interactions between the plant and the phytopathogenic fungus. This was demonstrated by altering susceptibility of plants harboring mutations in sugar transport proteins (STPs), like *Lr67*, a hexose transporter that confers multipathogen resistance in wheat and barley (Milne et al., 2019). [Lemonnier](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6446772/#bib22) et al. (2014) indicated that *Arabidopsis* plants, which are deficient in this sugar transporter (*STP13,* the homolog of *Lr67*), exhibit enhanced susceptibility to *Botrytis* and a reduced rate of glucose uptake (Lemonnier et al., 2014). However, the role of this sugar transporter may be different in a reproductive (sink) organ like the fruit as we tested in present experiments. Interestingly, the expression of several tomato sugar transporters were highly induced during pathogenic inoculation (Supporting Information Fig. S5). This includes the tomato homolog of *Lr67* (Sugar transporter 2; Solyc09g075820.3.1) and sugar transporter 12 (Solyc05g018230.3.1) that showed a higher expression in fruit inoculated by *Penicillium* than *Colletotrichum*. While on the other hand, inoculation with *Colletotrichum* enhanced the expression of sugar transporters 1 (Solyc02g079220.3.1) and 10 (Solyc03g006650.2.1) compared to *Penicillium* colonization. The observed re-programing of fruit sugar transporters (Supporting Information Fig. S5) during fungal colonization in parallel to increased expression of fungal MFSs suggest a role for sugar uptake in the arms race between the tomato fruit and the fungal pathogens. This is further supported by the observed correlation between the enhanced fungal MFS expression and enhanced colonization of *C. gloeosporioides* in the LowSC line, which indicate an increased demand for sugar energy to trigger colonization reactions and/or the need to export the antifungal compounds present in the host.

Interestingly, while mutating the MFS transporter *mfs1* was not found to modulate pathogenicity of *C. lindemuthianum* (Pereira et al., 2013), during *Botrytis cinerea* interactions with its cruciferous hosts, the MFS transporter *mfsG* was reported

to provide tolerance to glucosinolate-breakdown products and was required for pathogenicity (Vela-Corcía et al., 2019). The host glucosyltransferases expression responses that contribute to glucosinolate - metabolic products will be described in paragraph of host response to *C. gloeosporioides* attack. These findings indicate that both fungal and host MFS transporters may regulate their interaction during colonization.

Other highly expressed genes during *C. gloeosporioides* enhanced colonization of the LowSC lines included three cytochrome P450 genes, four FAD-binding domaincontaining protein genes and three ferric reductase-like transmembrane componentencoding genes. These might be involved in the detoxification of antifungal compounds produced by the host, in iron acquisition, and in producing reactive oxygen species (ROS) respectively (Hodges and Minich, 2015). This probably indicates that during its enhanced development on the LowSC fruit, the fungus undergoes rapid growth of its hyphae, which then invade and kill the plant cells by secreting enzymes that degrade plant polysaccharides, thereby releasing large amount of sugars that can be absorbed by the fungus. The fungus responds to this change in environmental carbon source during this stage of infection by activating the MFS genes (O'Connell et al., 2012).

*Analysis of the differentially expressed* P. expansum *genes during enhanced colonization of HighSC vs. LowSC tomato lines* 

Of the 369 genes differentially expressed during *Penicillium* colonization of HighSC and LowSC lines, 283 were upregulated during colonization of the HighSC fruit, and 86 were upregulated during colonization of the LowSC fruit. The genes that were upregulated on the HighSC fruit (Supporting Table 3) were analyzed for their contribution to the enhancement of *P. expansum* colonization of this line. Analysis of KEGG pathways and GO analysis indicated that carbohydrate metabolism is significantly modulated during colonization by both fungi as reported in Supporting Table S4 and Table S5. Among the significantly upregulated genes with the HighSC line (Fig. 6, Supporting Table S6), that encoding phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK; PEXP\_084860) showed the highest relative expression. PEPCK is an [enzyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme) of the [lyase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyase) family used in the metabolic pathway of [gluconeogenesis.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluconeogenesis) It converts [oxaloacetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxaloacetate) into [phosphoenolpyruvate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoenolpyruvate) and [carbon dioxide.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) PEPCK-C catalyzes an irreversible step of [gluconeogenesis,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluconeogenesis) the process whereby glucose is synthesized, and is essential in glucose homeostasis (Kelly and Hynes, 1981). Overexpresses of PEPCK-C, activates the [Krebs cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citric_acid_cycle) by *P. expansum* and explains the overproduction of C4 dicarboxylic acids such as acetic acid and gluconic acid (GLA) by *Penicillium* sp. (Supporting Information Fig. S2) (Prusky et al., 2004; Yang et al., 2015; Barad et al., 2016a). This response indicated that not only the host nutritional factors containing higher glucose and fructose levels but the pathogen itself modify its metabolism as a mechanism to enhance GLA accumulation (Fig. 6, Supporting Information Fig S2). This results in *P. expansum* being a fast developing necrotrophic parasite in fruit needing to kill the [host](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/hosts) cells to acquire nutrients following its penetration (van Kan, 2006), while hemibiotrophs, as *[Colletotrichum](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/colletotrichum)* spp. and others (Munch et al., 2008; Yi and Valent, 2013), may activate [transporters](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/transporters) for [nutrient uptake.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/nutrient-uptake) Interestingly, while the genomes of necrotrophs such as *P. expansum* and hemibiotrophs such as *Colletotrichum* spp. are both enriched for CAZy genes (Alkan et al., 2013a; Alkan et al., 2013b; Barad et al., 2016a) the glucose-dependent mechanisms of activation of those genes (Fernandez et al., 2012) probably differ. In both cases, their importance in plant [cell-wall](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/cell-walls) degradation and their contribution to colonization of the host fruit are clear (Blanco-Ulate et al., 2010).

The specific upregulation of glucose metabolic genes in the HighSC suggest that *Penicillium* may differentially sense the change in glucose levels between 29 mg (approx. 3%) and 15 mg/g f. wt (approx. 1.5% glucose) (Table 1) compared to the LowSC. This difference seems significant; however, both glucose concentrations are similarly inducing the expression of CreA (PEXP\_011320, the zing finger transcription factor responsible for carbon catabolite repression). This may imply that other pathways are involved in the induction of glucose metabolism during *Penicillium* pathogenic interaction with the HighSC fruits. In *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* glucose presence can be sensed by several signal transduction pathways (Conrad et al., 2014). In *Penicillium,* two of *S. cerevisiae* glucose sensing mechanisms were up regulated. In yeast, the Sugar Receptor-Repressor (SRR) Pathway encodes for 17 hexose transporters, which all have different affinity and kinetic properties in response to glucose. Two of these proteins, *Sc*Snf3 and *Sc*Rgt2, are not able to transport glucose, but sense this sugar. *Sc*Snf3 is the high-affinity sensor while *Sc*Rgt2 responds to higher levels of glucose (Ozcan et al., 1996). Analysis of homologues genes in *Penicillium* inoculated in the High and Low SC levels showed that the *Sc*Snf3 homolog (PEXP\_098600) is similarly expressed during inoculation of both hosts while *Sc*Rgt2 homolog (PEXP\_011530) is only expressed when inoculating HighSC fruit, which is in line with its role to respond to high glucose availability.

The second glucose sensing mechanism in *S. cerevisiae* is via the G-protein coupled receptor *Sc*Gpr1, which causes the activation of the cAMP/PKA pathway, resulting in rapid adaptation to the presence of glucose. cAMP/PKA signaling, is a positive-acting determinant of appressoria development and environmental sensing regulating growth and pathogenicity in response to carbon source availability (Ziv et al., 2008; Selvaraj et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2017). Transcript activation of PEPCK probably occurs through the activation of cAMP/PKA also in *Penicillium* (Van Ende et al., 2019). However, full activation of PKA pathway by glucose also requires glucose transport and its phosphorylation by one of the hexose kinases (Rolland et al., 2000; Rolland et al., 2001). Indeed, several *P. expansum* MFS genes (such as PEXP\_011530, PEXP\_034270 and PEXP\_094860, which are expected to encode sugar transporter according to their homology with known genes in other fungi) were up regulated during *Penicillium*  inoculation, suggesting their activation by the pathogen. Finally, the glucose repression pathway ensures repression of respiration and mainly the repression of the use of alternative carbon sources (Van Ende et al., 2019). This indicates that the effect of glycolysis on the virulence of fungal pathogens (Barelle et al., 2006) is signaled in *Penicillium* via the cAMP/PKA pathway

One other interesting upregulated gene in *P. expansum* colonizing the HighSC lines was *Grg*1 (PEXP\_013420), encoding a glucose-repressible protein. This gene affects the lifespan of fungal growth and is dependent on environmental conditions. The reduction of metabolic processes (e.g., growth on nutritionally poor media) is known to lead to an increase in lifespan and to rejuvenation of aging cultures (Tudzynski and Esser, 1977). The upregulation of *Grg*1 under HighSC conditions along with an enhanced colonization pattern suggest that the fungus senses the non-repressive conditions during *P. expansum* colonization of the HighSC line. This enhanced fungal growth in the colonized tissue, enhance the secretion of a possible pathogenicity factor (like organic acids) and further contribute to host colonization (Fig. 1 and 2).

*P. expansum* colonizing HighSC fruit also showed upregulation of eight cytochrome P-450 oxidases (PEXP\_036240, PEXP\_102150, PEXP\_063320, PEXP\_074010, PEXP\_099940, PEXP\_018660, PEXP\_073960, PEXP\_050580) and two ATP-Binding Cassette (ABC) transporters (PEXP\_044030, PEXP\_097150), which may also indicate the need for enhanced metabolic oxidation induced by *P. expansum* in the HighSC line. Those genes' ability to encode enzymes that neutralize toxic metabolites suggests that the enhanced colonization of *P. expansum* in the HighSC lines is also accompanied by the pathogen's ability to neutralize host-produced antifungal compounds. The further contribution of ABC transporters suggests the need for efflux of the accumulating detoxified material during fungal colonization.

Of significant importance was the upregulation, albeit at lower levels of expression, of several glycosidase families during the interaction of the HighSC line and *P. expansum*. These included the glycoside hydrolase superfamily (PEXP\_008790, PEXP\_060960, PEXP\_072880, PEXP\_103080, PEXP\_084210, PEXP\_015370), family 61 (PEXP\_053240), family 32 (PEXP\_001060), family 1 (PEXP\_044040), family 12 (PEXP\_033400), and family 48 (PEXP\_042510), all contributing to maceration of the host tissue. This might explain *P. expansum* enhanced necrotrophic attack under conditions of high sugar content and metabolize high sugar concentrations better than the hemibiotrophic *C. gloeosporioides*. Interesting is the induction of glycosyl hydrolases both by *P. expansum* during enhanced colonization of the HighSC as well as by *C. gloeosporioides* enhance colonization at LowSC lines*.* This indicates that while both pathogen may similarly activate their expression, it is carried out at different environmental conditions. In conclusion, sugar sensing is an important virulence factor for both *Penicillium* and *Colletotrichum* and emphasizes the importance of the dynamic of nutritional environment for the different tools used by *Penicillium* colonization.

## *Do LowSC and HighSC tomato lines respond differentially to* C. gloeosporioides *and* P. expansum*?*

Gene-expression profiling of the non-infected LowSC and HighSC lines and those infected by *C. gloeosporioides* and *P. expansum* was conducted in three replicates each. Eighteen libraries of single-end RNA sequences (deposited in NCBI SRA under Bioproject accession no. PRJNA480740) were mapped to the reference genomes of tomato (*S. lycopersicum*, build 3.10) (Sato et al., 2012) using Bowtie2 software (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). Heat-map analysis of the LowSC and HighSC lines showed a series of up- and downregulated genes that were differentially expressed according to cultivar and to the pathogen colonizing each cultivar (Supporting Information Fig. S6). The relative expression level of the identified transcripts in response to *C. gloeosporioides* and *P. expansum* inoculation are presented in Tables S7 and S8, respectively.

PCA of the differentially regulated RNA-Seq samples of non-inoculated (control) LowSC and HighSC lines, and those inoculated with *C. gloeosporioides* and *P. expansum* clearly indicated similar replications (Fig. 3B). The analysis included all host genes identified against published tomato sequences and their relative abundance when compared their modified expression by fungal inoculation in the RNA-Seq according to Supporting Tables S7 and S8. Transcription pattern of the two control lines (HighSC and LowSC) was rather similar and only minor differences were observed (as represented by PC2 that explains 9.6% of the differences, Fig. 3B). However, a significant difference in gene expression was observed between the control fruits and the inoculated fruits (as represented by PC1 that explains 75.7% of the differences, Fig. 3B). Thus, inoculation of both lines with *P. expansum* or *C. gloeosporioides* showed differential responses compared to the control lines (Supporting Information Fig. S6), but similarities in the lines' responses to *Colletotrichum* and *Penicillium* colonization attacks (Fig. 3B). This conclusion was similar to that reached in the metabolic PCA where the HighSC and LowSC lines showed similar polar metabolic responses (Fig. 3A) and also maximal up and down regulation of host genes by the *C. gloeosporioides*

in the HighSC lines (Fig. 7A,B). Interestingly, a relative large fraction of the tomato DEGs was observed in response to *Colletotrichum* (1813 up and 263 down regulated compared 119 up and 164 down respectively in *Penicillium*, Fig. 7C,D and Supporting Information Fig. S7). This imply that under conditions of high sugar, the hemibiotrophic *C. gloeosporioides* showed significant capabilities to remodel host transcriptome (as well as metabolome, Fig. 3). On the other side the necrotrophic development of *P. expansum* on the high sugar, involve a limited number of specific host DEGs (Fig. 7C, D and Supporting Information Fig. S7).

*Host response to* C. gloeosporioides *colonization of HighSC vs. LowSC tomato lines* Analysis of the differentially expressed genes in the HighSC line inoculated with *Colletotrichum* (Table 2 and Supporting Table S7) (conditions of reduced colonization, Fig. 1) showed a significant increase in genes related to glucosyltransferases (GO:0046527), UDP-glucosyltransferase activity (GO:0035251), UDPglycosyltransferase activity (GO:0008194) and also several transferases, transferring glycosyl groups (GO:0016757) and transferase activity, transferring hexosyl groups (GO:0016758). Plant UDP-glucose:phenylpropanoid glucosyltransferases (UGTs) catalyze the transfer of glucose from UDP-glucose to numerous substrates, and regulate hormone homeostasis, detoxification of xenobiotics and biosynthesis and storage of secondary compounds that play important roles in plant defense against pathogens (Dixon and Paiva, 1995; He and Dixon, 2000; Chong et al., 2001; Chong et al., 2002; Hanhineva et al., 2009; Tohge et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2015). This would fit with the

supposed role of UGTs as a first line of defense against stresses, continuously buffering environmental stresses and shaped according to changing conditions (Gachon et al., 2005). From a chemical point of view, sugar conjugation increased their stability, water solubility and the compartmentalization of metabolites in the vacuole. Upon the decompartmentalization caused by fungal cellular disintegration, they are released and further cleaved by β-glycosidases into their active form (Vogt and Jones, 2000; Lim et al., 2001).

The most highly expressed genes in the HighSC line were related to glycosylation, contributing to the accumulation of sugar free phenolic compounds (Table 3). Conjugation reactions catalyzed by UGTs may be critical in regulating the levels of signaling compounds such as salicylic acid (SA), by converting active molecules to inactive conjugated forms (Fraissinet-Tachet et al., 1998). This seems to be a novel mechanism activated by *Colletotrichum* but not by *Penicillium* infections on HighSC lines that contribute to differential colonization of host fruit.

Among the UGTs that could be involved in plant defense, a SA- and pathogeninducible UGT acting *in vitro* on the signaling molecules SA, benzoic acid, and cinnamic acid has been isolated in tobacco (Hennig et al., 1993; Lee and Raskin, 1998; Lim et al., 2001). Two tobacco genes (*Togt1* and *Togt2*), which are induced early by SA and during hypersensitive reaction (HR) (Fraissinet-Tachet et al., 1998), have been reported to encode UGTs that modulate hydroxycoumarins (Fraissinet-Tachet et al., 1998). These genes are homologous with a glucosyltransferase gene from tomato (*Twi1*) that responds rapidly to wound- and pathogen-related signals (O'Donnell et al., 1998). Also in the HighSC lines inoculated with *C. gloeosporioides*, 14 different glucosyltransferases were detected, some of them at their most significant levels of expression (Tables 2 and 3). Using a loss-of-function approach based on antisense inhibition of tobacco SA- and pathogen-inducible UGT (TOGT) in transgenic plants, the function UGT in virus resistance was clearly shown (Fedoroff et al., 1984; Kim et al., 2015; Shinde et al., 2017). In the present work, all of the significantly upregulated genes in the comparison of HighSC to LowSC lines inoculated with *C. gloeosporioides* were related to glycosylation activities, expressed in the HighSC lines with reduced colonization conditions (Tables 2 and 3). In addition, several genes resulting in this activity, such as UDP-xylose phenolic glycosyltransferase and jasmonate methyl esterase in the jasmonic acid (JA) pathways were also upregulated, confirming the results of the GO-enriched upregulation analysis (Table 2).

Other genes that may contribute to limited colonization of the HighSC lines (Supporting Table S7) by *C. gloeosporioides* include the methyl jasmonate esterase (Solyc03g044820.3.1). JA has been proven to be involved in plant resistance to pathogens by activating pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins such as PR-1, PR-3 and PR-8 (Reymond and Farmer, 1998). Some of the PR genes were activated, including those encoding pathogenesis-related protein 1 (Solyc00g174340.2.1) and a second pathogenicity-related protein (Solyc09g007020.2.1). The existence of a SAindependent induced systemic resistance pathway has been also studied in *Arabidopsis thaliana*, which is dependent on JA and ethylene signalling. This might explain the differential upregulation of the ethylene-forming enzyme (Solyc12g098850.2.1), where three genes encoding ethylene-responsive transcription factor (Solyc03g005520.1.1; Solyc11g011750.2.1 and Solyc02g090770.1.1) were activated (Farmer and Ryan, 1992). These results suggest that the differential response of HighSC vs. LowSC lines to *Colletotrichum* may modulate the induction of systemic acquired resistance in fruit, as evidenced by enhanced defensive capacity upon challenge inoculation (Farmer and Ryan, 1992).

Analysis of the differentially expressed downregulated genes in the HighSC line (upregulation in the LowSC, where *Colletotrichum* showed enhanced pathogenicity) (Supporting Table S7 and Table 2) included genes that may contribute to the reduced colonization. This indicates that *C. gloeosporioides* has a specific activation process on these tomato cultivars, as a significant increase in serine-type peptidase and hydrolase activities (GO:0008236 and 0017171) were observed (Table 2). These enzymes have broad proteolytic and metabolic activities, which are essential for several processes occurring during fungal colonization, and play a role in the activation of induced cell death as they contribute to the enhanced colonization of the LowSC line (Kaschani et al., 2009).

The oxidoreductase activity that significantly increased in the GO-enriched downregulated in the HighSC (upregulated in the LowSC, Table 2) may also contribute to general metabolic processes involved in pathogenicity. The multicopper oxidase enzyme, has the ability to catalyze oxidation of various phenolic and non-phenolic compounds, protecting the plant against fungal-produced compounds. This may contribute to host protection against energy-dependent programmed cell death

processes in which the dying cells undergo controlled oxidative stress by the accumulation of ROS, cell decomposition and component recycling. These general processes, in the more resistant interaction with HighSC lines, suggest a very specific and not previously reported response of the HighSC lines to *C. gloeosporioides* colonization.

#### *Host response to* P. expansum *colonization of HighSC vs. LowSC tomato lines*

Under the enhanced colonization by *P. expansum* of the HighSC lines, minor expression of the group of UDP- glucosyltransferases was observed as described for *Colletotrichum*. However, other genes related to transferase activity, transferring hexosyl groups (GO:0016758) (Supporting Table S8; Table 2) were highly expressed. Analysis of all the transferase-activity-encoding genes affecting the transferal of hexose groups, suggested that *P. expansum* activate of host cell-wall metabolism genes. This was accompanied by genes encoding carbohydrate metabolic processes, hydrolase activity and activities hydrolyzing O-glycosyl compounds (GO:0005975; GO:0004553; GO;0016787) that all of them contribute to the enhanced pathogenicity of *P. expansum* in the HighSC line.

In response to the restricted colonization of LowSC lines by *P. expansum,* a significant increase in the expression of genes related to hydrolase activity was induced. This included hydrolase activity acting on glycosyl bonds and on O-glycosyl compounds, all of which may contribute to the enhanced host susceptibility and maceration activity of the pathogen. Our metabolomics data indicated that glucose and fructose serve as major components of tomato fruit soluble solids, while sucrose and raffinose are present at much lower concentrations (Fig. 4, Supporting Table S1), which is in line with previous reports (Yelle et al., 1988). During colonization of HighSC fruit by either *P. expansum* or *C. gloeosporioides*, a significant increase in glucose, fructose and to some extent sucrose levels was observed, as compared to non-inoculated fruit (Fig. 4, top). Furthermore, colonization of LowSC fruits by *C. gloeosporioides* induced a mild, but significant, increase in fructose content. The marked increase in glucose and fructose levels as result of fungal infection raises the question of the source of these sugars. Notably, in both tomato lines, pathogen colonization of the fruit was accompanied by a significant decline in raffinose levels. Raffinose is a D-galactose and sucrose containing oligosaccharide. Degradation of this tri-saccharide may serve as a source for simple sugars' accumulation. However, raffinose is probably not the prime source due to its relatively low levels in the fruit. Interestingly, similar sugar accumulation in on-vine

However, this cannot explain the sugar accumulation of harvested tomato fruits, and further research is required in order to elucidate the source of the accumulated sugars. These findings might be of significant importance during the final necrotrophic stage of fruit decay caused by both pathogens that requires substantial availability of carbon source to support their metabolic activity demand of nutrients and energy.

Raffinose synthesis and accumulation in vegetative plant tissues is correlated with plant response to abiotic and biotic stresses (Sengupta et al., 2015). Thus, the significant decline in raffinose levels in both tomato lines (Fig. 4) as result of *P. expansum* inoculation was unexpected. The ability to utilize raffinose is not general to all fungi (Rehms and Barz, 1995). However, the decline in this tri-saccharide during colonization of *P. expansum* may indicate the ability of this pathogen to degrade raffinose in order to utilize it as carbon source (Fang and St Leger, 2010)*.* This ability to degrade raffinose may be significant to its virulence, as was previously shown for bacterial pathogens (Minhas et al., 2019). These results are in line with the transcriptomic analysis, which indicated that *P. expansum* has a significantly higher carbon metabolism than *C. gloeosporioides*.

On the other hand, *C. gloeosporioides*-colonization of both fruit, but more profoundly of LowSC fruit resulted in a significant accumulation of sorbitol and trehalose (Fig. 4). The source of these sugars is not easily determined as both, the fruit and the pathogen may synthesize them, and their net level is determined by the balance between synthesis and degradation. In line with this, the expression of tomato genes encoding for trehalose synthase and trehalose 6-phosphate phosphatase are highly induced during fungal inoculation of both fungi (Supporting Table S9), while fungal genes encoding trehalase were highly expressed by both fungi during infection. Nevertheless, while *P. expansum*  neutral trehalase (PEXP\_077900) is similarly expressed when infecting both tomato lines, the expression of *C. gloeosporioides* trehalase (lcl|AMYD01000660.1\_mrna\_2761; locus accession CGLO\_03156; protein accession EQB56810.1) during infecting of LowSC is ca. 8-10 fold higher than during HighSC infection. This can explain the higher level of trehalose in the LowSC fruit during *Colletotrichum* infection.

What is then the role of these sugars? Both sorbitol and trehalose can serve as an energy source or as osmoprotectants during stress response. Trehalose and its metabolic products were found to be involved in plant response to biotic stress by modulating host susceptibility to fungal pathogens. Specifically, trehalose was found to induce wheat defense and resistance toward powdery mildew and to increase tomato resistance against *B. cinerea* (Tayeh et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2016). In addition sorbitol has an antioxidant capacity and is known to be involved in protecting plants against salt and photooxidative stress as well as during pathogenic interactions (Williamson et al., 2002). Thus, the increase in sorbitol and trehalose may represent the fruit metabolic response to fungal colonization, which could regulate osmotic pressure and contribute to membrane stability (Petzold et al., 2006). On the other hand, trehalose can be synthesized by the fungi as both *C. gloeosporioides* and *P. expansum* express trehalose

related genes during infection. This may be important for their pathogenicity, as was shown for several fungi like *Cryptococcus spp.* where trehalose served a central importance in their virulence (Petzold et al., 2006; Ngamskulrungroj et al., 2009). To summarize, the fact that *Penicillium* induces more significant changes in the soluble sugars (glucose, fructose, and sucrose) while *C. gloeosporioides* induces accumulation of trehalose and sorbitol suggests a different mode of carbon source utilization between the two fungi.

#### Organic acids

Citric, malic and oxalic acids are the major organic acids in various tomato cultivars, in addition to pyruvic, fumaric and ascorbic acids (Hernández Suárez et al., 2007). While the tomato fruit of the two lines significantly differed in their sugar contents, fewer differences were found with regard to the organic acids (Supporting Information Fig. S1). During infection, the most interesting response was related to the accumulation of the organic acid GLA in the host tissue (Supporting Information Fig. S2). The concentration of GLA actually increased upon inoculation with both *P. expansum* and *C. gloeosporioides* compared to the non-inoculated lines; however, this increase was highest in both lines when inoculated with *P. expansum* (Supporting Information Fig. S2). This indicates that the accumulation of GLA is a pathogen-related rather than host-related response, specifically expressed in *Penicillium* (Bi et al., 2016) and confirms the strong relative expression of genes related to the Krebs cycle in this pathogen (Fig. 6). On the other hand, the level of ascorbic acid, which was significantly more abundant in LowSC fruit, decreased during infection with either fungus, but did not change in any of the treatments of the HighSC fruit, suggesting a host-specific role during pathogenic interactions (Supporting Information Fig. S2). This differential response by the organic acids clearly explains the previously reported results of Tannous et al*.* (2018), where a *CreA* (the global carbon catabolite regulator) loss-offunction *P. expansum* strain was unable to successfully colonize apples, as a result of ability to produce proteolytic enzymes and secrete GLA to the environment (Tannous et al., 2018).

#### Amino acids

While our library included all 21 natural amino acids, 14 were detected in the tomato samples (Supporting Information Fig. S3). The LowSC lines showed relatively higher levels of alanine, serine, aspartic acid, asparagine, and glutamine than the HighSC lines. The relative abundance of isoleucine, leucine, tryptophan, tyrosine, phenylalanine, valine, glycine, proline and glutamic acid was not significantly different between the two tomato lines. However, most of the changes were observed during infection. The amount of glutamic acid, phenylalanine and serine was significantly reduced in both tomato lines by both pathogens, suggesting that the level of amino acids is pathogen-dependent, possibly as a result of pathogen consumption. Comparison of the LowSC inoculated with *C. gloeosporioides* compared to its control showed a significant reduction of glutamine and asparagine. While in the HighSC fruit, the level of glutamine and asparagine did not change because of *C. gloeosporioides* inoculation, the abundance of these amino acids was enhanced by *P. expansum* in the LowSC line but significantly declined during colonization by *C. gloeosporioides*.

Assimilation of ammonia into glutamate and glutamine has been reported to play a central role in fungal nitrogen metabolism, as glutamate dehydrogenase, glutamate synthase, and glutamine synthetase are key enzymes involved in ammonia assimilation (Marzluf, 1997). The specific steps in these pathways vary with the organism and probably differ in *Penicillium* and *Colletotrichum*. In virtually all fungi's metabolism, glutamate (85%) serves as the nitrogen donor for biosynthetic reactions (Meti et al., 2011). This indicate that for *C. gloeosporioides*, under conditions of enhanced colonization of the LowSC line, the fungal favored nitrogen source is glutamine.

#### **Conclusions**

While both *Colletotrichum* and *Penicillium* metabolize glutamine and accumulate ammonia when grown in liquid media, this process is inhibited under conditions of HighSC, enhancing colonization by *Penicillium* (Bi et al., 2016). This suggests that ammonia does not contribute to *Penicillium* colonization due to sugar repression of this regulatory process. The impaired pathogenicity of the *Penicillium CreA* mutant (Tannous et al., 2018) and that of *C. gloeosporioides* in the HighSC lines suggest that the two pathogens have different carbon metabolic preferences during their virulence of postharvest hosts. The differential metabolic response of *P. expansum* may contribute to its colonization range of hosts (mainly deciduous fruit and some tropical ones) and its specificity for fruit tissue only. Enhance colonization by the necrotrophic pathogen may require enhanced fungal carbon metabolism as well as host modulating the transport of carbon metabolic sources all together contributing to fungal attack in those fruits (Fig. 8). *Colletotrichum* behavior is completely different from that of *Penicillium* which attacks a wider number of hosts and an overall larger number of plant tissues (fruit, leaves, stems, flowers) and its pathogenicity may result from differential fungal pathogenicity factors as well as of host defense factors. This possibility was observed in the response of HighSC lines colonized by *Colletotrichum* that showed almost 6 fold higher amount of differentially expressed genes (Fig. 7 C,D) in conditions of limited pathogenicity compared to *Penicillium* where under those conditions showed enhanced colonization. This wide modulation of genes (Fig. 5 and Fig. 8) may explain the wide range of hosts species and host tissues, colonized by *Colletotrichum* compared to the limited host fruit range colonized by *Penicillium* (Prusky et al., 2013). At the same time this may stress the importance of the dynamic changes in nutrient availability during fruit development and ripening as an inducing factor for enhanced *Penicillium* metabolic activity during colonization, explaining the importance of sugar changes on the differential response of fungal colonization of postharvest pathogens.

#### **Experimental procedures**

*Fungal isolates, plant cultivars and fruit inoculation*

Single-spore cultures of *C. gloeosporioides* (Penz.) isolate Cg-14 were obtained from a decayed avocado fruit (Wattad et al., 1994). The isolate was previously analyzed during tomato colonization (Alkan et al., 2015). The wild-type *P. expansum* isolate Pe-21 was

obtained from decayed apples (*Malus domestica* cv. Golden Delicious) purchased from a local market in Israel (Barad et al., 2016a). *C. gloeosporioides* was grown on M3S plates and *P. expansum* was grown on PDA plates at 25 o C in the dark. From both wildtype strains, conidia were harvested with 10 ml sterile distilled water supplemented with 0.01% (v/v) Triton X-100. Cells were visualized with a model BX60F-3 microscope (Olympus America Inc., Melville, NY, USA) and counted with a hemocytometer (Brand, GMBH, Wertheim, Germany).

Tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*) plants were grown in a greenhouse under standard conditions. The two lines compared are ARO breeding lines, ARO-BL7334 and ARO-BL7335. Each is a BC1F14 line that were derived in parallel from an initial cross between two breeding lines of undetermined provenance of similar size averaging ~20 g fruit and plant growth habit (non-determinate) but with different sugar accumulation patterns. The fruit of the high sugar parent continues to accumulate hexose until full ripe stage while the low sugar parent accumulates significantly lower hexose levels. The F1 was backcrossed to each of the parents and selfed selections from each backcross were made for an additional 13 generations, selecting for low and high sugar levels, respectively. For the experiments in this report fruit from each of the two lines were harvested at the fully ripe stage and brought to the laboratory for analyses. To standardize ripening stage of the fruit, the skin color (hue) of 10 tomato fruit was measured using a CR-400/410 Chromometer (Konica Minolta, Osaka, Japan) at four points on the equatorial part of each fruit (40 measurements per cultivar)(Sudheeran et al., 2018). Fruits with a color showing a similar hue angle (approximate 350±5) were selected for the experiments.

Harvested fruit was inoculated in the laboratory in an aseptically way after soap and 70% ethanol washing for 30 s each. The fruit was rinsed twice with sterile water and kept at room temperature for inoculation. The fruit were then inoculated in a sterile hood by placing 5 μl of a conidial suspension containing 5x103 spore on each of two 2 mm deep and 1-mm diameter wounds, spaced evenly in a circle around the upper part of the stem end of 30 to 40 fruit. Following inoculation, the fruit were incubated up to 6 days for *C. gloeosporioides* and 4 days for *P. expansum* (unless otherwise specified) at 25°C and 95% RH, in aseptically cleaned and covered containers containing sterile wet paper towels. Decay area of the colonized tissue of the infected fruit was recorded, and samples were collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent RNA analysis. Control tissue was obtained from the healthy Low and High sugar containing lines.

To analyze TSS, pH and acidity in the fruit, juice from five to six tomatoes (25 g tissue from each tomato in four replicates) was extracted using an HG-300 homogenizer (MRC, Jiangsu, China) and filtered through a gauze pad. TSS (%) of the juice was measured by digital refractometer (Atago, Tokyo, Japan). Then acid content of the fruit juice were determined with a Metrohm titrator (678 EP/KF processor, Herisau, Switzerland).

*Analysis of pH changes effected by* C. gloeosporioides *and* P. expansum

Tomato tissue pH was measured in the juice of homogenized solution of freshly harvested and by directly punching in fungal-colonized fruit, with a double-pore slim electrode (Hamilton) connected to a Thermo Orion Model 720A Plus pH meter.

#### *RNA extraction*

Total RNA was extracted from tomato fruit according to (Yang et al., 2008), with minor modifications: aliquots were taken from pooled samples of two inoculation areas on each tomato. The samples were ground to a fine powder in the presence of liquid nitrogen and transferred to 50-ml centrifuge tubes with 10 ml cetyltrimethylammonium bromide-RNA extraction buffer comprised of 100 mM Tris–borate pH 8, 2 M NaCl, 25 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) pH 8, 2% (w/v) CTAB, 2% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone, and 2% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol. The mixture was shaken for 3 min and then incubated at 65 °C for 15 min. Samples were extracted twice with an equal volume of 24:1 (v/v) chloroform:isoamyl alcohol, and the phases were separated by centrifugation at 10,000 × *g* for 10 min. Following centrifugation, LiCl was added to a final concentration of 2.5 M and RNA was allowed to precipitate overnight at 4 °C. The precipitated RNA was pelleted at 4 °C for 30 min at 10,000 × *g*, washed with 70% ethanol, and resuspended at 65 °C for 3 min in SSTE buffer comprised of 10 mM Tris pH 8, 1 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA pH 8, and 0.5% (w/v) SDS. Samples were extracted with equal volumes of 24:1 (v/v) chloroform:isoamyl alcohol, and with equal volumes of 24:1:25 (v/v) chloroform:isoamyl alcohol:water-saturated phenol, and the phases were separated by centrifugation at 10,000 × *g* for 10 min. The RNA was ethanol-precipitated overnight, and resuspended in diethyl-pyrocarbonate-treated water (Yang et al., 2008). The RNA was further treated with Turbo DNAse (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA) and kept at -80 <sup>o</sup> C until further use. Quality of RNA was determined using the microcapillary electrophoretic separation allowing the calculation of the RNA integrity number (RIN) that indicated the high quality of the RNA extracted.

#### *Preparation of libraries*

A 500-ng aliquot of total RNA from eight samples was processed with the Truseq Stranded Total RNA with Ribo Zero Plant (RS-122-2401) (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Libraries were evaluated with Qubit and TapeStation software (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA), and sequence libraries were constructed with barcodes to enable sample multiplexing. The results showed 13.2–16.9 million singleend 50-bp reads. These were sequenced on one lane of an Illumina HiSeq 2500 V3 instrument at the Bioinformatics Unit of the Weizmann Institute of Science, Rehovot, Israel.

For the fungi differentially expressed transcripts we used a threshold of FDR < 0.05 and absolute value of log2FC>=2. The differentially expressed matrix was normalized with TMM (trimmed mean of M values) method (part of edgeR package), then values were log2-transformed, median centered for visualization purposes and the heatmaps were created using pheatmap R package. For GO annotation of the fungi transcripts we used Blast2GO (Conesa et al., 2005). The KEGG annotation of the fungal transcripts were analyzed using KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS) (Moriya et al., 2007).We detected homologues between proteins of the two fungi (*C. gloeosporioides* and *P. expansum*) by using Proteinortho - Orthology detection tool version 5.16b (Lechner et al., 2011).

The annotations of *C. gloeosporioides* were taken from GEO accession no. GSE41844 (Alkan et al., 2013b). GO-enrichment was analyzed by Fisher's Exact Test with multiple testing correction of false discovery rates (FDR) lower than 0.05 (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). We compared the healthy LowSC and HighSC lines with their infected counterparts, and therefore 'upregulated/downregulated' refers to higher/lower expression of the corresponding transcripts in the HighSC line as Accepted Article

compared to the LowSCline. Heat maps and clustering of the genes were visualized with the R software pheatmap package (RDC, 2009).

We used a FDR threshold of <0.05 and log fold change greater than 1 or less than -1 for analysis of *C. gloeosporioides*, *P. expansum* and for tomato transcripts we used a threshold of FDR < 0.01 and absolute number of log 2 fold change grated than 2. For clustering analysis of the tomato genes, we used the CLICK algorithm found in Expander suite (Sharan et al., 2003). GO-enrichment analysis of tomato genes was performed using the agriGO version 2.0 server (Tian et al., 2017). GO enrichment for C. *gloeosporioides* and *P. expansum* genes was performed using Blast2GO software (Conesa et al., 2005).

#### Gene-expression analysis by qRT-PCR

Real-time qRT-PCR was performed with the StepOnePlus System (Applied Biosystems, Grand Island, NY, USA). PCR amplification was performed with 3.4 µl of cDNA template in 10 µl reaction mixture containing 6.6 µl mix from the SYBR Green Amplification Kit (ABgene, Surrey, UK) and 300 nM primers. Supporting Table S10 lists the forward and reverse primers for each of the indicated genes. The PCR was carried out as follows: 10 min at 94 °C, and 40 cycles of 94 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. The samples were subjected to melting-curve analysis: efficiencies were close to 100% for all primer pairs, and all products showed the expected sizes of 70 to 100 bp. All of the samples were normalized to endogenous control gene-expression levels, and the values were expressed as the change (increasing or

decreasing) in level relative to a calibrator sample. Results were analyzed with StepOnePlus software v.2.2.2. Relative quantification was performed by the ∆∆C<sup>T</sup> method according to (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). The ∆C<sup>T</sup> value was determined by subtracting the CT results for the target gene from those for the endogenous control gene—*18S* for *C. gloeosporioides, 28S* for *P. expansum* and *TIP41* (Solyc01g107415.1.1) for tomato analysis—and normalizing against the calibration sample (infected HighSC sample, that was set to 1) to generate the ∆∆C<sup>T</sup> values and is presented in Supporting Fig. S8-S9. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and three different biological experiments were conducted. One representative set of results is presented as mean values of 2-∆∆CT ± SE for each treatment. The results of this analysis is described in Supporting information.

#### *Metabolomics analysis*

Samples of control and infected tomato fruit were collected as described for the RNA-Seq.

#### *Soluble sugar analysis*

Soluble sugars were extracted in three consecutive soakings in 80% ethanol at 70°C. The ethanol from the three soakings was pooled for each sample and was then evaporated overnight at 55°C. Sugar residue was then dissolved in 1 ml H2O and filtered through a 0.25-μm micropore filter. Sucrose, glucose and fructose levels in the soluble sugar extracts were resolved by high-pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Petreikov et al., 2009).

#### *Metabolite extraction*

Polar substance extraction and analysis from these samples was performed as previously described in Malitsky *et al.* (2016) and Salem *et al.,* (2017) with some modifications (Malitsky et al., 2016; Salem et al., 2017): 10 mg of lyophilized tomato sample was mixed with 1 ml of a precooled (-20 <sup>o</sup> C) homogeneous 1:3 (v/v) methanol:methyl-tertbutyl-ether (TMBE) mixture. The tubes were vortexed and then sonicated for 30 min in an ice-cold sonication bath (taken for a brief vortex every 10 min). Then, a UPLC-grade water:methanol (3:1, v/v) solution (0.5 ml) containing 13Cand 15N-labeled amino acid standard mix (Sigma, Miamisburg OH, USA) was added to the tubes followed by centrifugation. The polar phase was re-extracted as described above with 0.5 ml of TMBE. The lower polar phase was stored at -80 <sup>o</sup> C until polar metabolite analysis. Finally, the polar phase samples were lyophilized and the pellets were dissolved using 150 µl DDW:methanol (1:1, v/v), centrifuged twice (at maximum speed) to remove possible precipitates, and injected into an LC–MS system.

#### *LC–MS analysis of polar metabolites*

Metabolic profiling of the polar phase was performed as described in Zheng *et al.* (2015) with minor modifications (Zheng et al., 2015). Briefly, analysis was performed using an Acquity I class UPLC System combined with a mass spectrometer (Thermo Exactive Plus Orbitrap, Mundelein, Illinois USA) which was operated in negative ionization mode. LC separation was done using the SeQuant Zic-pHilic (150 mm × 2.1 mm) with the SeQuant guard column (20 mm × 2.1 mm) (Merck, Darmstadt Germany). Mobile phase B was acetonitrile and Mobile Phase A was 20 mM ammonium carbonate with 0.1% (w/v) ammonia hydroxide in water:acetonitrile (80:20, v/v). The flow rate was kept at 200 μl min-1 and the gradient was: 0–2 min 75% B, 17 min 12.5% B, 17.1 min 25% B, 19 min 25% B, 19.1 min 75% B, 23 min 75% B.

#### *Polar metabolite data analysis*

Data were processed using TraceFinder Thermo Fisher software where detected compounds were identified by accurate mass, retention time, isotope pattern and fragments, and verified using an in-house mass spectrum library. Relative abundance of polar metabolites presented in Supporting Table S1 was performed by normalizing the peak area of each metabolite to the sample's dry weight. Data is presented as arbitrary units (AU).
