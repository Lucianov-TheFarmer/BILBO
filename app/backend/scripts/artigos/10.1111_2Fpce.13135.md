#### **Abstract**

The circadian clock enables organisms to rapidly adapt to the ever-changing environmental conditions that are caused by daily light/dark cycles. Circadian clock genes universally affect key agricultural traits, particularly flowering time. Here, we show that *OsPRR37*, a circadian clock gene, delays rice flowering time in an expression level-dependent manner. Using high-throughput mRNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on an *OsPRR37* overexpressing transgenic line (*OsPRR37*-OE5) and the recipient parent Guangluai4 (GL) that contains the loss-of-function *Osprr37*, we identify 14,992 genes that display diurnal rhythms, which account for 52.9% of the transcriptome. Overexpressing *OsPRR37* weakens the transcriptomic rhythms and alters the phases of rhythmic genes. In total, 3,210 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are identified, among which 1,863 rhythmic DEGs show a correlation between the change of absolute amplitudes and the mean expression levels. We further reveal that OsPRR37 functions as a transcriptional repressor to repress the expression levels and amplitudes of day-phased clock genes. More importantly, *OsPRR37* confers expanded regulation on the evening-phased rhythmic DEGs by repressing the morning-phased rhythmic DEGs. Further study shows that *OsPRR37* expands its regulation on flowering pathways by repressing *Ehd1*. Thus, our results demonstrate an expanded regulation mechanism of the circadian clock on the diurnal rhythms of the transcriptome.

#### **Brief summary**

Circadian clock universally regulates the rhythms of gene transcription. Here, we show that rice circadian clock gene, *OsPRR37*, globally regulates gene transcription in the changing diurnal environment. *OsPRR37* confers an expanded regulation of the evening-phased rhythmic DEGs by repressing the morning-phased rhythmic DEGs. Our results demonstrate an expanded regulation mechanism of the circadian clock on the diurnal rhythms of the transcriptome, which provides a potential way to improve rice agronomic traits by transcriptionally or genetically manipulating the circadian clock genes and clock-output pathways.

#### **Introduction**

It is a biological miracle that life arose on early earth, because the natural environment is not constant due to the daily rotation of the earth on its axis. This astrophysical movement results in day and night and is accompanied by diurnal changes in light, temperature, and other environmental characteristics. Over hundreds of millions of years, organisms have evolved an internal timer called a circadian clock to advance their capacity to better adapt to this changing planet (Dvornyk, Vinogradova, & Nevo, 2003). These evolved circadian clocks coordinate the rhythms of various physiological outputs, such as sleep-wake behavior in mammals and photoperiodic flowering in plants, with a diurnally changing environment (Greenham & McClung, 2015; Paschos, 2015).

The molecular components of the circadian clock are not conserved between animals and plants; however, the circadian clocks in these organisms share a similar network architecture of positive and negative feedback loops that are formed by transcriptional and post-translational regulation among the clock components (Doherty & Kay, 2010). Numerous studies in the model plant *Arabidopsis thaliana* have identified a central circadian clock loop that comprises two Myb-like transcription factors, namely, CIRCADIAN CLOCK-ASSOCIATED 1 (CCA1), LATE ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL (LHY), and a PSEUDO RESPONSE REGULATOR family member, specifically, the TIMING OF CAB EXPRESSION 1 (TOC1 or PRR1). In the transcriptional feedback loop, CCA1 and LHY function in the morning to repress the expression of *TOC1*, whereas evening emergence of TOC1 in turn suppresses the expression of *CCA1* and *LHY* by directly binding to the promoters of these genes (Alabadi et al., 2001; Gendron et al., 2012; Huang et al., 2012; Perales & Mas, 2007). Similarly, another three PRR family members, namely, PRR5, PRR7 and PRR9, repress the expression of *CCA1* and *LHY,* while CCA1 and LHY promote the expression of *PRR7* and *PRR9* to form another feedback loop in the clock network (Farre, Harmer, Harmon, Yanovsky, & Kay, 2005; Nakamichi et al., 2010, 2012; Wang, Kim, & Somers, 2013). Several studies that relate to the circadian clock have revealed the importance of *PRR* family genes in the formation of the plant circadian clock and in the circadian regulation of physiological responses, such as photoperiod control of flowering time, plant growth and stress responses (Fukushima et al., 2009; Nakamichi et al., 2007, 2009; Ruts, Matsubara, Wiese-Klinkenberg, & Walter, 2012; Yamashino et al., 2008).

As the network map of the circadian clock in *Arabidopsis* becomes much clearer, an increasing number of circadian clock homologs in various crops have been identified to play a major role in flowering time regulation (Bendix, Marshall, & Harmon, 2015). For example, *Ppd-H1*, an ortholog of *AtPRR7* in barley, regulates flowering phenotypes and affects adaptation to the photoperiod by altering the circadian expression of the photoperiod pathway gene *CONSTANS* and by reducing the expression of the key flowering regulator *FT* (Turner, Beales, Faure, Dunford, & Laurie, 2005). Another example is *SbPRR37*, which is a major repressor of *sorghum* flowering on long days (Murphy et al., 2011). In addition, eight *PRR* family genes in *Brassica rapa* are under circadian control and located close to QTLs for flowering time (Kim et al., 2007a, 2012). Rice has five members of the *OsPRR* gene family and other clock-associated genes that are highly conserved with clock genes in *Arabidopsis*

(Murakami, Ashikari, Miura, Yamashino, & Mizuno, 2003; Murakami, Tago, Yamashino, & Mizuno, 2007a, b). Recently, *OsPRR37*, an ortholog of *AtPRR7*, was discovered to pleiotropically regulate flowering time, plant height and grain yield (Gao et al., 2014; Koo et al., 2013; Liu, Liu, Zhang, & Xing, 2013; Yan et al., 2013). In a previous study, we revealed that *OsPRR37/GCA1* simultaneously contributes to variations in important agronomic traits and the corresponding general combining ability (Liu et al., 2015). Nevertheless, how Os*PRR37* associates with internal clock genes in rice to affect the transcriptomic changes in the diurnally changing environment remains unclear.

In the present study, we first show that the clock gene *OsPRR37* regulates rice flowering by enhancing photoperiod sensitivity, and that the flowering time is significantly correlated to the expression level of *OsPRR37*. We further use high-throughput mRNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on the time-course transcriptomes of the *OsPRR37* overexpression line of *OsPRR37*-OE5 and the recipient parent GL to identify the rhythmically expressed genes and reveal the alterations of the rice transcriptome under natural long-day conditions. To gain insight into the regulatory mechanism of *OsPRR37*-output genes, we focused on the statistical analysis of the differentially expressed genes that exhibited diurnal rhythms. We also demonstrate that OsPRR37 functions as a transcriptional repressor in the regulatory network of the circadian clock and primarily represses the transcription of morning-phased output genes. Finally, the transcription levels of the key flowering regulators in GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5 were profiled to demonstrate how the flowering pathways are altered by *OsPRR37* to delay rice flowering time.

#### **Materials and methods**

# **Plant materials**

The following materials were used in the present study: two elite rice varieties Guangluai4 (GL) and Teqing (TQ); a nearly isogenic line, NIL-*OsPRR37*, in the GL background with the introgressed segment of *OsPRR37* from TQ; two transgenic overexpression lines *OsPRR37*-OE5 and *OsPRR37*-OE9; and three F<sup>1</sup> hybrids. The three F<sup>1</sup> hybrids of GL/NIL-*OsPRR37*, GL/*OsPRR37*-OE5 and GL/*OsPRR37*-OE9 were derived by crossing the maternal parent GL to the paternal parents of NIL-*OsPRR37*, *OsPRR37*-OE5 and *OsPRR37*-OE9, respectively.

#### **Flowering time analysis**

The 20-day-old seedlings of GL, TQ and NIL-*OsPRR37* were transferred into four controlled growth chambers on June 19, 2013 at the China National Rice Research Institute, Hangzhou (30°05'N, 119°95'E), Zhejiang, China. The four growth conditions were 24°C/11.5 h, 24°C/14.5 h, 28°C/11.5 h and 28°C/14.5 h (Table S1). The flowering promotion rates by high temperature (28°C) and short-day (11.5 h) were calculated according to equations (1) and (2), respectively.

$$P_{28^{\circ}C} = (DTH_{24^{\circ}C/11.5h} - DTH_{28^{\circ}C/11.5h}) \div DTH_{24^{\circ}C/11.5h} \times 100\%$$
(1)

$$P_{11.5h} = (DTH_{28^{\circ}C/14.5h} - DTH_{28^{\circ}C/11.5h}) \div DTH_{28^{\circ}C/14.5h} \times 100\%$$
(2)

P28°C and P11.5h represent the promotion rates by high temperature (28°C) and short-day

(11.5 h), respectively. The DTHs that were followed by subscribed treatments are the number of days to heading in the corresponding treatments. The DTHs of ten plants for each genotype were measured in each treatment.

For the correlation analysis of flowering time and *OsPRR37* expression levels, the rice plants were cultivated under routine field management conditions on May 8, 2014 in Wuhan (30°52'N, 114°32'E), China. The seedlings were harvested at 45 days after seed sowing and used for RNA-seq and qRT-PCR. At least 20 plants of each genotype were recorded for the flowering time under natural long-day conditions. The highest and lowest environmental temperatures from May to September are provided in the supporting information (Fig. S1).

#### **RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR**

All plant materials were planted according to a randomized block design with three replicates. The precise sampling times were set as 4:00, 8:00, 12:00, 16:00, 20:00 and 0:00 over a single day. The uppermost fully expanded leaves from more than three seedlings were harvested, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored at -80°C for RNA extraction. The three biological repeats for each time point corresponded to triplicate blocks in the field. Total RNA was isolated from each leaf sample with a TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The total RNA was treated with RNase-free DNase I (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed with the same procedures as previously reported (Liu et al., 2015). The biallelic expression of *OsPRR37* and *Osprr37* was profiled by

using the primer pair of 5'-TGACAAAGCCTAGTACAAATAAGGAGAG-3' and 5'-CAGTGCTGTGCAGGATGAAATG-3', and the TQ allele of *OsPRR37* was profiled by using the primer pair of 5'- CCACTACAAAGAACGTTGTGACAAA-3' (the underlined nucleotides represent the 8-bp deletion site of the loss-of-function allele *Osprr37* in GL) and 5'-CCATTAGCCTTAACAGCTGAGGGT-3'.

#### **Library construction, RNA sequencing and alignment**

The kit NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module, NEB #E7490 (New England Biolabs, Inc.), was used to isolate mRNA from 1 µg of total RNA. The purified mRNA was used to construct the cDNA library according to the protocol that is provided with the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, NEB #E7530 (New England Biolabs, Inc.). The concentration and insert size of the cDNA library were verified by Qubit 2.0 and Agilent 2100. Quantitative PCR was applied to accurately quantify the effective concentration of the cDNA library. The library was sequenced by using an Illumina HiSeq2500 platform with a paired-end sequencing length of 125 bp (PE125) at Biomarker Technologies (Beijing, China). The raw reads that were produced by using the sequencing by synthesis (SBS) technique were filtered by removing the adapter and primer sequences and were further filtered based on a low-quality score. Specifically, reads in which more than 20% of the bases showed a quality score under Q20 were removed. The remaining high-quality reads were the final "Clean Data" that were used for the alignment analysis. These "Clean Data" were mapped to the rice reference genome of Nipponbare (IRGSP-1.0) by using

TopHat2, which is a sequence alignment software with a high efficiency (Kim et al., 2013; Langmead, Trapnell, Pop, & Salzberg, 2009).

#### **RNA-seq data normalization and filtration**

The normalized counts per million (CPM) was calculated according to Cuffdiff in the Cufflinks software (Jiang & Wong, 2009; Trapnell et al., 2010). The Pearson's correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the correlation between any biological repeats at the same time point (Schulze, Kanwar, Golzenleuchter, Therneau, & Beutler, 2012). To eliminate the non-expressed genes from the rice transcriptome, the genes that showed CPM > 0 in at least three samples in either GL or *OsPRR37*-OE5 were considered to comprise the entire transcriptome in the present study. To improve the power of subsequent statistical analyses, we further filtered out all lowly expressed genes by reserving the genes that had at least three samples with CPM > 5 in either GL or *OsPRR37*-OE5. These filtrations were based on the assumption that at least three samples in one genotype show CPM > 0 for an expressed gene and CPM > 5 for a highly expressed gene, because the tri-replicate sampling pattern was applied in the present study.

#### **Rhythmic gene analysis**

Rhythmic genes were identified by an algorithm in ARSER, which employs autoregressive spectral estimation to predict the periodicity of expression profiles, and subsequently uses a harmonic regression model to fit the time-course RNA-seq data (Yang & Su, 2010). The three replicates that were sampled in one day were considered as eighteen consecutive time-course samples across three days. The rhythmic parameters were estimated by three methods (yule-walker, mle and burg) in ARSER. To ensure that the output parameters were comparable among genotypes, the "ARS.R" script that is used in the MetaCycle package (https://github.com/gangwug/MetaCycle/blob/master/R/ARS.R) was modified to use the mle method only (Muller et al., 2016; Wu, Anafi, Hughes, Kornacker, & Hogenesch, 2016). Diurnal time-course data are expected to possess cycling periods of exactly 24 h due to the entrainment of the circadian clock by environmental signals such as light and temperature. Therefore, ARSER was run with the default parameters of a period range between 20 and 28 h. The "ARS.R" script was implemented in R (version 3.2.0). The output genes that were not covered by mle and marked as "default" were reserved because the default period was equal to the actual diurnal period of 24 h. The phase parameter that was produced by ARSER was used to generate a statistical chart of the phase distribution. Phases of more than 24 h were subtracted by 24 h prior to the distribution analysis.

We adopted two definitions of amplitude, namely, the fold-change amplitude (fAMP) and absolute amplitude (aAMP), in the amplitude analysis of rhythmic genes. The fAMP of rhythmic genes was defined as the fold change in expression level between the peak and trough expressions (Hughes, Grant, Paquin, Qian, & Nitabach, 2012). The peak-to-trough fold-change was calculated as the mean of the three maximum CPM values (Meanmax) divided by the mean of the three minimum CPM values (Meanmin). To enable calculations and reduce noise, the Meanmin was set to 1 CPM if the Meanmin < 1 CPM. The aAMP was considered to be half the distance from the peak to the trough of the diurnal curve, consistent with the amplitude in ARSER (Yang & Su, 2010). Thus, the aAMP was directly obtained from the amplitude outputs of ARSER. An analysis of Pearson's correlation coefficient was achieved by using the "R Stats Package". Heatmaps were generated by implementing the "gplots" package in R language.

#### **Differentially expressed gene determination**

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from highly expressed genes, we used the unpaired *t* test on the samples at each time point (by looking for DEGs that show a difference at each time point without considering the change trend among the time points) and the paired *t* test on all the time-course samples (by looking for DEGs that show significant difference from the same change trend). The three samples at each time point were considered to be three replicates in the unpaired *t* test with the equal variances assumption. Because only three replicates at each time point were used, the *P* values < 0.05 without FDR calibration were considered significant (Pawitan, Michiels, Koscielny, Gusnanto, & Ploner, 2005). However, the 18 samples of GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5 were regarded as consecutive time-course samples of 3 days with no repeat in the paired *t* test. The *P* values were further used for FDR calibration, and the genes with FDR < 0.05 were considered to be significant (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). These significant genes that were revealed by either the unpaired *t* test or paired *t* test comprise the significantly affected genes (SAGs). To obtain robust DEGs, we further filtered the SAGs with the fold change of the mean expression levels that was lower than 2/3 or higher than 4/3. The DEGs were divided into rhythmic DEGs and non-rhythmic DEGs based on whether the DEGs were previously identified as rhythmic genes or non-rhythmic genes. The fold change of the mean expression levels was calculated with the mean CPM value of *OsPRR37*-OE5 divided by the mean CPM value of GL (the mean CPM value of GL is 0.001 if it equals to 0).

#### **Transient LUC activity assay**

Full-length OsPRR37 and truncated Osprr37 correspond to proteins of the TQ and GL alleles, respectively (Liu et al., 2015). To investigate the transcriptional activation activity, OsPRR37 and Osprr37 were fused with the GAL4 DNA binding domain (GAL4BD) and co-transformed into rice protoplasts with a reporter construct that contained the 4×UAS region and a 35S promoter that was fused with the LUC cDNA (Cheng et al., 2015; Jing et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2011). To determine the transcriptional repression activity, a widely used transcriptional activator, VP16, was fused with the GAL4BD (GAL4BD-VP16) before the fusion with OsPRR37 and Osprr37. The *Renilla LUC* (*REN*) that was driven by the 35S promoter was used as the internal control. The relative activity was expressed as the ratio of LUC to REN. The LUC activity was detected with the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

#### **Results**

## *OsPRR37* **affects rice photoperiodic flowering in an expression level-dependent manner** The circadian clock genes of crop plants play an important role in regulating key agricultural traits, particularly flowering time (Bendix, et al., 2015). Photoperiod and temperature are the two main environmental factors of plant flowering (Song, Gao, & Luan, 2012). To investigate whether *OsPRR37* affects rice flowering time by enhancing sensitivity to the photoperiod or temperature, we analyzed the flowering time of the parents GL and TQ, and NIL-*OsPRR37* under four controlled growth conditions (for details see the Materials and methods section). The flowering times of GL, TQ and NIL-*OsPRR37* were significantly delayed by low temperature under short-day conditions (24°C/11.5 h compared with 28°C/11.5 h) (Fig. 1a). Additionally, the long-day photoperiod under high temperature conditions (28°C/14.5 h compared with 28°C/11.5 h) significantly delayed the flowering time of TQ and NIL-*OsPRR37* whereas GL showed no difference. These results indicate that TQ and NIL*-OsPRR37* show sensitivity to the photoperiod and that all three materials show sensitivity to temperature (Fig. 1a). In addition, low temperature and long-day conditions (24°C/14.5 h) have the strongest effect on delaying the rice flowering time. Further analysis of the rice flowering promotion rates by high temperature (28°C) and short-day (11.5 h) found that short-day increased the flowering promotion rate of NIL-*OsPRR37* by 19.7% and 12.4% over GL and TQ, respectively (Fig. 1b). However, the flowering promotion rate of NIL-*OsPRR37* by high temperature is merely increased by 1.8% and 3.1% over GL and TQ,

respectively. These results show that *OsPRR37* delays the rice flowering time mainly through enhanced rice sensitivity to the photoperiod and slightly affects rice sensitivity to temperature.

When estimating the general combining ability of rice (Liu et al., 2015), we observed that the flowering times of the three F<sup>1</sup> hybrids, GL/NIL-*OsPRR37*, GL/*OsPRR37*-OE5 and GL/*OsPRR37*-OE9, were later than GL but earlier than the parental parent with homozygous *OsPRR37* (Fig. 1c). This result indicates that *OsPRR37* exhibites a semi-dominant effect on the flowering time. To determine whether this semi-dominant effect is associated with the expression level of *OsPRR37*, we performed quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) with the primers for the biallelic expression of *OsPRR37/Osprr37* and the TQ allele *OsPRR37*. The mean expression levels of *OsPRR37/Osprr37* in NIL-*OsPRR37*, *OsPRR37*-OE5 and *OsPRR37*-OE9 were 0.9-, 2.0- and 1.5-fold that of GL/NIL-*OsPRR37*, GL/*OsPRR37*-OE5 and GL/*OsPRR37*-OE9, respectively (Fig. 1d). The almost equal expression level in NIL-*OsPRR37* and GL/NIL-*OsPRR37* is discordant with their differences in the flowering time. However, the expression of *OsPRR37* (the TQ allele) in NIL-*OsPRR37*, *OsPRR37*-OE5 and *OsPRR37*-OE9 was 1.7-, 2.3- and 1.9-fold that of GL/NIL-*OsPRR37*, GL/*OsPRR37*-OE5 and GL/*OsPRR37*-OE9, respectively, which coincides with the intermediate flowering time of the F<sup>1</sup> hybrids (Fig. 1e).

Furthermore, the flowering time of *OsPRR37*-OE5 and *OsPRR37*-OE9 were significantly delayed by 50.1 and 49.3 days, respectively, compared with the recipient parent GL; they were even delayed by 22.5 and 21.8 days, respectively, compared with NIL-*OsPRR37* (Fig. 1c). This extremely delayed flowering time positively correlates with the 4-fold higher expression levels of *OsPRR37* in *OsPRR37*-OE5 and *OsPRR37*-OE9 than NIL-*OsPRR37* (Fig. 1e). In addition, the Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (Spearman's *r*) between the DTH and *OsPRR37/Osprr37* expression levels for all seven rice materials was 0.93 (*P* = 6.7E-3), while the correlation between DTH and the *OsPRR37* expression levels was stronger (*r* = 1.00, *P* = 4.0E-4). Taken together, these results demonstrate that *OsPRR37* affects rice photoperiodic flowering in an expression level-dependent manner. These results also indicated that the expression profile of *OsPRR37*-output genes in overexpression lines is more profoundly altered than the expression profile of NIL-*OsPRR37* when compared with GL. To identify the robust *OsPRR37*-output genes, the overexpression line *OsPRR37*-OE5 and the recipient parent GL were selected for diurnal transcriptome analysis.

#### **Identification of diurnal rhythmic genes from the time-course transcriptomes**

To identify the diurnal rhythmic genes, we investigated the time-course transcriptomes of GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5 with RNA-seq. A total of 171.1 Gb clean data for 36 samples was obtained, and the paired-end read (fragments) number ranged from 13.4 to 24.7 million per sample. This sequencing dataset generated 81.9 and 89.2 Gb clean data in GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5, respectively. The percentage of nucleotides with sequencing quality values greater than or equal to Q30 (% ≥Q30) for all time-course samples ranged from 85.0% to 90.4% (Table S2). The clean dataset alignment showed that an average of 30.4 million reads were successfully mapped onto the rice reference genome, which accounted for 80.6% of the total reads (Table S3). Further identification of the uniquely mapped reads revealed that an average of 29.1 million reads had a unique mapped locus. These reads accounted for 77.0% of the total reads, which confirms that most of the sequenced reads had a unique locus in the rice genome. The replicate samples showed a high correlation of gene expression levels, with an average *r* square of 0.95 (Table S4). These high sequencing quality data will strengthen further investigations of the regulatory mechanism that underlies the transcriptomic changes.

With the primary filtration of non-expressed genes, the rice transcriptome comprises of 28,353 expressed genes in the present study. To improve the power of the statistical analysis, we further filtered out 5,367 lowly expressed genes and obtained 22,986 highly expressed genes, including 21,571 annotated and 1,415 un-annotated genes (the filtration details are described in the Methods section). The ARSER algorithm was applied on the time-course RNA-seq data of the 22,986 highly expressed genes and the genes with fdr\_BH<0.01 were considered to be rhythmic genes. Thus 12,828 and 12,277 rhythmic genes were identified from GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5, respectively (Fig. 2a). The combined set of rhythmic genes from GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5 resulted in a total of 14,992 rhythmic genes, which accounted for 65.2% of the highly expressed genes and 52.9% of the rice transcriptome. This percentage of rhythmic genes was higher than in a previous microarray study in which 34.7% of the transcripts were regulated by photo/thermocycles (Filichkin et al., 2011). These data indicate that more than half of the rice transcriptomes evolved to display diurnal rhythms during rice adaptation to the changing diurnal environment.

#### **Effects of** *OsPRR37* **overexpression on the diurnal rhythms of rice transcriptome**

Within the 14,992 rhythmic genes, 10,113 were shared by GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5, 2,715 were unique in GL and 2,164 were unique in *OsPRR37*-OE5 (Fig. 2a). These data show that the unique rhythmic genes in *OsPRR37*-OE5 are 20.3% less than GL. Furthermore, the ARSER output FDRs in *OsPRR37*-OE5 significantly correlated with GL (*r* = 0.471, *P* < 2.2E-16) and the Y-intercept of the line correlation was higher than the origin of axis, which indicates that the ARSER output FDRs in *OsPRR37*-OE5 were generally higher than in GL (Fig. 2b). These results suggest that the transcriptomic rhythms were weakened by the overexpression of *OsPRR37*.

The peak-to-trough fAMP and the aAMP that measured as half the distance from the peak to the trough are usually used to describe the amplitude of rhythmic genes (Hughes, et al., 2012; Yang & Su, 2010). To investigate how the amplitude was altered by the overexpression of *OsPRR37*, we first analyzed the fAMP of all 14,992 rhythmic genes in GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5. Interestingly, we found that the number of genes that were distributed in high fAMP, which ranged in the group from 8.5~9.0 to 10.5~, were all decreased in *OsPRR37*-OE5 compared with GL. In contrast, the number of genes that were distributed in low fAMP, which ranged from ~1.5 to 4.5~4.5 were all increased in *OsPRR37*-OE5 (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, the Chi-square test revealed that the distribution of the fAMP was significantly different between GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5 ( <sup>2</sup> = 66.45, *df* = 19, *P* < 1.0E-4). These results indicate that the overexpression of *OsPRR37* decreased the number of high-fAMP rhythmic genes and increased low-fAMP rhythmic genes. Second, the distribution of the aAMP was significantly different between GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5 ( <sup>2</sup> = 26.86, *df* = 11, *P* < 0.01) and mainly distributed at the aAMP of 25~6 and 26~7 (Fig. 2d). Similar to the change of the fAMP, the decreased rhythmic genes in *OsPRR37*-OE5 mainly occurred in the high aAMP from 26~7 to 210~, and the increased rhythmic genes were in the low aAMP from 21~2 to 25~6. Taken together, the number of rhythmic genes with high amplitudes (both the fAMP and aAMP) decreased, which potentially contributed to the weakened transcriptomic rhythms in *OsPRR37*-OE5.

By using the phases from the ARSER algorithm, we found that the rhythmic genes encompassed all phases of the day in GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5 (Fig. 2e). Specifically, a large proportion of rhythmic genes peaked in the morning (8:00 to 11:00) and afternoon (15:00 to 17:00) in both GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5. This result is different from a previous rice microarray study that showed that many transcripts peaked several hours before the light/dark transitions (dawn and dusk) (Filichkin et al., 2011). However, the present data show a bimodal phase distribution of rice rhythmic genes at several hours after sunrise and several hours before sunset. These data indicate that many rhythmic genes function in the morning and afternoon in rice. Moreover, the entire phase distribution between GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5 was significantly different ( <sup>2</sup> = 40.97, *df* = 23, *P* < 0.05). These results indicate that the overexpression of *OsPRR37* alters the phases of rhythmic genes.

### **Identification of** *OsPRR37***-output genes and the correlation analysis of expression levels and amplitudes**

To globally identify the genes that are regulated by *OsPRR37* (*OsPRR37*-output genes), we used an unpaired *t* test and a paired *t* test on the 22,986 highly expressed genes of the time-course RNA-seq data (Fig. 3a; for more details, see the Methods section). We initially found 10,073 significant genes in the unpaired *t* test (*P* < 0.05) and 5,884 significant genes in the paired *t* test (FDR < 0.05), which were combined, and this generated 11,679 SAGs. To obtain robust DEGs for further analysis, the SAGs were filtered by the criteria that the fold change of the mean expression levels (*OsPRR37*-OE5 / GL) must be lower than 2/3 or higher than 4/3. Finally, 3,210 DEGs were identified, including 1,863 rhythmic DEGs and 1,347 non-rhythmic DEGs (Fig. 3a). Both rhythmic and non-rhythmic DEGs could be *OsPRR37*-output genes, however, rhythmic DEGs should be closer to than non-rhythmic DEGs the regulation network of circadian genes (Atwood et al., 2011). To reveal the regulatory mechanism on the diurnal rhythms of *OsPRR37*-output genes, we primarily focused on rhythmic DEGs for further statistical analysis.

To reveal the relationships between the expression levels and the amplitudes in rhythmic DEGs, a correlation analysis between the changes of the mean expression level (Mean\_change) and amplitude (aAMP\_change and fAMP\_change) was performed. A positive correlation was found between the Mean\_change and aAMP\_change (*r* = 0.843, *P* < 2.2E-16). Although 91.3% of the rhythmic DEGs, including 1,097 up-regulated genes and 603 down-regulated genes, showed a positive correlation, we also observed that 8.7% of the

rhythmic DEGs, including 148 up-regulated genes and 15 down-regulated genes showed a negative correlation (Fig. 3b). This result demonstrates that the Mean\_change highly correlates with the aAMP\_change, and that the direction of change exhibits a relative independence. However, no correlation was found between the Mean\_change and fAMP\_change (*r* = -0.031, *P* = 0.18). The up-regulated genes with increased fAMP and decreased fAMP were 632 and 613, respectively, which suggests that no bias was observed for the up-regulated rhythmic DEGs (Fig. 3c). However, the number of down-regulated genes with decreased fAMP (373 genes) was greater than with increased fAMP (245 genes), which indicates a bias towards decreased fAMP for the down-regulated rhythmic DEGs. In general, although the aAMP and fAMP were usually used to describe the amplitude of rhythmic genes, only the aAMP\_change was correlated with Mean\_change in the rhythmic DEGs.

## **OsPRR37 represses the expression levels and amplitudes of day-phased circadian clock genes**

Among the 3,210 *OsPRR37*-output genes, 1,245 of the 1,863 (66.8%) rhythmic DEGs and 1,044 of the 1,347 (77.5%) non-rhythmic DEGs were up-regulated in *OsPRR37*-OE5. This unbalanced number of up- and down-regulated genes prompted further examination on which genes would contain the direct targets that were regulated by *OsPRR37*. One solution was to determine whether OsPRR37 functions as a transcriptional repressor or as an activator. Thus, the transcriptional activity of OsPRR37 was surveyed with a transient luciferase (LUC) expression system in rice protoplasts (Fig. 4a) (Cheng et al., 2015; Jing et al., 2013). The results show that no effect on the transcription of the LUC reporter gene was detected when the effectors were the fusion of the GAL4 DNA binding domain (GAL4BD) with OsPRR37 or Osprr37, which indicates that OsPRR37 had no transcriptional activation activity (Fig. 4b). However, the LUC reporter gene was dramastically activated by GAL4BD fused with a transcriptional activator VP16 (GAL4BD-VP16), which was almost completely inhibited by OsPRR37 (GAL4BD-VP16-OsPRR37) (Fig. 4c). Moreover, the truncated Osprr37 also showed a transcriptional repression activity, but the repression effect was significantly weakened compared with OsPRR37. Therefore, these data demonstrate that OsPRR37 functions as a transcriptional repressor and the C terminus of OsPRR37 play a role on the repression effect. This result also suggests that the direct targets of OsPRR37 are included in the down-regulated DEGs and that a large number of up-regulated DEGs are indirectly regulated.

To reveal how the clock genes are affected by the overexpression of *OsPRR37*, we monitored the diurnal patterns of *OsPRR37* and eleven other clock genes, including five day-phased genes (*OsCCA1*, *OsPRR73*, *OsPRR1*, *OsPRR59*, and *OsPRR95*) and six evening-phased genes (*OsGI*, *OsPCL1*, *OsELF3-1*, *OsELF3-2*, *OsZTL1*, and *OsZTL2*) (Greenham & McClung, 2015; Murakami et al., 2007b). The mean expression level of *OsPRR37* in *OsPRR37*-OE5 was elevated 4.6-fold higher than in GL, and the diurnal peak shifted from 12:00 to 16:00 according to the RNA-seq data (Fig. 4d), which was also verified by the qRT-PCR results (Fig. 1d and e). Interestingly, the day-phased genes *OsCCA1*, *OsPRR73* and *OsPRR59* were down-regulated by 34.9%, 21.0% and 42.7%, respectively, and their repressed expression levels encompassed the time points with significant differences that were revealed by the unpaired *t* test. However, the expression levels of *OsPRR1* and *OsPRR59* were only reduced by 9.4% and 2.7%, respectively. Further study found that their peaks (16:00) were significantly decreased, but the troughs (0:00, 4:00 and 8:00 for *OsPRR1*, 4:00 and 8:00 for *OsPRR59*) were increased, which indicates that *OsPRR1* and *OsPRR59* are indirectly regulated by *OsPRR37*. These results showed that the overexpression of *OsPRR37* repressed the transcription of *OsCCA1*, *OsPRR73* and *OsPRR59* and the peak expression of *OsPRR1* and *OsPRR59*. Moreover, the evening-phased genes *OsPCL1* and *OsELF3-2* were up-regulated, *OsGI* showed no difference, and *OsELF3-1*, *OsZTL1* and *OsZTL2* were down-regulated by no more than 8%. Taken together, these results indicate that *OsPRR37* mainly represses the transcription of the day-phased clock genes *OsCCA1*, *OsPRR73* and *OsPRR59* and indirectly regulate *OsPRR1*, *OsPRR59* and the evening-phased clock genes.

We further analyzed the proportion and direction of change in the aAMP and mean expression levels (Mean) for the circadian clock genes when comparing *OsPRR37*-OE5 with GL. Although the aAMP was proportionally changed with the Mean for Os*CCA1*, Os*PRR73*, Os*PRR95* and Os*PCL1*, we also observed that the aAMP and Mean were disproportionately changed for *OsPRR1* and *OsPRR59* and even changed in the opposite direction for *OsELF3-1*, *OsELF3-2*, *OsZTL1* and *OsZTL2*. The dramatically reduced aAMP (-30.8% and -22.4%) with little change in the Mean (-9.4% and -2.7%) of *OsPRR1* and *OsPRR59* was caused by the significantly decreased peaks and increased expression levels at/around troughs (Fig. 4d). However, the peaks of *OsELF3-1*, *OsELF3-2*, *OsZTL1* and *OsZTL2* were not significantly

changed; thus, the opposite change in the direction of *OsELF3-1* (Mean: -7.4%, aAMP: 0.36%), *OsZTL1* (Mean: -8.0%, aAMP: 0.07%) and *OsZTL2*(Mean: -5.3%, aAMP: 7.3%) resulted from the decreased troughs (Fig. 4d). However, the opposite direction in *OsELF3-2* (Mean: 9.8% and aAMP: -20.0%) was because of the significantly increased expression levels at the trough (around 8:00 and 12:00). These results demonstrate that the proportion and direction of change in the mean expression level and amplitude show a relative independence. Moreover, the aAMP of the studied day-phased genes *OsCCA1*, *OsPRR73*, *OsPRR1*, *OsPRR59*, *OsPRR95* and only one evening-phased genes *OsELF3-2* were decreased by *OsPRR37* overexpression. Taken together, *OsPRR37* mainly represses the amplitudes of day-phased circadian clock genes.

#### *OsPRR37* **confers an expanded regulation of** *OsPRR37***-output genes**

To reveal the regulatory mechanism of *OsPRR37*-output genes, we compared the diurnal expression patterns between GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5 by separately plotting the heatmaps of the down-regulated and up-regulated rhythmic DEGs. Strong diurnal rhythms with clear peaks and troughs were observed for both the down-regulated and up-regulated rhythmic DEGs (Fig. 5a and 5b). The peak phases encompassed all times of the day in both GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5. However, we observed that a large proportion of the down-regulated rhythmic DEGs showed peak phases in the morning while many up-regulated rhythmic DEGs showed peak phases in the evening (Fig. 5a and 5b). By using the phases that were estimated by ARSER, we generated the phase distribution with a kernel density estimation (KDE) curve for the down-regulated and up-regulated rhythmic DEGs in GL and *OsPRR37*-OE5, respectively (Fig. 5c and d). The distribution of phases was no longer bimodal similar to the global rhythmic genes (Fig. 2e). The phase of the down-regulated rhythmic DEGs was mainly distributed at around 9:00 in the morning (Fig. 5c). This distribution pattern was in phase with the diurnal expression pattern that *OsPRR37* gradually increased its expression level from the early morning until noon in non-transgenic NIL-*OsPRR37* (Fig. 1d). However, the phase of the up-regulated rhythmic DEGs was primarily distributed at approximately 0:00 at night (Fig. 5d), which exhibited in an antiphase with the diurnal expression pattern that *OsPRR37* was barely expressed at 0:00 in NIL-*OsPRR37* (Fig. 1d). These results reveal that *OsPRR37* mainly represses the transcription of morning-phased output genes and indirectly up-regulates evening-phased output genes. Furthermore, the up-regulated rhythmic DEGs accounted for 66.8% of the rhythmic DEGs, and the down-regulated rhythmic DEGs only accounted for 33.2%. Thus, these results indicate that *OsPRR37* confers an expanded regulation of the evening-phased rhythmic DEGs by repressing the morning-phased rhythmic DEGs.

To identify the overrepresented ontological categories in down-regulated and up-regulated rhythmic DEGs, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis by using agriGO v2.0 which particularly focuses on agricultural species (Tian et al., 2017). We found diverse enriched GO categories in the down-regulated rhythmic DEGs including the regulation of transcription, RNA biosynthetic process, regulation of RNA metabolic process, regulation of gene expression, response to hormone stimulus, response to abiotic stimulus, response to light stimulus, response to chemical stimulus, transcription regulator activity, etc. (Table S5). These results indicate a major role for *OsPRR37* in regulating gene transcription and the response to light or other stimuli. These findings are consistent with *OsPRR37* as both a transcriptional repressor and a clock gene that is regulated by light and other stimuli (Dubois, Claeys, Van den Broeck, & Inze, 2017; Farré & Kay, 2007; Haydon, Mielczarek, Robertson, Hubbard, & Webb, 2013; Murphy et al., 2011; Oakenfull & Davis, 2017). However, we found that the enriched GO categories in the up-regulated rhythmic DEGs were the protein amino acid phosphorylation, protein modification process, phosphate metabolic process, signal transducer activity and protein kinase activity, etc. (Table S6). These GO categories are primarily associated with the translational or post-translational protein level, which are the events subsequent to the gene transcription GO categories that are identified in the down-regulated rhythmic DEGs. These results confirm that the biological functions of down-regulated rhythmic DEGs are closer than the up-regulated genes to the regulation network of *OsPRR37*.

In addition, we also investigated the non-rhythmic DEGs with heatmaps and GO analysis. On the heatmaps of the non-rhythmic DEGs, the rhythmic peak or trough expression is almost undetectable, which is consistent with the non-rhythmic expression characteristics of DEGs (Fig. S2). Among the 1,347 non-rhythmic DEGs, the down-regulated genes only accounted for 22.5%, which was less than the down-regulated genes of the rhythmic DEGs (33.2%). This result indicates that the likelihood of direct targets is smaller in non-rhythmic DEGs than in the rhythmic DEGs. Furthermore, only a small number of genes were significantly enriched in a total of nine GO categories, including two for the down-regulated and seven for the up-regulated non-rhythmic DEGs (Table S7). The enriched GO categories, such as development process, response to biotic stimulus, signal transducer activity, calcium ion binding and electron carrier activity were associated with post-translational regulation processes. These results show that non-rhythmic DEGs are not likely directly regulated by *OsPRR37*, and we thus proposed that *OsPRR37* expands its regulation to non-rhythmic DEGs through the more closely regulated rhythmic DEGs.

#### *OsPRR37* **confers an** *Ehd1***-mediated expanded regulation on rice flowering**

To characterize the role of *OsPRR37* in flowering pathways, we next examine the diurnal expression pattern of the key flowering regulators by using the RNA-seq data. The results show that the expression levels of *Ehd1* and its downstream florigen gene *RFT1* were almost reduced to zero at all time points in *OsPRR37*-OE5 compared with GL (Fig. 6a). Furthermore, although the mean expression levels of *Ghd8* and *Hd1* were slightly reduced (-7.3% and -14.3%), *OsPRR37* overexpression markedly reduced its aAMP (-54.9% and -51.5%) by significantly decreasing peaks and increasing troughs (Fig. 6a). Both *Ghd8* and *Hd1* were confirmed to repress *Ehd1*(Nemoto, Nonoue, Yano, & Izawa, 2016; Wei et al., 2010; Yan et al., 2011), which indicates that the slightly reduced expression of *Ghd8* and *Hd1* was a feedback response to the significantly reduced expression of *Ehd1*.

We also analyzed the MADS-box genes that have been frequently reported to control rice flowering time and flower organ identity(Hu et al., 2015; Khanday, Yadav, & Vijayraghavan, 2013; Kim, Lee, Kim, Nam, & An, 2007b; Kobayashi et al., 2012; Ryu et al., 2009). Among the studied MADS-box genes, *OsMADS1* integrates transcriptional and signaling pathways to regulate rice floret specification and meristem identity(Hu et al., 2015; Khanday et al., 2013), and *OsMADS14*, *OsMADS15* and *OsMADS18* coordinately functions downstream of *RFT1* to promote reproduction transition(Kobayashi et al., 2012). In the present study, the expression levels of all four of these MADS-box genes were remarkably reduced at all time points of the day (Fig. 6b), which indicates that these four MADS-box genes are the downstream genes of *OsPRR37*. In contrast, the mean expression level of *OsMADS50* was increased by 56.3%, while the mean expression level of *OsMADS56* was slightly decreased by 12.6% (Fig. 6b), which is consistent with the antagonistic functions of *OsMADS50* and *OsMADS56* upstream of *Ehd1* (Ryu et al., 2009). Additionally, the increased expression of *OsMADS50* was a likely feedback response to the extremely reduced expression of *Ehd1*, which indicates that the flowering pathway that is mediated by *OsMADS50* and *OsMADS56* is indirectly affected by *OsPRR37* through the feedback regulation of *Ehd1*. Similarly, the slightly increased expression of *OsMADS51* was another feedback regulation by *Ehd1*, because *OsMADS51* transmits the flowering signal from *OsGI* to *Ehd1* (Kim et al., 2007b).

Taken together, these results reveal that the flowering regulators *Ehd1*, *RFT1*, *OsMADS1*, *OsMADS14*, *OsMADS15* and *OsMADS18* function downstream of *OsPRR37* to delay rice flowering. Interestingly, these six genes are morning-phased genes that show peaked expression at 8:00, whereas *Ghd8* and *Hd1* are evening-phased genes, and *OsMADS50*, *OsMADS56* and *OsMADS51* are morning-phased genes (Fig. 6a and b). This result confirms that *OsPRR37* repressed morning-phased genes to expand its regulation of the indirectly regulated genes. Because *Ghd8*, *Hd1*, *OsMADS50*, *OsMADS56* and *OsMADS51* are upstream of *Ehd1* and the *OsMADS14*, *OsMADS15* and *OsMADS18* are downstream of *Ehd1*, we propose that the *Ehd1*-mediated expanded regulation comprises the direct downstream pathways (*RFT1*, *OsMADS14*, *OsMADS15* and *OsMADS18*) and the indirect upstream pathways (*Ghd8*, *Hd1*, *OsMADS50*, *OsMADS56* and *OsMADS51*). Therefore, *OsPRR37* confers an expanded regulation of rice photoperiodic flowering pathways mainly by repressing *Ehd1*.

#### **Discussion**

In the present study, we presented the diurnal alterations of the rice transcriptome, the *OsPRR37*-output genes, the circadian clock genes and flowering pathways that are conferred by *OsPRR37*. Based on the results of the present and previous studies, we propose a model that *OsPRR37* confers an expanded regulation of *OsPRR37*-output genes. The overexpression of *OsPRR37* represses the transcription of day-phased clock genes *OsCCA1*, *OsPRR73* and *OsPRR95* and indirectly regulates the expression of *OsPRR1*, *OsPRR59* and the evening-phased clock genes (Fig. 7a). Furthermore, *OsPRR37* represses the transcription of the morning-phased down-regulated output genes. In contrast, the evening-phased up-regulated output genes are indirectly affected by an expanded regulation through the down-regulated output genes that are closer to the regulatory network of *OsPRR37*. Therefore, we propose that the circadian clock can expand its regulation on many output genes by regulating a small proportion of output genes that are closer to the clock regulation network. This model could well explain how the plant circadian clock regulates a large subset of the rhythmic transcriptome and many fundamental plant processes (Bendix, et al., 2015; Izawa et al., 2011; Nagel et al., 2015; Nakamichi et al., 2012). Here, we consider that the down-regulated output genes are closer to the circadian clock than the up-regulated output genes, because the direct targets of OsPRR37 are included in the down-regulated output genes. However, some of the down-regulated output genes are not directly but are indirectly repressed by OsPRR37, which indicates that *OsPRR37* confers a more expanded regulation of the rice transcriptome than the presented model.

We also propose a model to elaborate the role of *OsPRR37* in rice flowering pathways. In this model, the overexpression of *OsPRR37* remarkably reduces the expression level of *Ehd1* and *OsMADS1* (Fig. 7b). The almost complete absence of *OsMADS1* disrupts the downstream transcriptional and signaling genes, such as *OsMADS17* (an expression level was not detected in our RNA-seq data) and can thus barely promote floret specification and development through this pathway (Hu et al., 2015). On the other hand, *Ehd1* is repressed by *OsPRR37*, which results in the reduction of *RFT1* and leads to the inhibition of *OsMADS14*, *OsMADS15* and *OsMADS18*. Therefore, the flowering pathway that is mediated by *Ehd1* is completely inhibited. In addition, *Ehd1* could be repressed by *Ghd8* and *Hd1* and promoted by *OsMADS50* and *OsMADS51*. We propose that *Ghd8* and *Hd1* function synergistically with *OsPRR37*, while *OsMADS50* and *OsMADS51* function antagonistically with *OsPRR37* to regulate the expression of *Ehd1*. This model suggests that *OsPRR37* confers *Ehd1*-mediated regulation on rice flowering pathways and that *Ehd1* is the determinative factor that integrates multiple flowering signals to regulate rice flowering. Flowering genes have been frequently reported to influence rice yield(Gao et al., 2014; Koo et al., 2013; Li et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2015; Wei et al., 2010; Yan et al., 2013; Yan et al., 2011; Zhu et al., 2017). Our model of flowering pathways would be beneficial to choose and modify the key flowering regulators to improve rice yield in the future.

In the present study, the time-course transcriptomes were compared between *OsPRR37*-OE5 and GL under natural diurnal conditions. The parent GL contains the loss-of-function *Osprr37*, which is missing the CCT domain. The biallelic expression levels of *OsPRR37*/*Osprr37* in GL and NIL-*OsPRR37* showed that both of their diurnal phases were at 12:00 (Fig. 1d), which indicates that missing the CCT domain has little effect on changing its phase. However, the phases of overexpression lines were shifted to 16:00 and 20:00, indicating overexpressing *OsPRR37* alters its phase. We further show an expression level-dependent manner of the flowering time. In the transient LUC activity assay, the full-length OsPRR37 has higher repression activity than Osprr37 (Fig. 4c), which suggests that the highest repression activity is in overexpression lines and the lowest activity is in GL. These results indicate that the overexpression of *OsPRR37* has a more profound effect than the gain-of-function in NIL-*OsPRR37* on the output genes compared with GL. Therefore, we consider GL here as the wild type and characterize the global effects of *OsPRR37* on transcriptomes by overexpressing *OsPRR37* in GL. The effects of *OsPRR37* on the transcriptomes and specific pathways would be more contributed by overexpressing *OsPRR37* than by gain-of-function, but the regulatory mechanism of the affected genes would be the same. Nevertheless, the RNA-seq data of NIL-*OsPRR37* or transgenic line of functional genomic *OsPRR37* would be helpful if future studies are conducted to distinguish the effects of gain-of-function from the effects of overexpression.

Amplitude is a typical character for a rhythmic gene to determine whether the rhythm is strong or weak. A correlation analysis of the rhythmic DEGs found that the change of the Mean was highly correlated with the change of the aAMP but not correlated with the change of the fAMP (Fig. 3b and 3c). This finding was expected because the change of trough has a more significant effect on calculating the fAMP than the aAMP. However, we still found that 8.7% of the rhythmic DEGs show a negative correlation between the Mean\_change and aAMP\_change (Fig. 3b). Thus, we propose that the aAMP\_change correlates with the Mean\_change but sometimes with a relative independence on the change direction. This result was confirmed when we showed the changes of the aAMP and Mean in the circadian clock and flowering genes. For example, the aAMPs were changed to the opposite direction of Mean for the circadian clock genes *OsELF3-1*, *OsELF3-2*, *OsZTL1*, *OsZTL2* and flowering gene *OsMADS51* (Fig. 4d and 6b). In addition, we further observed a disproportionate change of the aAMP and Mean for the circadian clock genes *OsPRR1*, *OsPRR59* and flowering genes *Ghd8* and *Hd1*. Interestingly, the aAMPs of all of these four genes were more reduced than the Mean, which typically resulted from the increased troughs and decreased peaks. This independence was also confirmed by that the number of rhythmic genes that increased ~40% with increasing amplitude, although the mean level of gene expression did not change (Wang et al., 2015). Recently, amplitude was found to correlate with soil composition in populations of wild barley (*Hordeum vulgare* ssp. *spontaneum*) (Dakhiya, Hussien, Fridman, Kiflawi, & Green, 2017). Based on these results, we suppose that modifying amplitude of rhythmic genes is a potential approach to improve the agricultural traits of crops.

Notably, because the amplitudes are relatively independent of the mean expression levels, the DEGs cannot be simply determined by comparing the mean expression levels in circadian study. In the present study, we selected an unpaired *t* test at each time point for consideration and included the DEGs with opposite changes of peaks and troughs. However, the paired *t* test was used to consider the DEGs that show the significant change by comparing all the time-course samples, and this may not be identified as significant in the unpaired *t* test. These two types of significant genes comprise the 11,679 SAGs by overexpressing *OsPRR37*. However, the DEGs with the opposite change of peaks and troughs would also be underestimated when we use a filter criteria that the fold change of mean expression levels should be lower than 2/3 or higher than 4/3. We cannot say that this DEG identification process is superior, but it was suitable for the present study. An important future work is how to define the DEGs that display circadian or diurnal rhythms, for example, whether the difference at peaks and troughs should be considered when we identifying DEGs. If the difference at peaks and troughs should not be considered, the DEGs with the opposite change of peaks and troughs would be ignored such that their biological significance would also be overlooked. If the difference at peaks and troughs should be considered, we must determine whether the difference in peaks and troughs should be considered as equally important in DEG identification. Thus, researchers should pay more attention to the definition of DEGs that display rhythms and to the development of statistical methods to identify rhythmic DEGs.

Numerous microarray studies have identified clock-output pathways with laboratory treatment of constant light and temperature conditions (Covington, Maloof, Straume, Kay, & Harmer, 2008; Filichkin et al., 2011; Harmer et al., 2000; Khan, Rowe, & Harmon, 2010; Michael et al., 2008; Schaffer et al., 2001). More recent studies have begun to identify the clock-regulated genes and circadian-controlled pathways under light/dark or diurnal conditions by using microarrays and RNA-seq on time-course transcriptomes (Dubois et al., 2017; Filichkin et al., 2011; Hughes et al., 2012; Izawa et al., 2011; Rund, Hou, Ward, Collins, & Duffield, 2011). In addition, chromatin immunoprecipitations followed by sequencing (ChIP-Seq) have increasingly been used to identify the direct targets of circadian clock genes and the association of the histone modifications with diurnal gene expression (Baerenfaller et al., 2016; Ko et al., 2016; Nagel et al., 2015; Nakamichi et al., 2012). A combination of RNA-seq and ChIP-Seq as well as other high-throughput methods may be a trend for future studies of clock-output pathways. In the present study, we applied RNA-seq on experimental materials under natural long-day conditions to investigate the *OsPRR37*-mediated regulation on the diurnal rhythms of not only specific pathways but also the global transcriptomes. These results helped us not only to understand the regulatory mechanism of the global

transcriptomes that is conferred by *OsPRR37* but also to manipulate the specific clock-output pathways to improve rice yield.

**Figure 7.** Molecular models of the expanded regulation of the *OsPRR37*-output genes and flowering pathways. (a) The model of the expanded regulation of the *OsPRR37*-output genes. The circadian clock is fine-tuned by the changing diurnal conditions, such as light signals. An overexpression of *OsPRR37* leads to the down-regulation of the day-phased clock genes, including *OsCCA1*, *OsPRR73*, *OsPRR1*, *OsPRR59* and *OsPRR95*, probably through feedback regulation loops. Additionally, the day-phased clock genes affects the expression of the evening-phased clock genes such as *OsPCL1*, *OsELF3-1*, *OsELF3-2*, *OsZTL1* and *OsZTL2*. The altered circadian clock genes mainly repress the morning-phased output genes and activate evening-phased output genes. Because *OsPRR37* is a transcriptional repressor and

the number of repressed output genes is much smaller than the number of activated output genes, we propose that the circadian clock expands its regulation on many output genes by regulating a small proportion of output genes closer to the clock regulation network. (b) The model for the expansion of *OsPRR37* regulation of flowering pathways by repressing *Ehd1*. Initially, overexpressing *OsPRR37* reduces the expression level of *Ehd1* and *OsMADS1*. The almost complete absence of *OsMADS1* then blocks the downstream transcriptional and signaling genes such as *OsMADS17* and thus cannot promote floret specification and development. The reduced *Ehd1* also inhibits the transcription of its downstream florigen gene *RFT1*, which leads to the inhibited transcription of *OsMADS14*, *OsMADS15* and *OsMADS18* and finally delays the reproduction transition. Moreover, *Ehd1* was repressed by *Ghd8* and *Hd1* and promoted by *OsMADS50* and the *OsMADS51*. Because of the feedback regulation by reduced *Ehd1*, the expression levels of the repressor genes *Ghd8* and *Hd1* are decreased, whereas the promoter genes *OsMADS50* and *OsMADS51* are increased.
