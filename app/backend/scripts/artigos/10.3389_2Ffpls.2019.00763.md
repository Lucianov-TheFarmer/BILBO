#### 20 Abstract

21 Monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL) hydrolyzes monoacylglycerol, producing free fatty acid and glycerol. Although this enzyme has been shown to play important roles in mammal, its potential 22 23 function in plants remains poorly understood. In a survey of the MAGL genes in Brassica napus, we 24 found tapetal expression of *BnaC.MAGL8.a*, a homolog of *AtMAGL8*, results in male sterility in 25 Arabidopsis thaliana. Retarded tapetal PCD and defected defective pollen wall were observed in the transgenic plants. The tapetal cells became vacuolated at stage 9, and then degenerated at stage 11. 26 27 Most microspores degenerated with the tapetal cells, and only few pollen grains with an irregular-28 shaped exine layer were produced in the transgenic plants. Transcriptome analysis identified 398 29 differentially expressed genes. Most of them are involved in pollen development and stress response. 30 ABORTED MICROSPORES and its downstream pollen wall biosynthesis genes were down-regulated, 31 but genes related with reactive oxygen species homeostasis and jasmonates signalling were upregulated in the transgenic plants. These results suggest that expression of BnaC.MAGL8.a in 32 33 tapetum invokes stress response and impairs pollen development. The apparent phenotypic similarity 34 between *atgpat1* mutant and *BnA9::BnaC.MAGL8.a* transgenic plants lead us to propose a role for 35 monoacylglycerol (MAG) in pollen development in Arabidopsis. Our study provides insights on not

36 only the biological function of plant MAGL genes but also the role of MAG in pollen development.

#### 38 1 Introduction

39 In mammals, triacylglycerol (TAG) breakdown proceeds through sequential hydrolysis reactions

40 catalyzed by adipose tissue triacylglycerol lipase, hormone sensitive lipase, and monoacylglycerol

lipase (MAGL) (Labar et al., 2010). In addition to participate in fat mobilization, MAGLs also
 regulate the endocannabinoid signalling system by controlling the level of 2-arachidonoylglycerol,

43 which is a key messenger of the endocannabinoid system (Labar et al., 2010). The first *MAGL* gene

44 was cloned from mice (Karlsson et al., 1997), and then many *MAGL* identifications followed from

45 various species (Karlsson et al., 2001; Heier et al., 2010). MAGLs belong to the alpha/beta hydrolase

46 superfamily, containing the GXSXG motif and the catalytic triad of Ser, Asp and His.

47 In Arabidopsis, a total of 16 putative MAGL genes were identified (Kim et al., 2016). MAG

48 hydrolytic activity were measured with 11 recombinant proteins, among which, AtMAGL6 and

49 AtMAGL8 exhibited the highest MAGL activities (Kim et al., 2016). The involvement of *AtMAGL8* 

50 in lipid mobilization is supported by its preferential expression in developing pollen and germinating

51 seeds, the hydrolytic activity of AtMAGL8 towards the MAG containing eicosenoic acid (20:1), and

52 its oil body associated localization (Kim et al., 2016). AtMAGL3 has three two different enzymatic

roles: monoacylglycerol lipase, lysophospholipase, and monoacylglycerol acyltransferase (Gao et al.,
 2010; Vijayaraj et al., 2012). It is also named as lysoPL2 after its hydrolytic activity towards

55 lysophosphatidylcholine and protein sequence similarity with lysoPL1 (Gao et al., 2010). AtMAGL3

was proposed to protect the plant from heavy metal ions by replacing oxidized membrane lipids with

57 its protein partner acyl-CoA-binding protein 2 (ACBP2) (Gao et al., 2010).

58 The tapetum supports pollen development in an altruistic way, providing nutrients, enzymes, lipids

and other materials. In the bicellular pollen stage, tapetal cells undergo programmed cell death (PCD)

to convert their contents to building blocks for pollen development. Tapetal PCD is regulated by a

number of transcription factors including *ABORTED MICROSPORES (AMS)* (Sorensen et al., 2003;
 Xu et al., 2010), *MALE STERILE 188/MYB 103/MYB80 (MS188/MYB103/MYB80)* (Higginson et al.,

2003; Phan et al., 2012; Xiong et al., 2016) and *DYSFUNCTIONAL TAPETUM1 (DYT1)* (Feng et al.,

64 2012). Absence of these genes results in premature or retarded tapetal PCD and male sterility. AMS

acts as a master regulator in pollen wall development, regulating 8 sporopollenin synthesis genes

66 with *MS188* (Xiong et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018). In addition to transcription factors, reactive

67 oxygen species (ROS) also mediate tapetal PCD. It has been reported that the ROS levels peaked at

stage 8 and 9 and then decreased at stage 11 in the rice anther (Hu et al., 2011). Similar ROS patterns

69 were also observed in *Arabidopsis*, tobacco and tomato (Yu et al., 2017). *RESPIRATORY BURST* 

70 OXIDASE HOMOLOG E (RBOHE) encodes a NADPH oxidase producing ROS (Xie et al., 2014).

71 Tapetal PCD was delayed in the *rbohe* mutant, and *RBOHE* overexpression resulted in premature

72 tapetum degeneration (Xie et al., 2014).

73 Lipids are important for pollen development. The pollen wall comprises two layers, the exine and the

intine (Ariizumi and Toriyama, 2011; Zhang et al., 2016). Lipid derived compounds are the major

constitutes of the pollen coat and the outer exine (Ariizumi and Toriyama, 2011; Zhang et al., 2016).
 The pollen wall not only protects gamete from environmental stresses, but also plays an important

77 role in pollen-stigma communication (Wang et al., 2017; Nasrallah, 2019). Defected Defective pollen

wall frequently results in male sterility. Many lipid related genes are important for pollen wall

development, such as *MALE STERILITY2* (Aarts et al., 1997; Chen et al., 2011), *CYTOCHROME* 

80 P450 FAMILY 703 SUBFAMILY A POLYPEPTIDE 2 (CYP703A2) (Morant et al., 2007), CYP704B1

81 (Dobritsa et al., 2009), and ACYL-COA SYNTHETASE 5 (ACOS5) (de Azevedo Souza et al., 2009).

82 Moreover, loss of genes involved in glycerolipid synthesis also leads to male sterility. Glycerol-3-

- 88 In this work, we cloned *BnaC.MAGL8.a*, which encodes a MAG lipase, from an oil-seed rape cultivar
- 89 (Zhongshuang 11). AtMAGL8 is a promising candidate gene related with lipid mobilization, and it is 90 preferentially expressed in developing pollen and germinating seeds. In this study, we used the BnA9
- promoter and the cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter to drive *BnaC.MAGL8.a*

## 201 **3.3 Defected Defective** pollen development in the *BnA9::BnaC.MAGL8.a* plants

To unravel the mechanism of defective pollen formation, sections of anthers from the wild type and *BnA9::BnaC.MAGL8.a* plants were examined under light microscope. In the *BnA9::BnaC.MAGL8.a* plants, microspores were released from the tetrads as the wild type at stage 8 (Figure 4H and Figure S4E). At stage 9, the wild type tapetal cells initiated programmed cell death, evidenced by their reduced size (Figure 4C). However, in the transgenic plants, the tapetum became vacuolated and

207 abundant small vacuoles were presented in the tapetal cells. In addition, some of the microspores

208 were degraded at this stage (Figure 4I and Figure S4G). At stage 11, vacuolated tapetal cells and

microspores filled the locules and large vacuoles were present in the tapetum (Figure 4J and Figure
 S4H). Some of the tapetal cells were degraded, leaving cell debris among the microspores. Most

211 microspores were degraded, presented as empty exine ring in the section, and only a few still viable

212 (Figure 4J). At stage 12, tapetal PCD were completed and pollen grains were freely distributed in the

213 wild type (Figure 4E). In the BnA9::BnaC.MAGL8.a plants, the degenerated pollen grains were

214 attached to normal ones and all of them appeared stuck to the locule wall (Figure 4K). At stage 13,

215 only pollen debris and irregular pollen grains could be observed in the *BnA9::BnaC.MAGL8.a* plants

216 (Figure 4L).

# 248 **3.4** Transcriptome analysis of the *BnA9::BnaC.MAGL8.a* flower buds

249 To assess the genes associated with male sterility in the *BnA9::BnaC.MAGL8.a* plants, transcriptome

analysis was performed with young flower buds (stage 6-9) from the wild type and transgenic plants.
In addition to *BnaC.MAGL8.a*, 397 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified,

composing 157 up-regulated genes and 240 down-regulated genes (Table S5). The result of

#### **395 4.3** The potential role of MAG in pollen development

396 At stage 8, microspores are separated from the tapetum and obtain nutrients from the locule fluid.

397 The exact composition of the locule fluid remains poorly understood. MAG, as a transportable

398 metabolite, may serve as a locule fluid component. Arburscular mycorrhizal fungi and its host plant

399 evolve a symbiosis relationship: the fungi transports mineral nutrients to the plant and receives

400 MAGs and sugars as feedback (Jiang et al., 2017; Luginbuehl et al., 2017). ram2 mutant in <u>Medicago</u>

401 <u>truncatula</u> is defective in mycorrhizal fungal colonization and addition of C16 fatty acids

402 complements the mutation (Wang et al., 2012). Further analysis indicated that *RAM2* encodes a

403 GPAT synthesizing 2-monoacylglycerol, which likely exported to the fungi (Wang et al., 2012).

404 Pollen development requires plenty of lipids (Zhang et al., 2016). As <u>a high concentration of</u> free

405 fatty acid is toxic to plant cells, transporting MAG from tapetum is could be a safe and efficient way 406 to deliver lipid derived compounds to microspores. In the *atenat1* mutant, the tapetum became

406 to deliver lipid derived compounds to microspores. In the *atgpat1* mutant, the tapetum became 407 hypertrophic and even occupied half of the locule (Zheng et al., 2003). Then, the microspores and

408 tapetum degenerated, leaving collapsed pollen walls and several round pollen grains attached to the

409 locule (Zheng et al., 2003). The similar phenotype of the *atgpat1* mutant and the

410 BnA9::BnaC.MAGL8.a transgenic plants suggested a common mechanism for defect pollen

411 development. We speculate that MAG could be the link, as the mutation of AtGPAT1 and the

412 BnaC.MAGL8.a overexpression may cause a MAG shortage. Further investigation is needed to prove

413 the importance of MAG for microspore development.
