#### 1 ABSTRACT

2 The genome-wide abundance of two histone modifications, H3K4me3 and H3K9ac, both 3 associated with actively expressed genes, was monitored in Arabidopsis thaliana leaves at different time points during developmental senescence, along with expression in the form of 4 RNA-seq data. H3K9ac and H3K4me3 marks were highly convergent at all stages of leaf aging, 5 6 but H3K4me3 marks covered nearly twice the gene area as H3K9ac marks. Genes with the 7 greatest fold-change in expression displayed the largest positively-correlated percent change in 8 coverage for both marks. Most senescence up-regulated genes were pre-marked by H3K4me3 9 and H3K9ac, but at levels below the whole-genome average, and for these genes, gene expression increased without a significant increase in either histone mark. However, for a subset 10 of genes showing increased or decreased expression, the respective gain or loss of H3K4me3 11 12 marks were found to closely match the temporal changes in mRNA abundance. 22% of genes that increased expression during senescence showed accompanying changes in H3K4me3 13 14 modification, and they include numerous regulatory genes, which may act as primary response 15 genes.

#### 1 **INTRODUCTION**

Leaf senescence is the final nutritive stage of leaf development in which the organ that provided 2 3 sugars to the growing plant undergoes a controlled degradation process that recycles many of the nutrients located in the protein-rich photosynthetic apparatus. Leaf senescence is regulated by 4 5 developmental age and can be accelerated by adverse environmental conditions such as drought, nutrient deprivation and heat stress. Ethylene, jasmonic acid and salicylic acid promote, while 6 cytokinin prevents leaf senescence (Lim et al., 2007). Numerous microarray studies have 7 8 catalogued major changes in gene expression during leaf senescence and a large overlap with 9 defense responses has been noted (Guo et al., 2004; van der Graaff et al., 2006; Breeze et al., 10 2011; Guo and Gan, 2012).

11 Towards understanding the mechanisms that regulate gene expression during senescence,

12 numerous studies have demonstrated that transcription factors such as WRKY53 (Hinderhofer

and Zentgraf, 2001; Miao et al., 2004; Miao and Zentgraf, 2010), ORE1/NAC2 (Oh et al., 1997;

14 Matallana-Ramirez et al., 2013), and EIN3 (Li et al., 2013) promote leaf senescence, as well as

15 many others listed in the Leaf Senescence Database (Liu et al., 2011). In addition, mutants that

16 affect chromatin structure have been shown to affect senescence, however phenotypes are often

17 pleiotropic (Lim et al., 2007; Wu et al., 2008; Ay et al., 2009; Humbeck, 2013).

18 In a previous study, changes in two euchromatin histone modifications, H3K4me3 (Zhang et al.,

19 2009) and H3K27me3 (Zhang et al., 2007), were measured on a genome-wide scale by ChIP-seq

20 in young and old leaves (Brusslan et al., 2012). This study did not have accompanying gene

expression data and published microarray data (van der Graaff et al., 2006) were used to

22 compare gene expression and changes in histone methylation status. Even with these limitations,

a correlation between the H3K4me3 mark and gene expression was apparent. Genes that

displayed a gain in the H3K4me3 mark were most highly represented by genes expressed to a

25 greater extent in older leaves and the opposite distribution was observed for genes that lost the

H3K4me3 mark. Genes that lost the H3K27me3 mark were expressed in older leaf tissue, but

overall gene numbers were low and a gain of the H3K27me3 mark for down-regulated genes was

rarely observed.

H3K4me3 marks are localized to actively transcribed genes, but they become associated with
chromatin after RNA polymerase II binding (Kim et al., 2008; Malapeira et al., 2012) and are

1 thus considered to be marks for transcribed genes. However, interaction between the H3K4me3 2 mark and TAF3, a TFIID subunit of the pre-initiation complex, has been observed in human cells 3 (Lauberth et al., 2013), suggesting the H3K4me3 mark plays a role during the initiation of transcription. In addition, the Arabidopsis trithorax1 (ATX1) methyltransferase was found to be 4 important for recruiting TATA-binding protein and RNA polymerase II to promoters. 5 Interestingly this activity was independent of the ATX1 methyltransferase activity, which was 6 necessary for elongation of transcription (Ding et al., 2012). The extent of H3K4me3 7 modifications was recently shown to be greatest for cell identity genes, and in young leaves, 8 9 genes with the greatest breadth of H3K4me3 coverage were enriched for photosynthesis (Benayoun et al., 2014). 10

A second well-studied histone mark is H3K9ac (Zhou et al., 2010). Changes in H3K9 acetylation have been shown to correlate with changes in gene expression during de-etiolation and after UV-B treatment (Charron et al., 2009; Schenke et al., 2014). The timing of H3K9ac gain or loss closely paralleled gene expression changes for the circadian clock components and response to drought stress (Kim et al., 2012; Malapeira et al., 2012), or occurred after genes had been activated, in the case of flowering (Adrian et al., 2010). Thus the exact role of H3K9ac in gene activation is not yet defined, and it likely plays numerous roles.

18 In this study, both of these well-established histone marks, which positively correlate to gene 19 expression -- H3K4me3 and H3K9ac -- have been measured on a genome-wide scale using 20 ChIP-seq, along with accompanying RNA-seq data at different stages of leaf aging. Most (78%) senescence up-regulated genes were pre-marked with H3K4me3 and H3K9ac and most (85%) of 21 22 down-regulated genes retained both marks even when gene expression had dropped to low 23 levels. However, the breadth of the H3K4me3 and H3K9ac marks were positively related to gene 24 expression. For a subset of genes (22% of up-regulated genes and 15% of down-regulated 25 genes), a strong relationship between temporal changes in gene expression and gain/loss of the 26 H3K4me3 mark was observed during leaf aging. By identifying genes that show combined 27 changes in gene expression and histone marks, we have produced a list of genes that include 28 many known to play important roles in senescence, as well as potentially novel players in this 29 important biological process.

#### 2 RNA-SEQ GENE EXPRESSION ANALYSIS

RNA-seq was performed on mature, fully-expanded rosette leaf tissue at four time points: 29d, 3 4 35d, 42d and 57d (Supplemental Figure 1). These time points were chosen to encompass the steps of leaf senescence, which commences soon after the development of the inflorescence 5 6 (Breeze et al., 2011). Three independently sampled replicates were subject to RNA-seq using multiplex single-end Illumina sequencing (Supplemental Table 1). Replicates sampled at each 7 8 day show a high degree of similarity with the 29d samples being the most distinct (Figure 1A), allowing us to use the replicates to assess variation for confident differential expression analysis, 9 as well as to subsequently pool the replicate read count for the calculation of RPKM values. The 10 11 initial data analysis identified genes that showed significant changes in expression between 12 adjacent time points ( $\geq$  2-fold change in expression, p-value  $\leq$  0.05): 29-35d, 35-42d, and 42-57d. Confirming the distinct separation of the 29d replicates and those of the other time points 13 seen in the dendrogram of the clustering results (Figure 1A), the largest number of genes was up-14 (Figure 1B) or down- (Figure 1C) regulated during the first time interval, indicating that major 15 changes in gene expression occur prior to the visual manifestations of leaf aging: the loss of 16 chlorophyll. The greatest overlap between time intervals was between 35-42d and 42-57d for up-17 regulated genes and 29-35d and 35-42d for down-regulated genes (Figure 1B and 1C). A 18 hypergeometric test (Johnson et al., 1992) revealed a significant overlap between all intervals for 19 20 up-regulated genes (p-value  $\leq$  5e-04) indicating that although most genes are fully up-regulated by 35d, a significant proportion continue to be more gradually up-regulated across time points 21 22 (Figure 1B). The down-regulated genes only showed a significant overlap across the first two 23 intervals (p-value < 1.1e-81) indicating that a largely distinct set of genes decreases from 42 to 24 57d (Figure 1C).

We sought to generate a high-confidence set of senescence up-regulated genes (SURGs) and senescence down-regulated genes (SDRGs), by requiring that they showed significant changes in expression ( $\geq$ 2-fold, p-value  $\leq$  0.05) in two of six pairwise comparisons (29-35d, 29-42d, 29-57d, 35-42d, 35-57d and 42-57d). This analysis permits the inclusion of genes with significant changes in expression in just one interval; for example the distinct group of genes that are downregulated between 42-57d (Figure 1C) will be listed in the 29-57d, 35-57d and 42-57d pairwise

1 comparisons. To remove genes with low expression we also required that they have RPKM 2 values (after merging replicate data sets) above the median RPKM value (0.764 for 29d, 0.911 3 for 35d, 0.790 for 42d and 0.752 for 57d) at the time of higher expression. Figure 1 D and E show the robust up- and down-regulation of expression in the SURGs and SDRGs, respectively. 4 As was generally the case, the biggest changes in expression occurred between 29d and 35d, but 5 the respective upward and downward trends persisted for the duration of the time course. In 6 contrast to the SURGs and SDRGs, the expression distributions of all other genes show no trend 7 indicating the classification was reasonable (Figure 1F). Figure 1G shows that setting the 8 9 threshold at two of six pairwise comparisons resulted in a fair estimate of gene expression changes that represented a good compromise between overly stringent and more lenient criteria. 10 WRKY transcription factor genes, usually associated with senescence and thus representing a 11 12 likely false positive, were observed in the down-regulated category when only one of six pairwise comparisons was the threshold; conversely SAUR genes, down-regulated in senescence 13 and representing a likely false positive, were seen at increasing numbers in the up-regulated 14 category when only one of six pairwise comparisons was the threshold. Genes encoding bHLH 15 transcription factors showed no preference for up- or down-regulation during leaf aging and the 16 numbers of these genes became more plentiful as the threshold decreased. 17

18 The selection procedure described above resulted in 1432 SURGs (plus 11 pseudogenes and 6 transposable element genes) and 964 SDRGs (plus 4 pseudogenes and 5 transposable element 19 genes, Supplemental Data Set 1). Small numbers of transposable element genes, mostly 20 21 retroposons, and pseudogenes were both up- and down-regulated during leaf senescence. This 22 contrasts to animal cells where a general increase in expression and mobility of transposable 23 element genes has been observed in older somatic cells (De Cecco et al., 2013, Li et al., 2013). A 24 Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed on this list of SURGs and SDRGs and enriched 25 Biological Processes with false discovery rate (FDR) below 1% are shown in Table 1. Genes 26 related to defense, jasmonic acid and transport were enriched in SURGs as expected (Guo et al., 2004; van der Graaff et al., 2006; Breeze et al., 2011). In addition, enrichment for indole 27 28 glucosinolate synthesis genes suggested a role for these secondary metabolites during senescence 29 (Wang et al., 2013). SDRGs were enriched for photosynthesis and growth-related processes such 30 as response to auxin stimulus, response to light stimulus, response to gibberellin, lipid 31 biosynthesis, and cell wall organization.

# THE BREADTH OF HISTONE MARKS CORRELATES WITH GENE EXPRESSION DURING LEAF SENESCENCE

A recent study by Benayoun et al. (2014) showed that the extent of H3K4me3 marks was greatest for genes that impart cell identity. In leaves, for instance, a high proportion of genes with the broadest H3K4me3 coverage were assigned the photosynthesis GO term. With this in mind, the extent to which each gene was covered by the two respective histone modifications was determined. Using 100 bp bins that exhibited a significant enrichment in ChIP-seq read

1 counts relative to input  $(-\log(p-value \ge 4))$ , we calculated coverage both in terms of absolute base 2 pairs and as a proportion of the gene to normalize for length (TSS to TTS +/- 500bp to allow for 3 potential spreading of the marks beyond the confines of the gene body). Considering the absolute extent of significant H3K4me3 enrichment at 42d, the top 5% of genes had at least 1500 bp of 4 coverage by the mark (Figure 8A). As a proportion of the gene length, the top 5% of genes with 5 the highest coverage had at least 50% of their lengths significantly enriched for H3K4me3 6 (Figure 8B). In contrast, genes in the 95th percentile of H3K9ac coverage at 42d showed 7 significant ChIP-to-input enrichment over at least 900 bp (Figure 8C). Normalizing for gene 8 9 length, the top 5% of genes with the broadest H3K9ac enrichment had at minimum 32% of their gene bodies covered (Figure 8D). Similar overall coverage distributions were observed for the 10 11 respective marks at all leaf ages (data not shown), with H3K9ac observed to cover only 60-65% of the region covered by H3K4me3. 12

"Regulation of transcription" was enriched in 30d and 34d H3K9ac 17 18 samples, but not enriched in any of the H3K4me3 samples, suggesting that H3K9ac coverage 19 breadth may control regulatory loci. #### 4 **DISCUSSION**

The abundance of H3K4me3 and H3K9ac histone modifications change at specific loci during 5 6 numerous developmental transitions in plants (Kim et al., 2008; Charron et al., 2009; Kim et al., 7 2009; Adrian et al., 2010; Anzola et al., 2010; van Dijk et al., 2010; Brusslan et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2012; Malapeira et al., 2012; Molitor et al., 2014; Schenke et al., 2014), and thus it is likely 8 9 that the final stage of leaf development, leaf senescence, would be accompanied by changes in these two well-characterized histone modifications that positively correlate with gene expression. 10 Leaves were harvested from progressively more senescent plants and subject to RNA-seq and 11 12 ChIP-seq so that a direct correlation between gene expression and abundance and breadth of 13 histone modifications could be performed. Our data showed a strong correlation between the 14 breadth of both histone marks and gene expression. Differing amounts of H3K4me3 marks 15 correlated well to gene expression, however a weaker trend was observed between gene expression and the abundance of H3K9ac marks. 16

17 Our harvest protocol maximized material to allow for both nuclei and RNA extraction, and used multiple as well as larger leaves (approximately leaves 12-15) than leaf 7 which was used in an 18 19 extensive transcriptome analysis of developmental senescence (Breeze et al., 2011). RPKM values for 18 senescence marker genes and the corresponding cluster number previously 20 21 assigned are shown in Supplemental Table 3. Genes from cluster U1 were expressed early in 22 both experiments, genes from clusters U2 and U3 mostly showed the greatest fold-induction in 23 the first time interval (29-35d), while genes from the U5, U6 and U7 clusters showed highest expression at 57d, the final time point for the RNA-seq data. Although the current study did not 24 25 have the temporal refinement of the extensive transcriptome work, the intervals are generally 26 equivalent and trends should translate among studies.

The signal strength for the H3K4me3 peaks was higher than that of the H3K9ac peaks. A recent
analysis of multiple chromatin marks shows weaker peaks for numerous acetylation marks
compared to H3K4me3 marks, suggesting that acetylation marks are less pronounced than

1 H3K4me3 modifications in Arabidopsis (Sequeira-Mendes et al., 2014). The high coincidence of 2 H3K4me3 and H3K9ac marks (Figure 2) has been noted previously (Kim et al., 2008; Anzola et 3 al., 2010; Kim et al., 2010; Roudier et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2012; Malapeira et al., 2012) and supports the validity of the H3K9ac datasets. Although the coincidence of these two 4 modifications has been noted before, our observation that the extent of H3K9ac coverage per 5 gene was about 60% of the H3K4me3 coverage level is novel. For most genes, modifications 6 centered +400 bp from the TSS, but for genes identified in the H3K9ac\_GAIN\_clusters #1 and 7 #2, modifications that peaked at  $\sim$  -1200 bp from the TSS were also observed for both marks 8 9 (Supplemental Figure 3). The combination of the two marks in the promoter region might contribute to an upward trend in gene expression, however neither of these H3K9ac GAIN 10 11 clusters exhibited significantly increased gene expression (Table 2B). Promoter methylation for 12 H3K4me3\_GAIN genes did increase with plant age, but no clear peak was identified and the magnitude of the change was much less compared to the increase that centered at +400 bp from 13 the TSS. Thus, for these genes, the role of promoter histone modifications is unclear. 14

15 The breadth of histone mark coverage was found to correlate well with gene expression,

16 especially for H3K4me3 (Figure 8 and Supplemental Figure 5). Genes with the greatest fold-

17 change in expression had the largest corresponding changes in gene coverage by the mark. These

18 data support the fact that the extent of gene coverage, as well as the peak intensity, of these

19 histone marks is important for gene regulation. Since this study is based on an ensemble of cells,

20 the increase in the total histone mark signal at active genes could be due to the fact that an

21 increasing number of cells up-regulate the genes involved in the senescence process over time.

22 A similar experiment coupling expression to the H3K4me3 mark was performed on rice subject 23 to drought stress (Zong et al., 2013). Results of this study corresponded to our work noting that 24 genes with a significant change in the H3K4me3 mark and in gene expression (609 rice genes) 25 were directionally correlated such that gain of the H3K4me3 mark and up-regulation occurred in 26 89% of cases and loss of the H3K4me3 mark and down-regulation occurred in 90% of cases. 27 Identification of significantly changed H3K4me3 marks was less precise because only two 28 conditions, control and drought, were studied, and thus the significant change in three of six (or 29 two of three) pairwise comparisons that was done for this work, was not possible. The

30 observation that of the 4,387 rice genes with differential H3K4me3 modification levels, many

were not expressed (45%) or had no change in expression during drought (40%) might be due to
an over-estimation of genes with differential H3K4me3 marks.

3 The relatively few genes that showed significant changes in H3K4me3 (786 genes) and H3K9ac (278 genes) marks is surprising. Our work showed that only a small proportion of SURGs (22%) 4 5 and SDRGs (15%) had changes in the H3K4me3 mark, and an even smaller percentage (2% for SURGs and 3% for SDRGs) for the H3K9ac mark. This can be partially explained by the 6 stringent criteria used to call regions of significant change, but raising the p-value resulted in the 7 8 inclusion of genes that did not show convincing gains/losses when raw data were viewed. 9 Additionally, many SURGs already had both histone marks prior to the first leaf harvest, however levels were below the whole-genome average for H3K4me3 and did not change 10 significantly (Figure 4). For these genes, H3K9ac levels showed an increasing trend that peaked 11 12 above whole-genome time point averages, indicating that small changes in this mark do accompany changes in gene activation. SDRGs that did not show a significant loss in H3K4 13 14 trimethylation over the time points studied did display a decreasing trend in H3K4me3 marks, however they remained above the genome-wide average, and H3K9ac marks for these genes 15 16 were low during the entire time course. Thus, for the bulk of up-regulated genes, low levels of H3K4me3 marks were present prior to the first time point and H3K9ac showed an increasing 17 18 trend. For the bulk of down-regulated genes, the opposite was observed: low levels of H3K9ac 19 were present while H3K4me3 levels showed a decreasing trend. This reciprocal relationship between the two marks has not been noted previously, and is worth further study. 20

21 Pathogen infiltration of Arabidopsis leaves was found to result in increased expression of

22 WRKY70, PR1 and TH12.1. WRKY70 was found to have differential H3K4me3 and to be a

direct target of the ATX1 histone methyltransferase while PR1 and TH12.1 had constant levels

of H3K4me3 marks and were targets of WRKY70 (Alvarez-Venegas et al., 2007). The pathogen

25 infiltration study suggested that changing H3K4me3 may occur for primary-responsive

regulatory genes, but not for secondary response genes. In agreement with this observation, our

- 27 list of SURGs that displayed a gain of H3K4me3 (K4-SURGs) includes 4 WRKY and 6 NAC
- 28 TFs. Of these WRKY and NAC genes, mutant analysis has shown that WRKY75 (Li et al.,
- 29 2012), NAC016 (Kim et al., 2013) and NAC019 (Guan et al., 2014) are positive regulators of
- 30 senescence while WRKY54 (Besseau et al., 2012) is a negative regulator of senescence. Thus

1 numerous important primary regulators of senescence do display a concomitant change in

2 H3K4me3 marks and gene expression. A small number of regulatory genes displayed changes in

3 H3K9ac as leaf senescence progressed. K9-SURGs include WRKY41, which plays a protective

4 role during defense (Higashi et al., 2008) and NDR1/HIN1-Like10, a MAPK gene that is SA-

5 responsive during senescence (Zheng et al., 2004).

6 Of the 175 K4-SDRGs that displayed a loss of H3K4me3 marks and decreased gene expression,

8 (Mashiguchi et al., 2011; Stepanova et al., 2011; Won et al., 2011) and response (Spartz et al.,

9 2012; Spartz et al., 2014). Auxin synthesis sets in motion major changes in gene expression and

10 physiology (Guifoyle and Hagen, 2007) and the down-regulation of auxin synthesis and

signaling can be considered a primary event in the progression of senescence. Although reports

12 of the role of auxin in leaf senescence conflict (Quirino et al., 1999; Okushima et al., 2005; Hou

et al., 2013; Jiang et al., 2014), our molecular data suggest auxin action is down-regulated during

15 A well-characterized senescence regulator, *WRKY53*, showed a constant high level of H3K4me3

and H3K9ac marks in our study (Supplemental Figure 6A), thus indicating that not all important

17 regulators were identified as K4-SURGs. RPKM levels for *WRKY53* increased from 42.6 to

18 548.1 between 29d and 35d indicating that a large change in expression (13-fold) did occur

during the time points included in this study. *WRKY53* is one example of the many genes that are

20 marked prior to significant up-regulation of mRNA levels. *WRKY53* expression is down-

regulated by Whirly1 (Miao et al., 2013), which may explain the coincidence of low transcript

22 levels and high levels of H3K4me3 marks. Examples of post-transcriptional regulation mediated

by small RNAs have been identified during leaf senescence (Kim et al., 2009; Li et al., 2013;

Thatcher et al., 2014), and may also explain some of the inconsistencies between H3K4me3

25 marks and gene expression. *SAG12*, a molecular marker for senescence was up-regulated 3300-

fold between 29 and 57d (0.022 – 72.5 RPKM) and showed increased levels of H3K4me3 marks

27 250- 650 bp upstream of the TSS, but no clear H3K9ac marks (Supplemental Figure 6B),

suggesting that H3K4me3 modifications in the promoter can be associated with changes in gene

expression. This differed from our previous study in which *SAG12* was not marked by

H3K4me3 (Brusslan et al., 2012). The other gene that was reported to be strongly up-regulated

and that did not have H3K4me3 marks, At1g73220, was devoid of both marks in the current
study (Supplemental Figure 6C), however expression levels were low for this gene, and only
reached median RPKM levels at 57d. As four time points were included in this more
comprehensive work, these data have more certainty than our previous data.

5 During leaf senescence, changes in H3K4me3 marks reflected changes in gene expression to a 6 much greater extent than did H3K9ac marks. This is, in part, due to the slightly different ages of the H3K9ac ChIP-seq data set compared to the RNA-seq data set. However, the criteria for a 7 8 significant change required changes that covered two of three intervals for H3K9ac, thus the differences in leaf harvest dates (29d vs. 30d and 35d vs. 34d) for the two marks were minimized 9 by the nature of the analysis. A small number of genes that were specifically up- or down-10 marked between 29-35d, but not between the shorter interval, 30-34d, would be missed though. 11 12 In drought stress, H3K9ac and H3K4me3 marks increased in parallel for three droughtresponsive genes (Kim et al., 2008). During rehydration, expression of the drought-responsive 13 14 genes was down-regulated, and the H3K9ac marks were rapidly lost while the H3K4me3 marks were retained and decreased at a rate similar to mRNA levels (Kim et al., 2012). The drought 15 16 study was done on the time scale of hours while this developmental senescence study was performed on a time scale of days, yet in both H3K4me3 marks paralleled gene expression. It 17 18 should be noted, however, that H3K4me3 marks can change rapidly, as has been observed for clock genes in which both H3K9ac and H3K4me3 marks cycle each 24 hours (Malapeira et al., 19 20 2012). Sampling was always performed at the same time each harvest day, so diurnal cycles 21 would not interfere with our study.

22 In this study, the 387 K4-SURG genes were noted to have average H3K9ac levels at the earliest 23 time points that increased by 42d, unlike the H3K4me3 marks which at first were substantially 24 below average and greatly increased by the end of the time course (Figure 7). These data suggest 25 that H3K9ac modifications are present prior to H3K4 trimethylation for the K4-SURGs. In 26 animal systems, evidence supporting K4 trimethylation of acetylated H3 has been published 27 (Bradbury et al., 2005; Nightingale et al., 2007; Taverna et al., 2007), and it is possible that the 28 H3K9 acetylation serves as a template for the gain of H3K4me3 marks during leaf senescence. 29 Interestingly, for K4 SURGs which display the largest gain in H3K4me3 marks between the first 30 two time points (29-35d), additional H3K9ac acquisition, above average levels, only occurs at a

1 subsequent stage between 34 and 42d (Figure 7). Furthermore, those genes that gain H3K9ac

2 (K9\_SURGs) already show elevated levels of H3K4me3 and show no further increase in

4 prior to that of H3K9ac for these genes. Non-K4-SU/DRGs showed a reciprocal relationship

5 with one mark remaining at a basal low level while the other showed an increasing/decreasing

6 trend (Figure 4).

#### 7 CONCLUSIONS

8 Gene expression and H3K4me3 and H3K9ac marks were monitored at a genome-wide level at 9 four stages of leaf senescence. These two histone marks have been found to positively correlate 10 to gene expression in many organisms. Most genes were pre-marked with the two modifications 11 at levels below the whole-genome average prior to up-regulation. Most down-regulated genes remained marked, even when expression levels substantially dropped. A subset of genes (22% of 12 up-regulated genes and 14% of down-regulated genes), including known regulatory loci, showed 13 a strong correlation between gene expression and H3K4me3 marks. H3K9ac coverage per gene 14 was approximately 60% of that for H3K4me3, and the percentage of coverage for both histone 15 16 marks positively correlated to the fold-change in aging-related gene expression. These studies bolster support for a role of chromatin modification in the process of leaf aging. 17

22 Plants were grown as described (Brusslan et al., 2012) with the exception that the light intensity was 30  $\mu$ mols photons m<sup>-2</sup> sec<sup>-1</sup>, at 22°C, and the diurnal cycle was 20 hours of light and 4 hours 23 of darkness. The low light intensity was chosen because the leaves of older plants grown at 24 standard light intensities (120-150  $\mu$ mols photons m<sup>-2</sup> sec<sup>-1</sup> for 16h is recommended by the 25 26 Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center) became purple and stressed; to compensate for the low 27 light intensity, a longer light period, 20h, was utilized. Mature fully-expanded rosette leaves were harvested as shown by the pink arrows in Supplemental Figure 1. Newly emerging leaves at 28 29 the center of the rosette were not harvested and petioles were trimmed such that harvested tissue

1 contained leaf blades, only. No inflorescence tissue was harvested. The first harvest was 2 performed after the vegetative to reproductive transition when incipient bolts (0.5 to 3 cm) had 3 developed (29 day-old plants, 29d). Mature rosette leaf tissue was then harvested at 35d when bolts had lengthened and secondary bolts were forming, at 42d when siliques were present on 4 primary and secondary bolts and at 57d when about 50% of siliques were brown and dry and 5 6 chlorophyll loss was evident throughout the rosette leaves. Isolation of nuclei and chromatin 7 immunoprecipitation were performed as previously described with the exception that the final pellet was resuspended in 1 ml cold nuclei lysis buffer and 2 ml of cold ChIP dilution buffer. The 8 9 H3K9ac antibody (07-352) and H3K4me3 antibody (17-678) were purchased from Millipore. The H3K4me3 ChIP library was prepared for sequencing as described previously (Brusslan et 10 al., 2012) and the H3K9ac library was prepared using the Illumina TruSeq<sup>TM</sup> ChIP Sample 11 Preparation Kit - Set A. RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (Life Technologies) and RNA 12 was prepared for sequencing using the Illumina TruSeq<sup>TM</sup> RNA Sample Prep Kit v2-SetA. 13 Sequencing was performed at UCLA using an Illumina HiSeq2000 to produce 50bp single reads 14

#### 16 <u>RNS-seq data processing</u>

- 17 RNA-seq reads were mapped to the TAIR10 reference genome and gene annotation using
- tophat-2.0.4 (Trapnell et al., 2009) calling bowtie-0.12.8 (Langmead et al., 2009) using the
- 19 following parameters:
- 20 Tophat --solexa1.3-quals --no-coverage-search -g 1 –G <TAIR10 annotation>
- 21 HTSeq (Anders et al., 2014) was used to perform gene counts and DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014)
- 22 for differential analysis. Intersection and union analyses of gene lists were performed using the
- 23 Virtual Plant web site (Katari et al., 2010).
- 24 <u>ChIP-seq data processing</u>
- 25 ChIP-seq reads were mapped to the TAIR10 reference genome using bowtie-0.12.8 (Langmead
- et al., 2009) using the following parameters:
- bowtie -m 1 -v 2 -a --best --strata

Regions of significant H3K4me3 and H3K9ac ChIP enrichment were determined by comparing
the number of ChIP to input reads within 100bp bins across the genome. Bins showing –log(pvalue) ≥ 6 by the Poisson test were deemed to be significantly enriched. To ensure an even
higher level of confidence in these peaks, we required that at least two sequential 100bp bins
showed significant ChIP enrichment when comparing the locations of the two marks (Figure 2).
Pearson correlations and significance values were calculated using R's 'cor.test' function (R core
team, 2014).

#### 8 <u>ChIP-seq differential analysis</u>

Pairwise comparisons were made between ChIP signals at each time point for each histone mark,
using a Poisson test to determine regions of significant difference (-log(p-value) ≥ 6). Based on
these pairwise comparisons, a region was defined as displaying a consistent gain or loss of
histone marks during the course of the experiment, if changes were observed in at least three of
the six pairwise comparisons for H3K4me3 and two of the three pairwise comparisons for
H3K9ac.

#### 15 <u>K-means clustering</u>

For each mark, only regions displaying a consistent significant gain or loss of the mark during
the course of the experiment were considered (see ChIP-seq differential analysis\_section above).
In each of the four cases (significant K4me3 gain, K4me loss, K9ac gain, K9ac loss), -log(pvalue) results from the ChIP vs. input Poisson test (see above) were grouped by k-means
clustering (k=3) using the 'kmeans' function in the R package (R core team, 2014). Genes were
assigned to these peaks, if the peaks overlapped the TSS to TSS + 500bp region of the genes. In
some cases, multiple regions from a single gene were assigned to different clusters.

#### 23 <u>Gene coverage</u>

For each mark, the coverage of each gene was determined as the proportion of 100bp bins from TSS - 500bp to TTS + 500bp that exhibited a significant enrichment in ChIP-seq read counts relative to input ( $-\log(p-value) >= 4$ ). A lower threshold was chosen in this case as we were comparing IP to input as opposed to IP vs IP, which has the potential of containing more false positives, and because this helped to better capture the continuity in the breadth of coverage of significant histone mark deposition along the gene body. The top 5% of genes with the highest proportion of coverage at each time point were analyzed for absolute length of coverage and
 percent gene coverage.
