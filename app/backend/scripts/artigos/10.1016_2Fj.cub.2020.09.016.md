Chen et al. describe the Ca2+-responsive phospholipid-binding BON proteins as controlling global osmotic stress responses in plants including Ca2+ signaling, ABA accumulation, gene expression reprogramming, and plant growth. NLR immune receptors repress osmotic stress responses. BON proteins suppress NLR signaling to balance abiotic and biotic responses.

#### SUMMARY

Hyperosmotic stress caused by drought and salinity is a significant environmental threat that limits plant growth and agricultural productivity. Osmotic stress induces diverse responses in plants including  $Ca^{2+}$ signaling, accumulation of the stress hormone abscisic acid (ABA), reprogramming of gene expression, and altering of growth. Despite intensive investigation, no global regulators of all of these responses have been identified. Here, we show that the  $Ca^{2+}$ -responsive phospholipid-binding BONZAI (BON) proteins are critical for all of these osmotic stress responses. A  $Ca^{2+}$ -imaging-based forward genetic screen identified a loss-of-function *bon1* mutant with a reduced cytosolic  $Ca^{2+}$  signal in response to hyperosmotic stress. The loss-of-function mutants of the *BON1* gene family, *bon1bon2bon3*, are impaired in the induction of gene expression and ABA accumulation in response to osmotic stress. In addition, the *bon* mutants are hypersensitive to osmotic stress in growth inhibition. *BON* genes have been shown to negatively regulate plant immune responses mediated by intracellular immune receptor NLR genes including *SNC1*. We found that the defects of the *bon* mutants in osmotic stress responses were suppressed by mutations in the NLR gene *SNC1* or the immunity regulator *PAD4*. Our findings indicate that NLR signaling represses osmotic stress responses and that BON proteins suppress NLR signaling to enable global osmotic stress responses in plants.

#### INTRODUCTION

To survive in ever-changing environments that often impose abiotic and biotic stresses, plants have evolved sophisticated sensing and signaling networks to balance growth and defense. Although we have some knowledge about signaling mechanisms of light,  $CO_2$ , temperature, salt, hydrogen peroxide, and nutrients, how plants sense hyperosmotic stress caused by water deficit remains poorly understood [1–3]. It is not known whether or not there exists global regulators that control both early Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling and later osmotic stress responses.

Osmotic stress in plants triggers multiple responses including elevation of cytosolic  $Ca^{2+}$  (within seconds) [4]; activation of protein kinase (within minutes) [5]; regulation of  $Ca^{2+}$ -dependent responses [6]; accumulation of abscisic acid (ABA), stomatal closure, and gene expression changes (within hours) [7]; as well as long-term responses such as growth regulation, leaf senescence, and dormancy [8] (Figure 1). Prior studies have implicated  $Ca^{2+}$  channels and uniporters, including the mechanically activated nonselective cation channel OSCA1, the plastid K<sup>+</sup> exchange antiporter (KEA) transporters, and the

chloroplast-localized mitochondrial calcium uniporter (cMUC), in the regulation of cytosolic and stromal  $\mbox{Ca}^{2+}$  signals induced by hyperosmotic stress [6, 9, 10]. However, these factors do not appear to affect ABA accumulation, gene expression changes, plant growth, and activation of SnRK2 and RAF protein kinases under osmotic stress [6, 9-11]. RAFs are required for activation of SnRK2s [11-14]. The activated SnRK2s mediate multiple physiological processes such as stomatal closure, gene expression changes, and plant growth [15, 16]. However, RAFs and SnRK2s do not affect Ca<sup>2+</sup> signal induced by osmotic stress, and the activation of RAFs and SnRK2s by osmotic stress is not altered in the osca1 mutant [11], suggesting that OSCA1 and SnRK2/RAFs function independently in osmotic stress responses. Osmotic stress responses are controlled by both ABA-dependent and -independent processes [1, 17]. ABA accumulation is a key osmotic stress response, and it is dependent on the osmotic stress induction of the NCED3 gene, which encodes a rate-limiting enzyme in ABA biosynthesis [16]. ABA is sensed by the PYR/PYL/RCAR family of receptor proteins. The ABAbound PYLs interact with and inhibit clade A PP2C protein phosphatases, leading to the activation of SnRK2.2/3/6 protein

Little is known about the sensing and early signaling of osmotic stress that leads to ABA accumulation [\[25\]](#page-10-5). Rapid elevation of cytosolic Ca2+ is a very early event of osmotic stress response and has been used in genetic screens to identify osmotic stress sensing and signaling components. So far, the identified components that regulate the Ca2+ signal in response to osmotic stress do not affect ABA accumulation or other important osmotic stress responses, suggesting that they might not be the central component regulating all aspects of osmotic stress responses [\[6,](#page-9-3) [9](#page-9-6), [20,](#page-10-6) [26\]](#page-10-7). Here, we have carried out an aequorin luminescence-based genetic screen to identify *Arabidopsis* mutants with reduced Ca2+ signal in response to osmotic stress. We identified the copine proteins BONZAIs (BONs) as osmotic stress signaling components that regulate global osmotic stress responses including Ca2+ signaling, ABA accumulation, gene expression, and plant growth.

The copine proteins are evolutionarily conserved and are characterized by two Ca2+-dependent phospholipid-binding C2 domains and an von Willebrand A (VWA) domain in the C-terminal region [[27](#page-10-8), [28](#page-10-9)]. BON1 is localized to the plasma membrane and so are its rice homologs [[28](#page-10-9), [29\]](#page-10-10). This localization is through N-terminal myristoylation and is required for BON1 function [[30](#page-10-11)]. BON1 is able to bind Ca2+ through the C2 domains, and this

In the *bon1* mutants, the expression of a plant intracellular immune receptor or nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR) gene, *SNC1*, is increased, leading to the activation of *EDS1* and *PAD4-*dependent defense responses and consequently a dwarf phenotype [[31](#page-10-12)]. We report here that the BON proteins are upstream regulators of osmotic stress responses including Ca2+ signaling, ABA accumulation, gene expression reprogramming, and growth control. Our analyses revealed that osmotic stress responses are antagonized by NLR signaling, and suppression of NLR signaling by BON proteins is critical for osmotic stress responses.

#### RESULTS

#### OSMO1 Regulates Ca2+ Signaling under Osmotic Stress

In order to identify regulators of Ca2+ signaling in early osmotic stress response, we conducted a forward genetic screen based on the luminescent Ca2+ sensor aequorin. The luminescence emission of aequorin peaks at 465 nm, which falls within the absorption spectrum of chlorophyll *b* [\(Figure S1A](#page-9-9)). To reduce the interference from chlorophylls in young seedlings, we introduced the aequorin reporter system into the *sig6* mutant that has albino cotyledons [\(Figures S1B](#page-9-9) and S1C) [\[20](#page-10-6)]. The *sig6* seedlings had the same calculated Ca2+ elevation in response to osmotic stress or H2O2 treatment as the wild-type (WT) AEQ line with the Ca2+ reporter but with enhanced luminescence signals [\(Fig](#page-9-9)[ures S1](#page-9-9)D and S1E). An ethyl methanesulfonate-mutagenized mutant library was generated in this *sig6* reporter strain (which we refer to as AEQ*sig6*), and about 100,000 M2 seedlings were screened for putative mutants with reduced Ca2+ luminescence in response to mannitol treatment ([Figure S1F](#page-9-9)).

Among the 567 putative mutants isolated from the initial screen, one was confirmed in the next generation [\(Figure 2A](#page-3-0)), and we named the mutant *osmo1* (*reduced osmotic stress induced [Ca2+]cyt increase*). #### OSMO1 Encodes the Ca2+-Dependent Phospholipid-Binding Protein BON1

We isolated the *OSMO1* gene by map-based cloning and wholegenome resequencing. Both cloning approaches revealed that *osmo1-1* is a mutant allele of *BON1*, and we therefore renamed it as *bon1-7* [\(Figure S2F](#page-9-9)). The *bon1-7* (*osmo1-1*) mutant has a Gto-A mutation at the last nucleotide of the 6th intron of *BON1* [\(Fig](#page-9-9)[ure S2](#page-9-9)G). The mutation resulted in two splicing forms, both of which are different from the WT *BON1* transcript. In one form, the transcript was longer because of retention of the 6th intron and was less abundant than the transcript in the WT [\(Figure S2H](#page-9-9)). In the other form, the transcript was similar in size to the WT form but had a deletion of G at the first nucleotide of the exon after the mutation [\(Figure S2I](#page-9-9)), which resulted in a frameshift at codon 215 in the C2B domain [\(Figure S2](#page-9-9)J). To confirm that the mutation in *BON1* caused the Ca2+ signal defect, we used CRISPR/Cas9 to generate an additional *bon1* allele, *bon1-8*, in the AEQ line with the Ca2+ reporter apoaequorin [\(Figures S2G](#page-9-9) and S2K). Indeed, the Ca2+ signal triggered by mannitol was reduced in the *bon1-8* mutant compared to the AEQ WT [\(Figures 2D](#page-3-0) and [S2](#page-9-9)L). Furthermore, the Ca2+ signal defect of the *osmo1-1* mutant was complemented by the expression of WT genomic fragment of *BON1* [\(Figures 2](#page-3-0)C, 2D, and [S2](#page-9-9)M). These results show that *BON1* is the *OSMO1* gene that regulates the Ca2+ signal in response to osmotic stress.

#### BON Proteins Control Stress-Induced ABA Accumulation

Because *BON1* has overlapping functions with *BON2* and *BON3*, we examined additional osmotic stress responses in the *bon1* mutants and the *bon1bon2bon3* mutants. Two new triple mutant combinations were generated for analysis: *bon1- 9bon2-2bon3-4* (named *bon123t*, all from T-DNA insertions) and *bon1-10bon2-4bon3-5* (named *bon123c*, all from CRISPR/ Cas9 editing) [\(Figures S3A](#page-9-9)–S3C). Similar to the previously reported triple mutant *bon1-1bon2-2bon3-3* [\[35\]](#page-10-16), these two mutants also exhibited a seedling lethal phenotype. The shoot growth was arrested after the cotyledons opened, but root growth remained normal for a few days on the Murashige and Skoog (MS) nutrient medium. The *bon1bon2bon3* mutant was hypersensitive to water loss. *BON1* is expressed ubiquitously, while *BON2* is highly expressed in guard cells according to GUS staining and microarray expression analysis [\[35,](#page-10-16) [40](#page-10-25)] [\(Fig](#page-9-9)[ure S3](#page-9-9)D).When seedlings were removed from MS plates and kept at a high relative humidity (around 80%), the WT remained turgid for nearly 20 min, but *bon123t* mutant plants had wilted cotyledons in less than 2 min [\(Figure 3](#page-4-0)A). This hypersensitivity to water loss was also observed in the *bon1* single mutant. Water loss from detached leaves was also more rapid in the *bon1* mutants than the WT ([Figure S3](#page-9-9)E). This result suggests that the BON proteins are important for maintaining shoot turgor under dehydrating conditions. We further examined the stomatal response to osmotic stress in the *bon1* mutant seedlings. While the WT closed stomata after mannitol treatment, the *bon1* mutant only closed its stomata to half of the WT level ([Figure S3F](#page-9-9)).

The rapid water loss phenotypes of the *bon1* and the *bon123* mutants suggested that ABA accumulation may be impaired in these mutants. ABA concentration was therefore measured after seedlings were treated with mannitol. ABA content increased dramatically in the WT seedlings as well as the *bon1* single mutants and *bon2-2bon3-4* (*bon23t*) double mutant [\(Figures 3](#page-4-0)B and [S3](#page-9-9)G). In contrast, no significant increase was observed in the *bon123* mutant seedlings ([Figure 3B](#page-4-0)). We subsequently analyzed the expression levels of the rate-limiting ABA biosynthesis gene *NCED3* [[41\]](#page-10-26) and the key ABA catabolism genes *CYP707As* [\[42\]](#page-10-27). In the WT, *NCED3*, *CYP707A1*, and *CYP707A4* were all induced by osmotic stress. In the *bon123* triple mutants, *NCED3* had a substantially reduced expression compared to the WT both before and after mannitol treatment, whereas *CYP707A1* and *CYP707A4* had a higher expression compared to the WT before and after mannitol treatment [\(Figure 3C](#page-4-0)). #### The BON Proteins Control Gene Expression Reprogramming in Response to Osmotic Stress

To gain a genome-wide view of gene expression changes in the *bon* mutants in response to osmotic stress, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis on the *bon123t* mutant and the WT treated with 300 mM mannitol [\(Data S1](#page-9-9)). Compared to no treatment, 231 and 61 genes were highly induced (4-fold or greater, false discovery rate, p < 0.05, fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads [FPKM] > 50) in WT and *bon123t* seedlings, respectively, at 12 h after treatment. Among these genes, only eight were induced in both the WT and *bon123t* [\(Figure 4A](#page-5-0)). Therefore, most of the mannitol-induced genes in the WT were not upregulated in the *bon123t* mutant, and this is also evident in heatmap analysis ([Figure S4](#page-9-9)A). Gene ontology (GO) analysis indicated that these genes were involved in responses to abiotic stress, osmotic stress, water deprivation, desiccation, salt stress, cold acclimation, or ABA. In contrast, genes induced by osmotic stress in the *bon123t* mutant but not in the WT were not significantly enriched in any biological processes. For genes downregulated by mannitol treatment, none overlapped between the WT and the *bon123t* mutant [\(Fig](#page-9-9)[ure S4](#page-9-9)C). These results show that the *bon* mutations impaired most of the gene induction or repression that occurred in the WT in response to osmotic stress.

ABA treatment led to upregulation of 253 and 406 genes in the WT and *bon123t*, respectively, of which 128 genes were shared. Among these shared ABA-induced genes, 66 were upregulated by mannitol treatment in the WT but not in the *bon123t* mutant [\(Figure 4A](#page-5-0)). Among them, *RD22* and *P5CS1* are two genes that are induced by osmotic stress in an ABA-independent manner since their induction by osmotic stress is not impaired in ABA signaling mutants such as the *pyl* duodecuple and *snrk2.2/3/6* triple mutants [\[17\]](#page-10-2). Consistent with the *P5CS1* expression profile, proline accumulation was impaired in the *bon123t* mutant under both osmotic stress and ABA treatments ([Figure 4F](#page-5-0)). The reduced expression of cuticular wax biosynthesis genes suggests the nonstomatal water loss may be increased in *bon123* mutant seedlings. These results not only support a critical role of the BON proteins in the osmotic stress-induction of gene expression but also suggests a role in controlling ABA induction of a subset of the genes.

#### BONs Regulate Plant Growth under Osmotic Stress

We further analyzed plant growth in response to osmotic stress in the *bon* mutants. The *bon1* mutants have no drastic growth defects when grown on 1/2 MS plate, but they show reduced growth in soil [\(Figure S2E](#page-9-9)). This is likely caused by the relatively low air humidity under soil growth condition. Seedlings of WT and three *bon1* mutants of similar sizes were transferred to a control medium or a medium with NaCl, mannitol, or polyethylene glycol (PEG) that would pose osmotic stress. After 10– 15 days of growth, the WT and the three *bon1* mutants had similar rosette sizes and root lengths on the control medium, but rosette growth of the *bon1* mutants was severely inhibited on the medium with 75 mM NaCl, 50 mM mannitol, or 0.5 MPa PEG ([Figures 5A](#page-7-0), 5B, and [S5](#page-9-9)A). Quantification of rosette size and root weight revealed that rosette growth and root growth were more reduced in the *bon1* mutants compared to the WT on the medium with 50, 100, or 150 mM mannitol ([Figures](#page-7-0) [5](#page-7-0)C, 5D, and [S5](#page-9-9)B). In contrast, growth inhibition by ABA or H2O2 was similar for WT and the *bon1* mutants ([Figure S5](#page-9-9)A). The osmotic stress hypersensitive phenotype of the *bon1* mutant could be complemented by expression of *BON1* driven by its native promoter ([Figure S5](#page-9-9)C), indicating that BON1 represses growth inhibition induced by osmotic stress. No obvious growth defects

were found in the *bon2-2*, *bon3-4*, or *bon2-2bon3-4* mutants in soil or on MS plates [\(Figures S5](#page-9-9)D and S5E). However, the rosette growth under osmotic stress was more reduced in the *bon2* and *bon3* single mutants compared to the WT, and the growth of the *bon2bon3* double mutant was even more severely reduced [\(Fig](#page-9-9)[ure S5](#page-9-9)E). These results show that the BON proteins have critical and partially overlapping functions in maintaining plant growth under osmotic stress conditions.

We tested whether or not the growth defects of the *bon* mutants may be attributed to ABA-dependent osmotic stress responses. The growth inhibition by osmotic stress was examined in the ABA-deficient mutant *aba2-1* as well as the ABA signalingdefective *pyl* duodecuple and *snrk2.2/3/6* mutants. Under the treatment of 100 mM of mannitol, all these mutants exhibited the same morphological phenotype as the WT ([Figure S5F](#page-9-9)). This suggests that ABA signaling does not significantly affect the growth inhibition induced by osmotic stress. Nevertheless, addition of ABA slightly rescued the growth defect of the *bon1* mutant under osmotic stress. While the WT plants had reduced growth with added mannitol in the medium, the *bon1-9* mutant exhibited yellow and dying rosettes. Addition of ABA to the mannitol medium supported an enhanced growth of the *bon1- 9* mutant compared to the mannitol medium, and the WT and the *bon1-9* mutant exhibited similar growth on medium with mannitol and ABA [\(Figure S5G](#page-9-9)). It is also noted that co-treatment of mannitol and ABA led to chlorosis in the WT, suggesting that a balanced ABA signaling is important for osmotic stress response. Together these data suggest that hypersensitivity of the *bon1* mutant to osmotic stress in growth inhibition cannot be totally attributed to the lower accumulation of ABA or ABA signaling. Indeed, osmotic stress-induced ABA accumulation, although blocked in the *bon123* mutants, was not significantly reduced in *bon1* mutants ([Figures 3](#page-4-0)B and [S3G](#page-9-9)), even though the *bon1* mutants were hypersensitive to osmotic stress [\(Figure 5A](#page-7-0)).

#### BON Proteins Control Plant Growth and ABA

#### Accumulation under Osmotic Stress by Repressing NLR Signaling

BON proteins were shown to negatively regulate plant defense responses via the NLR intracellular immune receptors including SNC1, and the *bon* mutants have constitutive NLR activation at normal growth temperature and under non-stress conditions [\[31](#page-10-12)]. We found that NLR signaling was also constitutively activated in the *bon1* mutants under osmotic stress. *CBP60G* and *PR1*, marker genes for NLR activation and SA signaling had higher expression levels in the *bon1* mutant plants compared to the WT throughout the long-term (10 days) osmotic stress treatment ([Figure S5](#page-9-9)H). SA accumulated to a higher level in the *bon1* mutant than the WT under both control and mannitol treatments [\(Figure S5](#page-9-9)I). We therefore investigated the involvement of NLR signaling in the regulation of osmotic stress responses by BON1 protein. Elevated temperature was shown to reduce the growth defect of the *bon1* mutant because SNC1 activity is reduced at higher temperatures [\[31,](#page-10-12) [32\]](#page-10-13). We found that osmotic stress-induced growth defect and the accelerated water loss in the *bon1* mutant could be partially rescued by a higher growth temperature of 28C ([Figures S3](#page-9-9)J and [S5J](#page-9-9)), suggesting an involvement of NLR signaling in the osmotic stress response.

We further tested this hypothesis by analyzing osmotic stress responses in the *bon1* mutant combined with the loss of function in the NLR gene *SNC1* or the NLR signaling regulator *PAD4*. Mutations in these genes have been shown to inhibit immune response activation and suppress the growth defects in the *bon* mutants [\[31](#page-10-12)]. While the *bon1-1* and *bon1-9* mutants showed chlorosis upon mannitol treatment, the *bon1-1snc1-11* and the *bon1-9pad4* double mutants remained green and grew to a similar size as the WT [\(Figures 5E](#page-7-0), 5F, and [S5K](#page-9-9)). These results indicate that the BON1 protein maintains shoot growth under osmotic stress by restraining the NLR protein-mediated responses.

The loss of *PAD4* rescued the lethality [[35](#page-10-16)] and the hyper accumulation of SA of the *bon123* triple mutant ([Figure S5](#page-9-9)I). The *bon123pad4-1* quadruple mutant had a WT-like growth on the control medium, and it exhibited a similar growth inhibition in response to mannitol compared with WT, whereas the *bon1* mutant exhibited hypersensitivity to mannitol [\(Figure 5](#page-7-0)G). Hypersensitivity to water loss in the *bon* mutant was also rescued by the *pad4* mutation. While the *bon123t* mutant wilted within 2 min after being removed from culture medium, the *bon123 pad4-1* quadruple mutant remained turgid as the WT [\(Figure 5H](#page-7-0)). In addition, osmotic stress-induced ABA accumulation, which was blocked in the *bon123t* mutant, was restored in the *bon123 pad4-1* quadruple mutant ([Figure 5](#page-7-0)I). Consistent with the ABA level, osmotic stress-induced expression of ABA metabolism genes, such as *NCED3*, *CYP707A4*, and *CYP707A1*, all returned to the WT level in the *bon123pad4-1* quadruple mutant [\(Fig](#page-7-0)[ure 5J](#page-7-0)). Moreover, osmotic stress induction of *COR15A* and *RAB18*, which was blocked in the *bon123* triple mutant, was restored to the WT level in the *bon123pad4-1* quadruple mutant [\(Figure 5](#page-7-0)J). These results indicate that the altered osmotic stress responses of the *bon* mutants in plant growth, ABA accumulation, and gene expression are dependent on NLR signaling.

#### DISCUSSION

Here, we identify a critical role of BON proteins in the generation of early Ca2+ signal in response to osmotic stress as well as global osmotic stress responses including gene expression reprogramming, ABA accumulation, and plant growth modulation [\(Figures 6](#page-8-0) and [S6](#page-9-9)). Although *BON* genes have overlapping functions in mediating plant growth and stress responses, *BON1* has

For the regulation of ABA accumulation and gene expression changes under osmotic stress, *BON1* has an apparently equal function to *BON2* and *BON3* together ([Figures 3](#page-4-0)B–3D and [S3G](#page-9-9)–S3I). Very few genes are known to affect Ca2+ signal responses. Previous studies reported that osmotic stress-induced elevations of cytosolic and chloroplast stromal Ca2+ are affected by mutations in *OSCA1*, *CCX2*, *KEA*s, and *cMUC* [[6](#page-9-3), [9,](#page-9-6) [10,](#page-9-7) [43](#page-10-28), [44](#page-10-29)]. However, these genes seem to have little effect on downstream global osmotic stress responses. The identification of BON proteins in both processes suggest that these early Ca2+ responses and later osmotic stress responses may not be independent. BON proteins may function to regulate Ca2+ channels and uniporters and coordinate Ca2+-dependent and -independent signaling to control global osmotic stress responses.

BON proteins might be involved in osmotic stress sensing or early signaling. Osmotic stress causes multiple transient biophysical changes such as reduction of turgor pressure, shrinkage of the plasma membrane, and physical alterations of the cell wall. These changes could each be sensed by plant cells and in turn trigger the transient Ca2+ signal. Receptor-like kinases (RLKs) could be one type of those sensors because they have been reported to perceive other external stimuli and to mediate Ca2+-dependent and -independent processes [\[2,](#page-9-10) [45–](#page-10-30) [49](#page-10-30)]. Among them, Feronia (FER) interacts with the pectin component of the cell wall, and controls transient Ca2+ signal induced by osmotic stress in roots [[49\]](#page-11-0), suggesting a role of FER in sensing cell-wall perturbation caused by osmotic stress. FER functions together with BAK1 [[50](#page-11-1)], which controls Ca2+ signal by phosphorylating cyclic nucleotide-gated channels (CNGCs) [\[46](#page-10-31), [51\]](#page-11-2). Intriguingly, BON1 was reported to be phosphorylated by BAK1 *in vitro* [[52](#page-11-3)], and it will be interesting to determine if BON1 is connected with FER and BAK1 to mediate early osmotic stress signaling.

BON proteins could affect Ca2+ signal through several mechanisms. The BON1 protein binds to Ca2+ through its N-terminal C2 domains, and this binding property is essential for its function [\[30](#page-10-11)]. A recent structural study finds additional Ca2+ binding site in its C-terminal region and Ca2+ binding alters its conformation [\[53](#page-11-4)]. These results indicate that BON1 is a Ca2+-responsive protein, and therefore may contribute to Ca2+ homeostasis in response to an initial Ca2+ elevation. In addition, BON1 is shown to interact with two Ca2+ ATPases, ACA8 and 10, on the plasma membrane and regulates cytosolic Ca2+ oscillation in guard cells [\[37](#page-10-18), [38,](#page-10-19) [54](#page-11-5)]. It is possible that Ca2+ binding of BON1 could modulate the interaction of BON1 with ACA8/10 and influence Ca2+ signals. The contribution of the phospholipid binding property to the function of BON1 is not clear. It may confer BON1 a lipid or membrane accessibility and therefore affect its regulation of ACA8/10 and other membrane proteins [\[55](#page-11-6)].

Strikingly, our study reveals that osmotic stress responses are negatively regulated by NLR protein-mediated signaling. Elevation of NLR immune responses in the *bon* mutants impairs diverse aspects of osmotic stress responses including growth maintenance, ABA accumulation, and gene expression changes [\(Figure 6\)](#page-8-0). How NLR signaling affects osmotic stress responses needs to be further investigated. NLR activation causes drastic transcriptional and metabolite reprogramming, which might shift the balance of biotic and abiotic stress responses [\[56](#page-11-7), [57](#page-11-8)]. Activation of NLR could inhibit the ABA pathway and therefore repress osmotic stress responses. This is supported by earlier observation that activation of the NLR gene *SNC1* inhibits stomatal closure and gene expression in response to ABA [\[58,](#page-11-9) [59](#page-11-10)]. However, deficiency in ABA biosynthesis or signaling does not affect all osmotic stress responses, indicating a role of NLR signaling in ABA-independent osmotic stress responses as well. The Toll-like/interleukin-1 receptor (TIR) domains of TIR-NLR proteins including SNC1 function as enzymes that can cleave NAD+ and NADP<sup>+</sup> and produce nicotinamide, ADPR, and v-cADPR [[60,](#page-11-11) [61](#page-11-12)]. Whether and how these metabolites may affect Ca2+ homeostasis and osmotic stress responses is worth investigating in the future.

Intriguingly, unlike the other osmotic stress responses that are impaired in the *bon123* triple mutants, activation of SnRK2s by osmotic stress was enhanced in the *bon123* triple mutants. The SnRK2 proteins are known to be critical for stomatal closure, ABA accumulation, gene expression changes, and

plant growth modulation under osmotic stress [\[16,](#page-10-1) [62\]](#page-11-13). However, their activation did not lead to osmotic stress responses such as stomatal closure and gene expression in the *bon123* mutants. This indicates that activation of the SnRK2s are necessary but not sufficient for such responses in the *bon123* mutants. In addition, the hyper-activation of SnRK2s could be due to the lack of ABA accumulation, because this was also observed in the ABA signaling mutants [\[17\]](#page-10-2). The activation of downstream osmotic stress responses by SnRK2s may require Ca2+ signaling involving Ca2+-activated proteins. For instance, CPKs have differential Ca2+ sensitivities and have similar functions as SnRK2s in regulating stomatal closure and gene expression changes through phosphorylating ion channels and transcription factors, respectively [[63–71\]](#page-11-14). It will be of interest to determine whether and how the CPKs may contribute to SnRK2 regulation of downstream osmotic stress responses in plants.

In summary, we have identified the BON proteins as critical regulators of both the osmotic stress-triggered Ca2+ signal and an array of osmotic stress responses and revealed a global repression of osmotic stress responses by signaling from the NLR plant-immune receptor proteins. This points to an antagonism between abiotic and biotic responses where BON proteins likely have a balancing role.

#### <span id="page-14-0"></span>METHOD DETAILS

#### Optimization of Ca2+ Luminescence-Based Screens

The Ca2+ indicator line in the Col-0 accession with the 35S:apoaequorin construct pMAQ [\[75\]](#page-11-18) was transformed with the vector pSKI015 to generate an activation tagging T-DNA insertion mutant pools [[20](#page-10-6)]. A cotyledon-specific albino mutant AEQ*sig6* was isolated by its enhanced Ca2+ luminescence [\(Figures S1B](#page-9-9)–S1D), and used for generating an EMS mutagenized mutant pool (*sig6*M). Briefly, AEQ*sig6* seeds were soaked overnight and then treated with 0.4% EMS in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH7.5) for 8 h at room temperature with gentle shake. The M1 seeds were washed thoroughly (20 times) with dH2O, and sowed on 1/2 MS medium with 1% sucrose because the *sig6* mutant is a cotyledon-specific albino mutant. Eight-day-old seedlings were transferred to soil at approximately 15-20 seedlings per port. The M2 seeds were harvested in pools (1 pot per pool) and subject to Ca2+ luminescence-based screens.

#### Aequorin-Based Screens for Mutants with Reduced Ca2+ Signal

Ca2+ measurements were performed using transgenic *Arabidopsis* expressing apoaequorin as previously described [\[6,](#page-9-3) [20](#page-10-6)]. For initial screens, M2 seeds were sowed evenly on a 150 mm round Petri dish with a 1 cm interval. Nine-day-old seedlings were pre-sprayed evenly with coelenterazine solution (NanoLight, final concentration 10 mmol/L, 0.1% Triton X-100; 2 mL solution for one Petri dish), and incubated for 4 h at room temperature in the dark for aequorin reconstitution. The whole plate of seedlings was treated with stimuli in the dark to avoid autofluorescence of chlorophyll. The Ca2+ luminescence was immediately imaged using a cooled chargecoupled device (Princeton Instruments, New Jersey, USA) controlled by WinView/32 (Roper). The luminescence was recorded 2- 4 s after treatments and lasted for 2-5 min. According to the dose responses curves generated in Dr. Zhenming Pei lab and our previous study [[2](#page-9-10), [6,](#page-9-3) [20](#page-10-6)], the first image was captured after seedlings treated with 600 mM mannitol, and the same seedlings were treated with 10 mM H2O2 and calcium luminescence images were taken again. Seedlings with reduced osmo-triggered-Ca2+ luminescence and normal signal after H2O2 stimuli in cotyledons and leaves were selected. For secondary screens, the 150 mm Petri dish were equally separated into eight sectors and each sowed with seeds from one putative mutant or the wild type control. Ca2+ signal triggered by mannitol and H2O2 was imaged again and candidate mutants were further selected. After that, the candidates and WT were sowed on a 90 mm round Petri dish, and the Ca2+ signal was analyzed with three different batch of seeds.

#### Quantification of Ca2+ Signal

The stimuli triggered aequorin luminescence (Lstimuli) is measured by WinView/32 software. The rest reconstituted aequorin (Lrest) in seedlings was recorded by treatment with discharging buffer (2 M CaCl2, 10% ethanol). The total luminescence (Lmax) was calculated by Lrest plus Lstimuli. The calibration equation is adopted from the previously described Equation [[76](#page-11-19)]: pCa = 0.332588 (-log*k*) + 5.5593, where *k* is a rate constant equal to stimuli triggered aequorin luminescence (Lstimuli) divided by total luminescence (Lmax).

#### Map-Based Cloning

From the F2 seedlings of a cross of the *osmo1-1* mutant (in the Col-0 background) with wild type plants of the Landsberg *erecta* (L*er*) accession, 458 seedlings that exhibited growth defects under moderate drought were selected and analyzed with simple sequence length polymorphism (SSLP) markers between Col-0 and L*er*. The *OSMO1* gene was mapped to chromosome 5 between two SSLP markers Chr5-24724 (24.72Mb, located in BAC clone MCI2) and CH5R5 (25.19 Mb, located in BAC clone MRG21), using the markers CH520B, Chr5-2132, Chr5-22123, Chr5-22747, Chr5-23498, Chr5-24251, Chr5-25750, Chr5-25993, Chr5-26471 ([Figure S2](#page-9-9)F and [Data S2](#page-9-9)).

#### Whole-Genome Resequencing

The *osmo1-1* mutant was backcrossed to its wild-type starting strain AEQ*sig6*, and approximately 200 F2 seedlings with growth defects under moderate drought were pooled for whole-genome resequencing.

#### Plasmid Constructs

To generate *ProBON1:BON1* construct, the 5.52-kb *BON1* genomic fragment containing 2.28-kb *BON1* promoter were amplified using pENTR-BON1G primers [\(Data S2](#page-9-9)), and cloned into the pENTR vector using the pENTR/D-TOPO Cloning Kit (Invitrogen). The *BON1* genomic fragment was recombined into the pGWB16 binary vector using an LR reaction (Invitrogen). The resulting plasmid was transformed into *bon1-7* mutant using *Agrobacterium*-mediated transformation.

#### Measurement of Water Loss

Plants used for water-loss assays were grown under short-day conditions (10-h light/14-h dark) under well-watered condition. Whole rosettes of 4-week old were cut from the base and weighted at the indicated time points.

For dehydration assay, nine-day-old seedlings were transferred onto the platform of stereoscope (Leica, S8, APO) and monitored at indicated time points.

#### Measurement of ABA Content

Nine-day-old seedlings were transferred from 1/2 MS medium to medium with 200-300 mM mannitol for 12-16 h. Tissues were ground in liquid nitrogen. The ABA concentration was measured by UPLC-MS/MS (Plant Proteomics and Metabolomics Core Facility, Shanghai Center for Plant Stress Biology, CAS) as described [[77](#page-11-20)].

#### Measurement of Proline Content

Nine-day-old seedlings were transferred from 1/2 MS medium to moist filter papers with or without 400 mM mannitol or 30 mM ABA for 24 h. Tissues were ground in liquid nitrogen. Proline concentration was measured using the ninhydrin-based colorimetric assay as described [\[78\]](#page-11-21).

#### In-Gel Kinase Assay

The in-gel kinase assay was performed as reported [\[79\]](#page-11-22). The seedlings were treated with liquid MS medium with or without 0.8 M mannitol for 30 min. Twenty micrograms of total protein were used for electrophoresis, and histone (Sigma St. Louis, MO) was used as substrate. After electrophoresis, the gel was dried under vacuum at 80C for 1 h on filter paper, and exposed to a phosphor imager for 4 days. Radioactivity of 32P was detected with a Personal Molecular Imager (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA).

#### Western Blot Assay

Protein extraction, electrophoresis, and western bolt was performed as described using the polyclonal anti-phosphorylation antibodies for Ser175 in SnRK2.6 [[17\]](#page-10-2). The membranes were incubated at 4C overnight in TBST with 0.5% skim milk containing 1:5000 diluted anti-pS175 or anti-actin antibodies. After washing 3 times (10 min each) with TBST buffer, the membranes were incubated for 2 h at room temperature in TBST containing 1:5000 diluted goat anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated antibodies (Pierce, 32460). The membranes were then washed 3 times and detected using BeyoECL Plus (Beyotime).

#### RT-PCR, Real-Time PCR and RNA Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 9-day-old seedlings using Trizol (Invitrogen). Reverse transcription reactions were performed with 1 m<sup>g</sup> of total RNA by using HiScript II Q RT Supermix (Vazyme). RT-PCR was then performed for 35 cycles. Real-time PCR assay was performed using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) as previously described [\[8\]](#page-9-5).

For RNA sequencing, nine-day-old seedlings, treated with or without 100 mM ABA or 300 mM mannitol on 1/2 MS solid medium for 12 h [\[17](#page-10-2)], were prepared for RNA sequencing at Majorbio (Shanghai, China). The heatmap was formed by TBtools [\[72\]](#page-11-16).

#### <span id="page-15-0"></span>QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Quantification of band intensity in in-gel and immunoprecipitation kinase assays was performed using ImageJ. Student's t test was used to determine the statistical significance between wild type and mutants in assays related to Ca2+ signal, gene expression, ABA content, SA content, proline content, rosette width, root weight, water loss, stomatal aperture, and relative intensity (\*p < 0.05, \*\*p < 0.01, \*\*\*p < 0.001).
