# **Deep RNAseq indicates protective mechanisms of cold-tolerant** *indica* **rice plants during early vegetative stage**

**Raul Antonio Sperotto1 · Artur Teixeira de Araújo Junior2 · Janete Mariza Adamski<sup>3</sup> · Denise Cargnelutti4 · Felipe Klein Ricachenevsky5 · Ben‑Hur Neves de Oliveira2 · Renata Pereira da Cruz<sup>6</sup> · Rinaldo Pires dos Santos3 · Leila Picolli da Silva<sup>7</sup> · Janette Palma Fett2,3**

Received: 21 July 2017 / Accepted: 8 November 2017 © Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2017

# **Abstract**

*Key message* **Cold-tolerance in rice may be related to increased cellulose deposition in the cell wall, membrane fatty acids unsaturation and differential expression of several newly identified genes.**

**Abstract** Low temperature exposure during early vegetative stages limits rice plant's growth and development. Most genes previously related to cold tolerance in rice are from the *japonica* subspecies. To help clarify the mechanisms that regulate cold tolerance in young *indica* rice plants, comparative transcriptome analysis of 6 h cold-treated (10 °C) leaves from two genotypes, cold-tolerant (CT) and cold-sensitive (CS), was performed. Differentially expressed genes were identified: 831 and 357 sequences more expressed in the tolerant and in the sensitive genotype, respectively. The genes with higher expression in the CT genotype were used in systems biology analyses to identify protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks and nodes (proteins) that are hubs and bottlenecks in the PPI. From the genes more expressed in the tolerant plants, 60% were reported as affected by cold in previous transcriptome experiments and 27% are located within QTLs related to cold tolerance during the vegetative stage. Novel cold-responsive genes were identified. Quantitative RT-PCR confirmed the high-quality of RNAseq libraries. Several genes related to cell wall assembly or reinforcement are cold-induced or constitutively highly expressed in the tolerant genotype. Cold-tolerant plants have increased cellulose deposition under cold. Genes related to lipid metabolism are more expressed in the tolerant genotype, which has higher membrane fatty acids unsaturation, with increasing levels of linoleic acid under cold. The CT genotype seems to have higher photosynthetic efficiency and antioxidant capacity, as well as more effective ethylene, Ca2+ and hormone signaling than the CS. These genes could be useful in future biotechnological approaches aiming to increase cold tolerance in rice.

**Keywords** Cellulose · Cold tolerance · Cell wall · Transcriptome · Fatty acid · *Indica* rice

# **Introduction**

Rice is one of the world's most important staple foods; however, several abiotic stresses affect the physiological status of rice plants, leading to decreased grain yield. Low temperature is one of the major environmental factors limiting rice production, mostly in temperate and high altitude areas, due to the tropical origin of the rice species (Cruz et al. [2013](#page-24-0)). Cold temperature has the potential to affect growth and development of rice plants during any developmental stage, from germination to grain filling (Ye et al. [2009](#page-27-0)). During early growth stages, it can severely affect seedling establishment, leading to leaf chlorosis, growth retardation and decreased tillering (Cruz et al. [2013](#page-24-0)). Improved cold tolerance is, therefore, an effective way to increase rice production.

The degree of cold injury in rice plants depends on several factors, such as growth stage, duration of stress condition and severity. Some rice cultivars can tolerate stressful conditions and are able to grow under low temperature (Sanghera et al. [2011](#page-26-0)). According to several studies with large number of cultivars belonging to *japonica* and *indica* subspecies, cold tolerant cultivars usually belong to the *japonica* subspecies (Li et al. [1981](#page-25-0); Mackill and Lei [1997](#page-26-1); Cheng et al. [2007;](#page-24-1) Mertz et al. [2009](#page-26-2); Chawade et al. [2013](#page-24-2); Lindlöf et al. [2015](#page-25-1); Yang et al. [2015;](#page-27-1) Zhang et al. [2016](#page-27-2)), which are more adapted to temperate climates such as the ones in Japan, Korea and Java (Takahashi [1984](#page-27-3)). The *indica* subspecies includes cultivars better adapted to tropical and subtropical environments such as India, China, Indonesia and Brazil. However, low temperatures can be an important problem where almost the entire rice production is based on varieties from the *indica* subspecies. Therefore, studies aimed at understanding the genetic basis of cold tolerance in *indica* genotypes are needed. Fortunately, although less common, a few *indica* cold tolerant genotypes and favorable alleles were already described (Cruz and Milach [2004](#page-24-3); Zhang et al. [2012b;](#page-27-4) Bevilacqua et al. [2015](#page-24-4); Zhu et al. [2015\)](#page-28-0) and appropriate genotypes are available for breeding.

Low temperature affects chlorophyll content and fluorescence, interfering with photosynthesis in rice leaves (Kim et al. [2009\)](#page-25-2). Also, cold stress increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, which accumulate and impair metabolism via cellular oxidative damage (Nakashima et al. [2007](#page-26-3)). Nevertheless, rice plants have several strategies to respond to cold stress, which are not always effective, depending on the intensity. To cope with or adapt to cold stress, rice plants may increase cell membrane stability and modulate ribosomal activity in protein synthesis, helping to maintain cellular function under low temperature (Huang et al. [2012](#page-25-3)). Antioxidant protection is also increased to scavenge ROS and protect rice plants against oxidative damage (Sato et al. [2011;](#page-26-4) Fang et al. [2015\)](#page-24-5).

Genetic analyses indicate that cold tolerance in rice is a quantitative trait. Several QTLs related to cold tolerance in different developmental stages (germination, vegetative and reproductive) have been mapped in the rice genome (Andaya and McKill [2003](#page-24-6); Lou et al. [2007](#page-26-5); Mori et al. [2011](#page-26-6); Cruz et al. [2013;](#page-24-0) Kim et al. [2014;](#page-25-4) Ranawake et al. [2014](#page-26-7); Ma et al. [2015;](#page-26-8) Pan et al. [2015](#page-26-9); Zhu et al. [2015](#page-28-0); Shakiba et al. [2017](#page-26-10)). Therefore, several genes are involved in biochemical and physiological modifications, which ultimately lead to rice cold acclimation and tolerance. A large number of genes were identified and characterized as responsive to cold stress. Some of them lead to cold tolerance when over-expressed in rice plants (for a comprehensive review see Cruz et al. [2013](#page-24-0)). These cold-responsive genes encode signaling components (Saijo et al. [2000](#page-26-11); Wen et al. [2002](#page-27-5); Xiong and Yang [2003](#page-27-6); Chen et al. [2011](#page-24-7); Xie et al. [2012](#page-27-7)), chaperones (Lee et al. [2005](#page-25-5); Aalto et al. [2012](#page-24-8)) and transcription factors (Kim et al. [2009;](#page-25-2) Ye et al. [2009](#page-27-0); Tao et al. [2011](#page-27-8)), especially from the CBF/DREB1 family (C-repeat binding factor/dehydration responsive element binding—Dubouzet et al. [2003;](#page-24-9) Lee et al. [2004a;](#page-25-6) Ito et al. [2006](#page-25-7); Wang et al. [2008](#page-27-9)). Another protein family with central roles in rice responses to cold stress is the MYB family of transcription factors. Overexpression of *OsMYBS3* in rice plants repressed the CBF/DREB-dependent cold signaling pathway. Also, it was shown that *OsMYBS3* responds slowly to cold stress, which suggests that this gene acts in long-term cold adaptation in rice, whereas CBF/DREB genes are up-regulated in the short-term response to low temperature (Su et al. [2010](#page-27-10)).

It is important to note that most of the mentioned studies were performed with *japonica* genotypes. Therefore, the challenge still remains to develop cold tolerant *indica* genotypes suitable for high-latitude regions. To identify and characterize novel genes and metabolic pathways involved in rice cold tolerance, we used two *indica* rice genotypes (previously identified as cold-tolerant (CT) and cold-sensitive (CS) by Adamski et al. [2016](#page-24-10)) in parallel transcriptomic analyses under cold treatment (10 °C for 6 h), followed by in silico and physiological analysis. Our findings should contribute to a better understanding of the molecular and physiological mechanisms involved in cold tolerance in *indica* rice plants and could be useful for future biotechnological and breeding efforts.

# **Materials and methods**

#### **Plant materials and cold treatment**

Previously, we identified low temperature tolerant and sensitive genotypes from the *indica* subspecies (Dametto et al. [2015;](#page-24-11) Adamski et al. [2016\)](#page-24-10). At the vegetative stage, 90 genotypes were evaluated according to the percentage of three leaf-stage plant survival after 10 days at 10 °C (climatic chamber) and 7 days of recovery under normal temperature (in greenhouse conditions). In this screening, the sister lines (derived from the same cross) IRGA 959-1-2-2F-4-1-4-A and IRGA 959-1-2-2F-4-1-4-D-1-CA-1 were characterized, respectively, as tolerant and sensitive to low temperature stress. Percentage of plant survival after 10 days of cold treatment was about 90 and 15%, respectively, for the CT and CS genotypes (Adamski et al. [2016\)](#page-24-10). Visual symptoms of plants are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. In addition, photosynthetic performance was heavily affected by cold in both genotypes. However, the CT genotype was able to recover photosynthetic performance after cold exposure (Adamski et al. [2016\)](#page-24-10). The two genotypes were developed by the IRGA (Rio Grande do Sul Rice Institute) breeding program, as described in our previous work, and were also characterized as CT and CS during the germination stage (Dametto et al. [2015\)](#page-24-11).

Seeds from both selected genotypes were germinated for 5 days in distilled water and then planted into soil and grown in a greenhouse (temperature ranging from 20 to 30 °C, full daylight). At the three-leaf stage (approximately 20 days), plants were transferred to a climatic chamber and maintained at 10 °C for 6 h (light intensity of 40 μmol m−2 s−1). Three biological replicates (each one containing leaves of at least three plants) were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Samples were then maintained at −80 °C until RNA extraction.

#### **RNA extraction and comparative transcriptomic profiling by RNAseq**

Total RNA was extracted from rice leaves of nine plants using Concert Plant RNA Reagent (Invitrogen) and treated with DNase I (Invitrogen). Approximately 20 μg of total RNA was used in high-throughput cDNA sequencing by Illumina HiSeq 2000 technology (Fasteris SA, Plan-les-Ouates, Switzerland[—http://www.fasteris.com/\)](http://www.fasteris.com/). We constructed an individual single-end cDNA library for each rice genotype. The cDNA libraries were prepared according to Illumina's protocols. Briefly, RNAseq was performed using the following successive steps: poly-A purification; cDNA synthesis using a poly-T primer, shotgun method to generate inserts of approximately 500 bp; 3p and 5p adapter ligations; pre-amplification; colony generation; and Illumina singleend 100 bp sequencing.

All low-quality reads (FASTq value<13) were removed, and 3p and 5p adapter sequences were trimmed using Genome Analyzer Pipeline (Fasteris). The remaining low-quality reads with 'n' were removed using a Python script. After cleaning the data (low quality reads, adapter sequences), the mRNAseq data from the two libraries were aligned to the rice genome using the software Bowtie version 0.12.7. Only sequences with up to two mismatches to the rice reference genome ([http://rice.plantbiology.msu.](http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/pub/data/Eukaryotic_Projects/o_sativa/annotation_dbs/pseudomolecules/version_6.1/all.dir/) [edu/pub/data/Eukaryotic\\_Projects/o\\_sativa/annotation\\_dbs/](http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/pub/data/Eukaryotic_Projects/o_sativa/annotation_dbs/pseudomolecules/version_6.1/all.dir/) [pseudomolecules/version\\_6.1/all.dir/](http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/pub/data/Eukaryotic_Projects/o_sativa/annotation_dbs/pseudomolecules/version_6.1/all.dir/)) were used. The SAM files from Bowtie were then processed using Python scripts to assign the frequencies of each read and map them onto references. For data normalization, the scaling normalization method (Robinson et al. [2010\)](#page-26-12) was used. The R package EdgeR (Robinson et al. [2010\)](#page-26-12) was used to assess whether the sequences were differentially expressed. Sequences were considered differentially expressed when their adjusted *p* value was <0.000001 (according to EdgeR) and the foldchange (FC) was ≥1.2 (Supplementary Table 2).

The RNA-seq data obtained in this work was deposited on the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GEO[—https://](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/)—Barrett et al. [2013\)](#page-24-12) and can be accessed using the code GSE103480.

# **Analysis of gene ontology (GO) and metabolism terms**

The loci from differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with increased expression in plants from the CT or CS genotypes were used to search for gene ontology (GO) terms in both datasets. The rice proteome was downloaded from the Rice Genome Annotation Project [\(http://rice.plantbi](http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/)[ology.msu.edu/\)](http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/), and gene description and GO categories of all sequences in the rice genome were annotated using Blast2GO (<http://www.blast2go.com>; Conesa and Götz [2008\)](#page-24-13). Genes with unidentified GO categories were used in additional annotation searches using the Rice Annotation Project Database (RAP-DB[—http://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp](http://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp); Sakai et al. [2013\)](#page-26-13) and the Rice Genome Annotation Project (RGAP—rice.plantbiology.msu.edu; Kawahara et al. [2013](#page-25-8)). Synonym for gene symbols and gene names was obtained at the Oryzabase Integrated Rice Science Database ([https://](https://shigen.nig.ac.jp/rice/oryzabase) [shigen.nig.ac.jp/rice/oryzabase\)](https://shigen.nig.ac.jp/rice/oryzabase).

The metabolism category of each differentially expressed gene was also investigated, using MAPMAN (Usadel et al. [2009](#page-27-11)) and the KEGG database ([http://www.genome.jp/](http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) [kegg/;](http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) Kanehisa and Goto [2000\)](#page-25-9). Functional domains present in putative proteins encoded by DEGs were investigated using RAP-DB.

All differentially expressed sequences defined as transposons or retrotransposons were retrieved and used in a BLAST search against the Rice TE Database (Copetti et al. [2015](#page-24-14)). Only sequences with 100% identity were considered for classification of transposable element sub-class, within the same database.

# **Gene expression analysis by quantitative RT‑PCR**

An independent experiment was performed to check the gene expression of putative cold tolerance-related genes by RT-qPCR. Total RNA was extracted from rice leaves prior to plant exposure to cold and after exposure to 10 °C for 6 h using Concert Plant RNA Reagent (Invitrogen) and treated with DNase I (Invitrogen). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with reverse transcriptase (M-MLV, Invitrogen) using 1 μg of RNA. RT-qPCRs were carried out in a StepOne Real-Time Cycler (Applied Biosystems). All primers (listed in Supplementary Table 1) were designed to amplify 100–150 bp of the 3′-UTR of the genes and to have similar Tm values (60±2 °C). Reaction settings were composed of an initial denaturation step of 5 min at 94 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 10 s at 94 °C, 15 s at 60 °C (fluorescence data collection) and 15 s at 72 °C. Then, samples were held for 2 min at 40 °C for annealing of the amplified products and then heated from 55 to 99 °C with a ramp of 0.1 °C/s to produce the denaturing curve of the amplified products. RT-qPCRs were carried out in 20 μl final volume composed of 10 μl of each reverse transcription sample diluted 100 times, 2 μl of 10X PCR buffer, 1.2 μl of 50 mM MgCl2, 0.1 μl of 5 mM dNTPs, 0.4 μl of 10 μM primer pairs, 4.25 μl of water, 2.0 μl of SYBR Green (1:10,000, Molecular Probe) and 0.05 μl of Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase (5 U/μl, Invitrogen). Gene expression was evaluated using a modified 2−ΔCT method (Schmittgen and Livak [2008\)](#page-26-14), which takes into account the PCR efficiencies of each primer pair (Relative expression Tested gene/control gene = (PCReff CG)Ct CG/(PCReff TG)Ct TG). Rice ubiquitin 5 (*OsUBQ5*) was used as the control gene. Experiments were performed with three biological and four technical replicates.

#### **Comparisons with previous genome‑wide surveys of rice cold‑responsive genes and QTLs**

Genome position or RAP (Os ID) from each gene considered differentially expressed in this work was used to search for matches in lists of DEGs (induced or repressed by cold) in previous papers. For this reason, all genes reported in our work that could be found in previous lists of DEGs under cold treatment were considered matches and indicated in Supplementary Table 3, in individual columns for each paper and with indications of cold treatment conditions.

#### **Cellulose staining in rice leaves**

Rice plants of both genotypes (CT and CS) were grown for 20 days (up to three-leaf stage) and submitted to cold treatment (10 °C) for 10 days in a climatic chamber or maintained at greenhouse conditions (control treatment). Third fully expanded leaves of four plants per genotype and treatment were collected and sectioned in the middle region of the leaf blade. The sections (5 μm thick; 3–4 sections per leaf) were obtained on a Leica CM1850 Cryostat (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), stained with 0.01% Calcofluor White M2R (Fluorescent Brightener 28; Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for cellulose detection, and observed using a Leica DMRB light microscope, equipped with an epi-fluorescence system (Leica UV filter cube A, excitation filter BP 340–380 nm, suppression filter LP 425). The images were obtained with a Leica DFC500 digital color camera using an exposure time of 270 ms.

# **Lipid extraction and fatty acid determination**

Rice plants of both genotypes were grown for 20 days (three-leaf stage) and subjected to cold treatment (10 °C) for 10 days in a climatic chamber (light intensity of 40 μmol m−2 s−1, 16/8 h light/dark) or maintained at greenhouse conditions (control treatment). After grinding the leaves (approximately 2 g of dry matter), lipids were extracted as described by Bligh and Dyer [\(1959](#page-24-17)). Fatty acid composition was determined by Gas Cromatography, with lipids being saponified and metanolized with KOH solution, and then sterified and metanolized with H2SO4 solution (Hartman and Lago [1973](#page-25-12)). Methyl-ester fatty acids were analyzed with a Gas Cromatographer (Agilent Technologies—HP 6890) adjusted with a capillary column HP-INNOWax (60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) with flame ionization detector. Injection and detection temperature was around 250 °C and conduction was made by Nitrogen gas. Standardization of methyl-ester fatty acids and the subsequent retention times were used for fatty acid identification. Linoleic acid levels were expressed as the percentage of total fatty acids contained in the sample.

# **Systems biology analyses**

Whole interactome data for *Oryza sativa japonica* were downloaded from STRING V10.5 database (Szklarczyk et al. [2015](#page-27-13)) to build a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network model from selected genes based on the RNAseq experiment results. The proteins encoded by DEGs in the CT rice plants were used as input to generate the PPI network model with the following parameters: types of interaction set to be from database, experiment or co-expression and a combined score of at least 0.400. The chosen parameters comprise known protein associations coming from either curated databases (database) or from experimentally determined protein interaction (experiment), along with protein associations inferred from available co-expression datasets (co-expression). The resulting PPI network was loaded into Cytoscape software (Shannon et al. [2003](#page-26-17)) for visualization, topological properties calculation and clustering analysis.

The plugin Network Analyzer (Assenov et al. [2008\)](#page-24-18) was used to retrieve "degree" and "betweenness centrality" values of each node. Conceptually, the "degree" measures how highly connected is a node, whereas "betweeness centrality" measures how much flux of information goes through it. Proteins displaying high values of degree (called hubs) have a high number of interactors, while proteins with high values of betweeness (called bottlenecks) intermediate indirect interactions between a large number of other proteins. In this work, hubs and bottlenecks were identified as those with degree and betweeness values (respectively) two standard deviations above the mean.

The clustering analysis was performed through the MCODE plugin (Bader and Hogue [2003](#page-24-19)), which applies an automated algorithm to find densely connected subnetworks. By doing so, it identifies subsets of proteins with a high number of annotated interactions among them, which implies that they might form biological modules. The functional enrichment analysis of the nodes in each cluster was performed using the STRING V10.5 database with false discovery rate less than 0.05.

#### **Statistical analysis**

Mean values of linoleic acid and gene expression (RTqPCR) were compared by the Student's *t* test (*p*≤0.05, 0.01 and 0.001) using the SPSS Base 21.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., USA).

# <span id="page-4-0"></span>**Results**

#### **Overview of cold‑tolerant and cold‑sensitive cDNA library sequencing**

The rice genotypes IRGA 959-1-2-2F-4-1-4-A and IRGA 959-1-2-2F-4-1-4-D-1-CA-1 (hereafter "tolerant genotype" and "sensitive genotype") were previously characterized as tolerant and sensitive to low temperature stress, respectively (Dametto et al. [2015;](#page-24-11) Adamski et al. [2016](#page-24-10)). Leaves from plants of both genotypes under cold treatment (10 °C for 6 h) were used to identify differentially expressed mRNAs using the Illumina Platform. We chose to use 6 h of cold treatment to detect cold-specific and not general stressrelated transcripts. Deep sequencing generated approximately 20 million reads in each library (tolerant and sensitive genotypes). From these, 23.28% (4,910,832 reads) and 24.49% (4,964,985 reads) were mapped, respectively, in rice genomic regions.

Comparison of gene expression between the CT and the CS plants after low temperature treatment was performed as a preliminary approach, allowing the screening of genes possibly important for cold tolerance. Most of the sequences showed similar normalized number of reads in samples from both genotypes. This may be partially explained by the fact that the rice genotypes used in this study are sister lines (Dametto et al. [2015;](#page-24-11) Adamski et al. [2016](#page-24-10)). Overall, there were clear linear relationships in the gene expression levels among the two rice genotypes (*R*2=0.9629; Supplementary Fig. 2a). For this reason, we used an extremely rigorous adjusted *p* value (*p*<0.000001) as criteria for assigning a gene as differentially expressed (Supplementary Fig. 2b), together with a less rigorous fold-change as criteria for differential gene expression (FC≥1.2). It is well established that even small changes on gene expression can have biological meaning. Using these parameters, we found 1188 differentially expressed sequences, which were included in our analysis (Supplementary Table 2). From these, 831 sequences (69.9%) were more expressed in the CT genotype, while 357 sequences (30.1%) were more expressed in the CS one (Supplementary Table 2).

Interestingly, 20.76% of the sequences with higher expression in the CS plants can be described as transposons or retrotransposons (74 sequences/357 total), while their representation in the CT library was of only 2.64% (22 sequences/831 total). Different categories of transposons or retrotransposons are more represented in the CT or CS lists. There are six *long terminal repeat* (*LTR*) retrotransposons in the CT list (27% of 22), but only two (2.7% of 74) in the CS list. On the other hand, 71 (96% of 74) belong to the CACTA/En-Spm transposon category in the CS genotype, whereas two (9% of 22) belong to the same category in the CT one (Supplementary Table 2).

#### **Meta‑analysis of differentially expressed genes**

Supplementary Table 2 contains a list of 831 genes considered more expressed in the CT plants than in the CS ones. From those, 497 (59.81%) had been previously considered induced or repressed by cold treatment in rice plants (Supplementary Table 3) in other large-scale transcriptomic experiments (Rabbani et al. [2003;](#page-26-15) Cheng et al. [2007](#page-24-1); Mittal et al. [2012](#page-26-16); Zhang et al. [2012b](#page-27-4); Chawade et al. [2013;](#page-24-2) Lindlöf et al. [2015;](#page-25-1) Amaral et al. [2016\)](#page-24-15). Results from comparisons with data from Cheng et al. ([2007\)](#page-24-1) were not included in the table, because the experiments reported by those authors were performed with very young rice seedlings (stage S3), very different from plants around the vegetative stage 3 (V3) used in the present work and in the other reports. Moreover, only two matching genes were identified, both also identified as matches in the CT spreadsheet with at least one of the research papers present in the table (Os11g0106700— *Ferritin 1* and Os03g0147400—*Citrate transporter protein*).

This work contributes with a list of 334 potentially novel genes possibly associated with cold tolerance in rice. Considering the genes with the highest fold-change (above five), there are twelve "expressed proteins" and one "hypothetical protein". From those, nine have not been reported as related to cold responses in previous transcriptomic analyses of cold stress in rice, representing potential opportunities to identify new low-temperature tolerance strategies in rice plants. The list of genes more expressed in the CS genotype was also compared to previously published lists of genes induced or repressed under cold treatment. The percentage of genes previously reported as affected by cold treatment present in our CS list of genes was smaller (38%—136 out of 357) than in the CT list. Considering genes with fold-change higher than five, there are fourteen transposon or retrotransposon genes in the CT list and four in the CS list, with only one of them (from the CT list) previously related to cold responses (Supplementary Table 3).

Within the 100 genes with the highest fold-change (tolerant/ sensitive FC>3.85), 31 genes were co-localized with cold-tolerance QTLs (Supplementary Table 3—fourth spreadsheet). Although most genes that co-localized with previously identified QTLs located within only one QTL, 29 genes had two or more hits. #### **Cellulose staining and fatty acid composition**

Several genes related to cellulose synthesis had higher expression in the CT genotype (Table [1](#page-7-0); Fig. [3\)](#page-10-0), suggesting that tolerance could involve cell wall reinforcement. We used Calcofluor White to stain cell wall cellulose in leaf sections of plants of both genotypes exposed to control and low temperatures. Higher cellulose staining (higher fluorescence for the same exposure time) was clearly observed in mesophyll cell walls of the CT genotype (Fig. [6c](#page-13-0)) than in the CS one (Fig. [6](#page-13-0)d), after 10 days at 10 °C. Under control temperature, no visual difference was detected on the cellulose staining (Fig. [6a](#page-13-0), b).

Since we found higher expression of fatty acid desaturases in leaves from the CT genotype (Table [1;](#page-7-0) Fig. [4](#page-11-0)), we analyzed the levels of methyl-esther fatty acids in leaves of tolerant and sensitive genotypes. Levels of linoleic acid (C18:2n6) are increased in leaves of the tolerant genotype when plants are under low temperature stress (10 °C for 10 days, Fig. [7](#page-14-0)). However, under control conditions, both genotypes show similar levels.

#### **Computational systems biology analyses**

A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network containing a total of 454 proteins was formed (Fig. [8](#page-15-0)a and Supplementary Table 4), in which the nodes correspond to predicted proteins from the genes with higher expression in the CT than in the CS genotype under cold treatment (blue to black nodes in Fig. [8a](#page-15-0), according to fold-change). The formed PPI network contains 4114 interactions (edges). From the 831 genes with higher expression in the CT genotype, 377 were not able to form connections in the network, considering the three criteria used in the analysis. The twelve categories of metabolic processes with the highest representation in the network were attributed individual colors, to allow visualization of their distribution (Fig. [8b](#page-15-0)). From the clusters formed within the network, the two with the highest scores are shown in Fig. [8c](#page-15-0). Cluster I contains 55 nodes (proteins) and 1214 edges (interactions), with clustering score of 44.963. Cluster II contains 22 nodes and 188 edges, with score 17.905. Functional enrichment analysis showed that cluster I was more enriched with proteins from the metabolic process "photosynthesis". In cluster II, the metabolic processes "cellulose metabolic process", "cell wall organization" and "cellulose biosynthetic process" are more enriched, as well as the biological function "cellulose synthase (UDP-forming) activity". The false discovery rate of all enrichment tests was lower than 0.05.

Parameters indicative of network centralities were calculated for each node and a topology scatter plot was elaborated (Fig. [9a](#page-16-0)). The connectivity of each node is indicated by the number of its interacting partners ("node degree"). Highly connected nodes in a network are named as ''hubs'' (large circles in Fig. [9](#page-16-0)b). Betweenness centrality measures how frequently the shortest path connecting every pair of nodes is crossing a given node. All nodes with high betweenness values are named as ''bottlenecks'' (dark orange in Fig. [9](#page-16-0)b). We identified six nodes that are hub-bottlenecks, with values above the thresholds (two standard deviations above the averages) for both connectivity and betweenness centrality. Two of them are proteins from the "photosynthesis" metabolism (ATP synthase gamma chain and ferredoxin), one from "tetrapyrrole synthesis" (oxidoreductase, obviously important for photosynthesis due to its importance in chlorophyll synthesis), one from "fermentation" (aldehyde dehydrogenase), one from "lipid metabolism" (acetyl-CoA carboxylase) and one classified as "metabolism unknown" (ATP synthase F1, delta subunit family protein), which may be related to ATP synthesis in photosynthesis or respiration. We also identified twelve nodes which are bottlenecks (but not hubs). These bottlenecks are from the metabolism categories "lipid metabolism", "signaling", "cell wall", "RNA", "miscellaneous" and "unknown". From those, three are receptor kinases and three are cytochrome P450 proteins.

# **Discussion**

Growth and productivity of rice plants are negatively affected by low temperature, especially in tropical varieties, which are mostly from *indica* background. Here, we used RNAseq to identify genes differentially expressed in two sister lines that are tolerant and sensitive to cold during the vegetative phase of growth.

Our approach to screen for interesting genes was different from previous ones. We submitted both genotypes to cold treatment and identified genes more expressed in the CT or in the CS one. Other works have identified genes more expressed under cold treatment than under control conditions. Our approach proved to be effective in identifying novel genes, possibly associated with cold tolerance in rice: considering the genes with higher expression in the CT than in the CS genotype under cold treatment, 334 had not been previously reported as cold-responsive (Supplementary Table 3) and only 27% correspond to QTLs previously associated to cold tolerance in rice during the vegetative stage (Fig. [5](#page-12-0)). A total of 237 genes were "completely novel" considering these analyses, since they were not reported previously neither as cold-responsive in transcriptomic studies nor as co-localized with cold-tolerance QTLs. One example of those is a remorin-encoding gene (LOC\_Os03g02040.1, fold-change tolerant/ sensitive = 8.9, Supplementary Table 2). Remorin proteins seem to be specific to plants and have been divided in six subgroups. #### **Possible role of LTR retrotransposons on cold tolerance**

Transposable elements (TE) comprise about one-third of the rice genome (IRGSP [2005\)](#page-25-15) and are considered to contribute to phenotypic diversity and adaptation to environmental stress in plants (Song and Cao [2017](#page-26-19)). TE activity has been linked to several distinct mechanisms that ultimately result in increased phenotypic plasticity and adaptation to a wide range of stresses (Negi et al. [2016](#page-26-20)). Although TE activity (transcriptional activation and/or transposition) has been more frequently associated with heat stress, a few cases were reported in which cold treatment resulted in increased transcriptional activity of TEs in *Arabidopsis* (Zeller et al. [2009](#page-27-17); Tittel-Elmer et al. [2010\)](#page-27-18), orange (Butelli et al. [2012\)](#page-24-22) and maize (Makarevitch et al. [2015](#page-26-21)). In rice, cold treatment was associated with increased transcript abundance of centromeric retrotransposons in aerial tissue (Neumann et al. [2007\)](#page-26-22) and of *MITEs* (*miniature inverted-repeat transposable elements*) in anthers with cold-induced pollen sterility (Ishiguro et al. [2014](#page-25-16)). Interestingly, the cold-responsive DNA transposon *mPing* has a very high copy number in a cold-adapted rice cultivar and new insertions of the same transposon convert nearby genes into cold-responsive (Naito et al. [2009](#page-26-23)).

In our work, both the number of TEs and their category distribution were diverse between the two lists of genes. Fewer transposable elements were more expressed in the CT rice genotype than in the CS one. However, the first list contained 27% of *long terminal repeat* (*LTR*) retrotransposons, while the second list was made almost exclusively of *CACTA*/*En-Spm* transposons (Supplementary Table 2). *LTR* retrotransposons are the most predominant TE category in rice plants, with 340 families (El Baidouri and Panaud [2013](#page-24-23)). To be transpositionally active, *LTR* retrotransposons need to be transcribed and translated. Transcriptional activation of *LTR* retrotransposons has been reported to be induced by environmental stress such as iron excess and *in vitro* callus culture (Finatto et al. [2015\)](#page-25-17) and to change the expression of an adjacent gene (activator of anthocyanin synthesis) in a cold-dependent fashion in blood oranges (Butelli et al. [2012](#page-24-22)). *CACTA* elements (also called *En*/*Spm* family) are autonomous DNA transposons, which transpose via a DNA intermediate and have the core sequence CACTA in their terminal inverted repeats. *CACTA* transposons encode a DNA-binding protein and a transposase, both essential for their transposition (Wang et al. [2003](#page-27-19)). A protein similar to a transposase encoded by *CACTA* transposons accumulates in rice plants exposed to the fungus *Magnaporthe grisea* (He et al. [2000](#page-25-18)), but we found no previous reports linking increased expression of *CACTA* transposons with plant exposure to abiotic stresses. Therefore, we suggest that increased transcription of *LTR* retrotransposons may be related to cold tolerance in rice plants. A role of *CACTA* DNA transposons in cold-stressed plants deserves further investigation.

# **Cell wall reinforcement: a new strategy for cold tolerance in rice**

Our results from cellulose staining suggest that cold tolerance is associated with higher deposition of cellulose in the cell wall specifically during cold treatment, which is not observed under normal, non-stressing temperatures (Fig. [6](#page-13-0)). Expression of a number of cell wall-related sequences, including several genes associated with cell wall biosynthesis, was higher in the CT than in the CS genotype (Table [1](#page-7-0); Fig. [3](#page-10-0)). High activity of cellulose synthase (CESA) and cellulose synthase-like (CSL) proteins (Fig. [3](#page-10-0)a, b, d, e) may be important to maintain cellular integrity during cold stress. Since cellulose synthesis in cell walls is guided by microtubules (Endler et al. [2015\)](#page-24-20), higher expression of tubulin genes (Fig. [3](#page-10-0)g; Table [1](#page-7-0)—"Cell") may be part of the same response. COBRA and Cobra-like proteins are anchored in the plasma membrane and also take part in the process of cell wall synthesis (Endler et al. [2016](#page-24-24)). Studies in *Arabidopsis* and maize indicate the significance of COBRA in the proper elongation and morphology of roots and shoots (Chandran et al. [2016](#page-24-25)). In rice, mutations in *BC1* (*Brittle Culm1*, a cobra-like protein) result in lower cell wall thickness and cellulose content. It was suggested that the *BC1* gene controls the mechanical strength of monocots and plays an important role in the biosynthesis of the cell walls of mechanical tissues (Li et al. [2003](#page-25-19)). The higher expression of a *BC1* gene (*OsBCL1L4*) in the CT genotype, observed in our work (Fig. [3f](#page-10-0)), strengthens the hypothesis that strong cell walls are also important to cope with cold stress in rice plants.

High expression of cellulose synthase genes has been associated with tolerance to biotic (Zheng et al. [2013](#page-27-20)) and abiotic (Liu et al. [2012\)](#page-25-20) stresses in rice plants. Recently, it was demonstrated that maintenance of microtubule organization for cellulose synthase positioning and continuous cellulose deposition is key for biomass production during salt stress in *Arabidopsis* (Endler et al. [2015\)](#page-24-20). We detected higher cellulose staining on mesophyll cell walls of the CT genotype, when compared to the CS (Fig. [6](#page-13-0)). We suggest that a mechanism similar to what is observed in *Arabidopsis* during salt stress (Endler et al. [2015\)](#page-24-20) could be contributing for cold tolerance in rice.

It has been suggested that the initial molecular responses of Sijung (a CT *japonica* rice variety) plants may be mainly targeted at strengthening the cell wall and plasma membrane (Lindlöf et al. [2015\)](#page-25-1), while another tolerant variety seems to prioritize protection of chloroplast translation and detoxification processes. However, no genes related to cellulose synthesis were reported as cold-responsive by those authors, who based their suggestion on the cold activation of expression of genes related to calcium and phosphorylation signaling pathways, and of lipid transporters and exocytosisrelated proteins. Those processes could be coupled to cell wall and plasma membrane reinforcement. Maintenance of cell wall integrity seems to be an efficient approach to generate CT rice plants. For example, over-expression of *OsPRP3* (a proline-rich protein that determines extracellular matrix structure in floral organs) confers cold tolerance to rice plants (Gothandam et al. [2010\)](#page-25-21).

From the four *cellulose synthase* genes and five *cellulose synthase-like* genes that were more expressed in the CT plants in this work, *OsCESA5, OsCESA6, OsCSLE6* (Amaral et al. [2016\)](#page-24-15), *OsCESA1, OsCSLF6* and *CSLD2* (Chawade et al. [2013\)](#page-24-2) had been previously seen as induced by cold. However, those authors did not discuss those findings and did not consider the possibility of cellulose synthesis as a possible component of cold-tolerance mechanisms.

The higher expression levels of an *expansin* gene identified in this study (Table [1](#page-7-0); Fig. [3c](#page-10-0)) may also be related to the capacity of the tolerant rice genotype to continue growing under low temperature. Expansins facilitate cell wall loosening, necessary for cellular expansion, and are related to cold/ abiotic stress tolerance in cotton and *Arabidopsis* (Zheng et al. [2012;](#page-27-21) Abuqamar et al. [2013](#page-24-26)). In a chilling-sensitive *indica* rice cultivar (Pusa Basmati), expression of *expansin* is repressed after cold treatment (Mittal et al. [2012](#page-26-16)).

In the halophyte ice plant, the wound-induced protein WI12 preferentially accumulates in the cell wall and it was suggested that it plays a role in the reinforcement of the cell wall after wounding (Yen et al. [2001\)](#page-27-14). As suggested by our results (Fig. [3h](#page-10-0); Table [1](#page-7-0)—"Unknown"), this protein may also have a protective role after low temperature exposure.

Based on our gene expression analyses and the cellulose staining results, we suggest that cell wall reinforcement, including cellulose synthesis, can be an important component in cold-tolerance strategies in plants.

#### **Membrane alterations in the cold‑tolerant genotype**

Expression of fatty acid desaturases (such as *OsFAD8, OsFAD7* and *OsFAD2*; Table [1](#page-7-0)—"Lipid Metabolism", and Fig. [4a](#page-11-0)) and linoleic acid levels (Fig. [7](#page-14-0)) were higher in leaves of the CT genotype than in the sensitive one. Membranes are major targets for temperature acclimation strategies in plants (Román et al. [2012\)](#page-26-24). There is a strong association between temperature and fatty acid content of plant membranes (Iba [2002\)](#page-25-22). For example, increasing levels of saturated fatty acids were correlated with higher sensitivity to cold temperatures in *Arabidopsis* plants (Murata et al. [1992\)](#page-26-25), while lower temperatures are associated with increased production of unsaturated fatty acids in cotton plants (Kargiotidou et al. [2008\)](#page-25-23) and the maintenance of membrane fluidity, probably due to their lower melting temperatures (Iba [2002\)](#page-25-22). Two genes encoding chloroplast fatty acid desaturases (*OsFAD7* and *OsFAD8*) were more expressed in the CT plants (Table [1](#page-7-0)). It has been previously suggested that unsaturation of fatty acids by OsFAD8 is crucial for the acclimation of higher plants to low-temperature stress (Tovuu et al. [2016\)](#page-27-15). Knockout *OsFAD8* mutants have altered levels of fatty acid unsaturation, decreased membrane fluidity and higher sensitivity to short-term low-temperature stress (Tovuu et al. [2016](#page-27-15)). Over-expression of *OsFAD2* (a delta-12 fatty acid desaturase, the key enzyme for the conversion of oleic acid into linoleic acid) enhances tolerance to low temperature in rice plants at the vegetative stage (Shi et al. [2012](#page-26-26)). We also found this gene (*OsFAD2*) more expressed in the CT than in the CS genotype (Table [1\)](#page-7-0).

Interestingly, most studies indicate that linolenic acid (C18H30O2, with three double bonds) is mainly responsible for CT phenotypes (Kargiotidou et al. [2008;](#page-25-23) Cruz et al. [2010,](#page-24-27) [2013\)](#page-24-0) due to the maintenance of membrane fluidity and chloroplast function at low temperatures (Routaboul et al. [2000](#page-26-27); Zhang et al. [2012b\)](#page-27-4). In this work, leaf concentrations of this fatty acid did not change in the tested genotypes after exposure to 10 °C for 10 days (data not shown). Our data suggest that the cold-tolerant genotype is benefited from high levels of another unsaturated fatty acid (linoleic acid, C18H32O2, with two double bonds). It is possible that longer exposure to cold could lead to further unsaturation, converting linoleic acid molecules in linolenic acid. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that fatty acid unsaturation is related to cold tolerance in an *indica* rice genotype. Independently of accumulation of specific fatty acids, our data suggest that the cold tolerance mechanism in the genotype IRGA 959-1-2-2F-4-1-4-A is related to accumulation of unsaturated fatty acids in membranes.

Membrane composition can also be affected by differential regulation of the *cycloartenol synthase* gene, which was more expressed in the tolerant genotype than in the sensitive one under cold treatment (Supplementary Table 2; foldchange tolerant/sensitive=6.5). This enzyme catalyzes the first committed step in sterol biosynthesis in plants, which is the cyclization of 2,3-oxidosqualene to form cycloartenol. Sterols are major membrane components, together with glycerolipids and sphingolipids (Valitova et al. [2016](#page-27-22)). Other oxidosqualene cyclases convert the same substrate in other compounds, which are precursors for triterpene synthesis (Inagaki et al. [2011](#page-25-24)). Rice cycloartenol synthase has also been named as oxidosqualene cyclase 4 (OsOSC4, Inagaki et al. [2011\)](#page-25-24). The *OsOSC4* gene was reported to bear a transposon insertion, and its expression could not be detected in Nipponbare *japonica* rice (Inagaki et al. [2011](#page-25-24)). In the present work, however, its expression is 6.5 times higher in the CT than in the CS *indica* plants under cold treatment. Interestingly, the same gene was downregulated by cold in a CS *indica* rice genotype (Amaral et al. [2016](#page-24-15)). The role of cycloartenol synthase in rice cold tolerance deserves further investigation, since increased sterol synthesis and further changes in the ratio of molecular types of membrane sterols may help plants to better cope with stress. The lipid composition of plant membranes is known to be considerably altered by cold stress, including not only the degree of unsaturation of phospho- and glycolipids, but also the relative ratio of sterols (Valitova et al. [2016\)](#page-27-22).

A membrane-associated remorin protein, with a possible role in cell wall remodeling, may also be involved in cold tolerance, as discussed above.

# **Improved photosynthetic performance in the cold‑tolerant genotype**

We found several genes related to photosynthesis with higher leaf expression in the CT genotype after six hours of cold treatment (Table [1\)](#page-7-0), including genes related to the light-dependent reactions (*chlorophyll A-B binding proteins*, photosystems I and II components) as well as to the Calvin cycle, such as *RuBisCO small chain* (*OsRBCS2*) and *Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase* (*GAPDH*) (Table [1](#page-7-0); Fig. [4](#page-11-0)b, c). High expression levels of photosynthesis-related genes may account for the previously reported better photosynthetic efficiency of CT plants from the same genotype under low temperature stress (Adamski et al. [2016](#page-24-10)). Studies using chlorophyll fluorescence suggest that the reaction center of photosystem II (PSII) is one of the main targets of cold stress and that when the rice seedlings are chilled, the superstructure of chloroplasts is altered and the efficiency of electron transfer function decreases (Strasser et al. [2004](#page-27-23); Guo-Li and Zhen-Fei [2005\)](#page-25-25). We found that the photosynthetic performance index in relation to absorption (PIABS) and the total photosynthetic performance index (PIABS,total, that measures electron flux till the final electron acceptors of PSI), decreased greatly in both genotypes after cold stress. However, during the recovery period, the tolerant genotype showed considerable increase in these parameters (Adamski et al. [2016](#page-24-10)). This improved photosynthetic performance in the CT genotype may be related to improved fluidity of chloroplast membranes under cold stress due to higher expression of chloroplast fatty acid desaturases (Table [1\)](#page-7-0). Similar results were previously reported, with *F*v/*F*m ratio decreasing more pronouncedly in CS rice genotypes (Chawade et al. [2013](#page-24-2); Yang et al. [2015\)](#page-27-1). Higher expression of genes related to the carbon reactions in photosynthesis may also result in improved cold tolerance. Two *GAPDH* genes encoding chloroplast isoforms of the enzyme were more expressed in the CT than in the CS genotype under cold treatment (Supplementary Table 2). One of them (LOC\_Os04g38600) was previously shown to be differentially expressed in rice plants with increased resistance to fungal infection (Huang et al. [2014a,](#page-25-26) [b\)](#page-25-27). Therefore, increased expression of this gene may be important in a wide range of stress responses in rice plants. Interestingly, four out of the six hub-bottleneck proteins are related to photosynthesis, as pointed out at the "[Results](#page-4-0)" section.

#### **Boosted antioxidant capacity to handle cold stress**

Antioxidant capacity has been extensively related to different stress responses in plants. We found several related sequences with higher expression in the CT genotype, including glutathione peroxidases, superoxide dismutases, ascorbate peroxidase and catalase (Fig. [4d](#page-11-0); Table [1](#page-7-0)— "Redox" and "Miscelaneous"). In a previous study, we showed that plants from the CT genotype have higher SOD activity after 48 h and higher catalase activity after 72 h of cold treatment, when compared to the CS one (Adamski et al. [2016](#page-24-10)). Similarly, other studies with rice cultivars indicate the importance of increased antioxidant capacity for cold-tolerance (Bonnecarrère et al. [2011;](#page-24-28) Zhang et al. [2012](#page-27-16)a). Expression of *APX1* is highly induced after 6 h of low temperature stress in rice plants from a weedy CT *indica* variety from the USA (Bevilacqua et al. [2015](#page-24-4)). Transgenic rice overexpressing the ascorbate peroxidase *OsAPXa* exhibits elevated cold tolerance, likely due to increased H2O2 scavenging and decreased lipid peroxidation (Sato et al. [2011](#page-26-4)). A higher antioxidant capacity seems to be part of the tolerance mechanisms in the *indica* rice genotype studied here.

#### **Calcium signaling in the cold‑tolerant plants**

We detected several Ca2+-signaling-related genes differentially expressed in the tolerant genotype, including *Calmodulin-binding proteins* (Fig. [4](#page-11-0)f), *Calmodulin-related calcium sensor proteins* (*OsCML16* and *OsCML27*), *Calmodulin, Calcium-transporting ATPase* (plasma membrane type) and *calcium*/*calmodulin-dependent protein kinases* (Table [1—](#page-7-0)"Signaling" and "Protein"; Supplementary Table 2). The important roles of calcium in cold sensing and signaling during rice vegetative stage have already been revealed (Mittal et al. [2012;](#page-26-16) Zhang et al. [2013;](#page-27-24) Ma et al. [2015](#page-26-8)). Briefly, membrane rigidification-activated mechanosensitive or ligand-activated Ca2+ channels promote Ca2+ influx into the cytosol, an important and early event caused by cold stress (Chinnusamy et al. [2010](#page-24-29); Zhang et al. [2013](#page-27-24)). Subsequently, Ca2+ signatures are interpreted and amplified by calcium sensors, such as calmodulin, calmodulin-like proteins and calcium-dependent protein kinases (Zhang et al. [2013](#page-27-24)). Over-expression of *OsCDPK7* (a calciumdependent protein kinase; CAMK, LOC\_Os04g49510 in Table [1—](#page-7-0)"Protein") leads to enhanced cold tolerance, and the extent of cold tolerance correlates well with its level of expression (Saijo et al. [2000\)](#page-26-11). Yet, over-expression of the *Arabidopsis AtbZIP60* gene in rice plants leads to cold tolerance through the increased expression of Ca2+-dependent protein kinase genes (Tang and Page [2013\)](#page-27-25). Annexins are proteins able to interact with membrane phospholipids in a Ca2+-dependent manner and probably may function in the process of membrane resealing (Konopka-Postupolska et al. [2011\)](#page-25-28). Although classified in the metabolism category "Cell" (Table [1](#page-7-0)), we consider the annexin gene *OsANN9*, which was also more expressed in the CT plants (Fig. [4e](#page-11-0)), as related to Ca signaling. Some reports have linked annexin signaling to stress response and also with abiotic stress tolerance (Jami et al. [2012](#page-25-29); Lee et al. [2004b](#page-25-30)), including low temperatures in wheat and rice (Jami et al. [2012](#page-25-29); Winfield et al. [2010](#page-27-26)).

#### **Transcription factors more expressed in the cold‑tolerant plants**

Several cold-related transcription factors were detected in our experiments with higher leaf expression in the CT genotype, including genes from the *AP2, DREB, MYB, WRKY* and *NAC* families (Table [1—](#page-7-0)"RNA" and "Development"; Supplementary Table 2).

Six genes encoding Apetala2 (AP2) domain transcription factors were up-regulated in the CT genotype (Supplementary Table 2; Table [1](#page-7-0)—"RNA" and "Hormone Metabolism"), while no one in the CS plants. Interestingly, all of these six AP2 domain-containing proteins are considered Ethylene Response Factors (ERF). Some of those genes (*ERF103, ERF102* and *ERF3*) were previously reported as up-regulated by cold, drought and salt stress (Mishra et al. [2013](#page-26-28)). Five *AP2* genes that we found as up-regulated in the CT genotype had been previously identified in large-scale cold stress experiments in rice (Supplementary Table 3). Therefore, cold tolerance may require ethylene signaling, mediated by AP2 domain-containing transcription factors, in at least some rice genotypes.

Two *DREB1* genes were more expressed in the CT genotype (Table [1](#page-7-0)—"RNA"). *DREB1* genes represent one of the most important discoveries in the field of cold stress (Sanghera et al. [2011\)](#page-26-0), and manipulation of *DREB1* expression levels contributes to increased cold tolerance in rice plants (reviewed in Cruz et al. [2013](#page-24-0)). However, high *OsDREB1* expression *per se* is not enough to generate cold-tolerance, since *DREB1* genes were reported to be similarly expressed on both CT and CS rice genotypes (Chawade et al. [2013\)](#page-24-2). The OsMYBS3-mediated cold stress response is slow and occurs after the DREB1-mediated one, implying that there is an additional pathway controlling cold stress response in rice. Actually, MYBS3 can repress the expression of *DREB1* genes (Su et al. [2010](#page-27-10)). Higher expression of *OsMYBS3* in a CT rice cultivar has been reported and it was suggested that this gene plays key roles in the regulatory pathway of chilling stress tolerance (Zhang et al. [2012](#page-27-16)a). However, increased *OsMYBS3* expression after 5 h of cold treatment was also detected in a CS rice cultivar, weakening the hypothesis that this gene may be part of a cold-tolerance mechanism present in all rice genotypes (Mittal et al. [2012](#page-26-16)). In the present work, *OsMYBS3* expression was not considered significantly higher in the CT plants (fold change = 1.148). However, four "*MYB family transcription factors*", three "*Myb-like DNA-binding domain containing proteins*" and one "*Mybrelated transcription activator*" were more expressed in the CT genotype, while only two "*MYB family transcription factors*" were more expressed in the sensitive one (Supplementary Table 2). Interestingly, expression of one *MYB transcription factor* was 7.08 times higher in the CT than in the CS genotype (Table [1](#page-7-0)—"RNA") and had been previously reported as induced by cold treatment in rice (Supplementary Table 3). Further investigation is needed about a possible role of specific MYB transcription factors in responses to cold in rice plants.

The zinc finger protein ZFP182 is a transcription activator that positively regulates expression of stress defense genes in rice plants, enhancing cold tolerance when overexpressed (Huang et al. [2012](#page-25-3)). The gene encoding this protein (LOC\_Os03g60560.1) was 3.4 times more expressed in the CT genotype than in the CS (Table [1—](#page-7-0)"Unknown"). Interestingly, it has been suggested that, in addition to its role as a transcription factor, ZFP182 can also bind ribosomal proteins, modulating the efficiency of protein synthesis during stress (Huang et al. [2012\)](#page-25-3).

Another transcription factor found in our work as more expressed in the CT genotype is *OsWRKY45* (Table [1](#page-7-0)— "RNA"), which is able to enhance tolerance to low temperature in rice plants through the ABA signaling pathway when over-expressed (Tao et al. [2011](#page-27-8)). We also found two *NAC transcription factors* with increased expression in the tolerant genotype (Table [1—](#page-7-0)"Development"), both of which have been previously reported as differentially expressed under cold treatment (Supplementary Table 3). One of them has been named *Stress-responsive NAC1* (*SNAC1*; Hu et al. [2006](#page-25-31)). Expression of *SNAC1* is induced by abiotic stresses, including cold, and its over-expression in rice was shown to enhance drought and salt stress tolerance, likely due to better water balance under stress (Hu et al. [2006](#page-25-31)). *SNAC1* polymorphisms in rice accessions were associated with higher expression, indicating that there is variation in expression within rice natural diversity, which is likely to result in higher/lower abiotic stress tolerance (Songyikhangsuthor et al. [2014\)](#page-27-27). Recently, another *NAC* gene (*ONAC095*) was reported to act as a positive regulator of cold response in rice, having an opposite role (negative regulator) of drought response (Huang et al. [2016\)](#page-25-32).

#### **Hormone‑mediated regulation of cold responses**

The complex crosstalk between various hormones in response to cold stress seems to be more active in the CT genotype, which has 21 genes related to "Hormone metabolism", while the CS genotype has only 7 genes in this category (Supplementary Table 2). Besides those genes (such as *Cytokinin dehydrogenase precursor* and *ACC oxidase*), other genes related to hormone signaling were classified in the metabolism categories "Development" (*Auxin induced protein 5NG4*), "Transport" (*Auxin efflux carrier*), "Degradation of xenobiotics" (three *Gibberellin receptors*) and "Miscelaneous" (*Cytokinin-N-glucosyltransferase*) (Table [1](#page-7-0)). Plant hormones regulate plant growth and development, as well as responses to changing environmental conditions. By modifying the production, distribution or signal transduction of these hormones, plants are able to coordinate both growth and stress tolerance (Colebrook et al. [2014\)](#page-24-30).

Cytokinins mediate the responses to variable extrinsic factors and have a role in the response to biotic and abiotic stress (Werner and Schmülling [2009\)](#page-27-28). We detected higher expression of a cytokinin catabolic enzyme (*Cytokinin dehydrogenase*; Table [1—](#page-7-0)"Hormone metabolism") on the CT genotype than in the CS. The same gene was also expressed at higher levels in the shoots of TNG67 (CT) compared with those of TCN1 (CS) in response to cold stress (Yang et al. [2015\)](#page-27-1). It has been established that cytokinins play important roles in stress responses and adaptation, but do not act alone, being part of a complex network of multiple synergistic and antagonistic interactions between various hormones, such as abscisic acid, jasmonate, salicylic acid, ethylene and auxin (O'Brien and Benková [2013](#page-26-29)).

The gaseous hormone ethylene is produced in the presence of oxygen by the enzyme ACC oxidase, which was more expressed in the CT plants (Table [1\)](#page-7-0). It has been shown that most ethylene biosynthesis and signaling genes are upregulated in rice shoots of both CT and CS genotypes in response to low temperature (Yang et al. [2015\)](#page-27-1). Some ethylene biosynthesis genes were upregulated in roots of the CT genotype at low temperatures and displayed prolonged expression during recovery. It was suggested that the cold stress-mediated ethylene response may not be regulated at the transcriptional level in rice (Yang et al. [2015](#page-27-1)). Overexpression of the tomato *ethylene response factor LeTERF2* confers cold tolerance in *japonica* rice seedlings (Tian et al. [2011\)](#page-27-29). The up-regulation of six *Ethylene Response Factors* (*ERF, AP2 domain containing proteins*) seen in the CT genotype in the present work (see above—discussion about transcription factors) may be related to increased ethylene synthesis.

Auxin plays an important role in plant response to cold stress (Rahman [2013](#page-26-30)), and a number of auxin-related genes were regulated by late phase chilling stress in both *indica*

and *japonica* rice, indicating an interaction between auxin signaling and low temperature stress response (Zhang et al. [2012](#page-27-16)a).

The successful low-temperature germination of a CT genotype was associated with gibberellin signaling, probably related to a 33-fold higher GIDL2 protein expression when compared with a CS genotype (Lee et al. [2015](#page-25-33)). Reduction of gibberellin levels and signaling has been shown to contribute to *Arabidopsis* plant growth restriction on exposure to several stresses, including cold (Colebrook et al. [2014\)](#page-24-30).

The extent to which these hormones and signaling pathways affect cold tolerance is, however, yet to be uncovered.

#### **Stress‑related genes may help to cope with cold**

WCOR413 is a transmembrane protein possibly involved in cold acclimation, acting as a receptor of extracellular signals and helping to maintain membrane stability (Breton et al. [2003](#page-24-31)). Expression of *WCOR413* genes was induced by cold treatment in *Arabidopsis* and cereals, but not in CS rice plants (Breton et al. [2003](#page-24-31)). Our group has previously analyzed the expression of *OsWCOR413* gene in the same rice genotypes used in this work. We detected higher expression in the CT genotype than in the CS one before cold treatment (Adamski et al. [2016](#page-24-10)). Such result suggests that *OsWCOR413* may be important for intrinsic tolerance to chilling stress. In the present work, *OsWCOR413*expression was higher in the CT plants also after cold treatment (Table [1](#page-7-0)—"Stress"). No previous studies have reported cold induction of *OsWCOR413* expression in rice plants (Supplementary Table 3). It may be related to a cold tolerance strategy specific to a few rice genotypes, such as IRGA 959-1-2-2F-4-1-4-A. Additional studies (currently going on in our lab) are necessary to understand its possible role in cold tolerance in rice plants.

Very little is known about plant Lipocalins, except that they contain a cytosolic fatty-acid binding domain and function during plant development under environmental stresses, including low temperature exposure in wheat (Frenette Charron et al. [2002](#page-25-34)). Also, low expression levels are found in oat and barley, two plant species which are less cold tolerant than wheat. It has been suggested that Lipocalin expression is correlated with the capacity to develop freezing tolerance in different plant species (Frenette Charron et al. [2002](#page-25-34)). In this work, two *Lipocalins* were more expressed in the CT genotype than in the sensitive one under cold treatment (Table [1](#page-7-0)—"Transport" and "Stress"). One of them has been previously induced by cold treatment in rice (*OsTIL-2*, Supplementary Table 3) and the other one (*OsCHL*) is a chloroplast isoform.

Expression of the *Early Responsive to Dehydration* gene *ERD15* was higher in the tolerant genotype (Table [1—](#page-7-0)"Unknown"). In *Arabidopsis*, ERD15 proteins are central components in stress responses, including drought and freezing, functioning as negative regulators of ABA signaling, most likely acting in posttranscriptional regulation of gene expression (Aalto et al. [2012\)](#page-24-8). Rice orthologues, however, are poorly characterized. *ERD15* has been previously included in lists of genes induced in rice plants under cold treatment (Supplementary Table 3), but there is no functional demonstration of a role in cold stress tolerance.

Dehydrins are considered to function as chaperones and to stabilize proteins, membranes and vesicles in stressed plants. The rice LIP9 protein is responsive to cold stress in cell suspension cultures (Aguan et al. [1991\)](#page-24-32). The *OsLIP9* gene is up-regulated in *japonica* rice plants over-expressing *OsDREB1B* gene, and these transgenic plants also show improved cold-tolerance (Ito et al. [2006\)](#page-25-7). *OsLIP9* expression is induced by several abiotic stresses, including low temperature (Rabbani et al. [2003\)](#page-26-15). Our group analyzed the expression of *OsLIP9* in a previous work and we suggested that a cold-tolerance mechanism involving the dehydrin *OsLIP9* and *OsDREB1B* could be present in the CT genotype, where cold treatment resulted in higher *OsLIP9* expression in relation to the control treatment and to the CS genotype (Adamski et al. [2016](#page-24-10)). The results from the present RNAseq work, with high expression of *OsLIP9* in the CT plants (Table [1—](#page-7-0)"Unknown"), confirm our previous findings.

The *isocitrate lyase OsEnS-107* gene was also more expressed in the CT plants (Table [1](#page-7-0)—"Gluconeogenese/ glyoxylate cycle"). Isocitrate lyase is a characteristic enzyme of the glyoxylate cycle. This rice gene (also known as *Osl85*) was originally shown to be expressed in the endosperm (Nie et al. [2013\)](#page-26-31) and later to be upregulated in response to drought (Huang et al. [2014a](#page-25-26), [b](#page-25-27)) and flooding (Wu and Yang [2016](#page-27-30)). Activation of the glyoxylate cycle is a good alternative to obtain energy (replenishing the TCA cycle) or precursors for amino acid and carbohydrate synthesis in plants facing environmental stresses (Kunze et al. [2006](#page-25-35)). Interestingly, this gene was down-regulated in CS rice plants exposed to low temperature (Mittal et al. [2012](#page-26-16); Supplementary Table 3), while up-regulated by cold in the CT genotype tested in the present work.

#### **Differential regulation of protein degradation in cold tolerant and sensitive plants**

Several genes involved with protein degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway showed higher expression in the CT genotype (Supplementary Table 2 and Table [1](#page-7-0)—"Protein"), suggesting that a highly active ubiquitin–proteasome pathway can be involved in rice tolerance to low temperatures. Some of those genes encode proteins containing F-box domains.

It is well established that the ubiquitin/proteasome pathway is involved in the control of the cold stress response. The importance of OsHOS1 protein (a RING E3 ubiquitin ligase) in the modulation of the cold stress signaling in rice was confirmed (Lourenço et al. [2013\)](#page-26-32) through the interaction with and proteasome degradation of OsICE1, a master transcription factor of the cold stress signaling. Although these two genes were not significantly more expressed in either of the two rice genotypes we tested, 11 genes related to ubiquitin were more expressed in the CT genotype, while only two were in the CS plants (Supplementary Table 2). One of them (*OsUBC13*, Table [1](#page-7-0)—"Protein") has been previously related to responses to drought and salt stresses in rice (Zhiguo et al. [2015\)](#page-28-1) and another one (*OsUbi1*, Table [1—](#page-7-0)"Protein") was up-regulated by chilling stress in rice anthers (C98695 in Yamaguchi et al. [2004\)](#page-27-31). U-box proteins comprise a family of ubiquitin-protein ligases (Hatakeyama et al. [2001\)](#page-25-36). Two of those proteins were more expressed in the CT genotype (Table [1—](#page-7-0)"RNA").

As one of the largest gene families in plants, F-box domain proteins have important roles in regulating various developmental processes and stress responses (Yan et al. [2011\)](#page-27-32). F-box proteins contain a conserved F-box domain (40–50 amino acids) at their N-terminus, which is part of the Skp1-Cullin-F-box (SCF) E3 ubiquitin ligase (a complex of at least four polypeptides), having a crucial role in conferring specificity to the complex for appropriate target protein degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway (Smalle and Vierstra [2004](#page-26-33)).

Only a small portion of F-box proteins has been studied in rice. F-box proteins have been previously associated with cold-tolerance in rice. It has been suggested that an F-box protein gene (*Ctb1*) confers cold-tolerance to rice plants at the booting stage (Saito et al. [2010](#page-26-34)). This gene was not differentially expressed in our experiment, probably because we worked with much younger plants. On the other hand, it has been reported that the overexpression of an F-box protein gene (*OsMAIF1*) reduced abiotic stress tolerance in rice plants (Yan et al. [2011](#page-27-32)), suggesting that these proteins can play negative roles in response to abiotic stress.

In this work, *OsFBX235*, an F-box domain-containing protein, was the most clearly differentially expressed gene between tolerant and sensitive genotypes (fold change sensitive/tolerant = 1474/0; *p* value = 0), being highly expressed on the CS genotype (Supplementary Table 2). This gene is strongly expressed in the sensitive genotype in control conditions and further induced upon cold treatment, but absent in the tolerant one (Fig. [4](#page-11-0)h). Other three genes encoding F-box proteins were also more expressed in the CS plants. On the other hand, nine F-box proteins had higher expression in the CT than in the sensitive genotype (Supplementary Table 2; Table [1](#page-7-0)—"Protein" and "Unknown"). Thus, differential regulation of protein degradation might be related to cold tolerance and sensitivity.

*OsFBX28* is one of the F-box sequences with high expression in the CT genotype (Table [1—](#page-7-0)"Protein"). Expression of this gene has been reported to be responsive to cold stress in some genotypes, but such response is not always observed in a CT phenotype (Bevilacqua et al. [2015\)](#page-24-4).

# **Systems biology confirms results and gives new insights**

The most representative metabolism categories found in the PPI network were in good agreement with the category distribution found in the general analysis, which considered all genes significantly more expressed in the CT genotype. Accordingly, the subnetworks with highest scores (clusters I and II) were enriched with proteins related to photosynthesis and cell wall. Moreover, two-thirds of the proteins considered as hub-bottlenecks are related to photosynthesis, as discussed above. Identification of bottlenecks suggested that protein kinases and cytochrome P450 proteins may have important roles in cold responses in the tolerant genotype. Plant protein kinases are involved in signal transduction related to development and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses (Zhu [2016;](#page-28-2) Osakabe et al. [2013\)](#page-26-35). Two bottlenecks identified in this work are rice transmembrane protein kinases which have not been characterized (encoded by LOC\_Os11g36190.1 and LOC\_Os11g36090.1; Supplementary Table 4 and Table [1](#page-7-0)). However, both are encoded by genes that localize into a QTL related to cold tolerance at the seedling stage (Supplementary Table 3). Further studies should elucidate the roles of these genes. A receptor protein kinase precursor (encoded by LOC\_Os03g56270.1) was also identified as bottleneck. This protein has been named Receptor-Like Cytoplasmic Kinase 117 (OsRLCK117; Vij et al. [2008\)](#page-27-33) and was shown to be cold-responsive in previous transcriptome reports (Supplementary Table 3). Cytochrome P450 monooxygenases comprise a large family of heme proteins. In general, they are the terminal oxidase enzymes in electron transfer chains. In plants, P450s play critical roles in the synthesis of lignins, UV protectants, pigments, defense compounds, fatty acids, hormones and signaling molecules, as well as in the catabolism of endogenous and toxic compounds (Schuler and Werck-Reichhart [2003\)](#page-26-36). The acquisition of new metabolic pathways during land plant evolution may be related to the large diversification of P450 proteins (Mizutani [2012](#page-26-37)). From the three P450 proteins considered bottlenecks in this work, only one (LOC\_Os02g47470.1) has been previously shown to be cold-responsive (Supplementary Table 3). It would be interesting to investigate possible roles of these proteins in cold tolerance in rice.

# **Conclusion**

This study revealed different molecular and physiological mechanisms in response to low temperature stress in two *indica* rice genotypes with contrasting levels of cold-tolerance. Comprehensive gene expression using high-throughput RNAseq revealed several DEGs, indicating some that are good candidates for cold stress tolerance. The tolerant genotype has improved reinforcement of cell walls by increased cellulose synthesis and deposition, as well as higher levels of linoleic acid and higher expression of fatty acid desaturases and other lipid metabolism and membrane compositionrelated genes. The CT genotype seems to have higher photosynthetic efficiency, higher antioxidant capacity and more active Ca2+ and hormone-signaling compared to the CS genotype. Ethylene signaling, mediated by ethylene response factors (ERF, AP2-domain containing transcription factors) seems to be more effective in the CT genotype, as well as differential expression of genes related to protein degradation, such as those encoding F-box proteins. Increased activity of the glyoxylate cycle may provide alternative sources of energy during cold exposure. These processes could explain, at least partially, the observed cold tolerance. Possible roles of *long terminal repeat* (*LTR*) retrotransposons in cold tolerance deserves further investigation, as well as roles of specific protein kinase and cytochrome P450 genes revealed in this work. The importance of stress-related proteins, such as WCOR413, ERD15, lipocalins and dehydrins in coldtolerance mechanisms in rice plants also needs confirmation in functional studies. Even considering that mRNAs can be subject of post-transcriptional regulation, the differentially expressed sequences defined in this study do provide useful starting points for more in-depth analyses of the molecular and physiological mechanisms behind cold tolerance in rice plants, and therefore, may be used in future biotechnological applications.
