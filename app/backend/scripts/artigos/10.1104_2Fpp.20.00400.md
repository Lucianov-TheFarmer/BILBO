### **Abstract**

 Ovule formation is essential for realizing crop yield because it determines seed number. The underlying molecular mechanism, however, remains elusive. Here, we show that cell wall invertase (CWIN) functions as a positive regulator of ovule initiation in Arabidopsis (*Arabidopsis thaliana*). *In situ* hybridization revealed that *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* were expressed at the placenta region where ovule primordia initiated. Specific silencing of *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* by using targeted artificial microRNA driven by an ovule-specific SEEDSTICK promoter (*pSTK*) resulted in a substantial reduction of CWIN transcript and activity, which blocked ovule initiation and aggravated ovule abortion. There was no induction of carbon starvation genes in the transgenic lines and supplementing newly forming floral buds with extra carbon failed to recover the ovule phenotype. This indicates that suppression of CWIN did not lead to carbon starvation. A group of hexose transporters was downregulated in the transgenic plants. Among them, two representative ones were spatially co-expressed with *CWIN2* and *CWIN4*, suggesting a coupling between CWIN and hexose transporters for ovule initiation. RNA-Seq analysis identified differentially expressed genes encoding putative extracellular receptor-like-kinases (RLKs), MADS-box transcription factors including *STK*, and early auxin response genes in response to CWIN-silencing. Our data demonstrate the essential role of CWIN in ovule initiation, which is most likely to occur through sugar signaling instead of carbon nutrient contribution. We propose that CWIN- mediated sugar signaling may be perceived by, and transmitted through, hexose transporters or RLKs to regulate ovule formation by modulating downstream auxin signaling and MADS-box transcription factors.

### **Introduction**

 In higher plants, most carbohydrate making up the bulk of the biomass and crop yield originates from sucrose (Suc), making Suc a crucial yield determinant (Ruan, 2014). Suc is predominately derived from photosynthetically active leaves and translocated through phloem to sink organs. Upon being delivered to sinks, it is degraded either by sucrose  synthase (Sus; EC 2.4.1.13) or invertase (INV; EC 3.2.1.26). Sus reversibly degrades Suc into UDP-glucose (Glc) and fructose (Fru) in the presence of uridine diphosphate (UDP). INV, on the other hand, irreversibly hydrolyses Suc into Glc and Fru. Based on their subcellular location, INVs are classified into three subgroups: cell wall invertase (CWIN), vacuolar invertase (VIN) and the structurally unrelated cytoplasmic invertase (CIN, Sturm, 1999, Wan et al., 2018). CWINs, also referred as extracellular or apoplasmic invertase, are insoluble proteins ionically bound to cell wall with a pH-optimum of 3.5 ~ 5.0 (Roitsch and González, 2004). Although symplasmic transport through plasmodesmata (PD) is considered as a common pathway of Suc unloading, Suc can only be transported apoplasmically across cell wall matrix and plasma membranes in cellular interfaces lacking PD connection (Patrick, 1997). In this latter case, Suc is often unloaded from the sieve element-companion cell (SE/CC) to the surrounding cell wall matrix, and then taken up by recipient sink cells via Suc transporters (SUT) or hexose transporters (HXT) following hydrolysis of Suc by CWIN at the extracellular space (Braun et al., 2014; Li et al., 2017).

 The CWIN-SUT/HXT nexus could also operate in regions beyond the SE/CC unloading site for post-phloem delivery of assimilates in sink organs (Li et al., 2017). For example, as a common feature of all developing angiosperm seeds, the filial tissue (endosperm and embryo) is symplasmically isolated from the surrounding maternal seed coat in dicot or pedicel in monocot species. In this scenario, assimilates must be transported across the apoplasmic compartment between the maternal and filial tissues to nurture the developing embryo, where CWIN could play a critical role in seed development (Patrick 1997; Weber et al., 2005). A classic example is the maize (*Zea mays*) miniature-seed phenotype as a result of mutation of a CWIN gene expressed in endosperm transfer cell, the outermost cell layer of the filial tissue facing the pedicel (Miller and Chourey, 1992). Similarly, in fava bean (*Vicia faba*), CWIN activity at the innermost of the seed coat positively correlates with cell division of cotyledons, hence seed size (Weber et al., 1996). In developing tomato fruit (*Solanum lycopersicum*), a CWIN gene is mainly expressed in the phloem of placenta connecting the developing seed where unloading occurs apoplasmically (Jin et al., 2009; Palmer et al., 2015). Here, genetically elevating the endogenous CWIN activity increased seed weight (Jin et al., 2009), whereas silencing its expression resulted in stunted seed (Zanor et al., 2009). Similar role of CWIN has also been reported in other species including rice (Wang et al., 2008) and barely (Weschke et al., 2003).

 Apart from its function in Suc unloading, CWIN also plays a role in sugar signalling (Ruan et al., 2012). For instance, the maize miniature seed phenotype of CWIN-deficient

 mutant could not be recovered by the provision of exogenous hexose (Cheng and Chourey, 1999) and enhancing *CWIN* expression in shoot apical meristem accelerated flowering and increased inflorescence branching in *Arabidopsis* (Heyer et al., 2004). More recently, elevation of CWIN activity in tomato was found to induce the expression of R and PR genes in fruitlets (Ru et al., 2017) and to block programmed cell death under heat stress (Liu et al., 2016). These findings indicate signaling function of CWIN in development and defense, but does not differentiate from its role in providing sugar nutrients.

 Despite the extensive studies of CWIN as outlined above, it remains unknown whether and how CWIN may exert its potential role in the formation of seed precursors, ovules, at the pre-fertilization stage. Ovule initiation and establishment represents a crucial window that sets yield potential as ovule formation determines seed number. Progress in this frontier would not only contribute to our understanding on roles of CWIN-mediated Suc metabolism or signalling in the pre-fertilization reproductive development, but could also uncover promising targets for designing innovative approaches to enhance crop seed yield.

 In this study, we reported that two CWIN genes, *CWIN2* and *CWIN4*, were highly expressed in the placenta region of gynoecia where ovule primordia initiate. To determine their potential role in ovule development, we generated transgenic lines, in which an ovule- specific *SEEDSTICK* promoter (*pSTK,* Kooiker et al., 2005) was used to drive artificial microRNA (amiRNA*CWIN24*) specifically suppressing *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* during ovule formation. The *pSTK::*amiRNA*CWIN24* transgenic plants exhibited an inhibition on ovule initiation and an increased ovule abortion, which collectively resulted in > 50% seed loss as compared to WT. Surprisingly, measurement of the transcript levels of carbon starvation reporter genes and manipulation of source-to-sink ratio showed that the transgenic plants did not suffer from carbon starvation. This, together with the finding that assimilate is delivered symplasmically to the ovule primordia (Werner et al., 2011), shows that CWIN regulates ovule initiation through sugar signalling, rather than provision of carbon nutrient. Further transcriptomic analyses support a model that CWIN-generated hexose signals may be perceived by and transmitted through a cohort of plasma membrane hexose transporters or RLKs to modulate downstream candidate transcription factor and auxin signalling genes, thereby positively regulating ovule development.

#### **Results**

### *CWIN2* **and** *CWIN4* **were highly expressed in floral meristem, ovule primordia and young ovules in wild-type inflorescence**

 Among the four CWIN paralogs in *Arabidopsis*, *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* are known to be predominately expressed in reproductive organs (Schmid et al., 2005). As the first step to address the potential involvement of CWIN in ovule development, we performed *in situ* hybridization experiments to examine whether *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* were expressed in the cellular sites of ovule initiation.

 The analyses revealed that *CWIN2* mRNA was detected in the inflorescence meristem and floral primordium (Figure 1A), in comparison with the sense control (Figure 1B). The signal intensity of *CWIN2* transcript in the floral primordium appeared to be increased from the early stages 2 and 3 to the late stage of 5 when the primordia gave rise to gynoecium and stamens (Figure 1A). The transcript was also evidently detected in gynoecium and stamens at stage 7 (Figure 1A). Noticeably, strong expression of *CWIN2* mRNA was observed in the middle part of the gynoecium at stage 9 (Figure 1C) where ovule initiation takes place (Smyth et al., 1990). The transcript was also detectable in the developing ovules at stage11 (Figure 1D) and anthers (Figure 1C, 1D versus Figure 1E). Interestingly, abundant expression of *CWIN2* was found in the stigma papillae at the top of the gynoecium at stage11 (Figure 1D).

 *CWIN4* mRNA exhibited similar expression pattern to that of *CWIN2* in the inflorescence meristem and floral primordia (Figure 2). Evident expression of *CWIN4* was observed in the inflorescence meristem, floral primordium and early floral organ primordia from stages 1-7 (Figure 2A, 2B), including primordia of gynoecium and stamen, in comparison with the sense control (Figure 2C). Strong *CWIN4* mRNA signals were detected in the central part of the gynoecium when ovule initiates at stage 9 (Figure 2D), the same as that for *CWIN2* (Figure 1C). Expression of the *CWIN4* gene was also present in the developing ovules from stages 10 and 11 (Figure 2E–F) in comparison with the sense control (Figure 2G). However, the *CWIN4* mRNA signal strength became weaker in the developing ovules than that in the early stage of gynoecium (Figure 2B) and ovule initiation (Figure 2D).

## **The** *pSTK***::amiRNA***CWIN24* **transgenic plants displayed a significant reduction of** *CWIN2* **and** *CWIN4* **transcript levels at the ovule initiation stage**

 The data from the *in situ* hybridization experiments indicate a possible role of *CWIN*2 and *CWIN4* in ovule development. We thus further investigated whether disruption of *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* expression would affect ovule development. To achieve this, we employed an artificial microRNA (amiRNA) approach to silence the expression of the two CWIN genes by using an ovule-specific *SEEDSTICK* promoter (*pSTK*; Kooiker et al., 2005).

 Through transgene screening and selection analyses, we obtained 5 independent transgenic lines homozygous for the transgene *pSTK*-amiRNA*CWIN24* at T3 generation. Among them, lines 4-3, 80-4 and 106-2 displayed a severe reduction in seed number and silique length as compared to that of WT (Supplemental Figure S1). These lines were subsequently selected for detailed analysis. We first examined the expression level of *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* by using a pooled sample of the flower buds from stage 8 to 10 when ovules initiate from the placenta (Figure 1, Figure 2; Smyth et al., 1990). In comparison with the WT, the transcript levels of *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* were reduced by 40–45% and 30–54%, respectively, in the transgenic lines (Figure 3A, 3B). We further investigated whether the reduction of *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* transcripts had any effect on the expression of other members of the INV family (*CWIN1*, *CWIN5*, *VIN1* and *VIN2-* Wang and Ruan, 2012). None of these genes displayed altered expression levels as compared to WT (Figure 3C–F). The findings indicate specific suppression of *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* in *pSTK*::amiRNA*CWIN24* transgenic plants.

 We next examined whether suppression of *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* led to a decrease in the CWIN activity. Here, enzyme activity was assayed on the gynoecia-enriched samples that were carefully dissected from the same floral buds used for reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis. The dissection was based on the consideration that the ovule-specific STK-driven amiRNA*CWIN24* would specifically silence *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* in the placenta region and ovule primordia rather than that in the surrounding tissues such as anthers, sepals and petals where *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* were also expressed (Figure 1, Figure 2). The enzyme assay revealed that CWIN activity was reduced by about 40% in transgenic lines 4-3 and 80-4 and 18% in line 106-2 (Figure 3G). Neither VIN nor CIN acitivities were affetced in the transgenic lines (Figure 3G).

 Collectively, these data showed that the *pSTK*-driven amiRNA*CWIN24* specifically supressed *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* expression, leading to a reduction of CWIN activity at the ovule initatiation stage.

#### **Silencing** *CWIN* **inhibited ovule initiation and induced ovule abortion**

 We then investigated whether silencing of *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* impacted on ovule development. Phenotypic analyses revealed a significant reduction in silique length of the transgenic plants as compared to that in the WT (Figure 4A) with an average reduction of 50.8%, 62.6% and 56% in seed number per silique in lines 4-3, 80-4 and 106-2, respectively (Supplemental Figure S1; Supplemental Table S1).

 To determine the developmental basis for the reduction in seeds number, we counted the ovule number from cleared pistils dissected out from flower buds at stage 10-11prior to anthesis. The ovule number was significantly reduced to ~33–40 per pistil in the transgenic lines as compared to that of ~59 in the WT (Figure 4B, 4D, Supplemental Table S1). The pistil length was also reduced by 20–30% in the transgenic lines as compared to that in the WT (Figure 4B).

 The ratio of seed to ovule number was 0.95 in the WT, but was reduced to 0.62 ~ 0.74 in the transgenic lines (Supplemental Table S1), indicating aggravated ovule or seed abortion, manifested as gaps shown in the cleared siliques at stage 17 in the transgenic lines (Red arrows in Figure 4A). Under dissection microscope, a sustantial portion of residual white structures was clearly observed in the transgenic siliques (red asterisks in Figure 4C), a phenotype hardly observed in the WT siliques. The white tiny dots in the transgenic lines were morphologically resembled that of ovule abortion (Ebel et al., 2004). However, we cannot rule out the possibility that some may be residuals derived from early seed abortion. On average, there were 10, 12 and 15 white tiny dots (designated as ovule or seed abortion) per silique in line 4-3, 80-4 and 106-2, respectively, as compared to only 3 in the WT siliques, hence constituting a higher abortion rate in the transgenic lines (Figure 4E).

 Based on the knowledge of ovule number per pistil (Figure 4B, Supplemental Table S1), the number of ovules blocked from initiation in the transgenic pistils was calculated by subtraction of the number of transgenic ovules from that of the WT. It was found that on average 22, 26 and 20 ovules were blocked from initiation in line 4-3, 80-4 and 106-2, respectively (Supplemental Table S1), accounting for ~60–70% loss of the seed number with the remaining ~30–40% seed loss derived from ovule or seed abortion (Figure 4D, 4F). These findings demonstrate that the blockage of ovule initiation is the primary cause of reduction of seed number with ovule or seed abortion additionally contributing to seed loss in the transgenic plants.

# **Blockage of ovule initiation was not caused by carbon starvation in the CWIN-silenced plants**

 In Arabidopsis, Suc supply to young ovules follows a symplasmic pathway as evidenced by the unloading of GFP and fluorescent tracers from the placenta SE/CC to the ovule primordia at floral stage 9 (Werner et al., 2011). Given that CWIN typically plays a role in apoplasmic phloem unloading and post-phloem transport (e.g. Jin et al 2009, Palmer et al 2015), it is intriguing from carbon nutrient perspective that silencing of *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* inhibited ovule initiation (Figure 3, Figure 4). We thus conducted experiments to examine if inhibition of ovule initiation in the CWIN-suppressed transgenic plants was owing to insufficient carbon availability.

 We reasoned that if the blockage of ovule initiation in the transgenic plants (Figure 4) was due to shortage of assimilate supply, the ovule inhibition phenotype could be rescued or alleviated by supplying the ovules with more carbon nutrient. To test this, we trimmed 2/3 of the developing siliques to increase the source-to-sink ratio (Figure 5A), thereby allowing more assimilates to be available to form new ovules and siliques. As shown in Figure 5B–E, increasing the carbon availability to the shoot apical floral meristem failed to recover any of the phenotype in silique length and ovule or seed number of the transgenic plants as compared to that of untreated transgenic plants, excepting in line 4-3 where trimming increased the silique length slightly.

 To directly investigate if the transgenic floral buds were under C starvation, we measured the transcript levels of three carbon starvation report genes: *ATL8* (*At1g76410*), *KMD4* (*At3g59940*) and *OLEOSIN7* (*At5g56100*) that are strongly induced under sugar depletion condition (Lauxmann et al., 2016) in the flower buds at stage 8 to 10. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that none of these genes were induced in the transgenic plants (Figure 6). The above findings indicate that *pSTK*-amiRNA*CWIN24* plants did not suffer from carbon starvation and the ovule phenotype was not arisen from insufficient carbon supply.

# **RNA-seq analysis between** *pSTK***-amiRNA***CWIN24* **and WT plants: Overall quality and reliability assessment**

 To explore the underlying basis of CWIN-mediated ovule initiation, RNA-seq was conducted to compare the transcript profiles of flower buds pooled from stage 8 to 10 in a representative transgenic line, 4-3 and WT. After trimming, an average of 58.11 million and 55.58 million  clean reads were obtained from 4 biological replicates of the WT and the transgenic plants, respectively. Approximately 90% of the clean reads were uniquely mapped to the Arabidopsis Genome Reference (TAIR10, Supplemental Figure S2A). Principal component analysis (PCA) of the transcriptome revealed a clear separation between the transgenic line and WT and that the biological replicates were clustered closely in each genotype with no outlier detected (Figure 7A). Hence, all replicates from each genotype were used for subsequent identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). A total of 1940 DEGs were identified between transgenic and WT plants (Supplemental Table S2), of which 408 were up-regulated and 1082 were down-regulated (Figure 7B).

 To validate the RNA-seq analysis, we selected a subset of genes for RT-qPCR analyses across the *pSTK*-amiRNA*CWIN24* transgenic line and WT by using the same samples used for RNA-Seq. The analysis revealed that the transcript fold change of the seven selected genes from RT-qPCR measurement was overall consistent with that of RNA-seq (Supplemental Figure S2B). It was noticed that the fold change measured by RT-qPCR was generally lower than that from the RNA-seq analysis. This probably due to the RNA-seq being more sensitive and specific than the RT-qPCR (Griffith et al., 2010). Importantly, correlation analysis of these two data sets revealed a R<sup>2</sup> of 0.6756 (Supplemental Figure S2C), which is within the range of reported correlations between RNA-seq and RT-qPCR results (Xu et al., 2011). Together, the analyses indicate the reliability of our RNA-seq data.

# **A large number of early auxin-responsive genes were downregulated in response to CWIN-silencing**

 Given an increasing number of studies indicate the roles of sugar-hormone interaction in modulating plant development (León and Sheen, 2003; Wang and Ruan, 2013) and the importance of hormones in ovule initiation (Cucinotta et al., 2014), we closely examined hormone-related DEGs from the transgenic and WT plants.

 In total, 49 DEGs were classified to hormone category, which was subclassed into six groups (Figure 7C). Among them, genes involved in auxin, abscisic acid and ethylene metabolism were the top three groups, representing 53%, 16% and 14% of the total hormone genes, respectively. Genes involved in gibberellin (GA), jasmonate (JA), and cytokinin (CK) pathways were each less than 10% of the total hormone-related genes (Figure 7C). These  findings indicate that in comparison with other hormone pathways, auxin-related genes appeared to be affected the most in response to CWIN suppression.

 Among the 26 auxin-related genes, majority of them (19 out of 26) were early auxin response genes (Table 1). Remarkably, these early auxin response genes were all down- regulated in response to CWIN suppression, which includes 14 *SMALL AUXIN UP RNAs* (*SAURs*), 4 *AUX/IAA* and 1 *GH3s*. *AUX/IAA* encodes a short-lived nuclear protein that functions as repressor against auxin-inducible gene expression, in which AUX/IAA forms a heterodimer with ARFs to repress the transcriptional activity of ARF genes (Hayashi, 2012). *GH3* encodes an enzyme that catalyses the conjugation of IAA with amino acids to yield an inactive storage form of IAA (Ludwig-Müller, 2011), thereby modulating the intracellular IAA level. Among those 14 *SAURs* downregulated in the CWIN-silenced samples, 8 of them, *SAUR1*, *4*, *12*, *21*, *29*, *64*, *67* and *70*, were auxin-inducible, and one, *SAUR6,* was auxin repressive (Ren and Gray, 2015) with unknown function for the remaining 5 *SAURs*.

# **A cohort of MADS-box transcription factors were downregulated in** *pSTK***- amiRNA***CWIN24* **plants, and over-expression of one of them,** *SEEDSTICK***, partially complemented the silique and seed phenotype**

 A total of 76 DEGs encoding TFs was identified (classified into 30 TF families) from the transgenic samples (Supplemental Table S3), representing 5.1% of the 1490 DEGs. This was done by searching the Arabidopsis transcription database (PlantTFDB), which contains 2996 TFs, classified into 58 families (Jin et al., 2017). Previous studies in Arabidopsis have found that most of the transcription factors that function in ovule formation belong to the MADS box family including *SHATTERPROOF1* (*SHP1*), *SHP2* and *SEEDSTICK* (*STK*) that form a monophyletic clade with *AGAMOUS* (*AG*), sharing a common function in determining ovule identity and exhibiting overlapping expression patterns in ovule primordia (Pinyopich et al., 2003; Skinner et al., 2004). Indeed, RNA-Seq analysis revealed that two subfamilies of MADS family were significantly down-regulated in response to the *CWIN* suppression, including three MICK\_MADS TFs, *STK* (formerly named as *AGL11*), *AGL66* and *AGL104*, and one M\_type\_MADS TF, *AGL94* (Table 2). These findings suggest that MADS TFs may function as downstream targets of CWIN-mediated ovule initiation pathways. Interestingly, among the three of ovule identity genes *SHP1, 2*, and *STK*, only *STK* was down-regulated in  response to CWIN-silencing, suggesting CWIN-mediated ovule initiation may be exerted, in part, through modulation of *STK* expression.

 To test this possibility, we over-expressed *STK*, driven by its native promoter in the transgenic background to test whether the overexpression could complement the ovule phenotype. Here, a *pSTK*::STK (CDS) overexpression construct was transformed into the transgenic line 4-3. Two homozygous complementation lines 2-9 and 7-7 were identified, which exhibited partial recovery of the silique length phenotype as compared to the WT and the CWIN-silencing line 4-3 (Figure 8A). The *STK* mRNA level was fully recovered and increased in complementation lines 7-7 and 2-9, respectively (Figure 8B), leading to partial complementation of the phenotype on silique length and seed number (Figure 8C, 8D).

## *pSTK***-amiRNA mediated silencing of** *CWIN* **altered the expression of genes encoding receptor like kinases and small GTPases**

 As described above, a wide variety of hormone-related and TF genes exhibited differential expression in response to CWIN suppression. This, together with the findings that CWIN may regulate ovule initiation through sugar signalling (Figure 5, Figure 6), suggests that alteration in sugar concentration or flux by modulating CWIN in the extracellular must be sensed, probably by plasma membrane proteins (Ruan, 2014) for transduction of the signal into the intracellular space to regulate gene expression. Consistent with this view, 23 out of 28 DEGs encoding receptor-like kinases (RLKs), showed increased expression during ovule initiation in response to CWIN-silencing (Table 3). RLKs are a group of transmembrane proteins with an N-terminal extracellular domain, a transmembrane span and a cytosolic protein kinase domain, which are involved in a wide range of signal perception and transduction (Wu et al., 2016). We also found 15 DEGs encoding small GTPase-related proteins; all of them exhibited decreased expression during ovule initiation in the CWIN- suppressed plants as compared to those of WT (Table 3). Apart from the small GTPase genes, three genes encoding regulatory proteins of Rho GTPase were also observed (Table 3). These include Rop guanine nucleotide exchange factor 9 (ROPGEF9) and two Rho GTPase effector proteins RIC1 and RIC3 (Vernoud et al., 2003)

 We also noticed two wall-associated kinase (WAK) genes, *WAK1* and *WAK2*, were up-regulated in response to CWIN-silencing (Table 3). WAKs represent a unique class of RLK, which contains a extracellular domain that physically links with the pectin molecular of the cell wall and a cytoplasmic serine/threonine kinase domain (Kohorn et al., 2006).

## **Hexose transporter genes were largely repressed in response to CWIN-silencing during ovule initiation**

 Suc hydrolysis by CWIN generates Glc and Fru in the extracellular space, which are then taken up across the plasma membrane by hexose transporters (HXTs) into the adjacent cells with a co-regulation between the two observed in many cases (Ruan, 2014; Li et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2019). We therefore examined how the sugar transporter genes may have responded to silencing of CWINs in the *pSTK*-amiRNACWIN24 plants.

 We identified 9 DEGs encoding HXTs that consist of SWEETs, STP (Sugar Transporter) and TMT (Tonoplast Monosaccharide Transporter). They were all down- regulated in the CWIN-silencing transgenic plants (Table 3). Among these 9 DEGs, 5 encodes clade I or II SWEET transporters for hexose (Eom et al., 2015), including SWEET3, 4, 5, 7 and 8. Three of them encode STPs including STP2, 6 and 9. Functional assay in yeast cell revealed that STP9 mediated specific uptake of Glc in a proton-dependent manner (Büttner, 2010). STP2 and STP6 were characterised as high-affinity transporters of monosaccharides including Glc, mannose, the pentose xylose, with STP6 carrying additional capacity to transport fructose (Büttner, 2010). Apart from the above 8 hexose transporters predicted to operate on the plasma membranes, a gene encoding tonoplast monosaccharide transporter, TMT3, was also down regulated. Interestingly, expression of two Arabidopsis H<sup>+</sup> - ATPase (AHA) genes, *AHA6* and *AHA9*, were also down-regulated in the *pSTK*amiRNA*CWIN24* transgenic line as compared to WT (Table 3). H<sup>+</sup> -ATPase functions to promote proton pumping to apoplast (Hager, 2003), which generates the driving force for sugar uptake mediated by proton-coupled sugar symporters. By contrast, none of DEGs was found for Suc transporter s in the CWIN-silenced plants.

 The down regulation of 9 HXT genes in response to CWIN silencing prompted us to further examine whether they are spatially co-expressed with *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* during ovule initiation by performing *in situ* hybridization on sections of floral buds from stage 8 to 10 in WT plants. We selected *SWEET8* and *STP9* as the representatives of the respective *SWEET and STP* family because they displayed high transcript levels during ovule initiation in the WT (Table 3). In comparison with the sense probe control (Figure 9A), *STP9* mRNA signal was detected in section of floral bud at stage 8-10 (Figure 9B–D). Its transcript signals were observed in the middle part of gynoecium before ovule primordia initiation at stage 8, in the initiating ovule primordia at stage 9 and in developing ovules at stage 10 (Figure 9B–D).  Similar expression pattern was also observed for *SWEET8* mRNA (Figure 9E–H). Overall, the expression patterns of *STP9* and *SWEET8* were overlapped with that of *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* (Figure 1, Figure 2).

#### **Discussion**

## **CWIN plays an essential role in ovule initiation through sugar signalling rather than provision of carbon nutrient**

 CWIN-mediated sucrose metabolism and signalling is central to plant development including the formation of pollen fertility, seed and fruit set and stress response (Ruan et al., 2010; Ruan, 2014). It remains unknown, though, whether and how CWIN plays a role in ovule formation, a prerequisite for seed production. Here, we provide molecular and developmental evidence that CWIN plays a critical role in ovule initiation and growth in *Arabidopsis*. Among the *CWIN* gene family in *Arabidopsis*, *CWIN2* and *4* are predominately expressed in reproductive organs (Schmid et al., 2005), in which the two CWINs may play complementary or additive roles. *In situ* hybridization experiment revealed high expression of *CWIN 2* and *4* mRNAs in the placenta region where ovules initiate (Figure 1, Figure 2). Ovule-specific silencing of *CWIN 2* and *4* by using artificial microRNA approach resulted in significant and specific reduction of *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* transcripts, leading to a decrease in CWIN activity without impacting on *VIN* and *CIN* expression or activity (Figure 3). This molecular intervention reduced the seed number by more than 50% as compared that in the WT, mainly due to blockage of ovule initiation and, to a less extent, aggravated ovule abortion (Figure 4). These findings demonstrate that CWIN is critical for ovule initiation and differentiation. The findings fill a major knowledge gap in our understanding on roles of sugar metabolism or signalling in reproductive development and offers opportunities to potentially improve ovule development and hence seed yield.

 CWIN could contribute to sink development by facilitating phloem unloading of Suc and converting it into Glc and Fru as major nutrients and energy source. It is thus possible that the blockage of ovule formation in the CWIN-silenced plants may be owing to starvation of C nutrient in the floral buds. However, this is highly unlikely based on the following analyses.

 Firstly, artificially increasing assimilate availability to the newly forming floral buds of transgenic plants by increasing source-to-sink ratio failed to recover the ovule blockage  phenotype (Figure 5). Moreover, none of the CWIN-silenced transgenic plants exhibited induction of the carbon starvation reporter genes in the floral buds at stage 8-10 when ovules initiate (Figure 6). These data show that the transgenic plants did not suffer from C starvation. Secondly, Suc unloading from the gynoecia SE/CC to ovule primordia follows a symplasmic pathway through interconnecting plasmodesmata in *Arabidopsis* (Werner et al., 2011) and the amount of Suc required for ovule initiation would be very small in comparison with that for seed or fruit development (Palmer et al., 2015). Thus, CWIN is unlikely a major player in delivering Suc as a C nutrient for ovule initiation. We therefore conclude that the essential and positive role exerted by CWIN for ovule development (Figure 3, Figure 4) must be achieved through signalling effect rather than provision of nutrient.

# **Plasma membrane hexose transporters and RLKs may sense and transmit CWIN-mediated sugar signalling for ovule initiation**

 Given that CWIN is a sucrose-splitting enzyme functioning in the apoplasm, it is likely that any CWIN-induced changes in sugar concentrations or fluxes would be sensed by and transmitted through plasma membrane proteins of the adjoining cells. In agreement with this prediction, a group of HXTs from *SWEET* and *STP* family were downregulated in the *pSTK::*amiR*CWIN24* plants relative to the WT (Table 3). Among these HXTs, transcripts of two representative ones examined, *SWEET8* and *STP9*, were spatially co-localised with *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* during ovule initiation. Interestingly, although there are four Suc/H<sup>+</sup> symporters and 6 clade III SWEETs for Suc also expressed in the WT floral buds, none of them showed changes in transcript levels in the transgenic plants (Supplemental Table S3). These data suggest that CWIN-mediated sugar signalling may be transmitted by plasma membrane HXTs, but not Suc transporters, from the cell wall matrix into cytosol for ovule development. Alternatively, the alteration in extracellular sugar dynamics may be perceived by membrane receptor-like kinases (RLKs). To this end, 28 DEGs encoding RLKs were differentially expressed in the CWIN-silenced plants (Table 3). RLKs, a group of transmembrane proteins, have been recognised as sensory proteins, functioning in a wide range of signal perception and transduction in plants (Wu et al., 2016). Significantly, 8 out of the 23 upregulated RLKs in the transgenic plants were confirmed to be expressed in floral buds including ovules based on promoter::GUS analyses (Wu et al., 2016), which all belong to the LRR-RLKs (Table 3). Collectively, the data suggest that these LRR-RLKs may be  involved in the CWIN-mediated ovule initiation and probably function as sensors to sense extracellular sugar levels or fluxes.

 It is noticed that genes encoding constituents of the RLK-mediated intracellular signalling pathway, Rop signalling, were also differentially expressed in response to CWIN- silencing. As listed in Table 3, among those 15 small GTPase-related genes, 5 genes involved in the Rop signalling pathway were all downregulated. These include 2 Rops and 1 Rop regulatory protein ROPGEF9. There is evidence that LRR-RLK phosphorylates RopGEF to activate Rop signalling to promoter pollen growth (Miyawaki and Yang, 2014) or activates Rop signalling upon sensing auxin to promote the development of pavement cell in leaf epidermis (Xu et al., 2014) and root hair elongation in *Arabidopsis* (Duan et al., 2010). It will be of significance to examine whether the CWIN-responsive RLKs identified from this study (Table 3) may transmit extracellular sugar signals into intracellular space in similar manners.

 Apart from a large number of LRR-RLKs, *WAK1* and *WAK2*, encoding a different class of RLKs, were also up-regulated in response to CWIN-silencing (Table 3). A *wak2* knockout mutant exhibited reduced mRNA level and ativity of vacuolar invertase in the root, leading to arrested growth on sucrose deficient medium, providing a link between WAK and sugar siganling or metabolism (Kohorn et al., 2006). Our finding that silencing CWIN resulted in the up-regulation of *WAK1* and *2* indicates that expression of WAK genes themselves is subject to dynamics in extracellular sugar signaling. The extracellular domain of WAK is known to bind a polysachrride, pectin (Kohorn et al., 2006). It will be of significance to test if WAK binds Suc or hexose. If so, it could open up a new direction to determine the mechnism by which CWINs relay extracellular siganlling to intracellular compartments.

 Surprisingly, a group of aquaporins including 4 PIPs (plasma membrane intrinsic protein) and 2 TIPs (tonoplast intrinsic protein) was down-regulated in the CWIN-silencing transgenic plants (Table 3). It is unknown how dynamics in sugar metabolism signalling may regulate aquaporin gene expression. At the protein level, supplying sucrose to C-starved Arabidopsis seedling induced phosphorylation of aquaporins at their C-terminus, promoting pore opening (Niittylä et al., 2007), possibly through SIRK1, a receptor-like-kinase, upon interaction with two other kinases containing extracellular domains, QSK1 and QSK2 (Wu et al., 2019). Physiologically, the reduced PIP and TIP expression in response to CWIN silencing could disrupt water influx to the initiating ovules, contributing to their abortion.

# 483 CWIN-mediated signalling may be ultimately relayed through early auxin response 484 genes and MADS TFs to regulate ovule formation

The RNA seq analyses also revealed that over half (26 out of 49) of the hormone-related 485 DEGs were involved in auxin signalling with most of them (19 out of 26) being early auxin 486 response genes, including 14 SAURs, 4 AUX/IAAs and 1 GH3 (Table 1). This indicates that 487 the early auxin response genes, especially SAURs, may be closely involved in CWIN-488 mediated ovule initiation. Relevant to this point, SAUR16 has been shown to positively 489 regulate ovule number in Arabidopsis (van Mourik et al., 2017). SAUR16 shares 93% protein 490 sequence similarity with one of SAURs, SAUR12, identified in our DEGs (Table 1) 491 suggesting that SAUR12 may function similarly to SAUR16 in controlling ovule number. 492

Apart from 14 SAURs, 4 AUX/IAA genes were also downregulated in the CWIN-493 silenced plants (Table 1). AUX/IAA is an essential component of auxin-signalling pathway, in 494 which it functions as a repressor against auxin-inducible gene expression (Lau et al., 2008). 495 Disruption of AUX/IAA signalling pathway changed auxin signalling and homeostasis, and 496 497 impaired cell division and root development (Vanneste et al., 2005; Yan et al., 2013). These findings, together with the importance of auxin for organogenesis such as lateral root 498 initiation (Benková et al., 2003) and ovule formation (Galbiati et al., 2013), suggest that 499 suppression of AUX/IAA in the CWIN-silencing transgenic plants may disrupt the auxin 500 signalling at the site of ovule initiation resulting in blockage of ovule initiation. In this 501 context, Glc could transcriptionally regulate 62% of IAA-related genes in Arabidopsis 502 seedlings including those involved in auxin signalling such as SAUR, AUX/IAA and GH3 503 (Mishra et al., 2009). Together, the analyses indicate that the regulation of CWIN-mediated 504 sugar signalling in ovule initiation may be achieved in part through auxin signalling genes 505 such as SAURs and AUX/IAAs. 506

It is unclear as how down-regulation of SAURs could lead to inhibition of ovule 507 508 development. An increasing number of studies indicate that SAURs positively regulate cell expansion (Chae et al., 2012; Spartz Angela et al., 2012), in which SAURs represses PP2C.D 509 phosphatases (D clade subfamily of type 2C protein phosphatases) to activate plasma 510 membrane (PM) H<sup>+</sup>-ATPases (Spartz et al., 2014). Plasma membrane H<sup>+</sup>-ATPases pump 511 protons to apoplast (Hager, 2003), essential for maintaining cellular function for cell division 512 and expansion, characteristic of ovule initiation. These observations together with the finding 513 that two H<sup>+</sup>-ATPase genes were also down regulated in the CWIN-silenced plants (Table 3) 514

suggest that SUARs may contribute to ovule formation through positive effects on H<sup>+</sup> - ATPase.

 In addition to the auxin-related genes, a group of MADS-box TF genes including *STK*  (formerly as *AGL11*), *AGL66*, *AGL94* and *AGL104* were downregulated in transgenic plants (Table 2). Many MADs TFs such as AG, SHP1 and SHP2 function in ovule development (Skinner et al., 2004). Indeed, STK, phylogenetically close to AG and homolog of SHP1 and SHP2, has been proven to be critical for establishment of ovule identity and development (Pinyopich et al., 2003). Although there is a lack of functional analysis for the other three MADS TFs in ovule development, expression pattern and phylogeny analyses indicate that *AGL66*, *AGL94* and *AGL104* may also be involved in the CWIN-mediated ovule initiation. For example, *AGL104* transcript was detected in the placenta and ovule primordia (Par̆enicová et al., 2003) and *AGL66* and *AGL94* share a same monophyletic clade with *AGL104* (Smaczniak et al., 2012). To test whether those differentially expressed MADS TF genes were involved in CWIN-mediated ovule development, *STK* was over-expressed in the transgenic background, which led to a partial complementation of the ovule phenotype in the CWIN-silenced plants (Figure 6), pointing to *STK* as a downstream targets of CWIN- mediated ovule initiation pathway. It remains to be established as how CWIN-mediated sugar signalling could regulate *STK* expression. Nevertheless, sequence analyses using AtcisDB tool (http://arabidospis .med.ohio-state.ed/AtcicDB/) identified three ARF1 binding sites (TGTCTC) in the *STK* promoter ( -1261 ~ 1266; -2058 ~ -2063 and -3292~ 3297). The altered expression of auxin signalling genes (*SAURs* and *AUX/IAAs*) observed in the CWIN- silenced floral buds (see early Discussion, Table 1), may disrupt the binding of ARF1 with the *STK* promoter for its transcription, leading to the reduction of *STK* transcript, a possibility for future studies.

#### **A model on CWIN-mediated ovule development through sugar signalling**

 Base on the above analysis, we propose a model on how CWIN may regulate ovule development (Figure 10). Sugar signals generated by CWIN in the cell wall matrix may be sensed by and transmitted through plasma membrane-localized (i) hexose transporters to modulate cytosolic sugar homeostasis and sugar signalling and or (ii) RLKs to potentially interact with small GTPase (Rop) pathways in the cytoplasm. The CWIN-originated signals are then relayed to the nuclei to regulate expression of genes encoding auxin signalling

components and MADS-box TFs, thereby modulating ovule initiation and differentiation.
The model provides directions for future studies to determine the identity and the
transmission pathways of the sugar signals generated by CWIN for ovule development.

#### 575 **RNA extraction and RT-qPCR**

 Total RNA was extracted from a pooled sample of flower buds at stage 8-10 when ovule initiates (Smyth et al., 1990) using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacture's instruction. About 1 μg RNA was treated with DNase (Promega) to remove the residual genomic DNA. DNase treated-RNA was reversely transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript III system (Invitrogen) along with 50μM oligo(dT)<sup>20</sup> and 10μM dNTP according to the manufacturer's protocol.

 RT-qPCR was performed with SYBR Green fluorescent dye (Invitrogen) and Platinum *Taq* DNA polymerase (Invitrogen) on a Rotor-gene Q instrument (Qiagen). Gene specific primers used in this study were listed in Supplemental Table S5. The relative expression level (*R*) of each target gene is calculated based on the Pfaffl method (Pfaffl, 2001):

$$R = \frac{(E_{target})^{\Delta Ct, \text{ target (calibrator-test)}}}{(E_{ref})^{\Delta Ct, \text{ ref (calibrator-test)}}}$$

 In the above equation, and () are amplification efficiencies of the target and reference gene, respectively. ΔCt, target (calibrator-test) = Ct of the target gene in the calibrator minus the Ct of the target gene in the test sample, and ΔCt, ref (calibrator -test) = Ct of the reference gene in the calibrator minus the Ct of reference gene in the test sample. Among the four Arabidopsis reference genes recommended by Czechowski et al. (2005), namely the F-box protein gene *FBX* (At5g15710), polyubiquitin gene *UBC21* (At5g25760), *UBC9*(At4g27960) and the PP2A catalytic subunit gene *PP2A* (At1g13320), the *FBX* and *UBC21* showed the most stable expression among wild-type and transgenic cDNA samples based on analysis from geNORM software (http://medgen.ugent.be/;jvdesomp/genorm/). Therefore, these two genes were used as reference genes for qPCR analyses in this study.

#### **Design and synthesis of amiRNA against** *CWIN2* **and** *4*

 Taking the advantage of high specificity conferred by artificial miRNA (amiRNA) silencing technology (Tiwari et al., 2014), we designed the amiRNA*CWIN24* which targeted a 21 nt sequence conserved in *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* but not *CWIN1* and *CWIN5* (Supplemental Figure S3A), upstream of the CWIN catalytic domain (WECPD, Supplemental Figure S3B) for achieving maximal silencing effect against CWIN functionality. The amiRNA*CWIN24* was chosen based on the selection rules for amiRNA-target sequence (Eamens et al., 2014) to target a 'shared' 21 nt sequence conserved between *CWIN2* and *CWIN4* from 278 to 299 bp downstream of the start codon ATG (Supplemental Figure S3A, S3B). The approach of amiRNA-mediated gene silencing exploits the intrinsic nature of miRNA biogenesis pathway. Replacement of miRNA/ miRNA\* sequence of the endogenous pre-miRNA by amiRNA/amiRNA\* while maintaining the dsRNA hairpin stem-loop structure can generate the mature amiRNAs by the key enzymes recruited for the biogenesis of endogenous miRNA (Tiwari et al., 2014). Here, the endogenous miRNA159B biogenesis pathway was exploited, since miRNA159B is highly accumulated in the floral tissues (Allen et al., 2007). We replaced the miRNA159B/miRNA159B\* (guide and passenger strand, respectively) of the endogenous pre-miRNA159B with amiRNA*CWIN24* /amiRNA*CWIN24* \* (Supplemental Figure S3C). To mimic the pre-miRNA159B structure characterized with three mismatches at positions 12, 13 and 21 of the miRNA159B\* passenger strand (Supplemental Figure S3C), we introduced three mismatches at the same nucleotide positions of miRNA*CWIN24* \* passenger strand (Supplemental Figure S3C). This ensures that the modified pre- miRNA*CWIN24* fragment is recognised and processed by the endogenous miRNA biogenesis pathway. The modified pre-miRNA*CWIN24* (Supplemental Figure S3C) was synthesized (INTEGRATED DNA TECHNOLOGIES) with sequence of pre-amiRNA*CWIN24* list in Supplemental Table S5.

### **Construction of** *pSTK::amiRCWIN* **and its transformation into** *Arabidopsis*

 To achieve a specific silencing of *CWIN2* and *4* in ovule initiation site, an ovule-specific *STK* promoter comprising of 1.8 kb upstream and 1.1 kb downstream of the translation start codon containing the first exon and intron was used to drive the expression of amiRNA*CWIN24*. The 2.9 kb *STK* promoter was cloned into pART7rc vector with primers listed in Supplemental Table S5. *SacI*, *NotI*, and *XhoI* restriction sites were added to its 5' and 3' ends, respectively. The *pSTK* was introduced into vector pART7rc by replacing the CaMV 35S  promoter with restriction sites *SacI* and *XhoI*. Subsequently, the 258bp pre-synthesised artificial miRNA precursor (pre-amiRNA*CWIN24*) was cloned into the *pSTK-*ART7 vector with EcoRI and XbaI to form the *pSTK*-amiR*CWIN*-ART7rc vector. The gene cassette *pSTK*- amiR*CWIN24* was released by digestion with NotI, which was then cloned into the binary vector pBARTrc. To complement the reduction of *STK* expression in the *pSTK*-amiR*CWIN24*  transgenic plants, a complementary vector *pSTK-STK*(CDS)-pCAMBIA1300 was constructed to drive the expression of a 771bp *STK* coding sequence (CDS).

 Arabidopsis inflorescences were dipped in cell suspension of the *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* strain AGL-1 (Zhang et al., 2006b), which carried the target binary vector, for 10 s with gentle agitation. Dipped plants were removed from cell suspension and drained for 3- 5s, and were then covered with plastic film to maintain high humidity for 16-24 h before being moved to glasshouse for recovery and growth under normal conditions.

#### **Ovule, seed and silique phenotyping**

 For ovule phenotyping, flower buds at stage 10-11 (~2 to 1 days before flowering, Roeder and Yanofsky, 2006) were collected and fixed in ethanol and acetic acid (9:1) overnight, and then washed by 90% ethanol, followed by a further wash with 70% ethanol at a 30 min interval. Thereafter, flower buds were mounted in a mixture of chloralhydrate/glycerol/water (8:1:2) and cleared overnight. Cleared flower buds were dissected to expose the gynoecium and observed under differential interference contrast (DIC) of a Leica dissection microscope. For analyses on seed number and silique length, fully expanded siliques at stage 17 (~ 5 days after flowering, Roeder and Yanofsky, 2006) were collected from the main inflorescences of each genotype. These siliques were cleared and mounted as described (Berleth and Jurgens, 1993). For seed number counting, siliques were fixed and clear mounted using the same method as for ovule phenotyping.

### **RNA isolation and Illumina sequencing**

 Pooled sample of flower buds at stages 8-10 was harvested, which covers a stage prior to (stage 8), during ( stage 9) and after (stage 10) ovule initiation. RNA was extracted from the pooled sample of WT and transgenic line 4-3, which exhibits the most severe reduction in ovule number, using the Qiagen RNA Plant Mini Kit, followed by an on-column DNase treatment to eliminate gDNA by DNaseI digestion. Eight RNA extracts (4 biological  replicates x 2 genotypes), each contained at least 5 µg of total RNA, were sent to the Australia Genome Research Facility Ltd (AGRF) for RNA sequencing. RNA Quality was determined by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and only samples with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) over 8 were further processed for cDNA libraries preparation and sequencing. Sequencing of the cDNA libraries was performed using an Illumina Hiseq2500 with 125bp pair-end with an average sequencing depth of 30 million reads.

#### **Sequencing trimming and mapping and determination of differentially expressed genes**

 Raw reads were trimmed using CLC Genomics Workbench 10.1.1 (Qiagen) based on Ru et al. (2017) with some modifications. Briefly, raw reads were trimmed as following: (1) Removal of reads with quality scores less than 20 (base-calling error probability less than 0.01) and those with ambiguous nucleotides more than 2; (2) Adapter trimming and (3) Removal of reads with sequences length less than 15 nucleotides. To estimate gene expression level, trimmed reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis reference genome sequence (TAIR10) using the CLC Genomics Workbench, which allows unique mapping with a maximum of two mismatches. Reads mapped to unigenes were counted and used for expression analysis. The number of mapped reads per kilobase per million reads (RPKM) was used as a measure of the expression level of each gene. Using the CLC genomic RNA-seq differential analysis tool, differential expressed genes (DEGs) of transgenic samples as compared to WT samples were identified based on the following criteria; i) mean RPKM value >=1 in at least one sample either in WT or transgenic group. Only genes exhibiting a mean RPKM greater or equal to 1 were considered to be expressed; ii) false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 and iii) log2FC is great than 1 or less than -1. MAPMAN (Thimm et al., 2004) was used to identify functional categories of the DEGs. The RNAseq data have been uloaded to NCBI website <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/> with Data Assertion NO. GSE139917

**Figure 10**. A model of how CWIN-mediated extracellular sugar signaling may be sensed at the plasmamembrane and transduced to the intracellular space to modulate downstream pathways to regulate ovule initiation.

 In Arabidopsis gynoecia, sucrose (Suc) is unloaded symplasmically through plasmodesmata (PD) for ovule primordium formation. Some Suc must have escaped to the cell wall for hydrolysis by CWIN into glucose (Glc) and Fructose (Fru). Silencing of CWIN would result in changes in extracellular sugar level or flux generating sugar signal, which may be sensed by: 1) Receptor-like kinases (RLKs), transmitted through small GTPase (Rop) into cytoplasm, and regulate gene expression in nucleus and (2) Hexose transporters, including clade I and II SWEETs and H+/Hex symporters to control cytosolic hexose level and sugar homeostasis, which may then be sensed by hexokinase or other proteins to regulate the downstream gene expression. In this study, the expression of CWIN gene was silenced in the ovule initiating region. This genetic intervene reduced the expression of hexose /H+ symporters and SWEETs for hexoses and likely their activities, thereby affecting intracellular sugar homeostasis and signalling. In parallel, the expression of RLKs and Rops was increased and decreased respectively in response to CWIN silencing, suggesting a disturbance of RLK-Rop- mediated signalling pathway. Both scenarios could disrupt downstream molecular pathways required for ovule initiation including genes encoding MADS transcription factors such as STK and the early auxin signalling proteins SAURs, leading to blockage of ovule initiation. Solid and dash lines indicate known and predicted pathways, respectively. T-shaped lines and arrows refer to inhibition and promotion, respectively.
