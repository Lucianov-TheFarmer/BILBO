## **Abstract:**

Root hairs protruding from epidermal cells increase the surface area for water and nutrient uptake. Various environmental factors, including light, promote root hair formation in *Arabidopsis thaliana*. Light regulates the expression of a large number of genes at the transcriptional and post-transcriptional level; however, there is little information linking the light response to root hair development. Here, we describe a novel mutant, *light-sensitive root-hair development 1* (*lrh1*), that displays enhanced root hair development in response to light. Hypocotyl and root elongation was inhibited in the *lrh1* mutant, which had a late flowering phenotype. We identified the gene encoding the p14 protein, a putative component of the SF3b complex essential for pre-mRNA splicing, as being responsible for the *lrh1* phenotype. Indeed, regulation of alternative splicing was affected in *lrh1* mutants and splicing inhibitor treatment mimicked the *lrh1* phenotype. Genome-wide alterations of splicing including differential splicing events of light signaling- and clock-related genes were found in *lrh1* as well as a difference in transcriptional regulation of multiple genes including up-regulation of essential genes for root hair development. These results suggest that pre-mRNA splicing is a key mechanism regulating root hair development in response to light signals.

**Keywords:** *Arabidopsis thaliana*, light regulation, p14 protein, pre-mRNA splicing, root hair development, splicing factor

## **Introduction**

Plants require light not only for energy but also for the development of morphological features (i.e., photomorphogenesis). Light is a unique environmental factor that induces cotyledon opening and chloroplast development and reduces hypocotyl elongation, allowing plants to switch from heterotrophic to photoautotrophic growth (Kami et al. 2010). Full exposure of roots to light negatively regulates primary root length and lateral root number (van Gelderen et al., 2018). Conversely, light promotes the formation of root hairs (De Simone et al. 2000); root hairs protrude from epidermal cells, increasing the surface area for uptake of water and nutrients (Marzec et al. 2015; Tanaka et al. 2014). Root hair formation is also controlled by exo- and endogenous factors such as nutrients (e.g., phosphates, iron, and calcium) and phytohormones such as ethylene and auxin (reviewed by Grierson et al. 2014).

Various environmental cues alter the expression of genes that integrate light signaling with networks that regulate root hair formation. Light controls the expression of a large number of genes at the transcriptional (Lau and Deng 2012) and post-transcriptional level, by controlling the alternative splicing of pre-mRNA (Simpson et al. 2008), translation (Liu et al. 2012), post-translational modification (Hoecker 2005), and chromatin rearrangement (Van Zanten et al. 2010). Recent studies have greatly enhanced our understanding of light-dependent alternative splicing regulation (Zhang et al. 2017). Phytochrome regulates light-responsive alternative splicing (Shikata et al. 2014) via the splicing factor RRC1 (Shikata et al. 2012). Another splicing factor, SFPS, interacts directly with phytochrome B and thereby promotes photomorphogenesis (Xin et al. 2017). Light-regulated alternative splicing independent of photoreceptor pathways has also been reported. In light-grown plants, sucrose regulates alternative RNA splicing more effectively than does light signaling via phytochromes and cryptochromes (Hartmann et al. 2016). Furthermore, alternative splicing regulated by retrograde signals from the chloroplast has also been reported (Petrillo et al. 2014). Light thus controls alternative splicing, as well as other mechanisms that regulate photomorphogenesis, via multiple signaling mechanisms such as phytochrome signaling, photosynthetic sugar signaling, and chloroplast retrograde signaling.

We aimed to identify a novel regulator of light-dependent root hair formation. Screening of activation T-DNA tagging lines identified a novel mutant, *light-sensitive root-hair development 1* (*lrh1*), with enhanced root hair development in response to light. Expression of *At5G12190,* which encodes the p14 protein, a homolog of a component of the SPLICING FACTOR 3b (SF3b) protein complex (Kuwasako et al. 2008; Will et al. 2001), was knocked down in the *lrh1* mutant. SF3b is an integral part of the U2 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein complex (U2 snRNP), one of the core components of the spliceosome machinery (Spadaccini et al. 2006; Wahl et al. 2009). p14 protein recognizes a branch site adenosine in introns and is highly evolutionarily conserved, reflecting its essential function in pre-mRNA splicing (Will et al. 2001). The *Arabidopsis thaliana* genome contains two copies of genes encoding putative p14 proteins, At5g12190 (atP14-1) and At2g14870 (atP14-2) (Wang and Brendel 2004), but no functional analysis of p14 has been performed. We identified genome-wide alternations of splicing and transcription patterns in the *lrh1* mutant. In addition, treatment of wild-type plants with a splicing inhibitor triggered enhanced emergence and growth of root hairs, mimicking the *lrh1* mutant phenotype. These results suggest that a pre-mRNA splicing factor links light signals and root hair development.

## **Results**

### *Isolation of a novel mutant showing enhanced root hair development*

To identify novel factors related to light-regulated root hair formation, we screened RIKEN Arabidopsis activation-tagged T2 lines for mutants with enhanced root hair development. Seedlings grown in darkness for 3 days were transferred to light conditions with a 16 h photoperiod (hereafter "in the light") and grown for 2 days (3-day-dark/2-day-light conditions, hereafter abbreviated to "DL" conditions in Fig. 1A). Before transfer to light, the position of the root tip of each seedling was marked to distinguish the newly elongated region (NER) (Fig. 1B, region from yellow arrowhead to root tip). We selected T2 plants with dense root hairs in the NER after growth under DL conditions. T3 plants were grown hydroponically according to the method of De Simone et al. (2000), and mutants producing more root hairs during 4 days of light than under continuous darkness or low-fluence light were chosen. We obtained one T-DNA insertion mutant with longer and denser root hairs, and named *light-sensitive root-hair development 1* (*lrh1*). By backcrossing this mutant with wild-type Columbia (Col-0, hereafter Col) for three times we purified *lrh1* with single T-DNA insertion. *lrh1* seedlings were smaller than Col (Fig. 1C), but the F1 phenotype was resemble to Col, showing that *lrh1* mutation is recessive (data not shown).

To examine the effect of light on purified *lrh1* plants grown vertically on MS agar medium, we compared the root hairs of plants under continuous darkness (DD) and DL conditions (Fig. 1A). Nearly all *lrh1* seedlings formed root hairs in the NER, whereas approximately half of Col seedlings did not form observable root hairs (Fig. 1D). Further analysis of the number and length of root hairs revealed that *lrh1* seedlings formed many root hairs even under DD conditions, while Col did not (Fig. 1D–G). Furthermore, the *lrh1* mutant had statistically significantly more root hairs than Col under DL conditions (Fig. 1F). In addition, the *lrh1* mutant had seedling growth defects, with shorter hypocotyls and primary roots under both DD and DL conditions (Fig. 1H, I). The *lrh1* mutant also showed late flowering compared with Col (Supplementary Fig. S1).

#### *Identification of the gene responsible for lrh1 phenotypes*

We determined the T-DNA insertion site in *lrh1* using a plasmid rescue technique (Nakazawa et al. 2001), which identified a single T-DNA insertion in chromosome 5, at the 5' upstream region of *At5g12190*, just downstream of the putative RNA polymerase binding site (TATA box) (Fig. 2A). Since the T-DNA contained transcriptional enhancer sequences, it is probable that the *lrh1* phenotype was caused by overexpression of the genes proximal to the T-DNA insertion site. In addition to *At5g12190*, the genes *At5g12180* and *At5g12200* were located on either side of the inserted T-DNA. *At5g12180*, *At5g12190,* and *At5g12200* encode a calcium-dependent protein kinase CPK17 (Cheng et al. 2002), splicing factor P14-1 (Wang and Brendel 2004), and pyrimidine-degradation enzyme PYD2 (Gojkovic et al. 2003), respectively. Reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis demonstrated no statistically significant differences in *CPK17* or *PYD2* expression between Col and *lrh1* (Fig. 2B, C). By contrast, expression of *P14-1* was significantly reduced in *lrh1* compared to Col (Fig. 2D).

The reduced expression of *P14-1* suggested its probable correspondence to the recessive *lrh1* phenotype. Thus, we introduced the *P14-1* gene region containing ~1 kb 5' upstream and ~0.8 kb 3' downstream into the *lrh1* mutant. *P14-1* expression was recovered in this complementation line (*lrh1*+*P14-1*) (Supplementary Fig. S2). All *lrh1* mutant phenotypes, *lrh1*+*P14A1* line to the levels of those in Col (Fig. 2E-H). By contrast, *PYD2* driven by either a constitutive or root-hair-specific promoter did not rescue the *lhr1* phenotype (Supplementary Fig. S3; Supplementary Materials and Methods). Based on these observations, we concluded that *P14-1* is the gene responsible for the *lrh1* phenotype.

Next, we aligned the amino acid sequences of Arabidopsis P14-1 and its homolog P14-2 with human p14 protein (Kuwasako et al. 2008) (Fig. 2I). p14 is a component of the SF3b protein complex, which is essential for pre-mRNA splicing and highly conserved among eukaryotes (Will et al. 2001). Arabidopsis P14-1 possesses an RNA recognition motif (RRM) fold containing two conserved ribonucleoprotein (RNP) motifs and a C-terminal nuclear localization signal (NLS), similar to other p14 homologs (Kuwasako et al. 2008). However, P14-2 lacked the C-terminal portion (16 amino acids) of the RRM and the whole NLS that were conserved in the other p14 proteins (Fig. 2I), suggesting that P14-2 may not function as an authentic p14 protein.

#### *Involvement of Arabidopsis P14-1 in pre-mRNA splicing*

p14 protein is a component of the SF3b protein complex in the U2 snRNP (Kuwasako et al. 2008); therefore, we tested the molecular function of P14-1 as a splicing factor by RT-semiquantitative PCR (RT-sqPCR) analysis of genes encoding serine/arginine-rich (SR) and SR-like proteins (Manley and Tacke 1996), reported to have alternative splicing variants (Palusa et al., 2007). We detected different splicing patterns between *lrh1* and Col for seven genes (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. S4A). Different splicing patterns were frequently observed between *lrh1* and Col, and/or between DD and DL conditions even in Col (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. S4A). These results suggest that regulation of pre-mRNA splicing and light-condition-dependent alternative splicing are defective in the *lrh1* mutant (Hartmann et al. 2016; Petrillo et al. 2014; Shikata et al. 2014). The abnormal splicing patterns of *SR* genes were recovered in the *lrh1+P14-1* line (Supplementary Fig. S4B), strongly indicating that P14-1 functions as a splicing factor.

## *Transcriptome analysis revealed genome-wide alteration of gene expression in the lrh1 mutant*

Next, we performed the RNA-seq analysis of Col and *lrh1* seedlings, to elucidate genome-wide effects of the *lrh1* mutation on gene regulation. Total RNA samples were isolated from 5-d-old Col and *lrh1* seedlings grown under the DL Condition (Fig. 1), and subjected to the RNA-seq analysis using the Illumina-HiSeq platform (HiSeq4000, Illumina Inc.). The RNA-seq data showed that 1,452 and 950 genes were up- and downregulated in the *lrh1* seedlings compared with Col (fold change>2, q<0.05; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). In the upregulated genes, several known root hair-related genes, such as *BRI1 ASSOCIATED RECEPTOR KINASE 1* (*BAK1*), *ROOT HAIR DEFECTIVE 6-LIKE 2* (*RSL2*), *ROOT HAIR DEFECTIVE 2* (*RHD2*), *ROOT HAIR SPECIFIC 2* (*RHS2*), *RHS14, RHS16*, and *MARIS* (*MRI*) (Bruex et al. 2012; Grierson et al. 2014; Shibata et al. 2018; Wei and Li 2018), were included, as reflecting the enhancement of root hair formation in *lrh1* (Supplementary Table S1). The gene ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis on these differentially-expressed genes indicated that the genes for RNA metabolic regulation, such as RNA polymerization, pre-mRNA splicing, RNA degradation, and translation, were significantly enriched in the upregulated genes in the *lrh1* seedlings (Supplementary Table S3), suggesting that the knock-down mutation of *P14-1* changed RNA metabolism widely in seedlings. Furthermore, the 42 genes annotated as "response to stress (GO:0006950)" were overrepresented in the upregulated genes in *lrh1* (Supplementary Table S3). In this category, 13 genes encoding putative receptor kinase were included (Supplementary Table S4). Thus, it is possible that the receptor kinase-based protein phosphorylation stimulated by environmental stresses would be affected in *lrh1*.

Since the *P14-1* gene responsible for *lrh1* phenotype would function in pre-mRNA splicing (Fig. 3), we also checked effects of the *lrh1* mutation on alternative splicing. The splicing analysis using rMATS program (Shen et al. 2014) on the RNA-seq data demonstrated that the *lrh1* mutation strongly influenced alternative splicing regulation in seedlings (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S5). Especially, more than 50 % of genes with intron retention were significantly-differently spliced between Col and *lhr1* seedlings (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S5). Interestingly, the GO term enrichment analysis showed alternative splicing type-dependent accumulation of specific gene functions (Supplementary Tables S6-S9); in the category of skipped exon (SE), the genes with the GO term "cellular protein metabolic process (GO:0044267)" including ubiquitination-, phosphorylation- and proteasome-related genes, were significantly accumulated (Fig. 4A; Table S6), while the categories of alternative 5' splice site (A5SS) and 3' splice site (A3SS) contained the genes related to RNA metabolism, such as for regulation of mRNA metabolic process (GO:1903311) and RNA splicing (GO:0008380) (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S6-S9), in accordance with our RT-PCR results on *SR* genes shown in Fig. 3 (Supplementary Fig. S5). In the case of intron retention (IR) category, the GO term related to protein modification such as "phosphoprotein phosphatase activity (GO:0004721)", "protein kinase activity (GO:0004672)", and "ubiquitin-like protein transferase activity (GO:0019787)" were enriched in addition to "RNA splicing (GO:0008380)" (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S9). No GO term enrichment was observed for the Mutually exclusive exons (MXE) genes (Fig. 4A). These functional categories were overlapped with the enriched GO terms for the upregulated genes in *lrh1* as described above (Supplementary Table S3), suggesting that the metabolic regulation of mRNA and protein for environmental response would be strongly disordered in *lrh1*, not only through the altered transcript level, but also the alternative splicing regulation, possibly changing the functional domain structures of corresponding proteins.

# *The lrh1 mutation affected the alternative splicing regulation of light signaling and clock genes*

It has been shown that pre-mRNA splicing of light signaling and the circadian clock genes is critical for phytochrome-mediated light responses (Shikata et al. 2014). To obtain molecular clues into roles for P14-1 in alternative splicing regulation, we next checked the splicing patterns of genes for light signaling and clock regulation (Shikata et al. 2014) in the *lrh1* mutant. The splicing analysis on RNA-seq data indicated that the splicing patterns of multiple light signaling- and clock-related genes were affected in the *lrh1* mutant (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Table S5). Our RT-PCR analysis confirmed such abnormal splicing regulation experimentally, for *CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED 1* (*CCA1*; Filichkin et al. 2010)*, FAR-RED ELONGATED HYPOCOTYLS3* (*FHY3*; Ma and Li 2018), *PHYTOCHROME-ASSOCIATED PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 5* (*PAPP5*; Ryu et al. 2005), and *PSEUDO-RESPONSE REGULATOR 7* (*PRR7*; Kwon et al. 2014) (Fig. 4B).

Concerning an essential clock gene *CCA1*, we observed three alternative splice isoforms in *lrh1* mutant (Fig. 4B). Of these, the level of the functional isoform in *lrh1* was similar to Col, while each level of the two isoforms retaining a partial and a full length intron 4, which contain a premature termination codon (PTC; Filichkin et al. 2010), was different from Col (Fig. 4B). Because the PTC could be targeted for degradation by nonsense-mediated decay (NMD), the different levels of splice isoforms (Fig. 4B) indicate the altered expression of the functional CCA1 transcription factor in the *lrh1* mutant. In the case of another core clock gene *PRR7*, four splice isoforms corresponding to the functional isoform and the three alternative splice isoforms which contain the PTC (James et al. 2012; Kwon et al. 2014) were detected in Col and *lrh1* mutant (Fig. 4B). The abundance of the isoforms retaining intron 2/3 and skipped exon 4 in *lrh1* mutant (Fig. 4B; Kwon et al. 2014) suggests that functional PRR7 may be reduced in *lrh1*. The alteration of expression levels of these oscillators controlling morning and day shifts (Nakamichi 2011) indicates transcriptional regulation by circadian clock in *lrh1* mutant would be modified.

Concerning the splice isoforms of the *FHY3* gene involved in light signaling and other wide range of cellular processes (Ma and Li 2018), intron retention was clearly detected in the *lrh1* mutant (Fig. 4B). The role of these alternative splice isoforms is still unclear, but lack of the C-terminal domain could affect the FHY3 function as a transcription factor (Ma and Li 2018). PAPP5, a phytochrome-specific type 5 phosphatase, controls light signaling by enhancing phytochrome stability (Ryu et al. 2005). The subcellular localization of the protein seems to be controlled by the alternative splicing because its tomato orthologue LePP5 has the small and large splice isoforms localized in the nucleus and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), respectively (de la Fuente van Bentem et al. 2003). The decrease in the larger splice isoform of *PAPP5* in the *lrh1* mutant (Fig. 4B) suggests the ER-retained PAPP5 is reduced in *lrh1* and the different downstream events via the phytochrome signaling are caused. These results imply that P14-1 functions as one of modifiers for light-and clock-dependent regulation of alternative splicing.

## *Inhibition of RNA splicing by chemical treatment produced an lrh1-like root hair phenotype*

The molecular function of P14 raised the hypothesis that defects in pre-mRNA splicing regulation trigger abnormal regulation of root hair formation. To test this hypothesis, we utilized the splicing inhibitor pladienolide B (PB), which stalls spliceosome assembly at the A complex, the stage at which U2 snRNP joins the branch-point sequence (Effenberger et al. 2017). We first examined the length of primary roots and hypocotyls in Col plants treated with 0.1 to 0.5 µM PB in the light. Inhibition of hypocotyl elongation was observed in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5A). Inhibition of root elongation was also observed (Fig. 5B) as reported previously (Ling et al. 2017). We determined the number and length of root hairs in Col seedlings treated with 0.4 µM PB, because these had primary roots and hypocotyls of similar length to those of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-treated *lrh1* (Fig. 5A, B).

Under both DD and DL conditions (Fig. 1A), hypocotyl length was decreased by 0.4 µM PB treatment to similar lengths observed in the *lrh1* mutant (Fig. 5C). Primary root length of PB-treated Col plants was similar to that of *lrh1* plants under DD conditions, but was longer than that of *lrh1* plants under DL conditions (Fig. 5D). This detailed observation revealed that the inhibitory effects of PB on root elongation are affected by light conditions. During the initial 3 days of continuous dark conditions, PB significantly decreased primary root length (Supplementary Fig. S6A), similar to the *lrh1* mutation; however, under LD conditions, we detected no difference in root elongation between PB- and DMSO-treated Col plants after 2 days in the light (Supplementary Fig. S6B).

Since PB-treated plants sometimes showed severe growth inhibition, we analyzed the number and length of root hairs in individual plants possessing observable root hairs. In Col plants treated with 0.4 µM PB, the number and length of root hairs increased significantly under DL conditions (Fig. 5E–G). However, PB-treated Col plants under DD conditions had fewer root hairs than *lrh1* mutant plants (Fig. 5F). Surprisingly, PB-treated Col plants under DL conditions had many more root hairs than *lrh1* mutant plants (Fig. 5F). We further compared the density of root hairs (number/mm root) in the region elongated after 2 days in the light, because the NER of the root in PB-treated Col plants was longer than that of *lrh1*  plants in the light (Supplementary Fig. S6B). Root hair density in PB-treated Col plants was higher than that in DMSO-treated Col and *lrh1* mutant plants (Fig. 5H).

To evaluate the effects of the splicing inhibitor and the *lrh1* mutation on the splicing events, we tested the alternative splicing of *SR* genes by RT-PCR analysis. Of the tested four *SR* genes, the splicing pattern of *RSZ32* in Col plants treated with 0.4 µM PB closely resembled that in the DMSO-treated *lrh1* plants (Supplementary Fig. S7). The other two (*SR30* and *RS31a*) in PB-treated Col plants showed moderate patterns with those in DMSO-treated Col and *lrh1* mutant plants. The effects of PB treatment and the *lrh1* mutation on both promotion of the root hair development and alternative splicing of *SR* genes were not perfect matching, but nevertheless similar to each other. These results show that inhibition of pre-mRNA splicing abnormally enhances root hair formation and light modifies the effect of the splicing inhibition on root hair development.

## *Function of Arabidopsis P14-1*

We showed that repression of *P14-1* expression causes *lrh1* phenotypes. Many mutations affecting pre-mRNA splicing and plant phenotypes have been reported, and several mutants possess similar phenotypes to *lrh1,* such as the dwarf phenotype of the *mdf* mutant (Casson et al. 2009; Petricka and Nelson 2007), and delayed flowering (Swaraz et al. 2011). Other splicing mutants and RNAi lines display more severe phenotypes, such as embryonic lethal (Ohtani et al. 2013) and impaired reproduction (Aki et al. 2011). At present, however, there is no report of a splicing mutant that affects root hair development.

Numerous factors are involved in pre-mRNA splicing, and common phenotypes are not necessarily observed among the various already-known splicing mutants, with each mutant having distinguishing features (Aki et al. 2011; Casson et al. 2009; Ohtani et al. 2013; Swaraz et al. 2011). In contrast to mutants that display critical developmental abnormalities such as embryonic lethal (Ohtani et al. 2013), *lrh1* phenotypes represented comparatively minor morphological alterations such as retarded growth and enhanced root hair development. This suggests that a severe decrease of *P14-1* transcript in Arabidopsis is not lethal. As p14 deficiency in yeast delays proliferation, but is not lethal (Gottschalk et al. 2001), p14 may not be essential in Arabidopsis. Alternatively, the comparatively mild phenotype of *lrh1*  compared with severe phenotypes of other splicing factor mutants (Ohtani et al. 2013) and effects of splicing inhibitors at high concentrations (Ling et al. 2017) might be due to *P14-2*  functioning redundantly with other. The p14 protein and six other splicing factors form the SF3b complex, and SF3b binds other complexes such as SF3a, U2A, and U2B to form the U2 snRNP (Spadaccini et al. 2006; Wahl et al. 2009). An RNAi knockdown line of atSAP130, one of the components of SF3b, shows a dwarf and low-sterility phenotype (Aki et al. 2011). The SF3b complex also contains comparatively larger proteins such as atSF3b150 (150 kDa) and atSAP155 (155 kDa), suggesting that these larger proteins may strengthen the frame of the SF3b complex, and the complex may be able to function structurally without P14-1.

### *Pre-mRNA splicing is key for regulating root hair development*

A genome-wide analysis of transcriptional and splicing patterns in *lrh1* mutant revealed differences in transcription and pre-mRNA splicing of multiple genes related to various functions (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table S3, S6-S9). Of these, intron retention of genes classified into protein modification such as phosphoprotein phosphatase activity, protein kinase activity, and RNA splicing was obvious in *lrh1* mutant (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S9). Our RT-sqPCR also indicated that alternative splicing patterns of at least eight *SR* genes involved in the selection of pre-mRNA splice sites were different between the *lrh1* mutant and Col (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. S4). Alternative splicing of *SR* genes is controlled in a developmental- and tissue-specific manner and is also affected by hormones such as abscisic acid (ABA) and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA; Palusa et al. 2007). Mutation of a SR-like protein produces defects in ABA and glucose signaling (Carvalho et al. 2010), developmental defects (Ali et al. 2007), and genome-wide changes in alternative splicing of pre-mRNAs (Carvalho et al. 2016). Furthermore, splicing inhibitor PB induces intron retention of *SR* transcripts and activates a great number of genes responding to ABA, drought and salt stress (Ling et al. 2017). The different splicing patterns of *SR* genes in *lrh1* suggest that developmental regulation of splicing events and hormone- and stress-signaling was altered in *lrh1*, and that different *SR* isoforms induce further alternative splicing of pre-mRNA of their target genes, resulting in morphological alteration. In agreement with this theory, more than forty genes involved in stress responses including genes encoding putative receptor kinases were upregulated in *lrh1* (Supplementary Table S3 and S4). Upregulation of multiple receptor kinase genes in *lrh1* implies that protein kinase signaling cascade initiated by environmental stimuli might be modulated by aberrant pre-mRNA splicing in *lrh1* mutant.

Light causes dramatic genome-wide alternative splicing (Zhang et al. 2017). Alternative splicing also occurs in response to various abiotic stresses (Shang et al. 2017). Conversely, as described above, inhibition of constitutive and alternative splicing by a splicing inhibitor causes abiotic stress responses and ABA signaling (AlShareef et al. 2017; Ling et al. 2017). The splicing-defective mutant *lrh1* and treatment with the splicing inhibitor PB showed both the splicing alterations along with significant levels of intron retention (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S9; Ling et al. 2017) and enhanced root hair formation (Fig. 1, 5), suggesting that aberrant pre-mRNA splicing abnormally triggers the initiation of root hair development. Interestingly, PB promotion of root hair formation was observed under DL but not DD conditions (Fig. 5F), suggesting that a combination of splicing inhibition and light signaling strengthens the initiation and elongation of root hairs. The influence of the *lrh1* mutation on genome-wide alternative splicing, especially on splicing events of light signaling- and clock-related genes (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Table S5) indicates the transcriptional network regulated by light and clock in *lrh1* mutant would be different from Col. Indeed, more than 1,000 genes, including several important genes for root hair development, such as *RSL2* and *RHD2* (Grierson et al. 2014), were up-regulated in *lrh1* mutant (Supplementary Tables S1). We have not yet identified the genes with splicing defects that directly link light and root hair development; future work on *lrh1* will further clarify the mechanism by which light, along with other exo- and endogenous factors, influences root hair development through alternative splicing.

We demonstrated that inhibition of pre-mRNA splicing enhances light-mediated root hair formation. Thus, this study provides insight into how light and other environmental factors interact with one another to control root hair development via pre-mRNA splicing regulation.

#### **Materials and Methods**

#### *Plant material and growth conditions*

*Arabidopsis thaliana* (Col-0) was used as the wild type in all experiments. Seeds were surface sterilized and sown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 1% agar and 1% sucrose as described previously (Uenishi et al. 2014). After stratification at 4°C for 2 days, seeds were cultured vertically at 22°C under 16 h illumination (16L8D photoperiod) with cool white fluorescent light of 10 to 15 W/m<sup>2</sup> .

#### *Mutant selection*

T2 seedlings of more than 1000 RIKEN activation-tagged lines (Nakazawa et al. 2003) were grown vertically in the darkness for 3 days, transferred to the light conditions of with 16 h photoperiod, and then grown for 2 days (DL conditions in Fig. 1A). Five-day-old seedlings showing increased numbers of root hairs during light illumination following initial dark treatment were selected using a stereomicroscope and grown for seed harvest. Several T<sup>2</sup> plants were selected, and root hair phenotypes of plants grown hydroponically under continuous darkness or under light (16-h photoperiod) for 4 days were observed in T<sup>3</sup> seedlings according to the method of De Simone et al. (2000). One of these lines was finally selected as *lrh1*. The selection of *lrh1* was performed by K.S.-N. and the staff of the former Photobiology-2 team in Sendai RIKEN.

#### *RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis*

Plants (~100 mg) were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at 80ºC. Frozen samples were crushed with a Multi-beads Shocker (Yasui Kikai, Osaka, Japan), and total RNA was isolated using an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Tokyo, Japan) and treated with an RNase-free DNase Set (Qiagen). Alternatively, total RNA was prepared using a Cica Geneus RNA Prep Kit for Plant (Kanto Kagaku, Tokyo, Japan). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng total RNA in 20 μL reaction medium by reverse transcription using either an iScript gDNA Clear cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) or a ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan).

## *RT-sqPCR*

RT-sqPCR was performed with ExTaq HS DNA polymerase (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions using an Applied Biosystems Veriti® Thermal Cycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan). RT-sqPCR of *SR* genes was performed according to the method of Palusa et al. (2007) using cDNA (equivalent to 10 ng total RNA) as template. RT-sqPCR of light signaling and clock genes was also performed using the same methods as *SR* genes except the extension time (40 s). Amplified PCR products were electrophoresed, stained with ethidium bromide, and detected using either a FAS-II UV transilluminator and imaging system (Toyobo) or a Fujifilm LAS-3000 imaging system (GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, England).

## *RT-qPCR*

cDNA (equivalent to 12.5 ng total RNA) was used for quantitative PCR analysis with an Applied Biosystems StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific), using a KAPA SYBR® FAST qPCR Kit (Kapa Biosystems), according to the manufacturers' instructions. The following qPCR conditions were used: enzyme activation, 95ºC for 20 sec; denaturing, annealing, and extension, 40 cycles at 95ºC for 1 sec and 60ºC for 20 sec; dissociation, 95ºC for 15 sec, 60ºC for 1 min, and then 95ºC for 15 sec. Primer sets used are listed in Supplementary Table S10. Using the comparative Ct method (ΔΔCt method), the Ct values from different cDNA samples were normalized against those of EF1α and then compared. At least two to three independent samples were used.

#### *Complementation test*

A 2.6-kb genomic region containing the *P14-1* gene was PCR-amplified from genomic DNA of Col-0 using primers lrh-4671F and lrh-7288R (Supplementary Table S10) and DNA polymerase (KOD-plus-ver.2, Toyobo). PCR products were cloned into the vector ENTR/D-TOPO (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before recombination into the destination vector pGWB1 using Gateway technology (Nakagawa et al. 2007). The construct was introduced into *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* GV3101 and then into the *lrh1* mutant by floral dipping (Clough and Bent 1998). Transgenic plants were selected on both hygromycin and kanamycin for the *lrh1* allele and the *P14-1* transgene, respectively.

#### *Measurement of root hair number and length*

Arabidopsis seedlings were grown vertically on MS agar medium in square plates. After exposure to white light to promote germination, seedlings were grown in darkness for 3 days, transferred to light conditions with 16 h photoperiod, and then grown for 2 days (DL conditions in Fig. 1A). Upon transfer to light, the position of the root tip of each seedling was marked on the plate to distinguish the region elongating in the light. The number and length of root hairs developing from the primary root during culture under light conditions were measured from the root surface in contact with air using a stereomicroscope (MS5, Leica microsystems, Tokyo, Japan) with a transmitted illumination system. Images of root hairs and seedlings were captured using either a MS5 or an S8APO stereomicroscope (Leica microsystems) with a EC3 CCD camera (Leica microsystems) and LAS EZ software (ver. 3.1.0, Leica microsystems). The lengths of root hairs were analyzed using an Image J software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).

## *mRNA-seq library preparation and sequencing*

Total RNA samples were extracted using a Cica Geneus RNA Prep Kit for Plant (Kanto Kagaku, Tokyo, Japan) from 5-d-old seedlings of Col and *lrh1* grown under DL condition, according to the manufacturer's instructions, and their quality was assessed by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Inc.). The mRNA-seq libraries were constructed from the purified total RNA samples with the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina), according to the manufacturer's instructions. A total of 6 libraries (three biological replicates in each of Col and *lrh1* seedlings) were sequenced on 1 lane of HiSeq4000 (Illumina); to obtain the sequence data in fastq format, basecalling was performed by Illumina RTA software in sequencer and demultiplexing was performed by Illumina bcl2fastq 2.17 software based on index information.

#### **mRNA library data analysis and GO Term Analysis**

After pretreatment of depleting rRNA-derived reads by Tagdust2 program v2.33 (Lassmann 2015) and trimming adaptor / poor-quality part of a read by trimmomatic program v0.38 (Bolger et al. 2014), read data from each library was mapped independently using Hisat2 version 2.1.0 (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/; Kim et al. 2015) against the Arabidopsis genome (TAIR10) with Araport11 annotation (https://www.araport.org/downloads/Araport11\_Release\_201606) (Cheng et al. 2017). GFF version of the annotation file downloaded from above site was converted to GTF format with gffread utility (http://www.ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/gff.shtml#gffread). Hisat2 mapping was performed with the following options: "--dta", "--score-min L,0,-2.0", "--sp 4,2", "--rna-strandness RF", "--max-intronlen 10000", and the other parameters were set as default. Transcript expression was calculated using StringTie program version 1.3.4d (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie) (Pertea et al. 2015), with default settings except "-f 0.05", "-m 150", "-a 6", "--rf", and "-eB"; for relative expression level, TPM (transcripts-per-million) was adopted. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between two conditions (Col vs *lrh1*) were detected using R package TCC (version 1.22.1; Sun et al. 2013) which internally utilizing R package edgeR (version 3.24.2; Robinson et al. 2009); genes with a q-value of < 0.05 were regarded as DEGs and subsequently referred as up-/down-regulated ones according to log2 fold-change of average TPMs. For splicing analysis, we used the computer program replicate Multivariate Analysis of Transcript Splicing (rMATS) version 4.0.2 (http://rnaseq-mats.sourceforge.net/; Shen et al. 2014) with settings "--readLength 150", "--cstat 0.0001", "--libType fr-firststrand", "--nthread 4", "--tstat 4", and bam files of the Hisat2-output as the inputs.

For GO term analyses, DEG datasets between Col and *lrh1* with more than a 2-fold change were separated into up- or down-regulated gene sets, and then independently subjected to the PANTHER (Protein ANalysis THrough Evolutionary Relationships) Classification System (http://pantherdb.org/; Mi et al. 2018). The GO terms and pathways with a Benjamini & Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) of < 0.05 were considered to be statistically over-represented.
