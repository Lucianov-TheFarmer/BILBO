### 1. Introduction

Developmental progression is usually controlled by complex and precise sequences of gene expressions. It is tightly governed by the interconnected regulatory networks that integrate the DNA-binding proteins, transcription factors (TF), as well as the distribution of DNA *cis*-elements (motifs) in the genome [1–4]. In brief, a TF recognizes and binds to a short conserved motif located in the core of the promoter or in the distal *cis*-regulatory element, e.g. the enhancer, of its target gene to initiate or repress its expression.

The specific TF binding sites as well as their associated target genes can be easily determined by high-throughput sequencing technologies such as the widely-used Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) method [5,6]. However, unlike performing ChIPseq experiments in human or the model plant *Arabidopsis*, the application of such a technique is limited in soybean research due to the lack of transcription factor-specific antibodies and effective soybean transformation methods for the stable expression of epitope-tagged TF proteins. Only a few ChIP-seq studies have been reported in soybean thus far [7–11], hence largely hindering our understanding of the TF regulatory networks in soybean.

While the transient expression of targeted TF proteins in soybean protoplasts may be an alternative method to overcome the limitation stated above, the transformation rate of soybean protoplasts is too low to provide enough material for the traditional ChIP-seq pipeline. Therefore, in this study, we adopted the robust, low-input requirement ChIPmentation method, which has been widely used in mammalian research, to soybean. This technology introduces a Tn5 transposase tagmentation step to the traditional ChIP-seq library preparation [12]. The application of ChIPmentation has been successfully demonstrated in *Arabidopsis* [13], but has yet to be attempted in soybean.

In this study, we adopted and modified the ChIPmentation method to transiently express the tagged C2H2-type zinc finger TF, JAGGED 1 (GmJAG1) [14–17], in soybean protoplasts. The homolog of GmJAG1 in

*Arabidopsis*, AtJAG1, is a key TF involved in multiple developmental progress and gene regulation pathways [14–[16\]](#page-11-0).

GmJAG1 was located within a ~ 12 kb region in the *Ln* locus in the soybean Chromosome 20 that control leaf shape [\[16](#page-11-0)]. The GmJAG1 protein consists of three major domains: the Ethylene-responsive element binding factor-associated Amphiphilic Repression (EAR), C2H2 zinc finger DNA binding, and poline-rich domain. The single amino acid substitution in the EAR domain, Asp in Ln to His in ln, lead to the transition Ln to ln phenotype, which associated to the broad leaflet and less number of seed per pod (NSP) or narrow leaflet with more NSP, respectively [[16\]](#page-11-0). As a key transcription factor, using this new approach, we successfully determined the genome-wide distribution of GmJAG1 specific binding sites (GmJBSs), established a putative regulatory network centered on GmJAG1, and identified a novel GmJAG1-binding motif. Furthermore, we found that GmJBSs were associated with distinctive epigenetic states, such as the open chromatin or the H3K27me3 marks, to activate or repress gene expressions, respectively. Finally, we also discovered an enhancer-like feature of GmJBSs. Although the binding region in a transient expression system might not totally reflect the actual binding *in planta*, the new genomic information such as the novel motif and the putative enhancer-like element suggests that these data from ChIPmentation could provide valuable insights into our understanding of the TF regulatory patterns in soybean.

#### **2. Materials and methods**

#### *2.1. Plant material and growth condition*

Soybean (*Glycine* max [L.] Merrill) cultivar Williams 82 (W82) was used in this study. After surface sterilization with 75% ethanol, seeds were germinated on vermiculite for 4–5 days until the cotyledons have fully expanded. The soybean seedlings were then transferred to halfstrength Hoagland's solution for another 10–12 days for the development of healthy trifoliate leaves in the greenhouse (25–28 ◦C).

#### *2.2. Vector construction, soybean protoplast isolation and transformation*

The full-length coding sequence of *GmJAG1* without stop codon was amplified from W82 cDNA, using sequence-specific primers (Table S1), and then ligated into a linearized 35S:3Avi plasmid using *Bam*HI (NEB) and *Xho*I (NEB) via ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). After Sanger sequencing, 100 mL of overnight *E. coli* culture harboring the 35S:GmJAG1-3Avi plasmid (Fig. S1) was used for plasmid preparation by the Plasmid Maxi Kit (QIAGEN), and the endotoxin was removed by Trixon-114 (Sigma) [[18\]](#page-11-0).

Soybean protoplast isolation and transformation was done according to previous studies [\[19,20](#page-11-0)] with minor modifications described in the Supplementary Protocol.

#### *2.3. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)*

About 0.5 g of W82 first-trifoliate leaves were flash-frozen in liquid N2 upon harvest and the total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plant Mini RNA Isolation Kit (QIAGEN). The mRNA was isolated using 5 μg total RNA with magnetic Dynabeads™ Oligo(dT)25 (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's instructions. After fragmentation, the mRNA was reverse-transcribed using random primers followed by second-strand synthesis, end repairing, dA tailing, and the Y-shape adapter ligation (Vazyme). After the final amplification using VAHTS Multiplex Oligos Set 4/5 (Vazyme) index primers, the RNA-seq library was sent for sequencing on the Illumina XTen platform in the paired-end mode. The paired-end raw reads were trimmed for adaptors, and lowquality reads were removed using TrimGalore [\(http://github.com/Feli](http://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore)  [xKrueger/TrimGalore,](http://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) [https://github.com/marcelm/cutadapt\)](https://github.com/marcelm/cutadapt). PCR duplicates were removed by Picard tools [\(http://broadinstitute.github.](http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/)  [io/picard/](http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/)). The normalized gene expression level, fragments per

kilobase per million mapped reads (FPKM), was analyzed by the HISAT-StringTie pipeline with default parameters [\[21](#page-11-0)].

#### *2.4. Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq)*

The complete optimized protocol for the protoplast ChIP-seq library preparation using the ChIPmentation method is provided in the Supplementary Protocol.

Histone mark (H3K27me3) ChIP-seq was conducted following the procedure from a previous report [[22\]](#page-11-0). The chromatin was pull-down by the ChIP-seq grade anti-H3K27me3 antibodies (Diagenode, Cat.no. C15410195) and the recovered ChIP DNA library was constructed using TruePrep Index Kit V2 for Illumina are used (Vazyme, cat.no.TD202) following the user's manual. Input library preparation was processed by Tn5 following the manual instructions of the TD202 Kit.

The paired-end raw reads were trimmed for adaptors, and lowquality reads were removed using TrimGalore (default parameters) before they were aligned to the reference genome of soybean W82 version 4 using Bowtie2 [\[23\]](#page-11-0) with the parameter set as: –very-sensitive. The mapped reads containing *>*MAPQ30 (samtools view -q 30) were used for subsequent analyses and the peak-calling was done via MACS2 [[24\]](#page-11-0) and normalized with the input control. Parameters of peak calling for TF ChIP-seq was set as '-q 0.01 -g 1.0e+9' or '–trackline –extsize 147 –broad -q 0.01 –nomodel -g 1.0e+9 –buffer-size 500000' for histone ChIP-seq. ChIP-seq data profile and heatmap plots were compiled using Deeptools [\[25](#page-11-0)].

# *2.5. Assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATACseq)*

About 0.2 g healthy trifoliate leaves was flash-frozen with liquid N2 and ground to a powder. 10 mL of lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 0.4% Triton-X100, 1 mM Phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride [PMSF], 1 X protease inhibitor [PI, Roche], 5 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 20 mM NaCl, 80 mM KCl, 0.25 M sucrose) were added and the mixture was filtered through Miracloth (Millipore) and a 40-μm cell filter. The cleared lysate was centrifuged at 1200*g* for 10 min to pellet the nuclei, which were then washed with 1 mL Wash Buffer I (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 0.4% Triton-X100, 20 mM NaCl, 80 mM KCl, 1 mM PMSF, 5 mM 2-mercaptoethanol,1 X PI) followed by centrifugation at 1200*g* for 10 min. The pellet was then resuspended in 0.5 mL Wash Buffer II (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 5 mM MgCl2). One μL of the nucleic suspension were stained with 1× DAPI solution (Sigma) and 8 μL PBS buffer to calculate the concentration of the nucleic suspension by a hematocytometer under a fluorescence microscope (Leica). Approximately 105 nuclei were resuspended in 35 μL Wash Buffer II and 10 μL 5× transposase buffer and 5 μL Tn5 transposase (Vazyme) were added. The entire 50 μL reaction mixture was incubated at 37 ◦C for 30 min and the reaction was terminated by 10 mM EDTA. The resulting tagged DNA products were purified with the DNA Clean & Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research) and amplified using Q5 DNA polymerase (NEB) for ~10 cycle in a thermocycler (Bio-rad) using the following PCR protocol: 72 ◦C 3 min, 98 ◦C 0.5 min, 10 cycle for 98 ◦C 15 s, 63 ◦C 20 s and 72 ◦C 20 s. The libraries were purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman) following the manufacturer's protocol, and then sequenced on the Illumina XTen platform. The trimming and mapping of the reads were done in a similar way to the ChIP-seq data processing. The mapped read cut-off (*>*MAPQ30) was used for peak calling via Genrich (<https://github.com/jsh58/Genrich>) using the ATAC model (− j). Bedtools intersect [\(https://bedtools.readthedocs.io](https://bedtools.readthedocs.io)) was used for the determined of overlapping between ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq peaks with the default parameters.

#### *2.6. Motif analysis*

Motif analysis was done using the MEME suite software package with default parameters [\[26](#page-11-0)]. Meme-chip was used for *de-novo* motif prediction, AME for motif enrichment analysis, Fimo for mapping motifs to DNA sequences, and TOMTOM for motif comparisons. Soybean transcription factor (TF) motif position weight matrix (PWM) databases were downloaded from PlantTFDB [\[27\]](#page-11-0).

#### *2.7. Transient expression assay*

Transient expressions of promoter or enhancer activity were performed according to a previous report [[28\]](#page-11-0). In brief, the full-length coding sequence of *GmJAG1* was cloned into the pGreenII 62-SK vector between the *Hin*dIII and *Pst* I restriction sites as the effector. Promotor sequences (~2 kb upstream of transcription start site [TSS]) of genes to be tested were cloned into the pGreenII 800-mini35S:*Luciferase*  (LUC) vector between the *Hin*dIII and *Pst* I restriction sites as the reporters. The *Renilla* (Ren) reporter gene driven by the 35S promoter was used as the internal standard for normalization. All primers used for generating constructs for transient expression assays are listed in Table S1. *Arabidopsis* mesophyll protoplasts were prepared, transfected and cultured as previously described [[29\]](#page-11-0). Relative LUC/Ren activities were measured using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) according to the user's manual. The data represented the averages of four biological replicates.

# *2.8. Chromosome conformation capture (3C) DNA library and quantitative PCR (qPCR)*

The 3C DNA library construction was performed according to a previous study [[30\]](#page-11-0). Briefly, 0.5–1 g soybean leaf was fix using 1% formaldehyde in 1 X PBS buffer (Thermo) for 15 min and quench by 0.125 mM Glycine (Sigma). After fixation, the soybean leaf nuclei were isolated in 10 mL nuclei isolation buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 8.0, 250 mM sucrose, 1 mM MgCl2, 5 mM KCl, 40% Glycerol, 0.25% Triton X-100, 0.1 mM PMSF, 0.1% 2-mercaptoethanol), heated in 150 μL 0.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) at 65 ◦C for 5 min, and then the SDS was quenched by 20 μL 10% Triton X-100. The resulting nuclei were digested with *Dpn*  II (NEB) at 37 ◦C overnight. After inactivation of the enzyme at 65 ◦C for 20 min, 1 μL each of 10 mM dTTP, 10 mM dCTP, and 10 mM dGTP, 25 μL of 0.4 mM biotin-14-dATP (Sigma), and 40 U Klenow Fragment (NEB) were added to fill in the sticky ends. The blunt ends were then ligated with the T4 DNA ligase (NEB) following manufacturer's protocol. Nuclear DNA was released by adding proteinase K (NEB) and hold at 65 ◦C for 8 h and then isolated using the DNA Clean & Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research). The DNA was sonicated into ~500-1000-bp fragments and the biotin-labeled ligated fragments were pulled down with MyOne™ Streptavidin C1 Dynabeads (Invitrogen) for qPCR, according to manufacturer's protocol. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was used as the control template. The 3C experiments was performed at two biological replicates and qPCR was performed in triplicates with the TB Green Premix Ex II (Takara) following the manufacturer's instructions. qPCR was performed on a CFX384 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). Cq values of the target regions were normalized to *Glyma.20G116200* (serving as internal control) and then quantified relative to the genomic DNA. Primers used for 3C-qPCR experiments are listed in Table S1.

# *2.9. Update of transcription factor (TF) annotations in Williams 82 reference genome version 4*

The iTAK TF annotation pipeline [[31\]](#page-11-0) was used for W82 genome (v4) TF annotation. The new annotation of TFs was then integrated into the version 2 TF database from PlantTFDB database [\[27](#page-11-0)].

#### *2.10. Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)*

The full-length coding sequence of *GmJAG1* was cloned into the pMAL-c2X vector downstream and in-frame with the MBP coding sequence to generate the pMAL-*GmJAG1* vector. The pMAL-*Gmjag1*  vector was generated by a single-base mutation using the pMAL-*GmJAG1* vector as template by PCR amplification using a mutated primer, resulting in a D (aspartate)-to-H (histidine) switch in the EAR domain. After Sanger sequencing to confirm the sequences of the constructs, the vectors were transformed into Rosetta (DE3) *E. coli* competent cells. The recombinant protein was induced with 0.2 mM IPTG at 16 ◦C for overnight and purified by Amylose Magnetic Beads (NEB) according to the manufacturer's manual. 5′ -end biotinylated oligonucleotides containing the wild-type or mutated GmJAG1 novel motif were used as probes, while identical but unlabeled probes were used as competitors. The EMSA experiment was carried out using the EMSA/Gel Shift kit (Beyotime) and the chemiluminescent gel shift signal was detected by ChemiDoc Imaging Systems (Bio-Rad). Primers and oligonucleotide sequences were listed in Table S1.

#### **3. Results**

# *3.1. The binding preferences of GmJAG1 in the soybean genome*

To understand the TF regulatory networks in soybean, we selected GmJAG1 as the candidate TF and applied the ChIPmentation method using soybean mesophyll protoplasts to transiently express tagged GmJAG1 proteins. Determined empirically, this protocol only required about 106 transformed protoplasts (Fig. S1 and S2; see Materials and Methods). In total, 23.1 and 29.4 million (M) reads with high sequencing quality in two replicates were obtained for further analyses (Fig. S3, Table S2). We aligned these reads to the W82 genome (V4, [https://ph](https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov)  [ytozome.jgi.doe.gov\)](https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov) and identified a total of 39,409 GmJBSs common to both replicates, occupying ~2% of the genome ([Fig. 1,](#page-3-0) Table S3). GmJAG1 binding signal intensity coverage was enriched in the genic region, especially at the transcription start site (TSS) as well as the transcription end site (TES) compared to the random genomic control ([Fig. 2A](#page-4-0)). This observation was similar to the positioning of AtJAG1 binding sites in *Arabidopsis* [[32\]](#page-11-0).

In general, about 2000 to 4000 GmJBSs were found in each soybean chromosome [\(Fig. 2B](#page-4-0)). About 46.3% the GmJBSs occurred within two kilobases (kb) from the TSS of their nearest gene ([Fig. 2C](#page-4-0), Table S3). To describe the distribution of GmJBSs in the soybean genome, we first empirically partitioned the soybean genome into four categories: gene body (Gen), promoter (Pro), downstream (Dow) and distal intergenic region (Dis) ([Fig. 2](#page-4-0)D). For example, a GmJBS overlapping with a promoter region is referred to as a promoter GmJBS (ProGmJBS). We found that 65.8%, 41.6%, 28.8%, and 15.7% of GmJBSs could be labeled as genic GmJBSs (GenGmJBSs), ProGmJBSs, downstream GmJBSs (DowGmJBSs), and distal intergenic GmJBSs (DisGmJBSs), respectively ([Fig. 2](#page-4-0)E), with a similar distribution pattern across all the chromosomes ([Fig. 2F](#page-4-0)).

For gene association analysis, 13,986 genes harboring GmJBSs within the 2-kb promoter region were considered to be GmJAG1-targeted genes (JTGs) (Tables S4 and S5). Among these JTGs, we found that 2608 paralogous gene sets were co-targeted by GmJAG1, occupying about 42.8% (5987 out of 13,986) of the total JTGs [\(Fig. 1](#page-3-0) [yellow links], Table S6). This is probably due to the fact that soybean is a palaeopolyploid plant and a large number of paralogs have been retained in its genome [[33](#page-11-0),[34\]](#page-11-0).

# *3.2. Putative GmJAG1-TF regulatory network for soybean development*

Besides identifying the JTGs from the ChIP-seq data, by making use of the available soybean gene expression matrix from the Phytozome database (<https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov>), we also uncovered 2874 GmJAG1 co-expressed genes (JCGs) [\(Fig. 3A](#page-5-0) and B, Table S7). Furthermore, we found that 613 of the JCGs overlapped with JTGs ([Fig. 3B](#page-5-0)). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses of these 613 genes showed 31 significantly enriched GO terms ([Fig. 3](#page-5-0)C, Table S8), including those for transcription-related functions such as binding (GO:0005488),

#### *3.3. Identification of a novel GmJAG1-binding motif`*

The TF-motif interaction is one of the central events in a gene regulatory network [[1](#page-10-0)]. Although GmJAG1 might play a central role in a gene regulatory network as proposed above, the specific GmJAG1 binding motif was still unclear. Firstly, based on the known motifs in the soybean TF motif database (PlantTFDB, [http://planttfdb.cbi.pku.](http://planttfdb.cbi.pku.edu.cn/)  [edu.cn/\)](http://planttfdb.cbi.pku.edu.cn/), 102 TF-binding motifs out of 193 in the database were found to be enriched at GmJBSs (*p* value *<*0.001 and e value *<*0.001) (Table S11). These 99 TF-binding motifs belonged to 23 out of the 34 soybean TF families available in the database. The bHLH, ERF, C2H2, TCP, and bZIP families made up the five most-enriched TF families, which were normally distributed around the GmJAG1 peak center ([Fig. 4A](#page-6-0) and B). As GmJAG1 belongs to the C2H2 family, the C2H2 motif was automatically considered to be one of the GmJAG1-binding motifs. In order to identify potential novel motifs that were specifically recognized by GmJAG1, MEME, a motif-based sequence analysis software ([htt](http://meme-suite.org)  [p://meme-suite.org\)](http://meme-suite.org), was employed to predict the motifs from the

GmJBSs from our dataset de novo, which were then compared with PlantTFDB. As expected, several predicted motifs displayed high degrees of similarity (p value *<*1e-4) to the motifs in PlantTFDB, such as the bHLH-binding motif. A motif with a conserved core sequence of GTTGGA, and with low similarity to other TF motifs in the database, drew our attention [\(Fig. 4](#page-6-0)C). In addition, other tools such as Homer ([http://homer.ucsd.edu/homer/\)](http://homer.ucsd.edu/homer/) and ProSampler ([https://github.](https://github.com/zhengchangsulab/prosampler)  [com/zhengchangsulab/prosampler](https://github.com/zhengchangsulab/prosampler)) also predicted several motifs similar to GTTGGA (Fig. S4). In addition to GmJAG1 from soybean, AtJAG1 from *Arabidopsis* and Gmjag1, the single-amino acid-substitution variant of GmJAG1 in the EAR domain, were also able to recognize the GmJAG1 novel motif in the EMSA experiment (Figs. S5–S7). The recognition of Gmjag1 to this motif suggested that the mutation in EAR domain of GmJAG1 do not alter the DNA binding capacity so long as the C2H2 DNA binding domain remains intact. It is not surprising that AtJAG1 can also recognize the GmJAG1 novel motif, because *GmJAG1* was able to rescue the phenotype of the *Atjag1* mutant, indicating that the two proteins behave very similarly [[16\]](#page-11-0). Significant enrichment of the GmJAG1 novel motif (*p <* 1e-5) was

also observed at *Arabidopsis* JBSs (AtJBSs) [[32\]](#page-11-0) (Table S12), suggesting that the GmJAG1 novel motif is conserved and might be prevalent in those plant species that encode JAG1 homologs.

# *3.4. Chromatin accessibility and H3K27me3 marked distinct classes of GmJBS*

The epigenetic states of chromatin are usually closely associated with TF binding sites as an additional layer of gene regulation [[2](#page-10-0),[35\]](#page-11-0). To investigate the epigenetic states of chromatin at GmJBSs, such as chromatin accessibility and presence/absence of specific histone marks, we performed ATAC-seq and H3K27me3 ChIP-seq on soybean leaves (Table S2) to examine two common epigenetic marks at TF binding sites. We identified 29,619 accessible chromatin regions (ACRs) by ATAC-seq and 29,525 H3K27me3-enriched peak regions using ChIP-seq ([Fig. 5](#page-7-0)A, Tables S12 and S13). The ACRs can be further classified into GenACRs (for ACRs in the gene bodies; 46.1%), ProACRs (for ACRs in the promoter regions 2 kb upstream of TSS; 48.5%), DowACRs (for ACRs in the regions 2 kb downstream of TES; 21.2%), and DisACR (for ACRs in the distal intergenic regions; 27.3%), while 51.4%, 42.4%, 32.2% and 31.7% of H3K27me3-enriched peaks were found in the genic, promoter, downstream, and distal regions, respectively (Fig. S8).

By comparing the locations of these regions and histone marks to those of GmJBSs, 9945 GmJBSs were found to overlap with ATAC signals and 8895 were found to overlap with H3K27me3-enriched regions, which were referred to here as ATAC-marked GmJBSs (AGmJBSs) and H3K27me3-marked GmJBSs (HGmJBSs), respectively [\(Fig. 5A](#page-7-0)). According to previous reports, open chromatin regions located in distal regions were considered putative enhancers [35–[37\]](#page-11-0). #### **4. Discussion**

All this genomic information, such as the GmJAG1 target genes, the GmJAG1 novel motif, and the enhancer-like feature of DisGmJBSs, are valuable for the further understanding of the GmJAG1 transcriptional regulatory network in soybean. GmJAG1 protein containing an EAR motif in the N terminus was considered as a transcriptional repressor [[16,44\]](#page-11-0). Interestingly, from our GmJAG1 ChIP-seq data, we also observed that GmJAG1 might associate with AGmJBS to activate gene expression. This is not unique to GmJAG1. The potential capability of transcription activation by JAG1 protein was also observed in the *Arabidopsis* JAG1 ChIP-seq experiment [[32\]](#page-11-0). Given that the ChIP experiment was performed in vivo, one possibility is that the repressor function of the EAR motif in JAG1 is suppressed or counteracted by the other associated transcription factors, such as the TCP or MYB-related type of transcription factors. One potential molecular evidence in our study is that TCP and MYB-related motifs were enriched in the AGmJBS compared to the HGmJBS (Fig. S9).

Non-coding conserved motifs are an essential element of TF-motif regulatory modules [\[1\]](#page-10-0). According to the Human ENCODE project, the majority of functional non-coding DNA elements outside of the coding <span id="page-10-0"></span>region have been found to be associated with diseases such as cancer [[45,46](#page-11-0)]. The soybean genome has about one Giga bases [[33,34](#page-11-0)]. Recently, a pan-genome of wild and cultivated soybeans has been published, reporting about 26 high-quality versions of *de-novo* genome assembly [[47\]](#page-11-0). By a rough estimate, at least 60% of the genome is outside of the coding regions of protein-encoding genes, but it might be associated with gene regulation functions. A recent survey showed that distal regulator elements are prevalent in genomes, including that of soybean [[48\]](#page-11-0). Despite their potential importance, such non-coding sequences either in the core promoters or distal regulator regions have been rarely studied in soybean research. In soybean, the transcription network is governed by more than 4000 TFs (Table S4). Although about 10% of TFs have been predicted with at least one specific motif in the public database [\[27](#page-11-0)], the exact binding sites as well as any potential novel conserved binding motif/motifs were still poorly understood. This is largely due to the application limitation of ChIP-seq or its related methods in soybean. In this study, we successfully adapted the ChIPmentation method to soybean protoplasts and performed GmJAG1 ChIPseq. ChIPmentation requires low material input compared to the traditional ChIP-seq library preparation, such as the Tru-seq method that means a T-A ligation of a Y shape adapter for sequencing, and is therefore suitable for use with transformed soybean protoplasts [\[12](#page-11-0)]. From the GmJAG1 ChIP-seq data, we identified a novel GmJAG1 motif as well as several genomic features such as the enhancer-like DisGmJBSs. Hence, this new protocol would provide a more expedient approach for exploring TF binding preferences in the soybean genome and any associated discoveries in the downstream analyses of these noncoding regions. In addition to using protoplasts, this protocol could also potentially be adapted for use with hairy root transformation [[49\]](#page-11-0) or virus-mediated transient expression [\[50](#page-11-0)] that allows transient ectopic protein expressions in soybean. However, these applications will need further validation.

Extensive studies have revealed an important role of distal regulatory elements as enhancers in mammals and model plants [2[,36](#page-11-0)]. The prevalence and conservation of distal regulatory elements in most plant species have been elucidated in recent studies [[48\]](#page-11-0). Besides, a largescale analysis showed that most of the in vitro TF binding signals were enriched in the distal *cis*-regulatory elements in the maize genome [\[42](#page-11-0)]. TFs coupling with distal enhancers to regulate gene expressions has been reported in several studies in *Arabidopsis*. For example, plant nuclear transcription factor Y subunit C (NF-YC) binding to the distal CCAAT motif to regulate *Arabidopsis FLOWERING LOCUS T* expression to control flowering [\[51](#page-11-0)]. Mediator Subunit 25 (MED25) mediates the enhancerpromoter looping of jasmonic acid enhancers (JAEs) during JA responses [\[52](#page-11-0)]. In this study, we used ATAC-seq, H3K27me3 ChIP-seq as well as the publicly available LincRNA data to identify close to a hundred high confident distal enhancers which are rarely reported in the soybean genome. Furthermore, by integrating the above data with the GmJAG1 ChIP-seq results, we revealed a high likelihood that GmJAG1 may mediate long-range enhancer-promoter interactions as part of its gene regulation function. This was confirmed by 3C assay in two GmJAG1 target genes. These data suggested that TFs coupling with enhancers for gene regulation could be prevalent in soybean. However, to locate the precise enhancer-like elements as well as the enhancermediated DNA loops on a genome-wide scale in soybean, more highthroughput sequencing data would be necessary, such as in situ whole-genome High-throughput Chromosome Conformation Capture (Hi-C) [[41\]](#page-11-0) or its derivative, Hi-C followed by chromatin immunoprecipitation (HiChIP) [\[53](#page-11-0)], as well as Chromatin Interaction Analysis using Paired-End Tag sequencing (ChIA-PET) [[54\]](#page-11-0), which have yet to be applied to soybean research. Understanding the "all-vs-all" chromatin interactions in soybean via these technologies in combination with ChIP-seq data would be very useful for understanding the mechanisms of TF-mediated long-range gene regulation in soybean.

# **5. Conclusions**

In this study, we provided the data from GmJAG1 ChIP-seq coupled with ATAC-seq and H3K27me3 ChIP-seq to identify the target preferences of GmJAG1 on a genome-wide scale in soybean. The GmJAG1 target genes, the novel GmJAG1-binding motif as well as the enhancerlike feature of DisGmJBSs in soybean are valuable genomic information for further understanding the gene regulatory network and mechanisms of GmJAG1. By using GmJAG1 as an example, we have demonstrated that our optimized ChIPmentation protocol for soybean protoplasts will expedite the understanding of the transcriptional regulatory network in soybean.
