## **ABSTRACT**

Soybean aphid, is an important pest causing significant yield losses. The *Rag*2 locus confers resistance to soybean aphid biotypes 1 and 2. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses were done over a 48 h period after aphid infestation using near isogenic lines (NILs) differing at the *Rag*2 locus. Comparing the *Rag*2 and/or *rag*2 lines identified 3445 proteins with 396 differentially regulated between the two lines, including proteins involved in cell wall metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and stress response. RNAseq transcriptomic analysis identified 2361 genes significantly regulated between the resistant and susceptible lines. Genes up-regulated in the *Rag*2 line were annotated as involved in cell wall, secondary and hormone metabolism, as well as in stress, signaling and transcriptional responses. Genes down-regulated in the *Rag*2 line were annotated as involved in photosynthesis and carbon metabolism. Interestingly, two genes (unknown and mitochondrial protease) located within the defined *Rag*2 locus were expressed significantly higher in the resistant genotype. The expression of a putative NBS-LRR resistant gene within the *Rag*2 locus was not different between the two soybean lines, but a second NBL-LRR gene located just at the border of the defined *Rag*2 locus was. Therefore, this gene may be a candidate R gene controlling aphid resistance.

**Keywords:** Soybean, Aphid, Transcriptomic, Proteomic, NIL, Resistant, Susceptible

## **INTRODUCTION**

Soybean aphid (*Aphis glycines* Matsumura) is an insect belonging to the arthropod phylum which has recently become a very important pest of soybean in North America. The soybean aphid is an invasive species originating from eastern Asia and was first detected in the USA in 2000<sup>1</sup> . Aphids spread quickly and were detected in 30 different states by 2009<sup>1</sup> . This rapid spread is due to the ability of this insect to travel long distance in its winged form (alate) and the widespread presence in USA of its overwintering host *Rhamnus cathartica* (common Buckthorn)<sup>2</sup> . Eggs deposited on buckthorn hatch in March-April into wingless aphids (apteran) which then reproduce parthogenically for 2-3 generations. They differentiate into alate aphids which migrate in May-June from buckthorn to soybean, its main secondary host<sup>3</sup> . The high fecundity potential and the short development period of aphids enable rapid clonal multiplication on soybean (up to 15 generations) during the growing season. Under optimal conditions, the aphid population can double in 1.5 days. However, doubling time in the field usually takes longer and was estimated to be 6.8 days<sup>4</sup> . In autumn, aphids differentiate into winged males and females, which fly back to buckthorn where the females, after sexual reproduction, lay eggs that will hatch during the next spring season.

 The economic loss for the soybean industry due to the presence of aphid was estimated to be about 4 billion dollars annually<sup>3</sup> . Aphids use their stylet to reach, mainly intercellularly, the sieve element to ingest phloem sap. Although this type of feeding does not wound the plant as badly as chewing insects, the infestation of a single soybean plant by thousands of aphids can divert enough photosynthate to affect

soybean development. Furthermore, soybean aphids are also competent vectors to transmit Soybean or Alfalfa mosaic virus<sup>5</sup> . Aphids also secrete honeydew, which can promote fungal growth. The growth of saprophytic fungi on soybean leaves hinders stomatal function and prevents efficient light capture, reducing photosynthesis. Collectively, perhaps through a variety of mechanisms, aphid infestation can decrease soybean yield (up to 40%). Typical symptoms of aphid infestation include shorter plants with yellow leaves, with fewer pods containing fewer and smaller seeds with less oil than healthy soybeans<sup>6</sup> .

 Different management systems have been developed to control soybean aphid infestation. These include the use of insecticides, including pyrethroid and organophosphate types, with the recommendation that these be applied when the economic threshold of 250 aphids per plant is reached<sup>4</sup> . Although proper use of these insecticides can greatly reduce the damaging effects of aphids on soybean yield, this approach is costly (~33 \$/hectare), is detrimental for the environment, can lead to the development of insecticide resistant aphids and also adversely affects the population of insects that normally prey on aphids<sup>4</sup> . Biological control of aphids using predators is another method to control aphid multiplication and is based on the idea that aphids rarely reach the status of pest in Asia due to the presence of effective predators. Efficient predators of aphids present in USA include insects, such as the insidious flower bug (*Orius insidiosus*) or those belonging to the Coccinellidae (lady beetle) family<sup>1</sup> .

 The use of soybean lines naturally resistant to aphids is another management approach to control soybean aphids. Resistance mechanisms to insects are usually

classified in 3 categories including antibiosis, antixenosis and tolerance, depending on how plants can modify the development and behavior of the insect. Antibiosis resistance affects the biology of the insect (increased mortality, reduced fecundity) while antixenosis resistance affects the behavior of the insect (non-preference). Tolerance is another process when the plant is able to withstand the presence of the insect without significant impact on the host biology or behavior. Past surveys of the soybean germplasm collection identified soybean lines that exhibited resistance to *Aphis glycines*. Genetic analysis of these lines identified five resistance (*Rag, resistance to Aphis glycines)* loci, including *Rag*1, *Rag*2, *Rag*3, *Rag*4 and *Rag*5 . Of these, *Rag*1 and *Rag*2 are the best characterized. The single dominant locus, *Rag*2, providing resistance to biotypes (see below) 1 and 2 (based on soybean cultivar resistance patterns) of the soybean aphid, was mapped on soybean chromosome 13 from Plant Introduction (PI) 243540. Further genetic mapping of the *Rag*1 and *Rag*2 loci identified a 115 kb interval on chromosome 7 and a 54 kb interval on chromosome 13, respectively 7,8. Both intervals contain Nucleotide-Binding Site Leucine-Rich Repeat (NBS-LRR) genes (2 for *Rag*1 and 1 for *Rag*2), which were proposed as good candidates for aphid resistance genes. Indeed, the tomato *Mi1.2* and melon *Vat* genes, which encode NBS-LRR proteins, were previously shown to confer aphid resistance 9,10. However, at present, the molecular basis for aphid resistance in soybean conferred by the five *Rag* loci remains undefined. The first commercial soybean aphid resistant cultivar, containing the *Rag*1 gene, was released in 2010<sup>11</sup>. However, subsequently, aphids were identified that could overcome *Rag*1 resistance and were termed Biotype 2<sup>8</sup> . Likewise, aphids of biotype 3, able to develop on *Rag*1 and *Rag*2 plants, were described<sup>12</sup>. The appearance of these

aphid populations that overcome *Rag*1 and *Rag*2 resistance underlines the importance of further research to define the molecular basis for soybean aphid resistance with the ultimate goal of breeding plants with durable aphid resistance.

 Previous studies utilized proteomic analysis of the saliva of diverse aphids (i.e., isolated from pea, Russian wheat, green peach aphids) with the ultimate goal of identifying effector proteins that are injected into the plant to enhance susceptibility13-16 . Presumably it is these effector proteins that are recognized by the plant to induce resistance. Protein accumulation and gene expression were also analyzed to determine the effect of photoperiod on the reproductive mode of the pea aphid<sup>17</sup>, for which a genomic sequence is available<sup>18</sup>. A number of studies have also examined plant gene expression in response to aphid infection, including studies involving soybean/ soybean aphid19,20 , , Arabidopsis/green peach aphids22-24, Arabidopsis/cabbage aphids 25,26,cabbage/cabbage aphids <sup>27</sup>, mustard/cabbage aphids<sup>28</sup>, celery/green peach aphids , sorghum/greenbug aphids<sup>22</sup>, tobacco/tobacco aphid<sup>22</sup>, apple/rosy apple aphids<sup>22</sup> , barley/bird cherry oat aphids<sup>29</sup>, wheat/wheat aphids<sup>30</sup> or barley/Russian wheat aphids<sup>31</sup> . These studies resulted in the identification of hundreds of aphid responsive genes, which are mainly involved in photosynthesis, cell wall modification, oxidative stress, hormonal regulation, signaling or transcriptional regulation. However, in contrast to these numerous transcriptome studies, only a single publication, using wheat and cereal aphids <sup>32</sup>, utilized proteomic approaches to examine the plant response to aphid infection.

 In this study, we used both transcriptomic and proteomic approaches to examine the soybean response to aphid infestation over a 48 hour time course. In order to define

those responses specifically associated with resistance, we compared two soybean near isogenic lines (NILs) that differed at the *Rag*2 locus. The data obtained reveal a significant number of mRNA and protein changes between these two lines, including the differential expression of genes located in or near the *Rag*2 locus.

## **EXPERIMENTAL SECTION**

Procedures and Reagents:

#### *Development of NILs*

The homozygous aphid resistant (*Rag*2/*Rag*2) and aphid susceptible (*rag*2/*rag*2) NILs were developed by backcrossing the *Rag*2 gene from its original source, PI 243540, into soybean aphid susceptible (*rag*2) cultivar Wyandot (the recurrent parent). Wyandot is an F4-derived selection from a three-parent cross (Northrup King S29-18 × PI 274421) × Ohio FG1. Ohio FG1 was released by The Ohio State University and Ohio Agricultural Research and Development Center (OARDC) as a food-grade soybean<sup>33</sup>. A total of four backcrosses were used to develop the BC4F1 seeds. The F1 seedlings developed from the first cross and each backcross generation were screened for resistance to the soybean aphid biotype 2 in the greenhouse according to Mian et al.<sup>34</sup>. A BC4F2 plant segregating for resistance to the soybean aphid was identified by marker assisted selection and seeds from homozygous resistant (*Rag*2/*Rag*2) and homozygous susceptible (*rag*2/*rag*2) BC4F2:3 plants originating from the segregating BC4F2 plant were harvested as NILs.

*Soybean tissue collection*

The seedlings of the NILs were grown in an environment controlled greenhouse. The experimental design was a randomized complete block with four replications. For each NIL, four seeds per replicate for each of the four sampling times (0, 8, 24, 48 hrs after aphid infestation) were planted in 4-cm diameter x 15-cm deep plastic conetainers in a USDA greenhouse at Ohio Agricultural Research and Development Center (OARDC), Wooster, OH and the best two seedlings per conetainer were kept after germination. The conetainers were arranged in a rack with 8-cm x 12-cm spacing on a greenhouse bench. The greenhouse was maintained at approximately 26/22˚C day/night temperatures with 15 h light daily. The biotype 2 soybean aphids used in this study was from a growth chamber colony maintained at OARDC, Wooster, OH<sup>34</sup>. At the V2 growth stage, each soybean seedling was manually infested with 15 adult soybean aphids by placing the aphids on the top leaves of the seedling. For the 0 hr samples, just before infestation of seedlings with soybean aphids, the youngest unfolded leaves and the tips of two seedlings representing each replicate of each NIL were collected in a 2 ml tube and were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The samples for 8, 24, and 48 h after infestation, were collected in the same manner, except that the aphids were gently removed from the tissue by using a soft paint brush before tissue collection. The aphids continued to walk away from the resistant seedlings within 12 to 24 hours of placement on the seedlings, so more aphids were added to these seedlings every 12 hrs to have at least 15 aphids per seedling at all times.

*Protein and RNA extraction* 

Proteins and RNA were extracted from soybean leaves using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen; Carlsbad, CA) according to the manufacturer instruction with the following modifications. Trizol was supplemented with protease inhibitors (Sigma tablet, St Louis, MO, S8820) at a concentration recommended by the provider, before being added to the homogenized frozen tissue. After precipitation of the proteins with isopropanol (0.8 vol per vol of Trizol), pellets were homogenized using a tissue grind pestle (Kontes glass company; Bridgestone, NJ) before being washed 3 times with 0.3 M guanidine hydrochloride in 95% Ethanol. After the final wash, proteins were stored at -80ºC in 100% ethanol until quantification. Protein abundance was estimated using the Quick Start Bradford Dye Reagent and BSA from Bio-Rad (Hercules, CA).

 Once the last step of the manufacturer's instructions was reached for RNA isolation, RNA were further purified by adding 0.1 vol of sodium acetate (3M) and 1 vol of chloroform. After a centrifugation at 10000 g for 10 min at 4ºC, RNA present into the aqueous phase were retrieved and precipitated overnight at -20 ºC with 2.5 vol ethanol. The RNA were then washed with 75% ethanol, centrifuged and resuspended in DEPC water. Quantity and integrity of RNA were estimated for each extraction using a NanoDrop (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE) and by visual examination after electrophoresis in a 1.2% agarose gel, respectively.

## *Sample Preparation for MS analysis*

Fifty µg of total proteins were dissolved into Laemmli buffer, denaturated for 5 min in boiling water and placed on ice. Proteins were then separated by 1D SDS-PAGE using a 10% acrylamide running gel (5 cm) and a 5% acrylamide stacking gel (1.5 cm) until

the bromophenol blue reach the bottom of the gel. No sample was loaded into two consecutive wells to avoid protein contamination. Gels were stained with Colloidal Coomassie blue. Each gel lane corresponding to one sample were excised and cut into 12 bands of about 0.4 mm. Each band of the gel was minced into about 1 mm<sup>3</sup> cube and digested with trypsin as described in <sup>35</sup> with the following modification: gel plugs were reduced for 30 min at 56ºC with 10 mM DTT in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate and alkylated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark with 50 mM iodoacetamide in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate before being rehydrated with 100 µl of trypsin solution (0.02 µg/µl in 50mM ammonium bicarbonate, 10% acetonitrile; Promega, Madison, WI). After trypsin digestion, tryptic peptides were collected as described in<sup>35</sup>, lyophilized and reconstituded in 10 µl of 1% formic acid. Peptides were centrifuged at 10000 g for 5 min at 4ºC and 8 µl were transferred into MS vial (200046; Sun-SRi,Rockwood, TN) which were stored at 4ºC in the autosampler right before LC MS/MS analysis.

#### *LC MS/MS analysis, protein identification and quantification*

Tryptic peptides were analyzed on an LTQ Orbitrap XL mass spectrometer operated with Xcalibur (version 2.0.7) and coupled to a nanoflow Proxeon-EasynLC system (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA). Tryptic peptides (5µl) were loaded onto a C18 peptide Cap Trap (Michrom Bioresources, Auburn, CA) at a flow of 20 µl/min and a maximum pressure of 200 bars. Peptides were eluted using an 80 min linear gradient from 5 to 45% of solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 0.4 µl/min. and separated on fused silica analytical column (150 µM ID x 100 mm) packed with C18 (5 µM, 100 Å; Michrom Bioresources). Solvent A was 0.1% formic acid in MilliQ water.

Peptides were then electrosprayed using 1.7 kV voltage into the ion transfer tube (180ºC) of the LTQ Orbitrap operating in positive mode. The Orbitrap first performed a full MS scan at a resolution of 30000 FWHM to detect the precursor ion having a m/z comprised between 300 and 2000 and a +2 or higher charge. CID (35% normalized collision energy) was used to fragment the nine most intense precursor ion (1000 counts minimum; parent mass width: plus or minus 0.5 Da) of each full scan which were analyzed by the LTQ linear trap. Exclusion list (mass window: plus or minus 10 ppm) included most common tryptic ions having the following m/z: 421.76, 523.29, 737.71, 761.73, 1106.06, 1142.10, 1150.09. Xcalibur raw data files were imported into Sorcerer (version Version 3.5, Build 4.0.4; Sage-N research, Milipitas, CA) which extracted the peaks and used a Sequest algorithm (version 27) to match the MS and MS/MS spectra to protein sequences<sup>36</sup>. The database (Glyma 1.0; February 2011) used in this study contains 75778 soybean protein sequences deduced from the soybean genomic sequence and was concatenated with the addition of a decoy sequence, generated by decoy DB creator (www.p3db.org), for each soybean protein to estimate the false discovery rate. Search parameters for tryptic peptides included carbamidomethylation of cysteine and oxidation of methionine as fixed and variable medications, respectively. One miscleavage for trypsin was allowed. Mass tolerances were set at 10 ppm and 1.0 Da for the precursor ion and fragmented ions, respectively. The 12 Xcalibur raw files (12 gel bands) corresponding to proteins extracted from one sample were searched at the same time using Sorcerer to identify at once all the proteins present in a single sample. The results of the different searches were further analyzed using Scaffold (version 3.4.9; Proteome software, Portland, OR) to validate MS and MS/MS data. Only proteins

**Page 13 of 38**

identified with at least 2 peptides and a confidence higher than 99% were kept for further analysis. Protein Prophet algorithm was used to assign protein probability<sup>37</sup> . Peptide Prophet algorithm was used to filter out peptides with mass error higher than 10 ppm<sup>38</sup>. Proteins that contained similar peptides and could not be differentiated based on MS/MS analysis alone were grouped to satisfy the principles of parsimony. No proteins coming from the decoy database were identified using the search parameter described above. Protein abundance was determined by mass spectral counting and normalized in Scaffold using the quantitative value function. Statistical analysis (t-test) was performed to identify proteins regulated between *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines in response to aphid infestation or not. The cutoff applied to consider that a protein was significantly regulated were a p-Value<0.05 and a fold change ratio >2 or <0.5 between 2 samples.

#### *RNAseq; sample preparation and data analysis*

Complementary DNA libraries were built according to the manusfacturer's instruction (mRNA sequencing, Sample preparation guide; Illumina, San Diego, CA) using 10 µg of RNA extracted from *Rag*2 and *rag*2 leaves 0, 4h, 8h, 24h and 48h after aphid infestation. Two biological replicates per time point were performed. Each library was labeled with a 4 nucleotide specific barcode ACGT, CGTT, GTAT and sequenced using a HiSeq 2000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA). The 20 different samples were sequenced using 7 lanes of a single flow cell. Fifty base pair single read information was collected for each library. The HiSeq 2000 generated files in FASTQ format storing both sequences and base quality scores. Reads with low Phred score (<26) were removed. The filtered reads were then mapped to the reference genome of *Glycine max*<sup>39</sup> using

TopHat and its default parameter <sup>40</sup>. Reads mapping to more than one gene were excluded. mRNA abundance and differential gene expression were calculated using 2 different pipelines. The first pipeline employed HTSeq (http://wwwhuber.embl.de/users/anders/HTSeq/) to compute the read counts for each gene and edgeR to perform the statistical analysis<sup>41</sup>. The second pipeline used Cufflinks to obtain fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM) and Cuffdiff to carry out statistical analysis<sup>42</sup>. A gene was considered significantly regulated between 2 conditions when its relative fold change was higher than 2 or below 0.5 and its false discovery rate (FDR) was below 0.05 with both pipelines.

#### *Real time PCR*

RNA was extracted from 4 biological replicates of *Rag*2 and *rag*2 leaves using the same time points utilized for RNAseq were treated with Turbo DNase (Ambion, Austin, TX) according to the manufacturer's recommendations. cDNA synthesis, real time PCR and PCR product quantification were performed as described in<sup>43</sup> except for the use of *cons4* and *cons6* genes to normalize the gene expression data<sup>44</sup>. Primers were defined using Primer-BLAST<sup>45</sup> , *Glycine max* as a specificity check, an amplification size comprised between 80 to 115 bp and an optimum melting temperature of 60ºC. Primers used are summarized in Supplemental Table S1. A gene was considered significantly regulated by real time PCR when its relative fold change was higher than 2 or below 0.5 in at least 3 out of the 4 biological replicates and its p-Value (t-test) was below 0.1 when comparing *Rag*2 vs *rag*2.

## *Mercator, Mapman and metabolomic pathways*

Genes and proteins were classified into different functional categories (BIN number) using Mercator available at: http://mapman.gabipd.org/web/guest/app/mercator. These BIN numbers were then used to map all the genes and proteins onto different metabolomic pathways using Mapman (version 3.5.1)<sup>46</sup>. In order to discover metabolomic pathways differentially regulated between the *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines in response to aphid or no aphid, over represented BINs were identified using a Wilcoxon's test p-Value<0.05) available in Mapman. The genes and proteins of interest were further annotated by retrieving their KOG, PANTHER and PFAM domain prediction available at http://www.phytozome.net/ and performing a BLAST search.

## **RESULTS AND DISCUSSION**

*Proteomic differences between resistant and susceptible soybean NILs in response to aphid infestation* 

Changes in protein abundance between resistant (*Rag*2) and susceptible (*rag*2) NILs were analyzed for 4 different time points including 0h (no aphids), 8h, 24h and 48h after aphid infestation. Four biological replicates were performed for the different time points that were selected in order to analyze both early (8h) and later soybean responses (24h, 48h) to aphid infestation. The 8h time point was chosen since it was previously shown that aphids start feeding 7.5h after application on resistant soybeans<sup>47</sup>, which were described to affect aphid behavior within the same time frame (8h)<sup>48</sup>. Later time points (24h; 48h) were selected to study the proteomic changes in *Rag*2 and *rag*2 NILs on which aphids had been feeding longer (16 and 40h, respectively). The antibiosis

effect of *Rag2* plants on aphid numbers was compensated by adding 5-10 aphids on each resistant plant every 12 h in order to maintain a similar number of aphids on *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines and, therefore, compare molecular responses between the resistant and susceptible plants infested by similar aphid density.

 Proteins extracted from *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines were separated by 1Dimension-SDS-PAGE, which was cut into 12 bands for each sample. Each gel band was individually digested with trypsin and the complexity of the sample was further reduced by separating the tryptic peptides on a C18 reverse phase column. Peptide identifications and abundances were determined using an LTQ-Orbitrap and Sorcerer search engine. The proteins identified in *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines for each biological replicate are listed in Supplemental Table S2, S3, S4 and S5 for the 0h, 8h, 24h and 48h, respectively. Supplemental Table S2 to S5 also summarizes information on peptide (sequence match, SEQUEST XCorr score, peptide identification probability, charge) and protein identifications (accession number, identification probability, sequence coverage). A protein was considered present in a sample when it was detected in at least 3 out of the 4 biological replicates. The number of proteins detected in *Rag*2 or *rag*2 lines for each time point is summarized in Table I. An average of 2261 proteins was detected in the different samples with at least 2 peptides by 1D-SDS-PAGE-LC/MS/MS, starting with 50 µg of total proteins (Table I). A total of 3445 proteins were identified in *Rag*2 or *rag*2 lines in at least 1 time point (Supplementary Table S6). Their mass spectral counts in each line and time point are summarized in Supplementary Table S6. Annotation of these proteins based on PFAM, KOG and Panther prediction software are indicated as well. The resistance of soybean to aphids is probably due to the accumulation of proteins involved in stress response since 86% of the proteins belonging to this category were found more abundant in the *Rag*2 line, particularly 8h after aphid infestation. A PR10 (ribonuclease activity annotation), 2 splicing factors, 4 RNA binding proteins, a bromodomain transcription factor were less abundant in the resistant line than in the susceptible line. In contrast, the abundance of an Rpb5 RNA polymerase, the nucleolar protein NOP56, a fibrillarin, a spliceosomal proteins, an RNA binding protein, another PR10 and an HMG box related protein were found more abundant in the resistant line than in the susceptible one, suggesting that complex mechanisms controlling RNA levels are involved in the process of aphid resistance (Supplementary Table S7). Another important level of regulation implied in the control of the plant response to the aphids is signal transduction since the abundance of different kinases and G proteins were differentially affected between *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines following aphid infestation. Nine proteins from this functional category including 3 kinases (a receptor-like kinase, a S-locus lectin protein kinase, a serine/threonine protein kinase), 2 ADP ribosylation factors, 2 small GTP binding proteins, a guanine nucleotide-binding protein subunit beta-like and a calreticulin involved in sensing calcium level were found more abundant in the resistant line from 8h to 24h after aphid infestation. Only one protein (LRR serine/threonine protein kinase) from this category was found more abundant in the susceptible line 48h after the addition of aphids (Supplementary Table S7).

*Changes in mRNA abundance in aphid resistant and susceptible NILs in response to aphid infestation* 

The transcriptome of the *Rag*2 and *rag*2 leaves infested (4h, 8h, 24h and 48h) or not (0h) with aphids was analyzed using a RNAseq approach and two biological replicates of the same tissues that were used for the proteomic study. Two different pipelines, HTseq and Cufflinks, described in detail in the experimental procedures section, were used to analyze the high throughput sequencing data. Only genes detected in both pipelines were included into the statistical analysis aiming at identifying genes differentially regulated between the *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines in response to aphid infestation. An average of 42455 (±529) genes identified from 26.7 (± 2.69) million reads having a Phred score higher than 26 were detected in the resistant and susceptible soybean lines (Table I). The number of reads as well as the FPKM values matching the predicted soybean genes and their splicing variants are summarized in Supplementary Table S8 and were obtained with the HTSeq and Cufflinks pipeline, respectively. The expression of 46373 and 46494 genes was detected in at least one time point in *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines, respectively. The expression of most of the genes was detected at alltime points (*Rag*2: 38839 genes; *rag*2: 38587 genes) (Supplementary Table S8). Statistical analysis was performed to identify genes differentially regulated between *rag*2 and *Rag*2 lines at different time points after aphid infestation. A total of 2361 genes with a relative fold change higher than 2 or below 0.5 and a FDR below 0.05 (adjusted P value for HTSeq and q value for Cufflinks) with both pipelines were found regulated between the *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines and are summarized in Supplementary Table S9.

 The validity of the RNAseq data and its analysis using the HTSeq and Cufflinks pipelines was estimated by real time PCR. The genes for the qRT-PCR analysis were selected based on their response to aphid infestation (judged by RNAseq) and their predicted functions. The expression of 38 genes (including the 7 *Rag*2 genes) was quantified in *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines at 0h, 4h, 8h, 24h and 48h after aphid infestation by qRT-PCR using the same RNA used for the high throughput sequencing, as well as RNA from two, additional independent biological replicates. RNAseq and qRT-PCR results were compared by checking the expression level of the genes found significantly regulated between the *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines at 5 different time points (Supplementary Table S10). The percentage of correlation between the 2 approaches was 76.3%, 55.3%, 73.7%, 73.7% and 81.6% for the 0h, 4h, 8h, 24h and 48h time points, respectively. Furthermore, 89.5% of the RNAseq data was confirmed by qRT-PCR if one compares one time point to the other 5 (Supplementary Table S10). These data can be explained by the fact that the kinetics of the soybean response to aphid infestation differed slightly for identical time points, particularly for the earlier ones, and therefore support the idea of an increased correlation percentage from 4h to 48h after aphid infestation. Interestingly, the expression of Argonaute-4 like protein gene was found at least 10 times more abundant by both RNAseq and qPCR approaches in the *Rag*2 line in comparison to *rag*2 line from 0 to 48 h after infestation, suggesting the post-transcriptional gene silencing could play a critical role in aphid resistance (Fig 3A; Supplementary Table S9; S10).

 Likewise, both the RNAseq and qPCR data showed the up-regulation of a WRKY transcription factor (Glyma14g11960.1) from 4 to 48 hours and a Ser/Thr protein kinase

(Glyma06g40520.1) from 0 to 48 hours in the soybean line resistant to aphids in comparison the susceptible line (Fig 3A; Supplementary Table S9; S10). These 2 genes may be components of a signaling pathway leading to the activation of the plant defense reaction which can control the aphid proliferation. Indeed, a variety of WRKY transcription factors have been implicated in controlling the plant response to both biotic and abiotic stress<sup>49</sup> <sup>50</sup> .

 Previous work made note of the presence of NBS-LRR genes near the mapped *Rag*2 locus9,10. It was suggested that the NBS-LRR gene located near the *Rag*2 locus may be responsible for soybean aphid resistance<sup>8</sup> . Interestingly, the expression of this gene was not found significantly different by RNAseq and qPCR between the *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines (Fig 3B; Supplementary Table S9; S10), indicating that this gene may not be the resistance gene or, at least, its expression does not respond to infestation. The only genes within the *Rag2* locus found regulated by RNAseq and qPCR at many time points after aphid infestation between the resistant and susceptible lines are a gene of unknown function (Glyma13g25990) and a mitochondrial protease (Glyma13g26010) (Fig 3B; Supplementary Table S9; S10). More studies are required to define the role of these 2 genes in the mechanism of aphid resistance. However, it is interesting to note that a NBS-LRR gene (Glyma13g25970) located very near the left border of the mapped *Rag2* locus was induced approximately 5-fold, as measured by both RNAseq and qPCR, from 0 to 48 hours in the *Rag*2 line in comparison to *rag*2*.* This NBS-LRR gene (Glyma13g25970) appears to map outside of the window of the *Rag*2 locus defined by using PI 200538 as the source of *Rag*2 allele by researchers at Univ. of Illinois in Urbana-Champaign<sup>8</sup> .  The expression of 124 genes was differentially regulated in the *Rag*2 genotype compared to the *rag*2 susceptible line in the absence of aphid inoculation (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Table S9). The addition of aphids affected the expression of an additional 2237 genes between the *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines (Fig.1B; Supplementary Table S9). Interestingly, the gene and protein expression profile present a biphasic response in term of number of genes and proteins regulated at different time points after aphid infestation. The primary maximum response occurs 8 h after aphid infestation and is followed by a strong decrease in the number of genes and proteins regulated between the resistant and susceptible lines (Fig. 1). The secondary response occurring 48h after aphid infestation could be part of the plant defense mechanism leading to the resistance of soybean to aphids. A similar, biphasic plant response was noted previously in a transcriptomic study of the response of soybean to infection with the soybean Asian rust

pathogen <sup>51</sup> and was suggested to reflect the plant response to the *in planta* growth of the fungal pathogen.

 The genes differentially regulated between the *Rag*2 and *rag*2 lines are involved in many biological processes (Fig. 2B) which are described below. Sixty one genes involved in the photosystem (PS) category were found significantly regulated between the resistant and susceptible lines only after the addition of aphids (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S9). Most (92%) were induced in the susceptible *rag2* line suggesting that the aphids strongly affect the photosystem mechanism of susceptible soybean, which explains, at least in part, the negative effects of aphid infestation on soybean yield. Examples of photosynthesis genes found induced in the *rag*2 line include chlorophyll a-b binding proteins, genes involved in the photosystem function, cytochrome b6, ferredoxins, and genes involved in the Calvin cycle (phosphoribulokinase, ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase, phosphoglycerate kinase, fructose-bisphosphate aldolase, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase). Aphids can induce symptom of yellow and wilted leaves in susceptible plants. These symptoms might be explained by the destruction of the plant photosystem, which the plant continually tries to replace by inducing the photosynthetic genes but at an insufficient rate to be able to survive. Likewise, aphid infestation also strongly affects genes involved in the carbon (TCA / org) transformation category since 6 out of 7 genes (carbonic anhydrase, malic enzyme) from this category were induced in susceptible *rag2* soybean only in the presence of aphids (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S9).

## **CONCLUSIONS:**

The data presented compare the proteomic and transcriptomic responses of soybean NILs differing only at the *Rag*2 locus, which confers soybean aphid resistance. As expected, the resistant and susceptible soybean lines differed significantly in their response to aphid infestation. For example, the resistant line showed a significant upregulation of proteins and mRNA involved in biotic stress responses. Likewise, photosynthetic genes were strongly up-regulated in the susceptible lines, perhaps explaining, in part, the significant impact of aphids on overall plant yield, which cannot replace its photosystem as quickly as the aphids disrupt photosynthesis. Perhaps most notable, two genes (i.e., a gene of unknown function, Glyma13g25990, and a mitochondrial protease, Glyma13g26010) mapping within the *Rag*2 locus were significantly upregulated by aphid infestation. However, expression of the NBS-LRR gene mapping within the *Rag*2 locus and previously suggested as the determining factor in aphid resistance was not affected by aphid infestation. However, expression of a second NBS-LRR (Glyma13g25970) gene, which we found does map within the defined *Rag*2 locus derived from PI 243540, was significant upregulated in the presence

of aphids. Therefore, this gene may be an excellent candidate for defining aphid resistance conferred by the *Rag*2 locus.
