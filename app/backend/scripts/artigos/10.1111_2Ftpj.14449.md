#### Abstract

WRKY transcription factors play important roles in response to various abiotic stresses. Previous study proved that soybean GmWRKY54 can improve stress tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis. Here, we generated soybean transgenic plants and further investigated roles and biological mechanisms of GmWRKY54 in response to drought stress. We demonstrate that expression of GmWRKY54, driven by either a constitutive promoter (pCm) or a drought-induced promoter (RD29a), confers drought tolerance. GmWRKY54 is a transcriptional activator and affects a large number of stress-related genes as revealed by RNA-sequencing. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment and co-expression network analysis, together with measurement of physiological parameters, support that GmWRKY54 enhances stomatal closure to reduce water loss, therefore confers drought tolerance in soybean. GmWRKY54 directly binds to the promoter regions of genes including PYL8, SRK2A, CIPK11 and CPK3 and activates them. Thus GmWRKY54 achieves its function through ABA and  $Ca^{2+}$  signaling pathways. It is valuable that GmWRKY54 activates an ABA receptor and a SnRK2 kinase in the upstream position, unlike other WRKY proteins regulating downstream genes in ABA pathway. Our study reveals the role of GmWRKY54 in drought tolerance and further manipulation of this gene should improve growth and production in soybean and other legumes/crops under unfavorable conditions.

#### Introduction

Soybean is an important source of edible oil and protein. The soybean growing regions and production are limited due to the deficiencies in water supply. Water depletion is a complicated interplay of several factors, such as low soil moisture and extreme temperatures. In the coming age, we are facing a serious problem of a warmer earth, which could increase water content in the atmosphere but reduce moisture in soil, followed by high frequency of drought stress (Prudhomme *et al.*, 2014).

When persistently exposed to drought stress, plants suffer from multiple impairments, such as oxidative injury, membrane system damage, cellular ion leakage and protein denaturation (Joshi et al., 2016). During drought stress, the photosynthesis rate of a plant declines, CO<sub>2</sub> uptake decreases, biomass accumulation and yield are negatively affected (Chaves et al., 2009; Harb and Pereira, 2011; Pinheiro and Chaves, 2011). To adapt to drought stress, plants have evolved an integrated strategy, including signal perception and transduction, regulation of gene expression and biochemical and physiological responses (Huang et al., 2012). An effective and direct strategy to endure drought stress is to reduce water loss through closing stomata. The stomatal aperture is modulated by multiple factors, including environmental signals, biotic/abiotic stress, CO<sub>2</sub> concentration, light and plant hormones (Nilson and Assmann, 2007). Several hormones are involved in stomatal regulation, among which the stress hormone abscisic acid (ABA) plays the main role (Acharya and Assmann, 2009). In the process of ABA-mediated stomatal closure, ABA triggers cascaded activation of SnRK2.6/OST1 and the S-type anion channel SLAC1 (Lind *et al.*, 2015). The activated anion channels release anion, which is accompanied by the activated  $K^+$  efflux channels releasing K<sup>+</sup>. Therefore, both anion and cation are released out of the cell and thus reduce turgor of guard cells, which leads to stomatal closure (Roelfsema et al., 2012). Cooperating with ABA, Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling pathway is also involved in the regulation of stomatal aperture. Ca<sup>2+</sup>-mediated signaling cascade consists of two kinase systems, Ca<sup>2+</sup>-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs) and CBL (Calcineurin B like protein) interacting protein kinases (CIPKs). Both kinase systems could modulate the anion channels SLAC1 (Edel and Kudla, 2016).

During the signal transduction and adaptive response, the expression of a large number of drought responsive genes was changed. Drought responsive genes consist of effector genes This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

encoding chaperones, enzymes and ion/water channels etc, and regulatory genes encoding plenty of transcription factors (TFs). Several groups of TFs, such as AREB, DREB, NAC, MYB, bZIP and WRKY, respond to drought stress and act in a ABA-dependent or -independent manner (Joshi et al., 2016). The WRKY TFs are named based on their WRKY domains. The WRKY domain has about 60 amino acids in length and has a conserved heptapeptide motif (WRKYGQK) (Eulgem et al., 2000). WRKY TFs specifically bind to the *cis*-element called W-box (TTGAC[C/T]) and regulate expression of downstream genes (Ciolkowski et al., 2008). WRKY TFs from various plants played important roles in biotic, abiotic stress response and ABA signaling pathway. In Arabidopsis, AtWRKY11 and AtWRKY17 negatively affect resistance to Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato (Pst); AtWRKY40 is a negative regulator of ABA signaling; AtWRKY57 and AtWRKY63 play positive roles in drought tolerance (Journot-Catalino et al., 2006; Ren et al., 2010; Jiang et al., 2012). In rice, overexpression of OsWRKY11 or OsWRKY45 could enhance drought tolerance of transgenic plants (Qiu and Yu, 2009, Wu et al., 2009). Heterologous expression of wheat WRKY genes, TaWRKY2, TaWRKY10 or TaWRKY19, in Arabidopsis or tobacco confers drought or osmotic stress tolerance in transgenic plants (Niu et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2013).

In soybean genome, there are about 150~180 WRKY protein genes (Yin *et al.*, 2013; Bencke-Malato *et al.*, 2014; Song *et al.*, 2016). Before soybean whole genome was sequenced, we have identified 64 *WRKY* genes from soybean unigenes. Among these, *GmWRKY54*-transgenic Arabidopsis plants are tolerant to salt and drought stress. In contrast, *GmWRKY13*-transgenic Arabidopsis plants show sensitivity to salt and mannitol stress (Zhou *et al.*, 2008). Using a soybean transgenic hairy root system, we further found that GmWRKY27 associated with GmMYB174 to bind to the neighboring cis-elements for inhibition of a negative regulatory gene *GmNAC29* and finally improved stress tolerance (Wang *et al.*, 2015). Considering that the heterogeneous transgenic Arabidopsis plants may not fully reflect the native gene functions, we further characterized the biological functions of GmWRKY54 in transgenic soybean plants and found that GmWRKY54 increased drought tolerance in soybean plants. The GmWRKY54 may reach this function through activation of ABA and Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling pathways and closing of stomata.

#### Results

#### **GmWRKY54** protein has transcriptional activation ability

GmWRKY54 belongs to group two of WRKY proteins (one WRKY domain) (Fig. 1a). We used a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System in *Arabidopsis* protoplasts to examine the GmWRKY54 activity. The reporter (firefly *LUC* gene), internal control (pTRL) and the effectors (pRT-BD-GmWRKY54) were co-transfected into protoplasts for testing activation of LUC activity. The plasmid of pRT-BD-VP16 was used as positive control. We found that the relative LUC activity of BD-GmWRKY54 was about three-fold higher than that of the negative control (BD), indicating that GmWRKY54 has transcriptional activation activity (Fig. 1a).

#### GmWRKY54 confers drought tolerance in transgenic soybean plants

*GmWRKY54* is expressed in all soybean organs, while flowers and roots have higher expression than other organs (Fig. 1b). To investigate the functions of GmWRKY54 in soybean, we generated transgenic soybean plants overexpressing *GmWRKY54*. The gene was driven by a constitutive promoter of cestrum yellow leaf curling virus (p*Cm*) (Stavolone *et al.*, 2003). Transgenic lines with single T-DNA insertion were adopted. Null plants (NPs), which were segregated from each T1 transgenic plants (TPs), were used as corresponding controls. T3 homozygous TPs and NPs were grown in well-watered condition to observe growth status. Generally speaking, p*Cm-GmWRKY54* lines have similar size as NPs (Fig. S1).

Under well-watered condition, the *pCm-GmWRKY54* lines showed higher *GmWRKY54* expression of two to four-folds than NPs. After a 4-h–dehydration period by placing seedlings on bench at room temperature, the *GmWRKY54* expression was elevated to different levels (Fig. 1c). Two-week-old seedlings of similar size were subjected to drought treatment by withholding from water for two weeks, until water content dropped to about 20% of soil capacity. At this stage, NPs were dried more seriously than the corresponding TPs, indicating that the *GmWRKY54*-transgenic plants are tolerant to drought stress (Fig. 1d). Dry weight was measured to evaluate drought tolerance of these plants. Meanwhile, some seedlings were grown in small pots for survival rate test. All the transgenic plants had higher dry weight and survival rate than that of NPs (Fig. 1f and g). We further measured relative EL of soybean This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

leaves after two-week-treatment to evaluate membrane damage. The third trifoliate-leaves from the top were cut for relative EL measurement. After drought treatment, three pCm-GmWRKY54 transgenic plants had significantly lower EL than NPs (Fig. 1h). If TPs grew smaller than NPs in seedling stage in some circumstance, relative biomass was also measured to evaluate plant growth under drought stress (Fig. S2b). The dry weight was measured to calculate the ratio of drought-treated/well-watered as the relative biomass for each line. Three lines (A004A, A005B and A021A) had significantly higher relative biomass than NPs (Fig. S2). These results indicate that TPs have better performance than NPs under drought stress.

The *pRD29a-GmWRKY54* (stress-inducible promoter *pRD29a*) transgenic plants were also produced. T3 homozygous lines derived from single T-DNA insertion events were used for the analysis. Lines B003A is smaller than NPs. The first trifoliates of B016A and B010C are smaller than that of NPs, but the latter leaves are as big as NPs (Fig. 2b). Compared to NPs, some TPs even had lower *GmWRKY54* expression under well-watered condition, except B003A. After dehydration, all the TPs had higher increase in *GmWRKY54* expression than that of NPs, suggesting a possible mild induction of the transgene (Fig. 2a). We also subjected these seedlings to drought stress as described above. All the TPs had much better performance than NPs (Fig. 2b). The relative biomass of all the TPs was significantly higher than that of NPs (Fig. 2c). The leaves of *pRD29a-GmWRKY54* TPs also had lower EL than that of NPs after drought stress (Fig. 2d).

To emphasize that all these seedlings were kept in similar stress conditions, we measured SWC after harvesting the aerial parts of the seedlings. Results showed that all the pots had very consistent SWC of about 17%.

An *Agrobacterium rhizogenes*-mediated hairy root transformation system was used to test the phenotype of RNAi plants in water deficit. The K599 strain harboring the *GmWRKY54*-RNAi constructs were injected into the hypocotyl of soybean seedlings to generate WRKY54-RNAi hairy roots. The *GmWRKY54* expression in WRKY54-RNAi hairy roots drop to 60% of that in control hairy roots (K599) (Fig. 3a). The original roots were removed and the seedlings with hairy roots were subjected to 150 mM mannitol to simulate water deficit/osmotic stress. After kept in mannitol for 24h, all the trifoliate leaves of This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved. seedlings with WRKY54-RNAi hairy roots were seriously wilted, whereas the control seedlings (K599) still had some mildly wilted leaves (Fig. 3c). All the trifoliate leaves were cut to measure relative water content. The result showed that seedlings with WRKY54-RNAi hairy roots had less water content than the control seedlings (Fig. 3d).

Taken together, all these results indicate that *GmWRKY54* confers better drought tolerance in soybean plants.

#### RNA sequencing analysis reveals GmWRKY54-affected gene expression

Considering that the GmWRKY54 improved drought tolerance and may act as a transcription factor (Fig. 1 and 2), we examined genes regulated by GmWRKY54 using an RNA sequencing analysis. Null plants, two *pCm-GmWRKY54* lines (A004A and A005B) and one *pRD29a-GmWRKY54* line (B010C) from well-watered and dehydration conditions were used for RNA sequencing. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are defined as genes that have significant expression change after dehydration (dehydration Vs well-water). Null plants had 26761 DEGs, while line A004A, A005B and B010C had 30122, 29017 and 27924 DEGs respectively. Totally, there were 36024 DEGs that were changed in at least one plant.

We performed a GO analysis with the 36024 DEGs using a PAGE (Parametric analysis of gene set enrichment) method (Du et al., 2010). There were six biological processes enhanced higher in the three GmWRKY54-transgenic plants than those in NPs, including macromolecule biosynthetic process, RNA biosynthetic process, nitrogen compound metabolic process, primary metabolic process, macromolecule metabolic process and gene expression. Four molecular function terms also had higher increase in GmWRKY54-transgenic plants than in NPs, such as transcription regulator activity, DNA binding, chromatin binding and enzyme regulator activity (Fig. 4a, red asterisks in middle part). Several GO terms were down-regulated after dehydration, especially in TPs. Three biological processes, including protein metabolic process, photosynthesis, and cellular protein metabolic process were down-regulated more severely in TPs than those in NPs (Fig. 4a, blue asterisks in upper part). Three molecular function terms, such as catalytic activity, ribonucleotide binding, and transferase activity, had more reduction in TPs than those in NPs (Fig. 4a, blue asterisks in middle part). Five cellular component terms, such as extrinsic to This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

membrane, oxygen evolving complex, photosynthetic membrane, thylakoid, and membrane, were down-regulated more obviously in TPs than those in NPs (Fig. 4a, blue asterisks in lower part).

There were 96 genes enriched in the GO term of photosynthesis, we further performed a hierarchical cluster analysis of these genes. Under well-watered condition, the *GmWRKY54*-transgenic plants had higher expression (brighter yellow color) in most of these photosynthesis-related genes than that in NPs. After dehydration, the photosynthesis-related genes were differentially expressed between NPs and TPs, and TPs had lower expression (brighter blue color) of photosynthesis-related genes than NPs (Fig. S3 and Table S1). After drought treatment, a number of genes encoding photosystem (PS) I subunits and PS II subunits were down-regulated and this down-regulation was more severe in TPs than that in NPs (Fig. 4b, c). These results indicate that expression of the photosynthesis-related genes was substantially reduced in *GmWRKY54*-transgenic soybean plants under drought stress.

# GmWRKY54 promotes stomatal closure and hence reduces photosynthesis under drought stress

Since TPs had more reduction of photosynthesis-related gene expression than NPs (Fig. 4), we measured photosynthetic rate of pCm-GmWRKY54 TPs during dehydration process. The photosynthetic rate of TPs dropped rapidly from initiation of the dehydration to 2-h treatment, and then remained at very low levels. In NPs, the photosynthetic rate was maintained at a high level during 1<sup>st</sup> hour, and then decreased to a very low level from 2<sup>nd</sup> hour (Fig. 5a). From 1-h to 2-h treatment, the values were higher in NPs than those in TPs. These changes suggest that GmWRKY54 may cause an early and quick reduction in photosynthetic rate upon dehydration. The stomatal conductance and transpiration rate followed a pattern similar to that of photosynthetic rate except that NPs had an increase in the two parameters during 1<sup>st</sup> hour (Fig. 5b, c). The intercellular CO<sub>2</sub> concentration mildly increased in three transgenic lines and NPs. It should be noted that the values of CO<sub>2</sub> concentration in the three transgenic lines were higher than that in NPs, possibly consistent with the more reduction in photosynthesis of these TPs and hence less CO<sub>2</sub> fixation (Fig. 5d). All these results suggest that GmWRKY54 confers photosynthesis inhibition during dehydration.

Because the stomatal conductance was more reduced in TPs than that in NPs (Fig. 5b), we further subjected soybean seedlings to mild drought treatment and examined stomatal aperture under microscope. Under well-watered condition, most of the stomata were open and TPs had wider stoma than NPs. After drought treatment, stoma width of TPs dropped to 40-90% of that under well-watered condition. In contrast, stomata of NPs remained open, and the relative stoma width of NPs was greater than those of TPs (Fig. 5e, f). We subjected seedlings with WRKY54-RNAi or control hairy roots in 150 mM mannitol and examined their stomatal aperture. After 30 min, most stomata of seedlings with control hairy roots closed obviously to a width of 40% of that under well-watered condition. In contrast, stomata of seedlings with WRKY54-RNAi hairy roots still kept open. Their stoma width was 80% of that under well-watered condition (Fig. 5g, h). These results indicate that GmWRKY54 promotes stomatal closure of soybean plants for drought tolerance.

### **GmWRKY54** activates genes in ABA and Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling pathways

As a transcription activator (Fig. 1a), GmWRKY54 may activate downstream genes for stomatal closure and drought tolerance. We located these downstream genes through identification of genes that showed negative correlation with the photosynthesis-related genes. We performed a co-expression network analysis using photosynthesis-related genes and other DEGs (36024). Totally 2952 DEGs showed correlated expression patterns with the 96 photosynthesis-related genes (r-value > 0.8 or r-value < -0.8) (Table S2), among which 2603 DEGs were negatively correlated with the photosynthesis-related genes. We annotated these 2603 DEGs and found 34 genes enriched in ABA or  $Ca^{2+}$  signaling pathway. These 34 genes had negative r-value (r-value < -0.8) against most photosynthesis-related genes (green edges), but had positive correlation with PsbA-1, PsbA-2, PsbE and Psb29.4, which were up-regulated after dehydration. Among the 34 genes, 17 genes had higher expression in all the three transgenic lines than that in NPs after dehydration (orange, red or deep red color), and 11 genes had higher expression in at least one transgenic line after dehydration (grey color) (Fig. 6). However, six genes had lower expression in TPs than that of NPs after dehydration (light or deep blue color) (Fig. 6 and S4). These results suggest that the 17 genes, which are involved in ABA and/or Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling pathway, may be part of the direct target This article is protected by copyright. genes of GmWRKY54 for stomatal closure and drought tolerance.

Since the processes of RNA biosynthesis, transcription regulator activity, DNA binding, and chromatin binding had greater increase in TPs than in NPs (Fig. 4a), we examined whether GmWRKY54 regulated expression of transcriptional factors (TFs). We searched the TFs that negatively correlated with photosynthesis-related genes as described above and 55 TFs were found. Their expression patterns showed negative correlation with most of the photosynthesis-related genes, except for *PsbA-1*, *PsbA-2*, *Psb29.4*, *PsbE* and *PsbN* (Fig. S5). All these 55 TFs had higher expression in TPs than in NPs after dehydration (Fig. S6), suggesting that they may be the possible downstream TFs of GmWRKY54.

We further tested the gene expression using quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR). Genes of ABA or  $Ca^{2+}$  pathway were chosen for the analysis, including *CIPK11-2*, *CIPK11-4*, *CIPK12*, *PYL8-1*, *PYL8-2*, *PYL12*, *MAP3K17*, *CYP70A1*, *PP2C-67*, *SRK2A*, *CPK3-1*, and *CPK3-2*. Under well-watered condition, some of the genes showed slightly higher expression in TPs than in NPs. After dehydration, the expression of these genes was more significantly increased in most transgenic lines compared to NPs (Fig. S7). These results indicate that the GmWRKY54 may activate genes of ABA,  $Ca^{2+}$  and/or DREB-related pathways for stress regulation.

All the genes tested above have w-box elements in their promoter regions, which could be the possible binding sites of GmWRKY54. We further tested the DNA-binding ability of GmWRKY54 towards these promoter regions using the EMSA assay. The GST-GmWRKY54 protein was expressed and tested for binding with different probes. GmWRKY54 could recognize and bind to the promoter regions of five genes including *SRK2A*, *CIPK11-2*, *CPK3-1*, *CPK3-2* and *PYL8-1*. When we mutated the w-box, GmWRKY54 could not bind to these promoter regions any more (Fig. 7a, b). To perform the promoter-LUC assay, the 2-kb promoter regions of *CIPK11*, *CPK3-1*, *CPK3-2* and *PYL8* were cloned in front of the LUC gene (the promoter of *SRK2A* was unable to clone). The results showed that GmWRKY54 could recognize the promoter regions of these four genes. GmWRKY54 significantly triggered the activation of *proCPK3-1*:LUC and moderately activated the other three in vivo (Fig. 7c). These results indicate that GmWRKY54 directly binds to the promoter and activates expression of these six genes.

Evaluation of the yield-related traits in *GmWRKY54*-transgenic soybean plants under drought stress

We further examined the yield-related traits of the *GmWRKY54*-transgenic plants in greenhouse. Only *pCm-GmWRKY54* transgenic plants were analyzed due to seed availability. Four well-watered and drought-stressed replicated plots were arranged, and each plot had 15 plants (one plant per pot) for each line (Fig. S8). In addition to the four lines, one more pCm-GmWRKY54 line (C004A, Fig. S9) was also included in the analysis. For drought stress treatment, eight rounds of dry down experiments were conducted and after the treatment, the soybean seeds were harvested and weighed for comparison with the well-watered controls.

Under well-watered condition, four transgenic lines showed reduced seed weight compared to NPs. Only one line (A003A) showed increase of seed weight on average compared to NPs. After drought stress, the seed weight per plant was all reduced, with the line A003A having the highest seed weight. The seed weight reduction rate was also compared, and the line A004A, which was probably the most drought-tolerant (Fig. 1), exhibited the lowest reduction in seed weight after drought stress (Table S3). The very low seed weight in line A005B under both well-watered and drought stress condition may be due to the abnormal and/or delayed maturity (Fig. S8). The 100-seed weight was also examined (Table S4). Under well-watered condition, two transgenic lines (A004, A021A) with relatively higher drought tolerance, had reduced 100-seed weight compared to null and other transgenic lines. Under drought stress, similar trend was noted. These results indicate that the high expression of *GmWRKY54* tends to have less reduction in seed weight after drought stress.

#### Discussion

Our study demonstrates that *GmWRKY54* promotes drought tolerance in soybean. The following pieces of evidence support the conclusion. The transgenic soybean plants, harboring *GmWRKY54* gene driven by either a constitutive promoter or a drought-inducible promoter, showed better performance after drought stress; the soybean seedlings with WRKY54-RNAi hairy roots were more sensitive to water deficit (osmotic stress) (Fig. 1, 2 and 3).

The expression of GmWRKY54 is induced by abiotic stress such as salt and drought (Zhou et al., 2008). Though driven by a constitutive promoter (pCm), it looks like that GmWRKY54 is induced by drought treatment even stronger (Fig. 1c). This phenomenon was also observed in other research (Cao et al., 2006). The activity of constitutive promoter is also known to be influenced by several factors, including development and growth environment. For example, expression of a 35S-driven gene could be suppressed by low temperature and UV light, but increased by high temperature (Boyko et al., 2010). A possible reason is that these constitutive promoters from plant virus also contain certain binding elements of stress-related TFs, such as HSF-binding elements (Pauli et al., 2004). Thus, our work suggests that the constitutive promoter pCm is also inducible when used in stress treatment. GmWRKY54 confers drought tolerance through regulating expression of its downstream genes. However, the transcriptional changes under drought stress occur at a genome-wide level; the key genes hide in the tens of thousands of DEGs. Through functional annotation and enrichment of the DEGs, we found that photosynthesis-related GO terms showed greater decrease in TPs than that in NPs (Fig. 4). Physiological tests were consistent with the transcriptional change (Fig. 5). In photosynthetic organisms, photosynthesis is essential for growth and it is important for them to maintain the balance between growth and survival under different conditions. It is reasonable that they would take the priority of survival when captured in stresses. For example, when stresses occur in cyanobacterium, photosynthesis is usually down-regulated, whereas stress response is up-regulated (Ge et al., 2017). Similarly, decline of photosynthesis is the primary effect of water deficit, and secondary effect would occur along with the continuous drought stress (Chaves et al., 2009). In the present finding, more reduction of photosynthesis was observed in GmWRKY54-transgenic plants after drought stress, This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

suggesting that the priority of transgenic plants was adapting to stress in order to survive.

It should be noted that the down-regulation of photosynthesis is not the direct effect of GmWRKY54, for GmWRKY54 is a transcriptional activator (Fig. 1a). When moderate water deficit began, transpiration rate of certain plants remained high or even increased, as observed in wild barley and our experiments (Galkin *et al.*, 2018). A possible reason that NPs opened stomata wider was to gain higher transpiration pull, so as to absorb more water. Obviously, this strategy failed, when drought continued and even went worse. GmWRKY54 confers drought tolerance in TPs due to more precise signal transduction, which leads to timely stomatal closure (Fig. 5). This change finally led to restriction of  $CO_2$  diffusion and down-regulated after dehydration especially in TPs, several photosynthesis-related genes were up-regulated, such as *PsbA*, *PsbE*, *PsbN* and *Psb29.4* (Fig. S3). These genes are up-regulated probably for the self-protection, such as oxidation detoxification, recovery from photo-inhibition and response to excess light (Keren *et al.*, 2005; Torabi *et al.*, 2014; Sugiura *et al.*, 2015; Huo *et al.*, 2016).

To disclose what lies between stomatal closure and photo-inhibition, we adopted a strategy to perform co-expression network analysis using all the DEGs against photosynthesis-related genes. Logically, the direct downstream genes of GmWRKY54 would modulate stomatal closure or photoinhibition, and they would have completely opposite expression patterns from the photosynthesis-related genes. We did find many DEGs that were negatively correlated with photosynthesis-related genes and many of them belong to ABA or  $Ca^{2+}$  signaling pathway (Fig. 6). It is known that ABA and  $Ca^{2+}$  form an integrated signaling pathway, which consists of receptors, protein kinase, TFs and anion channels. This complicated signaling pathway takes part in modulating stomatal aperture and drought adaption (Lim et al., 2015; Lind et al., 2015; Edel and Kudla, 2016). In the ABA-mediated short-term process, several ABA receptors (PYR/PYLs) were proved to regulate stomatal aperture in a quantitative manner (Gonzalez-Guzman et al., 2012; Lim et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2016), among which we identified homologue PYL8-1, PYL8-2 and PYL12 from soybean. Besides, the other two genes PP2C-67 and SRK2A were also up-regulated by GmWRKY54, which also involved in ABA response. In the ABA-mediated mav be This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

MAP3K-MAP2K-MAPK (mitogen activated protein kinase) signaling module, the Arabidopsis *MAP3K17* and *MAP3K18* are induced by abiotic stress and positively regulate the long-term response of drought stress (Boudsocq *et al.*, 2015; Danquah *et al.*, 2015; de Zelicourt *et al.*, 2016). Confirmed in our study, we identified soybean homologue *MAP3K17* and *MAP3K18*. Moreover, we also found an ABA catabolism gene *CYP707A1*, which is responsible for ABA hydroxylation (Umezawa *et al.*, 2006; Dong *et al.*, 2015), suggesting that GmWRKY54 may also be involved in ABA homeostasis during drought stress (Fig. 6).

In Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling pathway, expression of genes encoding calcium dependent protein kinase (CDPK/CPK) and CBL (Calcineurin B-like protein) interacting protein kinase (CIPK) homologues was enhanced by GmWRKY54 in all the transgenic plants after dehydration (Fig. 6). It has been reported that CPK3, CPK6 and CPK10 were involved in ABA and Ca<sup>2+</sup> mediated stomatal closure in Arabidopsis (Mori *et al.*, 2006; Zou *et al.*, 2010). Besides, the CBL9/CIPK23 complexes also participate in stomatal closure via activation of SLAC1 (Maierhofer *et al.*, 2014). CIPK11 (also named PKS5) plays an important role in ABA-mediated gene expression through phosphorylation of ABI5 (Zhou *et al.*, 2015). Overexpression of *CIPK*12 in rice improves drought tolerance in transgenic plants (Xiang *et al.*, 2007).

GmWRKY54 regulates expression of dozens of stress-related TFs, including DREBs (Fig. S5). AP2/ERF/DREB is a superfamily of TFs, in which DREBs regulate expression of large numbers of stress-induced genes and play positive roles in response to various stresses in ABA-independent manner (Lata and Prasad, 2011; Mizoi *et al.*, 2012). DREB1, also known as CBF (C-repeat binding factor), is responsible for cold tolerance (Gehan *et al.*, 2015). Recent researches indicate that DREB1D/CBF4 also enhances drought tolerance in plants (Haake *et al.*, 2002; Guttikonda *et al.*, 2014). However, the two *DREB1Ds* may not be the direct targets of GmWRKY54, since GmWRKY54 could not bind to their promoter regions. All the studies above are consistent with our findings, suggesting that our strategy to discover the downstream genes and the molecular mechanism of GmWRKY54 is rather practical.

Generally, the interaction between WRKY and ABA-signaling pathway described above occurs in the downstream position, especially focuses on downstream TFs (Jiang *et al.*, 2017). For examples, AtWRKY40 binds to the promoter regions of ABA-signaling genes, such as This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

*ABI4*, *ABI5*, *DREB1A* and *ABF4*. Through interacting with AtWRKY18 and AtWRKY60, AtWRKY40 represses several of them (Liu *et al.*, 2012; Rushton *et al.*, 2012). AtWRKY63 activates even more downstream genes, such as activates *ABF2*, *RD29A* and *COR47* (Ren *et al.*, 2010). However, our finding reveals that WRKY protein could be involved in ABA-signaling pathway rather early, for GmWRKY54 directly activates an ABA receptor gene (*PYL8*) and a SnRK2 kinase gene (*SRK2A*). Importantly, GmWRKY54 also activates *CPK3* and *CIPK11*, which helps to integrate calcium-signaling with ABA-signaling during drought response (Edel and Kudla, 2016) (Fig. 7). GmWRKY54 improves signaling cascades of the two pathways and causes activation of large numbers of stress-related genes, which greatly enhances drought tolerance (Fig. 6 and Fig. S5). Our finding greatly increases understanding of the roles that WRKY TFs could play in ABA-signaling, which has not been reported before.

It should be noted that high expression of *GmWRKY54* may have agronomic effect. Under well-watered condition, higher expresser of *GmWRKY54* tends to have lower seed weight per plant, whereas the low expresser (i.e., A003A) appears to have the highest seed weight (Table S3). It is likely that high expression of the *GmWRKY54* would activate more downstream events and cost much energy and many resources, leading to a low seed yield. The reduced photosynthesis after drought stress may also be one of the reasons for yield decrease. We propose that young seedlings overexpressing *GmWRKY54* have better chance to survive during drought stress (Fig. 1). But during reproductive period, it is better to focus resources on producing more seeds. Thus, we suggest that screening transgenic lines that have relatively low expression but also drought tolerance (e.g., A003A) would be beneficial to produce satisfied yield. Besides, Zhao *et al.* suggested that drought-induced promoter may reduce the impact of constitutive promoter on plant growth (Zhao *et al.*, 2016). For practical purpose, precise manipulation of the gene expression and more transformation events may be adopted to select proper transgenic lines that have both drought tolerance and satisfied production.

Taken together, our work reveals that GmWRKY54 confers drought tolerance in soybean plants through enhancing ABA/Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling for stomatal closure and activating expression of large numbers of stress-related TFs. Moreover, we also demonstrate an efficient and This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

accurate method of finding downstream genes from mass transcriptome data. Thus, our work provides valuable information for the genetic modification of crops and the research of plants in response to drought stress.

# Experimental procedures

### Plant materials and transformation

Soybean cultivar Williams82 (*Glycine max*, Wm82) was used for soybean transformation. Seeds were germinated on soil in greenhouse equipped with supplemental lighting and cooling systems. Environmental setting for soybean growth was 16 °C at night and 28 °C at daytime with the photoperiod of 16:8 (day: night). For soybean transformation, the coding sequence of *GmWRKY54* was cloned from soybean cDNA and integrated into binary vector CONS101 and CONS102, both carrying aminoglycoside 3'-adenyltransferase gene that confers resistance to spectinomycin. The *GmWRKY54* gene in vector CONS101 and CONS102 was driven by a constitutive promoter (p*Cm*) (Stavolone *et al.*, 2003) and a drought-inducible promoter (p*RD29a*) (Yamaguchi-Shinozaki and Shinozaki, 1993) respectively, pRD29a is the promoter region derived from the Arabidopsis *RD29a* (Responsive to Dessication 29a). The vectors were then transformed into soybean by agrobacterium-mediated method. The regenerated plants were analyzed by Taqman assay, single-copy transgenic lines were selected and grown in greenhouse. The seeds from homozygous plants were used for this study. Seeds from NPs segregated from each transgenic line were also collected and used for comparison.

#### Transcriptional activation assay

The transcriptional activation assay was performed using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Ohta *et al.*, 2001; Yoo *et al.*, 2007). A firefly *LUC* gene, a *Renilla* LUC gene (pTRL), pRT-BD-GmWRKY54 and pRT-BD-VP16 were used as reporter gene, internal control, the effector and positive control respectively (Wei *et al.*, 2009; Wei *et al.*, 2015). #### Performance of the transgenic soybean under drought stress

Two-week-old seedlings were used for drought treatment. For each pot, same amount of soil was used and the soil was fully watered before drought treatment. Water supply of these seedlings was stopped for about two weeks. Then the aerial parts of the seedlings were harvested and dried in the oven at 80 °C for at least 72h and the biomass (dry weight) was measured. After cutting the seedlings, we randomly picked 10 pots from transgenic and null plants to measure water content. The soil was weighted before and after drying in the oven to calculate water content. Water content (%) = (Final soil weight – Dry soil weight) / Dry soil weight x 100. Results showed that all these pots had very consistent water content (about 17% in average). The relative biomass was calculated as a ratio of dry weight from drought-stressed plants/dry weight from well-watered plants (Harb and Pereira, 2011). The biomass trial was split-plot design with 15 replications. For root length measurement, distance between the genesis position and the end of the longest root was measured using ImageJ software. Each experiment was repeated twice.

#### Agrobacterium rhizogenes-mediated transformation of and mannitol treatments

The sense and the antisense fragments of *GmWRKY54* (357-730bp) were cloned into the SacI/KpnI and the XbaI/SalI sites of pZH01 respectively. The soybean cultivar KF1 and the *A*. *rhizogenes* strain K599 were used for transformation. The hypocotyls of five-d-old KF1 seedlings were injected with K599 harboring the pZH01 construct and kept in high humidity. The hairy roots were generated from the wound two weeks after injection. The main roots were cut and the seedlings were moved to water until the hairy roots grew to 10cm. Then, the seedlings were transferred to 150 mM mannitol for 24h. All the trifoliates were cut and dried at 65°C for 2 d to measure water content: RWC (%) = (water content after salt treatment) / (water content in water) (Kereszt *et al.*, 2007; Wei *et al.*, 2017).

#### Measurement of photosynthetic rate

Four-week-old pCm-GmWRK54 transgenic soybean plants were exposed in the air for dehydration. During 4-h-dehydration, photosynthetic rate, stomata conductance, intercellular CO<sub>2</sub> concentration and transpiration rate were measured every hour using portable photosynthesis system LI-6400XT (LI-COR Biosciences). For each transgenic and null line, 6 plants were measured.

#### Relative Electrolyte Leakage (EL) assay

Two-week-old seedlings were treated with drought stress for two weeks, and  $3^{rd}$  trifoliate-leaves from the top were measured for Relative EL. Soybean leaves were immersed and vacuumed in water and then measured for conductivity (K1). The leaves were then destroyed completely through autoclaving. Conductivity (K2) was measured again until water temperature dropped to room temperature. Relative EL = K1/K2, and 5 plants of each transgenic and null line were measured (Wei *et al.*, 2017).

#### Yield test of transgenic soybean plants under drought stress

Yield trial of p*Cm-GmWRKY54* transgenic plants was run in greenhouse because of permit limitation. Four-week-old seedlings of each line were transplanted into big pots filled with the same amount of soil. From V4 stage, withheld watering till 90% NPs showed moderate stress phenotype (leaf rolling and wilted plant), then fully watered the plants for recovery; totally 8 rounds of drought and recovery were introduced. The yield trial was split-plot design, and there were 4 plots for each treatment and 15 plants per line in each plot. Plot yield (grain weight) and 100-gain weight were measured and normalized to that at the same level of seed water content (12%).
