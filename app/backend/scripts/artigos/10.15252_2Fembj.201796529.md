# Article

# A plant effector-triggered immunity signaling sector is inhibited by pattern-triggered immunity

Noriyuki Hatsugai<sup>1</sup> , Daisuke Igarashi1,<sup>2</sup> , Keisuke Mase1,† , You Lu<sup>1</sup> , Yayoi Tsuda<sup>1</sup> , Suma Chakravarthy<sup>3</sup> , Hai-Lei Wei<sup>3</sup> , Joseph W Foley4,<sup>5</sup> , Alan Collmer<sup>3</sup> , Jane Glazebrook<sup>1</sup> & Fumiaki Katagiri1, [\\*](http://orcid.org/0000-0001-6893-3788)

### Abstract

Since signaling machineries for two modes of plant-induced immunity, pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI), extensively overlap, PTI and ETI signaling likely interact. In an Arabidopsis quadruple mutant, in which four major sectors of the signaling network, jasmonate, ethylene, PAD4, and salicylate, are disabled, the hypersensitive response (HR) typical of ETI is abolished when the Pseudomonas syringae effector AvrRpt2 is bacterially delivered but is intact when AvrRpt2 is directly expressed in planta. These observations led us to discovery of a network-buffered signaling mechanism that mediates HR signaling and is strongly inhibited by PTI signaling. We named this mechanism the ETI-Mediating and PTI-Inhibited Sector (EMPIS). The signaling kinetics of EMPIS explain apparently different plant genetic requirements for ETI triggered by different effectors without postulating different signaling machineries. The properties of EMPIS suggest that information about efficacy of the early immune response is fed back to the immune signaling network, modulating its activity and limiting the fitness cost of unnecessary immune responses.

Keywords deep perturbation; fitness cost of immunity; network resilience; plant immune signaling network

# Introduction

The signaling network that regulates innate immune responses in plants upon perception of pathogen attack is highly resilient. Plant pathogens are under strong selection for the ability to compromise plant immune signaling (de Jonge et al, 2011; O'Brien et al, 2011). Furthermore, microbial pathogens can evolve much faster than plants. Thus, the plant immune signaling network has apparently evolved to have a high level of resilience to withstand pathogen attack on its internal nodes without heavily relying on its own evolutionary adaptation (Katagiri & Tsuda, 2010). Another property of the plant immune signaling network is tunability. Immune responses are energetically expensive (Bolton, 2009) and often damaging to plants as well as to pathogens. Unnecessary immune induction costs plant fitness (Heidel et al, 2004; Vos et al, 2013). However, some molecular cues used by plants to detect pathogen attack cannot distinguish pathogens from non-pathogens [e.g., microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs), see below]. If strong immune responses are induced upon recognition of such unreliable cues, probabilistically the fitness cost of misfiring immune responses surpasses their potential benefit. Thus, the immune signaling network needs to have the ability to quantitatively tune its output according to the reliability of pathogen attack cues.

Two modes of plant immunity are well defined: pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI; Jones & Dangl, 2006; Dodds & Rathjen, 2010). PTI is triggered by recognition of molecular cues that are conserved among related microbes (MAMPs) by cognate pattern-recognition receptors (PRR) on the plant cell membrane. Examples for the model plant Arabidopsis include a 22-amino acid segment of flagellin (flg22) conserved among Gram-negative bacteria and recognized by the PRR FLS2 (Zipfel et al, 2004) and an 18-amino acid segment of elongation factor Tu (elf18) conserved among bacteria and recognized by the PRR EFR (Zipfel et al, 2006). MAMP recognition initiates a cascade of signaling events in the immune signaling network resulting in induction of a battery of immune responses, including production of reactive oxygen species, deposition of callose against the cell wall, and transcriptional induction of many defense genes (Zipfel & Robatzek, 2010; Newman et al, 2013). signaling and sufficiently compromise PTI to allow pathogen proliferation (Jones & Dangl, 2006). This PTI-compromised state is called effector-triggered susceptibility (ETS). Plants evolved another way to detect pathogen attack by directly or indirectly recognizing some pathogen effectors by cognate resistance (R) proteins (Dodds & Rathjen, 2010; Jones et al, 2016). For example, effectors of the Gram-negative bacterial pathogen Pseudomonas syringae, AvrRpt2 and AvrRpm1, are recognized by Arabidopsis R proteins RPS2 and RPM1, respectively (Bent et al, 1994; Mindrinos et al, 1994; Grant et al, 1995). This recognition by R proteins triggers signaling events for ETI. Generally, ETI is associated with strong immune responses, typically including programmed cell death of plant cells at the infection site, which is called a hypersensitive response (HR; Mur et al, 2008; Coll et al, 2011). It is likely that misfiring ETI would carry a high fitness cost.

A major challenge in studying such a highly resilient network stems from the fact that perturbing part of the network typically does not have a strong impact on the network output and consequently does not provide much information about the underlying mechanisms, which is called an identifiability problem (Jaqaman & Danuser, 2006). To overcome this problem, we previously reduced the network consisting of hundreds of molecular species, such as genes and proteins, to four subnetworks, which we call signaling sectors (Tsuda et al, 2009). The signaling sectors were disabled by mutations in the hub genes of each sector in Arabidopsis: the DDE2, EIN2, PAD4, and SID2 genes for the jasmonate (JA), ethylene (ET), PAD4, and salicylate (SA) sectors, respectively (Tsuda et al, 2009). Whereas perturbations of the individual sectors separately had limited effects on immunity, simultaneous perturbations of all four signaling sectors (in a quadruple mutant, quad) resulted in loss of most of PTI triggered by flg22 (flg22-PTI) and ETI triggered by AvrRpt2 (AvrRpt2-ETI; Tsuda et al, 2009). These observations justified reducing the network to this scale, which is a network of the four signaling sectors. Then, we generated comprehensive combinatorial perturbations of the signaling sectors, that is, 16 combinatorial mutants, including wild type and quad. The analysis of immunity levels in the 16 combinatorial mutants allowed us to take a reductionist approach to estimate the contributions of each individual sector and two-, three-, and four-way interactions among the sectors. We call this approach network reconstitution and the obtained sector contributions and interactions the signaling allocations. Using network reconstitution, we showed that flg22-PTI and AvrRpt2-ETI extensively share the signaling machinery but use the same machinery differently: The signaling sectors strongly compensate one another in AvrRpt2-ETI, while they are partly compensating and partly synergistic in flg22-PTI (Tsuda et al, 2009).

In our previous study of ETI signaling, the trigger of the signaling events was not simplified. AvrRpt2 was delivered from a P. syringae strain through type III secretion, which unavoidably delivered MAMPs to the plant in addition to AvrRpt2. Therefore, both PTI and ETI signaling were triggered in the system, so we could not exclude the possibility that PTI signaling influences ETI signaling (i.e., a potential non-negligible PTI:ETI interaction). In the current study, we used in planta expression of AvrRpt2 to trigger only ETI signaling in the network state of all four sectors disabled. This further reduction in the system revealed that there is another signaling sector (which we call the ETI-Mediating and PTI-Inhibited Sector or EMPIS) that can mediate signaling for an HR. The function of EMPIS was compensated by the four sectors and strongly inhibited by PTI signaling—therefore, both deep perturbations of the network and simplification of the ETI trigger were required for discovery of EMPIS. We also showed that with EMPIS, apparent differences in plant genetic requirements for the HR triggered by different effectors delivered from a bacterium can be explained by the relative timing of PTI and HR signaling reaching EMPIS. Furthermore, the role of EMPIS was not limited to the HR as it also affected slower ETI responses profiled by transcriptome response analysis. EMPIS may have a function of tuning immune output for plant fitness by aborting execution of more-costly ETI if early PTI is effective.

### Results

#### The HR was suppressed in quad when AvrRpt2 was delivered from bacteria but not when AvrRpt2 was expressed in planta

Common experimental designs, in which plant responses to a pathogen (PTI response) and an isogenic pathogen with an ETItriggering effector gene are compared (PTI vs. PTI + ETI + PTI:ETI), do not allow estimation of the effects of ETI and PTI:ETI separately. We separated ETI from PTI, and consequently from PTI:ETI interaction as well, by expressing AvrRpt2 in planta using an estradiolinducible promoter system (Ed-AvrRpt2; Zuo et al, 2000). Induction of AvrRpt2 expression by estradiol treatment in wild-type Col-0 resulted in an HR dependent on the cognate resistance gene RPS2 (AvrRpt2-HR; Tsuda et al, 2012). AvrRpt2 expression in the quad mutant (Ed-AvrRpt2 quad) resulted in AvrRpt2-HR as well (Fig 1A). In Fig 1, the amount of electrolytes leaked from dying plant cells in leaf disks was recorded as a quantitative measure of the HR cell death. The AvrRpt2-HR observed in Ed-AvrRpt2 quad was a genuine ETI-associated HR since it was dependent on the cognate R gene; the HR did not occur in Ed-AvrRpt2 quad rpm1 rps2 upon estradiol treatment although AvrRpt2 mRNA and protein accumulated to high levels (Fig EV1). We used an rpm1 rps2 double mutant because RPM1 recognizes AvrRpt2 weakly as RPS2 and RPM1 both guard a common effector target protein, RIN4 (Kim et al, 2009). The HR induction in Ed-AvrRpt2 quad was unexpected because the AvrRpt2- HR was abolished in quad when AvrRpt2 was delivered from the bacterium P. syringae pv. tomato DC3000 (Pto; Tsuda et al, 2009; Fig 1B). AvrRpt2-HR was also abolished in quad when AvrRpt2 was delivered from effector-less strains, such as the polymutant Pto D36E (Wei et al, 2015) or the engineered Pseudomonas fluorescens strain EtHAn (Thomas et al, 2009; Fig 1C and Appendix Fig S1), which excludes effectors other than AvrRpt2 from a possible explanation for the discrepancy in AvrRpt2-HR induction in quad between in planta AvrRpt2 expression and bacterial delivery of AvrRpt2. Neither is a possible high level of AvrRpt2 protein accumulation by in planta expression a likely explanation. When ETI was triggered by in planta AvrRpt2 expression, accumulation of AvrRpt2 protein was strongly suppressed to an undetectable level, whereas AvrRpt2 protein accumulated to a high level when ETI was not triggered in Ed-AvrRpt2 quad rpm1 rps2 (Fig EV1C). The transgene Ed-AvrRpt2 in all lines used in the study originated from a single plant transformation event and was introduced into different backgrounds by genetic crosses, and thus, the transgene in its chromosomal context was genetically identical in all lines. #### EMPIS mediates HR signaling and is inhibited by PTI signaling

To explain the above observation, we hypothesized that PTI signaling triggered by MAMPs unavoidably presented by bacterial strains inhibits EMPIS that compensates HR signaling by the four sectors and remains functional when the four sectors are disabled in *quad* (Fig 2A). The hypothesis predicts that the HR is inhibited by PTI signaling in *Ed-AvrRpt2 quad* but not in *Ed-AvrRpt2*. To test this prediction, we investigated the effects of two PTI-inducing MAMPs, flg22 and elf18, on *Ed-AvrRpt2*-induced HR in wild type and *quad*. Plants carrying *Ed-AvrRpt2* were treated with one of the MAMPs or mock, simultaneously with estradiol (Fig 2B). A macroscopic HR was observed in the *Ed-AvrRpt2* leaves regardless of the presence or absence of MAMPs (Fig 2C). In contrast, the macroscopic HR was undetectable or weak in *Ed-AvrRpt2 quad* leaves after treatment with a MAMP (Fig 2C). No HR was observed in *Ed-AvrRpt2 rpm1 rps2* leaves included as a negative control (Fig 2C). Quantification of the strength of the AvrRpt2-HR by the electrolyte leakage assay confirmed the macroscopic observations of HR inhibition by MAMPs in *quad* (Fig 2D). The estradiol-inducible system was inhibited by MAMP treatment 24 h prior to inducer treatment (Igarashi *et al*, 2013). #### Differences in the plant genetic requirements for AvrRpt2- and AvrRpm1-HR can be explained by differences in the timing of the HR signaling

An HR triggered by another *P. syringae* effector, AvrRpm1 (AvrRpm1-HR) is, unlike AvrRpt2-HR, intact in the *quad* mutant

when AvrRpm1 is delivered from Pto (Tsuda et al, 2009) or Pto D36E (Appendix Fig S2). A common interpretation based on conventional genetics would be that this difference in plant genetic requirements for AvrRpt2-HR and AvrRpm1-HR is because AvrRpt2-HR and AvrRpm1-HR use different signaling machineries. One clear difference between AvrRpt2-HR and AvrRpm1-HR is that AvrRpm1-HR is much faster than AvrRpt2-HR when the effectors are delivered from P. syringae (Ritter & Dangl, 1996; Fig 1B and Appendix Fig S2). This response timing difference was conserved even when AvrRpt2 and AvrRpm1 were expressed in planta using the estradiolinducible promoter system (Fig 1A and D), strongly suggesting that the process causing this timing difference occurs after AvrRpt2 and AvrRpm1 are delivered to the plant cell. Given this HR kinetic difference, the EMPIS hypothesis can explain the difference in the genetic requirements for AvrRpm1- and AvrRpt2-HR without postulating a difference in signaling machinery: The signal from the R protein to EMPIS arrives before EMPIS is inhibited by PTI signaling in the case of AvrRpm1-HR, whereas the signal arrives after EMPIS inhibition in the case of AvrRpt2-HR (Fig 2A; indicated by "variable rate" in the figure). To test this kinetic difference hypothesis, Ed-AvrRpt2 guad and Ed-AvrRpm1 guad were compared after treatments with flg22 or elf18 together with estradiol in different timings.

We first demonstrated that AvrRpm1-HR in *Ed-AvrRpm1 quad* is a genuine ETI-associated HR as no HR was observed in *Ed-AvrRpm1 quad rpm1 rps2* after estradiol treatment (Fig EV2). When the plants were treated with the MAMPs and estradiol simultaneously, significant HR inhibition was observed only in *Ed-AvrRpt2 quad* but not in *Ed-AvrRpm1 quad* (Fig 3A, C and E). However, when the plants were treated with a MAMP 1 h prior to estradiol treatment, significant HR inhibition was observed in both *Ed-AvrRpt2 quad* and *Ed-AvrRpm1 quad* (Fig 3B, D and F). This finding that earlier treatment with a MAMP can inhibit an AvrRpm1-HR in the *quad* background supports the kinetic difference hypothesis.

We also tested for inhibition of AvrRpm1-HR by early MAMP treatment in *quad* when AvrRpm1 is delivered from bacteria. When the plants were co-infiltrated with the MAMPs flg22 or elf18 and *Pto* D36E *AvrRpm1*, a small but significant decrease in the HR was observed in *quad* (Fig 4A and C). When the plants were treated with MAMPs 1 h prior to *Pto* D36E *AvrRpm1* inoculation, a larger HR decrease was observed (Fig 4B and D). # Slower ETI-specific gene induction is inhibited by PTI signaling in *quad* more strongly than faster gene induction

The signaling kinetics-based explanation of the difference between AvrRpt2-HR and AvrRpm1-HR phenotypes in *quad* background suggests that slower ETI responses in *quad* are more strongly inhibited by PTI signaling than faster ETI responses. This notion was tested by investigating the PTI signaling effect on the ETI-specific transcriptome response in *quad*. The *Ed-AvrRpt2 quad* plants were treated with mock, flg22, Ed, or flg22&Ed, and the treated leaf tissue was harvested at 0, 60, 120, 180, and 300 min post-treatment for RNA-seq analysis. For each gene at each time point, the PTI, ETI, and PTI&ETI effects were defined as (flg22 – mock), (Ed – mock), and (flg22&Ed – mock), respectively. The genes for which at least one of the PTI, ETI, and PTI&ETI effects were significant at one or more of 60, 120, 180, and 300 min (q < 0.05 and difference from

mock > 2-fold) were selected for further analysis (8,763 PTI/ETIresponsive genes). Among the PTI/ETI-responsive genes, the genes for which the ETI effects were more than fourfold higher (difference on a log2-scale > 2) than the absolute value of the PTI effects were identified as ETI-specific, induced genes at each time point. For the genes that satisfied the conditions at more than one time point, the genes were assigned only to the earliest time point to capture the beginning of their responses (7, 30, 113, and 284 genes for the times 60, 120, 180, and 300 min, respectively; Fig 5A). To gain a genome-wide view of the response, the ETI-specific, induced genes at 60 min, which had only seven genes selected, were omitted from the subsequent statistical analysis. Similarly, the genes for which the PTI effects were more than fourfold higher than the absolute value of the ETI effects were identified as PTI-specific, induced genes (105, 103, 122, and 50 genes for 60, 120, 180, and 300 min, respectively; Fig 5A; the list of the genes is given in Table EV1, ETI.PTI.spec.gene.list). It is clear from the numbers of PTI- or ETIspecific, induced genes and from the distributions of the PTI/ETIresponsive genes in Fig 5A that the overall PTI response is faster than the ETI response. We do not know whether this difference in response rates represents true dynamical differences of the signaling events or results from the Ed-inducible system taking time to accumulate a sufficient amount of AvrRpt2 protein in the cell. We were not able to determine the minimal amount of AvrRpt2 protein sufficient to trigger ETI because AvrRpt2 protein did not accumulate at a detectable level when ETI was triggered (Appendix Fig S1). Thus, we cannot exclude the latter possibility.

When the distributions of the effects of PTI signaling on the ETIspecific, induced gene responses were compared (Fig 5B, left panel, "PTI&ETI – ETI"), the medians for 120, 180, and 300 min were 0.047, 0.008, and 0.426 (in log2 scale), respectively. The medians at 120 and 180 min were not significantly smaller than 0 (P = 0.41 and 0.61, respectively, one-sided post hoc test based on 10,000 bootstrapped sets), strongly suggesting that the beginning part of AvrRpt2-ETI signaling, including signal perception, was not affected by PTI signaling. On the other hand, the median of the PTI signaling effect at 300 min was significantly more negative than the median for 120 min (P = 0.028, one-sided post hoc test based on 10,000 bootstrapped sets). This more negative PTI signaling effect indicates that inhibition of ETI-specific gene induction was stronger at 300 min than at 120 min on the genome scale. This observation supports the notion that slower ETI responses in quad are more strongly inhibited by PTI signaling than faster ETI responses. In contrast, the median for the effect of ETI signaling on the PTIspecific, induced gene induction at 300 min was not significantly more negative than that at 120 min (P = 0.127, one-sided post hoc test based on 10,000 bootstrapped sets; Fig 5B, right panel). Therefore, on the genome-scale inhibition of slower gene induction was specific to ETI-specific, induced genes. There were exceptions to this genome-scale pattern. There are some ETI-specific, induced genes whose induction was enhanced by PTI signaling (those with positive values in Fig 5B, left panel). Thus, different ETI responses are influenced by PTI signaling differently, but the predominant pattern is that slower ETI responses are more strongly inhibited than faster ETI responses by PTI signaling in quad.

# Discussion

Previously we showed that the four signaling sectors, JA, ET, PAD4, and SA, form a strongly buffered network by compensating each other during AvrRpt2-ETI, likely resulting in network resilience (which we previously called "robustness") against perturbations from pathogen effectors (Tsuda et al, 2009). EMPIS is yet another compensating signaling sector, so the actual network is more resilient than previously understood. EMPIS is also strongly inhibited by PTI signaling, which obscured EMPIS in our previous study using bacterial delivery of AvrRpt2 (Tsuda et al, 2009). Furthermore, the ability of the experimental system to trigger PTI and ETI at separate times enabled us to show that the apparent difference in plant genetic requirements for AvrRpt2-HR and AvrRpm1-HR can be explained by a kinetic difference in ETI signaling. We also demonstrated that the inhibition of EMPIS by PTI signaling can be observed not only in the HR but also in the collective behavior of slower transcriptome responses, suggesting that this EMPIS inhibition by PTI signaling results in overall inhibition of slower ETI responses in quad. The ETI-specific, induced genes we identified in Ed-AvrRpt2 quad (Fig 5A) behaved similarly in Col-0 in response to AvrRpt2 and AvrB when the effectors were delivered from Pto (Appendix Fig S6, based on the data from Tao et al, 2003). Like AvrRpm1, AvrB is recognized by RPM1 (Bisgrove et al, 1994). The similar responses of the ETI-specific, induced genes during RPS2- and RPM1-mediated ETI suggest that the inhibition of EMPIS by PTI signaling observed with the collective behavior of slower transcriptome responses when ETI was triggered by AvrRpt2 (Fig 5B) can also be observed when ETI is triggered by AvrRpm1 as long as PTI is triggered sufficiently early (e.g., a situation similar to Fig 3B).

It should be noted that discovery and characterization of EMPIS was possible only because we applied an intensive reductionist approach to a complex signaling network. First, we reduced the immune signaling network to a network of four major signaling sectors. Use of the network scale of the four signaling sectors was justified as the simultaneous removal of the four signaling sectors almost abolished AvrRpt2-ETI (Tsuda et al, 2009): That is, at this scale the mechanism of how network resilience emerges can be dissected. Highly compromised network resilience achieved by simultaneous removal of the four signaling sectors allowed us to discover EMPIS, which is concealed in the intact wild-type network by compensations from the four other sectors.

Second, we reduced the input to the system to only pathogen effectors that are recognized by the known R proteins. Standard procedures to measure the effect of ETI use: (i) a plant genotype inoculated with a pathogenic strain or with an isogenic strain expressing an ETI-inducing effector or, (ii) a susceptible plant genotype and an isogenic genotype carrying the R gene inoculated with a pathogenic strain expressing the corresponding effector gene. Comparing the plant response to Pto EV and Pto AvrRpt2 is an example of procedure (i) (Fig 1B). In both procedures, the actual immune response events are quite complex. When plants are susceptible to the pathogen, the pathogen not only triggers PTI in the plant but also compromises PTI through the actions of various pathogen effectors (ETS; Jones & Dangl, 2006). Thus, the immune state of the susceptible plants in procedures (i) and (ii) is described by the PTI:ETS interaction. Accordingly, the immune state of the resistant plants in procedures (i) and (ii) is described as PTI: ETS + ETI + PTI:ETS:ETI. Thus, the difference between two plant immune states is ETI + PTI:ETS:ETI. For this difference to represent the ETI effect only, the PTI:ETS:ETI interaction must be negligible, which is not necessarily true. Thus, we used strain Pto D36E, which does not deliver effectors other than the ETI-triggering effector in question. This allowed us to remove the ETS effect from the system. By comparing the plant response to Pto D36E EV and Pto D36E AvrRpt2, we reduced the complexity of the comparison to PTI and PTI + ETI + PTI:ETI (Fig 1C); that is, the difference represents ETI + PTI:ETI. Using Ed-AvrRpt2 to directly express the ETItriggering effector in planta allowed us to detect the ETI effect only (Fig 1A). Since suppression of the HR phenotype in quad was observed in ETI + PTI:ETI but not in ETI only, we demonstrated that the difference in the HR suppression in quad is due to PTI:ETI and that the PTI:ETI interaction is not negligible. In addition, with Ed-AvrRpt2, we isolated ETI signaling from PTI signaling and tested for effects of PTI signaling on ETI signaling.

Furthermore, our ability to manipulate PTI and ETI signaling separately in quad background enabled us to investigate the timing effect of two antagonistic signals, inhibitory PTI and activating ETI signals, converging onto EMPIS. Genetic requirements in the plant for AvrRpt2-HR and AvrRpm1-HR are clearly different when the effectors are delivered from bacteria: AvrRpt2-HR is abolished in quad but AvrRpm1-HR is intact in quad (Tsuda et al, 2009; also compare Fig 1C and Appendix Fig S2). A conventional genetic interpretation of these results would be that AvrRpt2-HR and AvrRpm1-HR use different signaling machineries. However, discovery of EMPIS, which mediates ETI signaling but is inhibited by PTI signaling, together with the long-known fact that the HR occurs faster during AvrRpm1-ETI than AvrRpt2-ETI (Ritter & Dangl, 1996; also compare Fig 1A and D for in planta expression of the effectors) led us to a kinetic difference hypothesis: The fast AvrRpm1-ETI signal goes through EMPIS before EMPIS is inhibited by PTI signaling, but EMPIS is already inhibited by PTI signaling by the time the slow AvrRpt2-ETI signal reaches EMPIS. Consistent with a prediction made from this hypothesis, we observed that earlier MAMP treatment in quad led to inhibition of AvrRpm1-HR for both in planta expression of AvrRpm1 (Fig 3B) and bacterial delivery of AvrRpm1 (Fig 4C and D). Thus, we demonstrated that a simple hypothesis of kinetic difference can explain the genetic requirement difference without postulating a more complex hypothesis requiring different signaling machineries for similar cellular responses, ETI triggered by different effectors. This demonstration underscores the importance of network dynamics in studying the network behavior, as dynamic differences could result in qualitatively different behaviors.

Plants could not be treated with MAMPs very long before induction of in planta effector expression or inoculation of Ptoderived strains for effector delivery because PTI signaling inhibits both these effector delivery processes rather fast. Figure EV4 and Appendix Figs S4 and S5 show that MAMP treatment 3 h prior to inducer treatment or bacterial inoculation in either wild type or quad decreased both estradiol-induced effector expression and effector delivery from bacteria. The effects were more severe in wild type than quad (See Fig 4F for the reduced effect of PTI signaling on bacterial effector delivery in quad), probably because PTI signaling is impaired in quad (Kim et al, 2014). Judging from the transcriptome response, the impairment of PTI signaling in quad is relatively minor up to 9 h after flg22 treatment although the impairment 18 h after flg22 treatment was substantial [Fig EV5, based on the data GEO accession GSE78735 (Hillmer et al, 2017)]. Fortuitously, this relatively minor reduction in MAMP effects in quad at early times gave us sufficiently wide time windows to explore effects of earlier MAMP treatment.

Molecular identification of EMPIS components will be a key to furthering the current study. EMPIS should have three characteristics: (i) It can mediate ETI signaling even in the absence of the four signaling sectors, (ii) its ETI signaling function is compensated by the four signaling sectors, (iii) its ETI signaling function is inhibited by PTI signaling. We consider that a mechanism centered by MAP kinases (MPK) 3 and 6 is a good candidate for EMPIS. Previously, we showed that signaling mediated by MPK3/6 can compensate SA signaling during ETI (Tsuda et al, 2013), which possibly confers characteristics (i) and (ii). We also showed that the activation modes of MPK3/6 are distinct during PTI and ETI: transient and sustained activation, respectively (Tsuda et al, 2013). This may suggest that there is interference between two activation modes, which could confer characteristic (iii). For example, if a rapid turnoff (i.e., dephosphorylation) of MPK3/6 during PTI were due to activation of an MPK3/6 phosphatase(s), such elevated phosphatase activity could interfere with sustained activation of MPK3/6 for ETI signaling. Furthermore, the RNA-seq data analysis showed that mRNA induction of MKK4, which can activate MPK3/6 (Asai et al, 2002), was inhibited by PTI signaling in quad, which is consistent with the notion that MPK3/6 may mediate the EMPIS function. Functions of MPK3/6 are largely overlapping, and an mpk3 mpk6 double mutant is embryo lethal (Wang et al, 2007). Thus, it is technically challenging to directly test this hypothesis that MPK3 and 6 are EMPIS components. However, a recent report of a successful application of engineered MPK3 and 6 (MPK3/6SR) that are chemically inhibitable (Su et al, 2017) could open a route to directly test the hypothesis using quad mpk3 mpk6 MPK6SR plants.

Immune responses are energetically expensive (Bolton, 2009) and/or damaging to plants. Thus, unnecessary immune induction costs plant fitness (Heidel et al, 2004; Vos et al, 2013). We extend this concept to the following notion: plants may monitor the success of immune responses, and if an earlier immune response is successful, the plants abort the immune program and prevent a later response from occurring. In other words, the immune signaling output can be tuned according to the efficacy of the output. EMPIS appears to be a mechanism that monitors strength of PTI signaling and aborts the ETI program if PTI is not compromised by pathogen effectors. A similar scenario has been reported for potato ETI against potato virus X mediated by the R gene Rx (Bendahmane et al, 1999). This strong immunity is not associated with an HR when the virus is used to inoculate plants carrying Rx. However, an HR occurs when the cognate effector virus coat protein alone is directly expressed in Rx+ plants. The authors speculated that with the intact virus, later ETI responses, including the HR, were aborted when the earlier ETI response was determined to be successful based on no further accumulation of the viral coat protein.

In the intact (wild-type) signaling network, the HR signaling function of EMPIS is compensated by those of other signaling sectors, and the HR still occurs even when PTI is strong and EMPIS is inhibited. What is the advantage of having EMPIS inhibited by PTI? We speculate that other sectors are used to monitor different measures of immune response success. For example, other sectors may be used to monitor later PTI signaling and/or very different measures of immune success, such as cell integrity and overall metabolism. In this way, later ETI responses can be aborted only when all the compensating sectors are inhibited, that is, only when all different measures indicate early immune response success. This proposed requirement that multiple different criteria are met is reasonable because otherwise pathogens would easily evolve to "fake" one early immune response success measure using effectors and manipulate plants to abort ETI. Thus, in addition to resilience against network perturbations, this highly buffered network may have evolved for integration of multiple lines of information to enforce conservative decisions of whether further immune program execution should be aborted.

### Materials and Methods

#### Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana accession Col-0 was the background of all the plants used in this study. Arabidopsis quad (Tsuda et al, 2009), rpm1-3 (Grant et al, 1995), rps2-101C (Mindrinos et al, 1994), rpm1-3/rps2-101C (Mackey et al, 2003), Ed-AvrRpt2 (Tsuda et al, 2012), and Ed-AvrRpm1 (Tsuda et al, 2013) were previously described. We generated Ed-AvrRpt2 quad, Ed-AvrRpt2 quad rpm1 rps2, Ed-AvrRpm1 quad, and Ed-AvrRpm1 quad rpm1 rps2 by standard genetic crosses, tracking the mutations by PCR product length difference or cleaved amplified polymorphic sequence (CAPS) markers (Tsuda et al, 2009). Arabidopsis plants were grown in a controlled environment at 22°C with a 12-h photoperiod and 75% relative humidity.

#### Bacterial strains and preparation of inocula

Pto strains carrying the pVSP61 empty vector (EV), AvrRpt2, and AvrRpm1 were grown overnight in King's B medium supplemented with 50 lg/ml of rifampicin and 30 lg/ml of kanamycin. Pto hrcC were grown overnight in King's B medium supplemented with 50 lg/ml of rifampicin. Pto D36E EV, Pto D36E AvrRpt2, and Pto D36E AvrRpm1 were grown overnight in King's B medium supplemented with 50 lg/ml of rifampicin, 50 lg/ml of spectinomycin, and 30 lg/ml of kanamycin. Pto D36E carrying AvrRpm1sp-Cya (pNR527; Upadhyaya et al, 2014) were grown overnight in King's B medium supplemented with 50 lg/ml of rifampicin, 50 lg/ml of spectinomycin, and 50 lg/ml of gentamycin. The bacteria were harvested by centrifugation, washed, and diluted to the desired density with water.

#### Electrolyte leakage assay

Electrolyte leakage from leaves was measured essentially as described previously (Hatsugai et al, 2016). Two leaf disks with a 7.5 mm diameter were cut out using a cork borer from two leaves after bacteria or estradiol infiltration and floated in 2 ml distilled water. After 30 min, the water was exchanged to fresh water (2 ml). The conductance of water was measured with an electrical conductivity meter (B-173, Horiba) at the indicated hours after infiltration.

#### Quantitative RT–PCR (qRT–PCR) analysis

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Quantitative RT–PCR was performed using a Superscript III Platinum SYBR Green One-Step qRT-PCR kit (Invitrogen) and a Roche LightCycler480 machine. Primers for qRT–PCR are listed in Appendix Table S1. The relative log2-expression values were obtained by subtracting the C<sup>t</sup> values for each gene from the corresponding C<sup>t</sup> values of the Actin2 gene (At2g18780).

#### Cya assay

The cAMP concentration was measured essentially as described previously (Lu et al, 2015). Inoculum of Pto D36E carrying AvrRpm1sp-Cya was prepared as described above and was infiltrated into the Arabidopsis leaves. Leaf samples were collected at 0 and 24 h and were processed to extract cAMP, as described (Casper-Lindley et al, 2002). The cAMP concentration was measured with a cAMP enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (ENZO Life Sciences), following the manufacturer's protocol.

#### SDS–PAGE and immunoblotting

Protein samples in 2× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer were boiled for 5 min, separated on 15% SDS polyacrylamide gels, and blotted to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes according to the manufacturer's instructions (Bio-Rad). Membranes were incubated with 5% (wt/vol) nonfat milk dissolved in TBST buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% (vol/vol) Tween-20, pH 7.6], before incubating with anti-HA antibody (clone 3F10; Roche). After washing the membrane with TBST buffer, the secondary antibody, anti-rat immunoglobulin G (IgG) horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate (Cell Signaling), was incubated with the membrane. The blots were developed using the Pierce ECL Western blotting substrate (Pierce Biotechnology).

#### RNA-seq analysis

Two leaves per plant selected from leaf numbers 7–9 of 4-week-old Ed-AvrRpt2 quad plants were infiltrated with one of (i) mock (0.1% ethanol), (ii) 1 lM flg22, (iii) 10 lM estradiol (0.1% ethanol), or (iv) 1 lM flg22 and 10 lM estradiol (0.1% ethanol) at 3–4 h after the subjective dawn. The infiltrated leaves from two plants with the same treatment were harvested, pooled, and flash-frozen at one of 0 (untouched), 60, 120, 180, or 300 min after treatment. Three independent replicate experiments were conducted. Total RNA was extracted from each of the 60 tissue samples using TRIzol (Invitrogen). Preparation of RNA-seq libraries from total RNA was performed using the Smart-3SEQ method (Foley et al, manuscript in preparation), which was adapted from the 3SEQ method (Beck et al, 2010). The libraries were multiplexed and sequenced as 50-bp single reads on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 equipment. For each sequenced library, reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis reference genome (TAIR10) based on gene models from "Araport11\_20151202" (Cheng et al, 2017) using STAR aligner (version 2.4.2a; Dobin et al, 2013), and only uniquely mapped reads were used in the following analysis. For each gene of a library, the total read count and the read count based on unique molecular identifiers (Kivioja et al, 2012) were weighted-averaged and rounded to an integer value, which we call the read count per gene.

The read-count-per-gene data were analyzed according to Hillmer et al (2017). Genes were not included in the downstream analysis if no transcript was detected for the gene in more than nine libraries and the 15th largest transcript count value was less than 25. For the remaining 19,839 genes, if transcript count values from all three replicates were 0 for one genotype–treatment combination, the value from the library with the largest 90th percentile value was adjusted to 1. This procedure avoided over-estimation of fold-change values for genes with undetectable expression in some genotype–treatment combinations. For each gene, a negative binomial generalized linear model (glm-nb) with the log-link function was applied to estimate mean expression values and standard errors using the glm.nb function in the MASS package in the R environment (version 3.3.1; Venables & Ripley, 2002). Within this model, for each library, the log-ratio of the expression value at the 90th percentile relative to the maximum 90th percentile value among all the libraries was used as an offset to normalize the read counts across the libraries. Comparisons of gene expression between genotypes or between treatments were conducted by ztest with log2-scaled mean values and standard errors estimated from the glm-nb. The two-tailed P-values derived from all the comparisons were adjusted to q-values through a false discovery rate (FDR)-based multiple testing correction procedure (Storey & Tibshirani, 2003).

#### GO enrichment analysis

The GO enrichment analysis in the entire Arabidopsis genome context was performed using the tool at [http://www.geneontology.](http://www.geneontology.org/page/go-enrichment-analysis) [org/page/go-enrichment-analysis](http://www.geneontology.org/page/go-enrichment-analysis), which used the PANTHER Overrepresentation Test (release 20170413) and the GO annotation by The Arabidopsis Information Resources (Gene Ontology Consortium validation date, May 5, 2017). A binomial test is used in The PANTHER Overrepresentation Test (Mi et al, 2013), and the obtained P-value was Bonferroni-corrected across the GO terms of the domain for each gene set. For the GO enrichment test comparing the lower and upper 25% of the ETI-specific, induced genes at 300 min according to the (PTI&ETI – ETI) value and the test for the MKKs, CRKs, or RLPs within the "kinase activity" genes, two-tailed Fisher's exact test was used. The P-values from the former tests were Bonferroni-corrected across the selected GO terms, and the uncorrected P-values are given for the latter tests.
