#### 41 **ABSTRACT**

42

43 The branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) leucine, isoleucine and valine are among nine 44 essential amino acids that must be obtained from the diet of humans and other animals, 45 and can be nutritionally limiting in plant foods. Despite genetic evidence of its 46 importance in regulating seed amino acid levels, the full BCAA catabolic network is not 47 completely understood in plants, and limited information is available regarding its 48 regulation. In this study, transcript coexpression analyses revealed positive correlations 49 among BCAA catabolism genes in stress, development, diurnal/circadian and light 50 datasets. A core subset of BCAA catabolism genes, including those encoding putative 51 branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase subunits, is highly expressed during the night in 52 plants on a diel cycle and in prolonged darkness. Mutants defective in these subunits 53 accumulate higher levels of BCAAs in mature seeds, providing genetic evidence for their 54 function in BCAA catabolism. In addition, prolonged dark treatment caused the mutants 55 to undergo senescence early and over-accumulate leaf BCAAs. These results extend the 56 previous evidence that BCAAs can be catabolized and serve as respiratory substrates at 57 multiple steps. Moreover, comparison of amino acid profiles between mature seeds and 58 dark-treated leaves revealed differences in amino acid accumulation when BCAA 59 catabolism is perturbed. Together, these results demonstrate the consequences of 60 blocking BCAA catabolism during both normal growth conditions and under energy-61 limited conditions.

#### 63 **INTRODUCTION**

64

65 The branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) Leu, Ile and Val are among nine 66 amino acids essential for humans and other animals because they cannot be synthesized 67 *de novo* (Harper et al., 1984). Plants synthesize BCAAs and are the main source of these 68 essential nutrients in the diets of humans and agriculturally important animals. In addition 69 to their nutritional value, BCAAs and BCAA-derived metabolites such as glucosinolates, 70 fatty acids and acyl sugars, contribute to plant growth, development, defense and flavor 71 (Mikkelsen and Halkier, 2003; Taylor et al., 2004; Ishizaki et al., 2005; Slocombe et al., 72 2008; Araujo et al., 2010; Ding et al., 2012; Kochevenko et al., 2012).

73 The BCAA biosynthetic pathway and its regulation have been investigated in 74 *Arabidopsis thaliana* and other plants for the past two decades, in large part because of 75 the commercial importance of herbicides that inhibit acetohydroxy acid synthase, which 76 is the committing enzyme of BCAA biosynthesis (Singh and Shaner, 1995; Aubert et al., 77 1997; Singh, 1999; McCourt et al., 2006; Tan et al., 2006; Binder, 2010; Chen et al., 78 2010; Yu et al., 2010). Strong correlations between the levels of free BCAAs were found 79 in wild-type *A. thaliana* seeds and tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*) fruits (Schauer et al., 80 2006; Lu et al., 2008), which suggests co-regulation of biosynthesis and/or degradation. 81 This presumably is due - at least in part - to the fact that they share four common 82 biosynthetic enzymes and three catabolic steps. However, despite long-term interest in 83 the desirability of optimizing the content of these essential amino acids in plants, the 84 genes and proteins that constitute the full BCAA catabolic network are not completely 85 characterized in *A. thaliana* or any other plant, and there is much to learn about the 86 genetic and biochemical regulation of this process.

87 Recent observations using mutants blocked in BCAA catabolism (Figure 1) 88 indicate that the regulation of seed amino acid metabolism has unexpected, and 89 potentially important, features. Previous work revealed that mutants defective in the *A.*  90 *thaliana* mitochondrial enzymes branched-chain aminotransferase 2 (BCAT2), 91 isovaleryl-CoA dehydrogenase (IVD), α (MCCA) and β (MCCB) subunits of 3- 92 methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase, and hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA lyase (HML) exhibit 93 increases in all three free BCAAs in mature seeds (Gu et al., 2010; Lu et al., 2011;

94 Angelovici et al., 2013). While it is logical that defects in the enzymes early in the 95 catabolic pathway (the *bcat2* and *ivd1* mutants) would cause accumulation of Leu, Ile and 96 Val, it was not expected that mutants blocked in three enzymes specific to Leu

97 degradation (*mcca1*, *mccb1* and *hml1*) would also accumulate Ile and Val. Even more 98 surprising is that biosynthetically unrelated amino acids - including His and Arg - 99 accumulate to higher levels in seeds of *ivd1*, *mcca1-1*, *mccb1-1* and *hml1* mutants 100 compared to the wild type (Gu et al., 2010; Lu et al., 2011). This suggests that the *A.*  101 *thaliana* amino acid networks are more interconnected than previously thought, and 102 reveals that there are important gaps in our knowledge of the regulation of amino acid 103 metabolism.

104 Recent studies in *A. thaliana* revealed that BCAA catabolism plays physiological 105 roles beyond maintaining free amino acid homeostasis (Ishizaki et al., 2005; Araujo et al., 106 2010). In addition to catalyzing the third step in the degradation of BCAAs, IVD helps 107 plants survive under energy-limited conditions by serving as a source of electrons for the 108 mitochondrial electron transport chain via the electron-transfer flavoprotein α and β 109 subunits (ETFα and ETFβ) and the electron-transfer flavoprotein ubiquinone 110 oxidoreductase (ETFQO) (Figure 1). Two lines of evidence for this role are that the *ivd1-* 111 *2* mutant becomes senescent faster than wild type in prolonged darkness, and mutants 112 defective in ETFβ and ETFQO accumulate more free BCAAs and the IVD substrate 113 isovaleryl-CoA (Ishizaki et al., 2005; Ishizaki et al., 2006; Araujo et al., 2010). In 114 addition, the transcripts of the functionally validated BCAA catabolism genes *BCAT2*, 115 *IVD1*, *MCCA1* and *MCCB1* rapidly increase following transition from light to dark, and 116 this increase is inhibited by sucrose (Fujiki et al., 2000; Che et al., 2002; Binder, 2010; 117 Angelovici et al., 2013). These observations suggest that IVD and other BCAA catabolic 118 enzymes contribute to plant fitness under energy-limited conditions.

119 Although genetic and biochemical evidence exists for the participation of *A.* 120 *thaliana* enzymes BCAT2, IVD, MCCA, MCCB and HML in BCAA catabolism (Gu et 121 al., 2010; Lu et al., 2011; Ding et al., 2012; Angelovici et al., 2013), much less is known 122 about the genes and encoded proteins for the branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase 123 (BCKDH) complex. Published biochemical evidence demonstrated the BCKDH complex 124 enzyme activity in isolated *A. thaliana* mitochondria (Taylor et al., 2004). The better 125 characterized mammalian BCKDH is comprised of multiple copies of three proteins: the 126 α-ketoacid dehydrogenase/carboxylase E1 (E1α and E1β), dihydrolipoyl acyltransferase 127 E2, and dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase E3 (also known as the mitochondrial lipoamide 128 dehydrogenase, mtLPD) (Mooney et al., 2002). This multi-mega-Dalton molecular mass 129 complex catalyzes the second step of BCAA degradation, converting branched-chain 130 ketoacids (intermediates in BCAA biosynthesis and catabolism) into acyl-CoA esters 131 (Figure 1). The complex is homologous to mitochondrial and chloroplastic pyruvate 132 dehydrogenase complexes, and mitochondrial α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex, 133 and shares enzyme subunits with the Gly decarboxylase complex (Oliver, 1994; Mooney 134 et al., 2002). In addition, the BCKDH complex is hypothesized to contribute to the 135 mitochondrial electron transport by providing NADH to complex I and the internal 136 alternative NADH dehydrogenases (Schertl and Braun, 2014).

137 The size of the BCKDH complex has hindered a detailed *in vitro* characterization 138 in plants, and identification of the plant BCKDH complex subunits is based upon 139 sequence annotation rather than functional analysis. Pairs of paralogous genes are 140 annotated as encoding *A. thaliana* E1α, E1β and E3 subunits, and one gene is annotated 141 for E2. These assignments are based on: 1) sequence similarity with proteins identified 142 from other organisms, especially mammals, and 2) mitochondrial localization evidence 143 using tandem mass spectrometry, rather than enzyme activities (Fujiki et al., 2000; 144 Mooney et al., 2000; Taylor et al., 2004).

145 We describe results from a functional genomics analysis of genes annotated as 146 encoding subunits of the BCKDH complex. A group of eight validated or hypothesized 147 BCAA catabolic genes were found to be coexpressed. Mutants in these genes accumulate 148 higher levels of BCAAs in seeds and have enhanced senescence and increased amino 149 acid accumulation in leaves during prolonged darkness. These data provide experimental 150 evidence for the participation of BCKDH subunit genes *E1A1*, *E1A2*, *E1B1*, *E1B2* and *E2* 151 in BCAA catabolism, reinforcing the importance of BCAA catabolism in regulating 152 amino acid homeostasis under day-night cycles and prolonged darkness, and during seed 153 development. These results are consistent with the hypothesis that the *A. thaliana* BCAA 154 catabolic network interacts with energy metabolism at multiple steps.

# 159 **Known and Hypothesized** *A. thaliana* **BCAA Catabolism Genes Form a** 160 **Coexpression Module**

161 Transcripts of the functionally validated *A. thaliana* BCAA catabolism genes 162 *BCAT2*, *IVD1*, *MCCA1* and *MCCB1* were reported to increase rapidly following the 163 transition from light to dark (Daschner et al., 2001; Fujiki et al., 2001; Schuster and 164 Binder, 2005; Araujo et al., 2010; Angelovici et al., 2013). These results led us to 165 hypothesize that candidate genes encoding additional enzymes in BCAA catabolism 166 could be identified by their coexpression with these known genes. Coexpression analysis 167 was performed with 13 genes encoding proteins proposed (based on sequence similarity) 168 or experimentally validated to participate in BCAA catabolism (the gene list is shown in 169 Table I). First, to determine the extent of coexpression among these genes, pairwise 170 Pearson's Correlation Coefficients (PCCs) were calculated, and average-linkage 171 hierarchical clustering was performed with four datasets: development, abiotic and biotic 172 stress, and light datasets from AtGenExpress (Schmid et al., 2005; Kilian et al., 2007), 173 and the diurnal/circadian dataset from the DIURNAL database (Mockler et al., 2007) 174 (Figure 2A; Supplemental Table S1).

175 The degrees of coexpression among the five validated BCAA catabolism 'bait' 176 genes (*BCAT2*, *IVD1*, *MCCA1*, *MCCB1* and *HML1*) were evaluated to establish the 177 foundation for our coexpression analyses. In the stress dataset, all five bait genes form a 178 coexpression module with significant expression correlations (Figure 2; Supplemental 179 Figure S1). In the other three datasets (development, diurnal/circadian and light), four out 180 of the five bait genes form coexpression modules. These modules (one from each dataset) 181 contain three common bait members - *BCAT2*, *IVD1* and *MCCA1* - with *MCCB1* in only 182 the diurnal/circadian and the light modules, and *HML1* only in the development module 183 (Figure 2A; Supplemental Figure S1). Because of the significant expression correlations 184 among bait genes, these results indicate the feasibility of this approach for identifying 185 candidate genes in the BCAA catabolic pathway. We next evaluated coexpression of the 186 eight proposed BCAA catabolism genes with the five bait genes (dotted squares in Figure 187 2A; Supplemental Figure S1). The stress coexpression module contains the largest

188 number of members: five proposed genes in addition to the five bait genes. Furthermore,

- 189 four genes proposed to encode proteins of BCAA catabolism and four validated bait
- 190 genes are in the light and the diurnal/circadian coexpression modules. In contrast, none of

191 the proposed genes is in the development module that contains four bait genes. In 192 summary, highly interconnected modules containing validated and proposed BCAA 193 catabolic enzyme genes emerged from three datasets: stress, diurnal/circadian, and light. 194 These modules have eight members in common: the validated bait genes *BCAT2*, *IVD1*, 195 *MCCA1* and *MCCB1*, and proposed BCKDH genes *E1A1*, *E1B1*, *E1B2* and *E2* (Figure 196 2A; Supplemental Figure S1; Supplemental Table S1).

197 The observation that four out of five bait genes - and four out of eight proposed 198 BCAA catabolic enzyme genes - are strongly coexpressed in the diurnal/circadian dataset 199 (Figure 2A; Supplemental Figure S1), suggests that these genes are coordinately 200 regulated by light and the circadian clock. Further examination of transcript profiles 201 confirmed common oscillation patterns among these eight validated or proposed BCAA 202 catabolism genes under most diurnal/circadian experiments (Supplemental Figure S2). It 203 is notable that these gene transcript levels are reduced during the day, and increased 204 during the night in the rosette leaves of 4-week-old, short-day-grown (8h light/16h dark) 205 plants (Figure 2B). This common oscillation pattern is consistent with the previous 206 observation that free BCAA levels fluctuate in day/night cycles and peak towards the end 207 of the day (Gibon et al., 2006; Espinoza et al., 2010), suggesting that up-regulation of the 208 catabolism genes contributes to the decreased free BCAAs at night. In addition, the eight 209 transcripts also oscillate in a similar fashion in constant light after entrainment in 210 light/dark or hot/cold cycles (Supplemental Figure S2), consistent with the hypothesis 211 that BCAA catabolism is subject to regulation by the circadian clock. These results 212 further demonstrated the coexpression of BCAA catabolic enzyme gene transcripts in 213 diurnal/circadian conditions, especially on a day/night cycle.

#### 215 **Distinct Expression Patterns among BCAA Catabolic Enzyme Paralogs**

216 There are four pairs of paralogous genes encoding known or proposed enzymes in 217 the first two steps of BCAA catabolism (Table I). However, it is not known whether all 218 paralogs participate in BCAA catabolic process because BCAT activities are involved 219 both in BCAA biosynthesis and degradation (Diebold et al., 2002), and some BCKDH 220 subunits are similar to those of pyruvate dehydrogenases in mitochondria and 221 chloroplasts, and mitochondrial α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and Gly decarboxylase 222 (Oliver, 1994; Lutziger and Oliver, 2001; Mooney et al., 2002). We hypothesized that 223 genes encoding these BCAA degradation enzyme paralogs would be coexpressed with 224 the documented catabolic enzymes because of their significant expression correlations 225 revealed from our previous coexpression analyses (Figure 2A; Supplemental Figure S1). 226 Indeed, four out of eight paralogs - *BCAT2* and BCKDH subunits *E1A1*, *E1B1* and *E1B2* 227 - are members of the coexpression modules identified from the light and the 228 diurnal/circadian datasets (Figure 2A). This observation suggested that these proteins 229 have roles in BCAA catabolism.

230 Expression divergence is often observed among paralogous gene pairs that can be 231 due to modification of regulatory elements during evolution (Shariati and De Strooper, 232 2013), and likely results in function diversification (Stern and Orgogozo, 2008). To 233 evaluate the degree of expression divergence, we compared the expression profiles of the 234 four paralogous pairs. The flowering and seed development datasets were included in this 235 analysis because the known BCAA catabolism genes *BCAT2*, *IVD1* and *MCCA1* and 236 *HML1* have increased transcript levels during flowering and seed development 237 (http://bar.utoronto.ca/efp/cgi-bin/efpWeb.cgi). In addition, data for the short-day growth 238 (8h light/16h dark) experiments were also selected because of the highly coordinated 239 transcript oscillations described above (Figure 2B). All four pairs of paralogs were found 240 to have divergent expression profiles in these datasets that followed two general patterns 241 (Figure 3). First, expression of paralogs *BCAT1/2*, *E1A1/2* and *E1B1/2* was anti-242 correlated during the late stages of seed development (Figure 3, left panel; PCC value *r*=- 243 0.98, -0.55, -0.87, respectively, for the last four time points), suggesting more important 244 roles in seed amino acid regulation for the isoforms encoded by the more highly-245 expressed transcripts. Varying degrees of anti-correlation were also found for *BCAT1/2*, 246 *E1A1/2* and *mtLPD1/2* under short day (Figure 3, right panel; *r*=-0.41, -0.82, -0.32, 247 respectively). The second pattern of expression divergence is illustrated by *E1A1/2*, 248 *E1B1/2* and *mtLPD1/2* under short day growth conditions, and *mtLPD1/2* during 249 development, where one copy is expressed at a higher level than the other (Figure 3) 250 (Ganko et al., 2007; Zou et al., 2009). Among these pairs, the expression profiles of *E1B1* 251 and *E1B2* are most similar (*r*=0.66). In summary, examples of divergent expression 252 profiles of paralogs can be distinguished in the development or short day diurnal cycling

253 datasets, indicating divergence in mRNA regulation. The differences in expression 254 patterns suggest a possible functional divergence between the paralogs. In addition, this 255 expression divergence under short day also explains why *BCAT1*, *E1A2*, *mtLPD1* and 256 *mtLPD2* - genes proposed for BCAA catabolism - are not found in the coexpression 257 module in the diurnal/circadian dataset (Figure 2; Supplemental Figure S1).

#### 259 **Double Mutants of BCKDH E1**α **Subunits Accumulate Increased Seed Free BCAAs**

260 The coexpression analyses led to the hypothesis that the putative BCKDH E1α1, 261 E1β1, E1β2 and E2 subunits, which are co-regulated with the known BCAA catabolic 262 enzymes, function in BCAA degradation (Figure 2A). Because *ivd1-2*, *mcca1-1*, *mccb1-* 263 *1*, *hml1* (Gu et al., 2010; Lu et al., 2011) and *bcat2* mutants (Angelovici et al., 2013) have 264 increased free BCAAs in mature seeds, we hypothesized that mutants defective in *bona*  265 *fide* BCKDH subunits would share this phenotype.

266 To test this idea, free BCAA content of mature dry seed was examined in 267 homozygous mutants of proposed BCAA catabolic enzyme genes (Figure 4). 268 Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that *e1a1-1*, *e1a1-2*, *e1a2-1* and *e1b2-1* behave as 269 null alleles, while *mtlpd2-2* showed approximately 50% reduction of the *mtLPD2* 270 transcripts relative to wild type (Supplemental Figure S3). Unfortunately, no homozygous 271 T-DNA mutant lines could be identified for *E1B1*, *E2* or *mtLPD1*. The positive control 272 mutants *bcat1-1*, *bcat2-1*, *ivd1-2*, *mcca1-1*, *mccb1-1*, and *hml1-2* (Figure 4; Supplemental 273 Table S2) displayed BCAA changes similar to those previously reported (Gu et al., 2010; 274 Lu et al., 2011; Angelovici et al., 2013). Some of the single mutants defective in the 275 putative BCKDH subunits yielded small but significant (p<0.05) changes in free BCAAs. 276 For example, both *e1a1* mutants and *e1b2-1* had modest increases (1.5 to 5.8 fold relative 277 to the wild type) in Ile and Val, or all three BCAAs (Figure 4; Supplemental Table S2). 278 We tested the hypothesis that partial genetic buffering was responsible for the modest 279 increases in the single mutants by examining free seed BCAA levels in *e1a1-1*; *e1a2-1* 280 and *e1a1-2*; *e1a2-1* double mutants, defective in both genes annotated as encoding E1α 281 subunits. Consistent with this hypothesis, both double mutants had large and statistically 282 significant (p<0.05) increases in seed free BCAAs: 45-, 22- and nine-fold increases for 283 Leu, Ile and Val, respectively (Figure 4; Supplemental Table S2). These data support the 284 hypothesis that E1α1 and E1α2 contribute to the degradation of BCAAs during seed 285 development.

286 Because no null *e1b1* mutants were available, constructs encoding artificial 287 microRNAs (amiRNAs) targeting *E1B1* were transformed into the *e1b2-1* mutant 288 background in an attempt to reduce expression of both paralogous E1β subunit genes 289 (Supplemental Figure S4). True breeding homozygous T3 transgenic lines from two 290 independent primary transformants had *E1B1* transcript reduction to ~30% of the wild

291 type level (Supplemental Figure S4A). The free amino acid content from the seeds of 292 homozygous T3 lines exhibited moderate but significant (p<0.05) BCAA increases 293 compared with wild type (Supplemental Figure S4B). Thus, modest reduction of the 294 *E1B1* transcript in the *e1b2-1* mutant background results in more seed free BCAAs. This 295 supports the hypothesis that both E1β enzyme paralogs participate in BCAA catabolism 296 during seed development. Genetic analysis of the role of mtLPDs was not possible since 297 no null alleles were available for *mtLPD1* or *mtLPD2*.

#### 299 **BCAA Catabolic Mutants Exhibit Early Senescence under Prolonged Darkness**

300 The increased seed free BCAAs in the mutant and amiRNA lines deficient in 301 *E1A1*, *E1A2*, *E1B1* and *E1B2* support the hypothesis that the tested genes encode proteins 302 of BCAA catabolism. Published studies revealed that transcripts of the known BCAA 303 catabolism genes *BCAT2*, *IVD1*, *MCCA1* and *MCCB1* increase following transition from 304 light to dark in plants grown under light-dark conditions and during prolonged darkness

305 (Daschner et al., 2001; Fujiki et al., 2001; Schuster and Binder, 2005; Araujo et al.,

306 2010). We used RNA-Seq analysis to ask whether the transcripts for the proposed 307 BCKDH subunits are also upregulated in plants subjected to prolonged darkness. These 308 experiments were performed using rosette leaves from 5-week-old, short-day-grown (8h

309 light/16h dark) Col-0 wild-type plants moved to constant darkness for 6h, 24h, 48h and 310 72h (Figure 5). The transcripts of the eight genes found in the coexpression modules 311 derived from stress, light and diurnal/circadian datasets (Supplemental Figure S1) were 312 increased nine- to 400-fold within the first 6h of prolonged darkness, and remained high 313 until the last time point (gene names highlighted in blue in Figure 5). These results are 314 consistent with the hypothesis that BCAA catabolic enzymes - including BCKDH 315 subunits E1A1, E1B1, E1B2 and E2 - have one or more physiological roles in the dark.

316 To explore this idea, BCAA catabolic mutant leaf morphology was monitored 317 during prolonged darkness for 15 days. Prior to dark treatment, all of the lines being 318 assessed were green and showed normal rosette leaf morphology (Figure 6; Supplemental 319 Figure S5). As previously described for *ivd1-2* (Araujo et al., 2010), we found that 320 mutants defective in the other validated Leu catabolic enzyme genes - *MCCA1*, *MCCB1* 321 and *HML1* - exhibited enhanced dark-induced senescence phenotypes (Figure 6A; 322 Supplemental Figure S5). These results demonstrated that early leaf senescence is 323 observed in mutants blocked from the middle to the end of the Leu catabolism pathway. 324 We asked whether the putative BCKDH subunit mutants had abnormal dark-induced 325 senescence. The two mutants defective in the putative E1A1 subunit gene that is 326 upregulated in the dark (*e1a1-1* and *e1a1-2*) showed early dark-induced senescence 327 (Figure 6A; Supplemental Figure S5). Double mutants that combine these mutations with 328 the loss of function allele for the paralogous gene (*e1a1-1; e1a2-1* and *e1a1-2; e1a2-1*

329 double mutants) exhibited senescence phenotypes similar to the *e1a1* single mutants. 330 These results are not surprising because – in contrast to the dark inducibility of *E1A1* 331 transcript – *E1A2* mRNA is not induced in the dark (Figure 5). In fact the *e1a2-1* single 332 mutant did not exhibit enhanced dark-induced senescence compared to the wild type 333 control, even after 15 days. Two other single mutants, *bcat2-1* and *e1b2-1*, also did not 334 exhibit enhanced senescence. To assess viability following the 15-day dark treatment, 335 plants were transferred back to 8h light/16h dark photoperiod and examined for new 336 growth after one week. All genotypes recovered from this prolonged dark treatment 337 except for *ivd1-2*, which had dry and yellow leaves at the end of the prolonged dark 338 treatment (Figure 6A; Supplemental Figure S5). These results demonstrate that, in 339 addition to *IVD1*, the disruption of *E1A1*, *MCCA1*, *MCCB1* or *HML1* also results in early 340 senescence in prolonged darkness.

341 To complement the analysis of gross morphological changes, the maximum 342 photochemical efficiency of Photosystem II (Fv/Fm) was used to quantify leaf senescence 343 caused by prolonged darkness (Oh et al., 1997). Mutant chlorophyll fluorescence was 344 indistinguishable from that of wild type prior to dark treatment (Figure 6B, day 0). In 345 contrast, on day nine the mutants that showed early senescence also exhibited statistically 346 significantly lower Fv/Fm values relative to the corresponding wild type (Figure 6B).

#### 348 **Enhanced Leaf Free Leu, Ile and Val Accumulation during Prolonged Darkness**

349 The *ivd1-2* mutant was demonstrated to have increased leaf free BCAAs in 350 prolonged darkness (Araujo et al., 2010). To test whether other mutants defective in 351 BCAA catabolism also show enhanced BCAA accumulation, free rosette leaf amino acid 352 content was analyzed in wild type and catabolic mutants subjected to prolonged darkness. 353 All three BCAAs increased in dark-treated wild-type plants at all three time points, and 354 further increases due to blocked BCAA catabolism were seen for some combinations of 355 mutants and amino acids (Figure 7; Supplemental Table S3). The effects of all mutations, 356 except for *e1a2* and *e1b2*, were strongest and most consistent for Leu (eight to 14 fold 357 increases compared with wild type at day nine), with the least significant increases in Ile. 358 Of the mutants, *e1a2-1* and *e1b2-1* displayed the least significant increases compared to 359 wild type and this is especially obvious for Leu and Val. The small impact of these 360 mutations is reminiscent of the results with free seed amino acid changes (Figure 4; 361 Supplemental Table S2). The *E1B1*-silenced *e1b2-1* plants were also evaluated for leaf 362 amino acid content after six days in prolonged darkness. The homozygous T3 lines from 363 two independent primary transformants showed stronger BCAA increases compared with 364 the *e1b2-1* single mutant and wild type (Supplemental Figure S4C). In summary, the 365 BCAA increases in the mutants support the hypothesized roles of E1α1, E1β1 and E1β2 366 BCKDH subunits in BCAA catabolism - especially in Leu degradation - during 367 prolonged darkness.

368 Taken together, analysis of the mutants and amiRNA lines demonstrated that 369 disruption of BCAT2, E1α1, E1β1, E1β2, IVD, MCCA, MCCB, HML enzymes led to 370 early senescence and/or increased leaf free BCAAs in prolonged darkness. These results 371 further support the hypothesis that the full BCAA catabolic pathway plays a role in plant

- 372 survival under carbon-limited conditions. These data also add to the evidence that *E1A1*,
- 373 *E1B1* and *E1B2* encode subunits of the BCKDH complex.

# 375 **Beyond Leu, Ile and Val: Blocking Branched-Chain Ketoacid Dehydrogenase** 376 **Causes Broad Changes in Leaf and Seed Amino Acids**

377 In addition to accumulating seed free BCAAs, *ivd1-2*, *mcca1-1*, *mccb1-2* and 378 *hml1-2* mutants were previously shown to have increased levels of biosynthetically 379 seemingly unrelated amino acids, with increased Arg and His common to all (Gu et al., 380 2010; Lu et al., 2011). We asked whether this inter-pathway phenomenon was observed 381 in mutants annotated as defective in BCKDH subunit genes (Figure 8; Supplemental 382 Tables S3 and S4). The most obvious sign of cross-pathway seed amino acid 383 accumulation was seen in the *e1a1-1; e1a2-1* and *e1a1-2; e1a2-1* double mutants, which 384 had two to seven fold and statistically significant (p<0.001) increases in Arg, His, Met 385 and Ser (Figure 8A; Supplemental Table S2). The *e1b2-1* mutant also had more modest, 386 but quite widespread increases in multiple seed amino acids, despite the presence of the 387 intact paralogous *E1B1* gene. As previously reported for mutants blocked later in the 388 pathway (Gu et al., 2010; Lu et al., 2011), high seed His was a hallmark of all the 389 putative BCKDH subunit mutants, with the exception of *e1a2-1*. The positive control 390 *ivd1-2* mutant showed the expected highly pleiotropic amino acid changes in both seeds 391 and leaves by the end of the dark treatment: it exhibited significant increases for 16 out of 392 19 amino acids detected in seeds as was previously reported (Gu et al., 2010; Lu et al., 393 2011) (Figure 8A; Supplemental Table S2), and for 17 out of 19 leaf amino acids by the 394 end of the nine-day dark treatment (Figure 8B; Supplemental Table S3). The *ivd1-2* 395 mutant displayed severe dehydration and senescence when tissue was extracted after 9d 396 in the dark. Thus, the data from 6d dark treated *ivd1-2* mutant plants - which is similar to 397 the 9d data in Figure 8B (Supplemental Table S4) - is likely to be more reliable. With the 398 possible exception of increased Asp and Met, dark-induced leaf amino acid changes were 399 less consistent across the mutants in BCKDH and in other enzymes of BCAA catabolism. 400

402 **DISCUSSION** 

403

404 Advances in genomics technologies create new opportunities to identify candidate 405 genes and proteins for complex physiological and biochemical processes. This has led to 406 a situation where it is relatively inexpensive and straightforward to mine data and create 407 hypotheses, while testing theories is often the costly and time consuming step. Analysis 408 of components of large multi-subunit enzymes creates a special challenge requiring 409 expression of multiple subunits, assembly of the component parts and establishment of an 410 *in vitro* activity assay that faithfully represents the *in vivo* process. The plant megadalton, 411 multi-subunit BCKDH enzyme is an example of such a complex. Here, we took a 412 systems approach to hypothesize functions of putative BCKDH subunits through a 413 combination of sequence similarity, transcript coexpression analyses, and mutant 414 characterization. We provided evidence for the participation of putative BCKDH subunits 415 in BCAA catabolism, and demonstrated examples of partial genetic buffering and 416 functional divergence between enzyme paralogs. Moreover, the data revealed that BCAA 417 catabolic mutants defective at multiple steps in the pathway exhibit early senescence with 418 high leaf free BCAAs in prolonged darkness, supporting a previous hypothesis that 419 BCAA degradation generates alternative sources of energy in plants under energy-limited 420 conditions (Ishizaki et al., 2005; Araujo et al., 2010).

# 422 **Considerations in using transcript coexpression analysis in gene functional** 423 **characterization**

424 There are important considerations in using gene expression analysis to develop 425 or test hypotheses regarding gene function. First, it is essential to have a set of validated 426 'bait' genes for the analysis; in this study five bait genes of BCAA catabolism were 427 employed (Table I). Second, success of the approach requires that appropriate transcript 428 profiling data be available. In this study we explored multiple microarray datasets (Figure 429 2 and Supplemental Table S1); these included a developmental dataset, with a variety of 430 tissues ('development'), plants grown under differing light and temperature regimes 431 ('diurnal/circadian' and 'light'), as well as abiotic and biotic stress conditions ('stress'). 432 The broad choice of types of datasets was important in this study because each yielded 433 coexpression modules of different sizes and structures (Supplemental Figure S1). For 434 example, analysis of the stress dataset produced a module with the largest number of 435 nodes (10), while the diurnal/circadian dataset yielded a smaller (eight) but more highly 436 connected module (Supplemental Figure S1). In contrast, the development dataset 437 provided the least information, with a weakly connected module of four bait genes that 438 had no significant expression correlation with the proposed BCAA catabolic enzyme 439 genes (Supplemental Figure S1). Taken together, our results demonstrate the importance 440 of using validated bait genes and different types of expression data to identify candidate 441 genes.

442 Coexpression analysis can be confounded in a number of ways. One scenario is 443 when paralogous genes exist, with one copy that actively responds to environmental 444 perturbations, while the other generally expresses at a much lower level (Keith et al., 445 1991; Duarte et al., 2006; Ganko et al., 2007; Szekely et al., 2008; Zou et al., 2009). For 446 example, we found that the E1α subunit gene *E1A1* coexpressed with the other core 447 BCAA catabolic genes, while the paralog *E1A2* did not (Figure 2A; Supplemental Figure 448 S1). Consistent with different roles for the two paralogous genes, the *e1a1* mutants had 449 high leaf BCAAs under prolonged dark conditions, while the *e1a2-1* mutant did not. In 450 contrast, both E1α subunit genes are required to maintain BCAA homeostasis in seeds 451 (Figure 4; Figure 7), emphasizing that individual paralogs can have diverse roles 452 throughout development and in response to the environment.

453 Using coexpression analysis to develop hypotheses regarding function also can be 454 confounded if the protein product participates in multiple different processes. For 455 example, mtLPD is proposed to function as a subunit of the mitochondrial pyruvate 456 dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, Gly decarboxylase and BCKDH 457 complexes (Lutziger and Oliver, 2001). Needs for this subunit in physiologically diverse 458 processes might be responsible for the observed lack of co-regulation with other known 459 and proposed genes of BCAA degradation (Figure 2A; Supplemental Figure S1). In 460 addition, the single-copy and experimentally validated hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA lyase 461 gene *HML1* was coexpressed with other BCAA catabolic genes in the stress and the 462 development datasets (Figure 2A; Supplemental Figure S1). We hypothesize that lack of 463 membership of *HML1* mRNA in the light and the diurnal/circadian coexpression modules 464 reflects that this enzyme participates in other metabolic processes (Ashmarina et al., 465 1994; Ashmarina et al., 1999). Alternatively, perhaps this and other non-coregulated 466 BCAA catabolic enzymes are subject to post-transcriptional regulation.

#### 468 **Evidence that BCAA catabolism and energy metabolism interact at multiple steps**

469 In this study, we demonstrated that eight out of the 13 proposed or validated 470 BCAA catabolism genes are coexpressed and share common transcript oscillation 471 patterns in diurnal and circadian treatments (Figure 2; Supplemental Figure S2). These 472 findings are consistent with the observed fluctuation of free BCAA levels on diel cycles 473 (Gibon et al., 2006; Espinoza et al., 2010), and demonstrate the physiological importance 474 of BCAA catabolism at night. BCAAs are proposed to provide their downstream 475 catabolic products - acetoacetate, acetyl-CoA and propionyl-CoA - to the tricarboxylic 476 acid (TCA) cycle for energy generation (Figure 1) (Anderson et al., 1998). In addition, 477 Mentzen and coworkers, identified a coexpression supermodule capable of maintaining 478 cellular energy balance via catabolism using coexpression analysis with microarray data 479 from 70 experiments (Mentzen et al., 2008). This supermodule contained genes encoding 480 enzymes in the catabolism of amino acids (including BCAAs), carbohydrates, lipids and 481 cell wall components. Despite the proposed role of BCAA catabolism in energy 482 generation, no vegetative or reproductive phenotype was observed for BCAA catabolic 483 mutants grown in photoperiods with 16h, 12h or 8h light (Lu et al., 2011, and day 0 in 484 Figure 6 and Supplemental Figure S5 of this study), in contrast to the reported aberrant 485 reproductive development of the *mcca1* and *mccb1* mutants (Ding et al., 2012). Our 486 results suggest a limited role of BCAA catabolism in providing TCA cycle substrates in 487 day/night cycling conditions (Lu et al., 2011 and this study).

488 While diurnal regulation is more physiologically relevant, the prolonged darkness 489 assay is experimentally convenient for studying the regulation of energy metabolism and 490 interacting metabolic processes in plants. Success using such an approach was previously 491 demonstrated in identifying and characterizing mutants defective in genes participating in 492 energy-related processes including autophagy, mitochondrial electron transport chain and 493 starch metabolism (Gibon et al., 2004; Ishizaki et al., 2005; Ishizaki et al., 2006; Liu and 494 Bassham, 2012). During energy-limited conditions, autophagy was demonstrated to 495 promote organelle degradation including chloroplasts, partially contributing to the 496 increase of free amino acid pools including free BCAAs in vegetative tissues (Hanaoka et 497 al., 2002; Wada et al., 2009; Izumi et al., 2010). Consistent with this idea, we observed 498 dramatic free amino acid increases including BCAAs, aromatic amino acids, Lys, His, 499 Asn and Arg in the wild type within three days in prolonged darkness (Supplemental 500 Table S3). In addition, Lys metabolism was previously demonstrated to interact with 501 plant energy metabolism and the high seed Lys KD genotype showed reduced levels of 502 the TCA cycle intermediates (Angelovici et al., 2011).

503 Our results are consistent with the notion that multiple steps of the BCAA 504 degradation pathway provide alternative sources of energy under long-term dark 505 treatment conditions. Prior to our study, IVD was the only enzyme in BCAA catabolism 506 that was shown to play a role in plant survival in energy-limited conditions, because *ivd1-* 507 *2* exhibited enhanced senescence relative to the wild type in prolonged darkness (Araujo 508 et al., 2010). The authors hypothesized that the early senescence of *ivd1-2* resulted from 509 deficiency in supplying electrons to the mitochondrial electron transport chain and/or 510 providing the BCAA catabolic products to the TCA cycle (Ishizaki et al., 2005; Araujo et 511 al., 2010) (Figure 1). In our study, BCAA catabolic mutants defective in enzymes both 512 upstream (*e1a1* single mutants and *e1a1; e1a2* double mutants) and downstream (*mcca1-* 513 *1*, *mccb1-1* and *hml1-2* mutants) of IVD displayed enhanced senescence in prolonged 514 darkness (Figure 6; Supplemental Figure S5), supporting the hypothesized role of BCAA 515 catabolism in providing TCA cycle substrates in energy-limited conditions. Interestingly, 516 *ivd1-2* exhibited dehydrated and yellowed rosette leaves more rapidly than the other 517 mutants (Supplemental Figure S5), and was the only mutant that did not recover after the 518 15d dark treatment (Figure 6). This is consistent with the hypothesis that IVD influences 519 energy homeostasis in multiple ways, not only by providing BCAA catabolic CoA 520 intermediates to the mitochondrial electron transport chain, but also by catabolizing 521 additional substrates such as phytanoyl-CoA and aromatic amino acids (Araujo et al., 522 2010). The observation that *e1a1* single mutants and *e1a1; e1a2* double mutants, which 523 are defective in subunits of the upstream BCKDH complex, showed enhanced senescence 524 at a weaker level than *ivd1-2* (Figure 6; Supplemental Figure S5) supports the 525 hypothesized broader substrate range for IVD. Taken together, our results provide genetic 526 evidence supporting the hypothesized interaction between BCAA catabolism and energy 527 metabolism at multiple steps, and are consistent for a broader substrate range for IVD in 528 plants.

#### 530 **Broader Implications**

531 This and previously published work on amino acid catabolism have a variety of 532 implications for our understanding of plant metabolism and attempts to improve 533 productivity and nutritional quality of plants. First, a growing body of evidence points to 534 major roles of amino acid catabolism and inter-conversion in regulating levels of six of 535 the nine free essential amino acids (Ile, Leu, Lys, Met, Thr and Val) in seeds of a variety 536 of plants (Karchi et al., 1994; Zhu and Galili, 2003; Jander et al., 2004; Joshi et al., 2006; 537 Lee et al., 2008; Gu et al., 2010; Lu et al., 2011; Angelovici et al., 2013). These results 538 indicate that modifying catabolism has the potential to improve the nutritional quality of 539 crop seeds and vegetative tissues. However, a variety of pleiotropic effects were seen for 540 these examples ranging from increased seed Cys in *tha1* mutants (Lu et al., 2008) to 541 broader amino acid increases in Lys overproducing lines (Karchi et al., 1994; Zhu and 542 Galili, 2003) and BCAA catabolic mutants (Gu et al., 2010; Lu et al., 2011; and this 543 study), and defects in vegetative and seed development or seed viability (Zhu and Galili, 544 2004; Lee et al., 2008; Ding et al., 2012). It is not surprising that these relatively 'old' 545 processes are broadly connected and difficult to perturb without resultant undesirable 546 effects (Milo and Last, 2012). While these pleiotropies create hurdles for metabolic 547 engineering of nutritional quality, understanding the basis for these syndromes should 548 lead to a deeper understanding of the architecture of these and other metabolic networks. 549

#### 570 **Transcript Coexpression Analysis**

571 Coexpression analysis of the previously documented and hypothesized BCAA 572 catabolic enzyme genes was performed with the following microarray datasets: stress 573 (AtGenExpress, abiotic and biotic stress treatments in roots and shoots with time points 574 from 0.5 to 24h) (Kilian et al., 2007), development (AtGenExpress, atlas of 575 developmental stages consisting of a variety of tissues) (Schmid et al., 2005), light 576 (AtGenExpress, light treatments of different wavelengths, fluence and durations) (Kilian 577 et al., 2007) and diurnal/circadian (Diurnal, combinations of light and temperature 578 conditions) (Mockler et al., 2007). The development, light and diurnal/circadian data 579 were downloaded in a Robust Multi-array Average normalized form from: 580 http://www.weigelworld.org/resources/microarray/AtGenExpress/ and 581 ftp://www.mocklerlab.org/diurnal/. For the stress dataset, the degrees of differential 582 expression (in the form of fold change) was obtained from an earlier study (Zou et al., 583 2009). For all datasets, pairwise PCCs were calculated among the genes of interest using 584 SciPy library in Python (http://www.scipy.org/) (Jones et al., 2001). To identify gene 585 pairs with significantly higher than randomly expected Pearson's Correlation Coefficient 586 (PCC) values, 500,000 gene pairs were selected randomly from each dataset to calculate PCCs and establish a null PCC distribution. The 95th 587 percentile of each null PCC 588 distribution was used as the threshold for calling two genes as significantly positively 589 correlated with a 5% false positive rate (arrows in Figure 2A; Supplemental Figure S1; 590 Supplemental Table S1). Coexpression modules were defined by clustered genes with 591 significant correlations in each dataset. Heat maps were generated with levelplot within 592 the R lattice package (Sarkar and Sarkar, 2007).

#### 627 **RNA-seq Analysis**

Six samples were multiplexed 638 and sequenced in one lane using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencer, and 50 bp single 639 end reads were generated. A total of six lanes were sequenced for 36 samples.

The transcript levels represented as Fragments Per Kilobase 649 of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) were visualized with CummeRbund 650 version 2.6.1 (Goff et al., 2012) in R version 3.0.3 (Statistical Package, 2012). The RNA-651 Seq dataset was deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene 652 Expression Omnibus under GEO accession number GSE67956.

Graphs were constructed using development (top left), stress (top right), diurnal/circadian (bottom left), and light (bottom right) datasets.
