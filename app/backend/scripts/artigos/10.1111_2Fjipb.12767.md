#### **Abstract**

Although the mechanism of DNA methylation-mediated gene silencing is extensively studied, relatively little is known about how promoter methylated genes are protected from transcriptional silencing. SUVH1, an Arabidopsis Su(var)3-9 homolog, was previously shown to be required for the expression of a few promoter methylated genes. By chromatin immunoprecipitation combined with sequencing, we demonstrate that SUVH1 binds to methylated genomic loci targeted by RNA-directed DNA methylation. SUVH1 and its homolog SUVH3 function partially redundantly and interact with three DNAJ domain-containing homologs, SDJ1, SDJ2, and SDJ3, thus forming a complex which we named SUVH-SDJ. The SUVH-SDJ complex components are co-localized in a large number of methylated promoters and are required for the expression of a subset of promoter methylated genes. We demonstrate that the SUVH-SDJ complex components have transcriptional activation activity. SUVH1 and SUVH3 function synergistically with SDJ1, SDJ2, and SDJ3 and are required for plant viability. This study reveals how the SUVH-SDJ complex protects promoter methylated genes from transcriptional silencing and suggests that the transcriptional activation of promoter methylated genes mediated by the SUVH-SDJ complex may play a critical role in plant growth and development.

#### **INTRODUCTION**

DNA methylation at the C-5 position of cytosine is a conserved epigenetic mark required for transposon silencing, genome stability, and regulation of gene expression in many eukaryotes (Chinnusamyet al. 2008; Law and Jacobsen 2010; He et al. 2011; Borges and Martienssen 2013; Li et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2018). In *Arabidopsis thaliana*, DNA methylation is established by RNA-directed DNA methylation pathway and is maintained by the DNA methyltransferases MET1, CMT3, and CMT2, respectively, at CG, CHG, and CHH sites (C is A, T, and C) (Stroud et al. 2013; Zemach et al. 2013; Matzke and Mosher 2014; Du et al. 2015). In the mutants affecting DNA methylation, the silencing of transposable elements is released, in some cases resulting in instability of the genome (Mirouze et al. 2009; Tsukahara et al. 2009). Transposable elements are not only enriched in pericentromeric heterochromatin regions but also dispersed in gene-rich euchromatin regions especially for plants with larger genomes (Ahmed et al. 2011; Zhang et al. 2018).

DNA methylation silences both transposable elements and genes with promoters containing transposable elements. Although promoter DNA methylation usually represses gene expression (Kinoshita et al. 2004; Henderson and Jacobsen 2008; Law and Jacobsen 2010; Wei et al. 2017), it is also known to promote gene expression (Zhang et al. 2006; Lei et al. 2015; Williams et al. 2015), While DNA methylation-mediated transcriptional silencing is extensively studied (Zhu et al. 2016), relatively little is known about how promoter methylated genes are protected from transcriptional silencing.

Arabidopsis 5-mC DNA glycosylase family proteins, including ROS1, DME, DML1, DML2, and DML3, are responsible for initiating active DNA demethylation by a base excision repair pathway (Gong et al. 2002; Gehring et al. 2006; Ortega-Galisteo et al. 2008). The DNA glycosylases tend to target transposable elements adjacent to genes and thus prevent spreading of DNA methylation and transcriptional silencing from transposable elements to nearby genes (Penterman et al. 2007; Gehring et al. 2009; Hsieh et al. 2009; Tang et al. 2016). An anti-silencing protein complex, IDM, was thought to target ROS1, resulting in DNA demethylation at specific genomic loci (Qian et al. 2012; Lang et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2015; Duan et al. 2017; Li et al. 2018). The active DNA demethylation mechanism is involved in diverse biological processes including seed development and dormancy (Gehring et al. 2009; Zhu et al. 2018), fruit ripening (Liu et al. 2015; Lang et al. 2017), stomata formation (Yamamuro et al. 2014), and nodule development (Satge et al. 2016), suggesting biological significance of the active DNA demethylation mechanism. Although ROS1 is a major ubiquitously expressed 5-mC DNA glycosylase involved in DNA demethylation, the loss-of-function mutant of ROS1 was not observed to show obvious developmental defects except for enhancement of seed dormancy and overproduction of stomatal lineage cells (Zhu et al. 2018; Yamamuro et al. 2014). We predict that, in addition to active DNA demethylation, there may exista distinct mechanism which is important for protecting genes from potentially harmful DNA methylation and transcriptional silencing.

While the anti-silencing protein complex IDM was reported to target ROS1 for active DNA demethylation and thereby suppress gene silencing, an independent study showed that the same complex functions to suppress gene silencing primarily at a downstream step of DNA methylation (Li et al. 2017). Thus, the IDM complex is most likely to possess both DNA demethylation-dependent and independent roles in suppression of gene silencing even though the DNA demethylation-independent mechanism is elusive. A genome-wide DNA methylation analysis indicated that many promoter methylated genes are not silenced (Zhang et al. 2006; Li et al. 2016), suggesting that, in addition to active DNA demethylation, a distinct mechanism may be required for counteracting DNA methylation and transcriptional silencing without alteration of DNA methylation. The Arabidopsis BRAT1-BRP1 complex identified in our previous study is responsible for preventing transcriptional silencing of methylated genomic loci through both DNA demethylation-dependent and –independent mechanisms (Zhang et al. 2016), suggesting that the DNA demethylation-independent mechanism is likely to be a potentially ubiquitous anti-silencing mechanism. SUVH1, an Arabidopsis Su(var)3-9 homolog, was identified as an anti-silencing factor that is required for the expression of promoter methylated genes without altering DNA methylation (Li et al. 2016). However, it is necessary to investigate the molecular mechanism and to determine whether the mechanism has biological significance.

Here, our study demonstrates that SUVH1 binds to highly methylated genomic loci at the whole-genome level, suggesting a direct role of SUVH1 in promoting the expression of promoter methylated genes. We show that SUVH1 functions redundantly with its close homolog SUVH3and identify three SUVH1/3-interacting DNAJ domain-containing proteins (SDJ1, SDJ2, and SDJ3). We demonstrate that these proteins form a tight protein complex (hereafter named SUVH-SDJ)required for suppression of gene silencing. More importantly, we show that the complex is essential for plant growth and development.

#### **RESULTS**

#### **SUVH1, SUVH3, SDJ1, SDJ2, and SDJ3 form a protein complex**

To investigate the function of SUVH1 in Arabidopsis, we generated transgenic plants harboring a native promoter-driven *SUVH1* fused with an epitope tag and affinity purified proteins that associate with SUVH1. As detected by mass spectrometry, SUVH1 is co-purified with SUVH3 and three DNAJ domain-containing proteins, which we hereafter named SDJ1 (SUVH-interacting DNAJ domain-containing protein 1), SDJ2, and SDJ3 (Figure 1A). Further, we generated transgenic plants expressing epitope-tagged versions of SUVH3, SDJ1, and SDJ3, and affinity purified proteins that associate with SUVH3, SDJ1, and SDJ3. By mass spectrometry, we found that SUVH3, SDJ1, and SDJ3 co-purified all the SUVH and SDJ proteins identified in the SUVH1 purification (Figure 1A). Thus, the SUVH and SDJ proteins are likely to interact with each other *in vivo*. We performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) to validate the interaction of the SUVH and SDJ proteins. The co-IP experiment confirmed the interaction of SUVH1-MYC with SDJ1-FLAG, the interaction of SDJ1-FLAG with SDJ3-MYC, and the interaction of SUVH3-FLAG with SUVH1-MYC and SDJ1-MYC(Figure 1B, 1C).

To determine whether the SUVH and SDJ proteins form a tight complex or transiently interact with each other, we generated transgenic plants expressing the SUVH1-FLAG fusion protein in the *sdj1 sdj2 sdj3* (*sdj1/2/3*) triple mutant and in the wild-type control and tested whether the *sdj1/2/3* mutations affect the SUVH1-FLAG elution pattern in the gel filtration experiment. As indicated by immunoblotting, SUVH1-FLAG signal was present in high-molecular-weight fractions (20 and 22) in the wild-type plants but the signal was almost completely eliminated in the *sdj1/2/3*  mutant plants (Figure 1D), suggesting that SDJ1, SDJ2, and SDJ3 are required for forming a large SUVH1-containing protein complex. Similarly, we generated transgenic plants expressing the SDJ1-FLAG fusion protein in the *suvh1 suvh3* (*suvh1/3*) mutant and in the wild-type control. Like the SUVH1-FLAG signal, the SDJ1-FLAG signal also appeared in the high-molecular-weight fractions 20 and 22 in the gel filtration experiment (Figure 1D). The SDJ-FLAG signal was markedly reduced in the *suvh1/3* mutant plants compared to that in the wild-type plants (Figure 1D). Thus, the SUVH and SDJ proteins form a tight SUVH-SDJ complex *in vivo*.

# **The SUVH-SDJ complex is required for the expression of promoter methylated genes**

SUVH1 was reported to be required for the expression of a luciferase (LUC) reporter transgene driven by a methylated *35S* promoter in a reporter system, named *YJ* (Li et al. 2016). To determine whether the SUVH-SDJ complex is required for the expression of the *LUC* reporter gene, we introduced the *YJ* luciferase reporter transgene into the *suvh1*, *sdj1*, *sdj2*, *sdj3* single mutantsand the *sdj1/2/3* triple mutant by crossing. We generated CRISPR-CAS9-mediated *suvh3*mutationsin the *YJ* plants in both the wild type and the *suvh1* mutant backgrounds to generatethe *suvh3* single mutant and the *suvh1/3* double mutant harboring the *YJ* luciferase reporter gene (Figure S1A, S1B).As indicated by luminescence imaging and RT-qPCR, the expression of *LUC* was significantly reduced in the *suvh1* single mutant (Figure 2A, 2B); this result is consistent with the previous study reporting the effect of *suvh1* on the expression of *LUC* (Li et al. 2016). In the *suvh3* single mutant, the expression of *LUC* was not affected (Figure 2A, 2B). The *LUC* expression was reduced to a greater extent in the *suvh1/3* double mutant than in the *suvh1* single mutant (Figure 2A, 2B), suggesting that SUVH3 functions redundantly with SUVH1 in promoting the expression of the *LUC* reporter gene. The *LUC* expression was moderately reduced in the *sdj1* and *sdj3* single mutants but was slightly reduced in the *sdj2* single mutant (Figure 2A, 2B). In the *sdj1/2/3* triple mutant, the reduction of the *LUC* expression was significantly enhanced compared to each of single mutants (Figure 2A, 2B), suggesting that SDJ1, SDJ2, and SDJ3 function partially redundantly in promoting the expression of the *LUC* reporter gene.

The expression of several promoter methylated endogenous genes was reported to be reduced in the *suvh1* mutant (Li et al. 2016), suggesting the expression of these genes is dependent on SUVH1. We asked whether the expression of these promoter methylated genes is dependent on the SUVH-SDJ complex. We performed RT-qPCR to determine the expression of two previously reported promoter methylated genes, *AT1G52040* and *ESM1*. The expression of the two genes was markedly reduced in the *suvh1* single mutant but was either not affected or mildly reduced in the *suvh3* single mutant (Figure 2C). In the *suvh1/3* double mutant, the expression of *AT1G52040*was reduced to a similar extent with that in the *suvh1* single mutant, whereas the expression of *ESM1* was reduced a greater extent than in the *suvh1* single mutant (Figure 2C). The expression of *AT1G52040*was moderately reduced in the *sdj1* and *sdj3* single mutants and to a less extent in the *sdj2* single mutant; the reduction of the expression of this gene was weakly enhanced in the *sdj1/2/3* triple mutant compared to each of the single mutants (Figure 2C). The reduction of the expression of *ESM1* was substantially enhanced in the *sdj1/2/3* triple mutant compared to each of the single mutants (Figure 2C). These results suggest that the two SUVH homologs and the three SDJ homologs identified in this study are all required for the expression of the promoter methylated genes.

#### **Identification of SUVH-SDJ interaction domains**

To characterize the interaction of the SUVH and SDJ proteins, we assessed the interaction by yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays, indicating that SUVH1 and SUVH3 interact with all the three SDJ proteins and showing that SDJ3 interacts with SDJ1 and SDJ2 (Figures 3A; S2A). However, the interaction between SUVH1 and SUVH3 was not identified in the Y2H assay (Figures 3A; S2A), even though the interaction was demonstrated in Arabidopsis plants (Figure 1A, 1C). It is possible that the SDJ proteins may function as bridge proteins that are responsible for linking SUVH1 and SUVH3 in the SUVH-SDJ complex.

To identify interaction domains that are responsible for forming the SUVH-SDJ complex, we generated a series of truncated versions of SUVH3 and SDJ1 in the Y2H assays (Figure S2B). Based on the Y2H results (Figures 3B; S2C), an N-terminal domain of SUVH3 is both necessary and sufficient for the interaction of SUVH3 with SDJ1, SDJ2, and SDJ3; conserved PHD fingers of the SDJ proteins (SDJ1-PHD, SDJ2-PHD, and SDJ3-PHD1) are responsible for the interaction of the SDJ proteins with SUVH1 and SUVH3. Considering that the interaction of SDJ3 with SDJ1and SDJ2 was detected in the Y2H assay (Figure 3A), we tested whether the conserved SDJ3-PHD1 is also responsible for the interaction with SDJ2 and SDJ3. However, the SDJ3-PHD1 interacts with SUVH1 and SUVH3 but not with SDJ2 and SDJ3 (Figure S3), suggesting that the SDJ3-PHD1 is specifically responsible for the interaction with SUVH1 and SUVH3.

### **The conserved PHD finger is required for the formation and functioning of the SUVH-SDJ complex**

Based on the alignment of the PHD fingers of SDJ1, SDJ2, and SDJ3, we found that the PHD fingers are highly conserved in the three SDJ proteins (Figure 3C). SDJ3 contains not only the conserved PHD finger (SDJ3-PHD1) but also an additional putative PHD finger (SDJ3-PHD2) behind the conserved one (Figure 3C). In the SDJ3-PHD2, a conserved N-terminal sequence is absent, which may be the reason why the SDJ3-PHD2 fails to interact with SUVH1 and SUVH3 (Figure 3C). To gain further insight into the function of the conserved PHD fingers, we introduced point mutations into the conserved N-terminal sequence of the SDJ3-PHD1 and checked whether the mutant versions of the SDJ3-PHD1 interact with the SUVH and SDJ proteins by Y2H assays. The mutant SDJ3-PHD1 versions harboring Y658A/P659A mutations or a Y658A mutation failed to interact with SUVH1 and SUVH3 but maintain the interaction with SDJ3 (Figure 3D), suggesting that the Y658/P659 residues of SDJ3-PHD1 is specifically responsible for the interaction of SDJ3 with SUVH1 and SUVH3 in the Y2H assay.

To investigate the function of the conserved PHD fingers in Arabidopsis, we introduced the Y658A/P659A mutations into an epitope-tagged *SDJ3* construct and transformed the mutant version of *SDJ3* into Arabidopsis plants. By affinity purification combined with mass spectrometry, we found that the mutant version of SDJ3 failed to co-purify SUVH1, SUVH3, SDJ1, and SDJ2 even though the SDJ3 protein was identified by itself (Figure 3E), suggesting that the Y658A/P659A mutations affect the interaction of SDJ3 with SUVH proteins in the SUVH-SDJ complex. The effect of the mutations on the interaction of SDJ3 with SUVH1 was further confirmed by co-IP (Figure S4). These results suggest that the conserved PHD finger is required for the formation of the SUVH-SDJ complex in Arabidopsis. Further, we checked whether the mutations affect the function of SDJ3 in the expression of promoter methylated genes. Our RT-qPCR experiment indicated that the expression levels of three promoter methylated genes, *AT1G52040*, *ESM1*, and *ESP*, were reduced in the *sdj3* mutant (Figure 3F). The reduction was complemented by the wild-type *SDJ3* but not by the mutant version of *SDJ3* (Figure 3F). These results suggest that the conserved PHD fingers facilitate the formation of the SUVH-SDJ complex and thereby promoting the expression of promoter methylated genes.

#### **SUVH1 binds to methylated genomic loci**

To investigate SUVH1-binding loci at the whole-genome level, we generated transgenic plants harboring a native promoter-driven *SUVH1* genomic sequence fused with a GFP epitope (*SUVH1-GFP*) and then then performed chromatin immunoprecipitation combined with sequencing (ChIP-seq) using the transgenic plants. As expected, a large number of SUVH1 peaks were identified in the *SUVH1-GFP* transgenic plants as compared to the wild-type control plants.SUVH1 peaks identified in the three independent experiments are highly overlapped; the 7618 overlapping peaks were subjected to further analyses (Data Set 1). The SUVH1 peaks are enriched in promoters and TEs but not in gene bodies (Figure 4A). In the genic region, SUVH1 signals form a sharp peak in the promoter near the transcription start site (TSS) and form a weak peak around the transcription end site (TES) (Figure 4B). In the transposable element (TE) region, SUVH1 signals are enriched throughout the TE body when TEs are shorter than 500 base pair (bp) and tend to be enriched at two edges of the TE body when TEs are longer than 500 bp (Figure 4B).

On the Arabidopsis chromosomes, SUVH1 signals are predominantly enriched in pericentromeric heterochromatin regions and are also dispersed in two arms of the chromosomes (Figure 4C). This SUVH1 profile on the chromosomes is highly similar with the DNA methylation profile (Lister et al., 2008), implying that SUVH1 may bind methylated genomic regions. Thus, we assessed DNA methylation levels of the SUVH1 peaks and found that the average DNA methylation level of the SUVH1 peaks is much higher than that of random peaks at all three cytosine contexts (Figure 4D), indicating that SUVH1 binds methylated genomic regions. Considering that SUVH1 is enriched in promoters, we investigated whether SUVH1 tends to bind methylated promoters. By comparing DNA methylation levels of SUVH1-bound promoters and random promoters, we found that the SUVH1-bound promoters have much higher DNA methylation levels than the random promoters at all cytosine sites (Figure S5). As shown by scatter plots (Figure 4E), the levels of SUVH1 enrichment on promoters are positively correlated with the promoter methylation levels, confirming that the binding of SUVH1 to promoters is related to DNA methylation.

Considering that SUVH1 binds to methylated genomic regions, we asked whether DNA methylation is affected in the *suvh1/3* double mutant and the *sdj1/2/3* triple mutant as compared to the wild type. We conducted whole-genome bisulfite sequencing to determine whether DNA methylation is affected in the *suvh1/3* and *sdj1/2/3* mutants as compared to the wild type. Our analyses indicated that DNA methylation levels are not significantly affected in the *suvh1/3* mutant and the *sdj1/2/3* mutant relative to in the wild type (Figures S6, S7A); the result is consistent with the previous study reporting the effect of*suvh1*on DNA methylation at the whole-genome level (Li et al., 2016). The result supports the notion that the SUVH-SDJ complex functions at a downstream step of DNA methylation.

To determine which DNA methylation components are responsible for DNA methylation of SUVH1 peaks, we assessed DNA methylation levels of SUVH1 peaks in the wild type and in the mutants that are defective in different DNA methylation components (Stroud et al. 2013). These components include the maintenance DNA methyltransferase MET1; the chromatin-remodeling protein DDM1, which is required for maintenance of DNA methylation; the CHG methyltransferase CMT3; the CHH methyltransferase CMT2; and the Pol IV largest subunit NRPD1, which is one of core components of the RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) pathway (Law and Jacobsen, 2010; Matzke and Mosher, 2014). As shown by heat maps, DNA methylation is significantly higher in SUVH1 peaks than in their flanking regions at all three cytosine sites (CG, CHG, and CHH) (Figure 4F, 4G; Supplemental Figure 7A, 7B), confirming that SUVH1 specifically binds to methylated genomic loci.

Intriguingly, CHH methylation of SUVH1 peaks is exclusively dependent on NRPD1 and to a much lesser extent on the other DNA methylation components (Figure 4G). Especially, CHH methylation of the SUVH1 peaks is almost completely independent of CMT2 (Figure 4G). RdDM specifically targets short TEs, which are dispersed in chromosome arms, and the edges of long TEs, which are enriched in pericentromeric heterochromatin regions (Zhong et al. 2012; Zemach et al. 2013). In contrast, CMT2 is predominantly responsible for CHH methylation of the body regions of long TEs in pericentromeric heterochromatin regions (Zemach et al. 2013; Stroud et al. 2014). Our analyses suggest that SUVH1 binds to methylated genomic regions that are specifically targeted by RdDM.

CG methylation of SUVH1 peaks is predominantly dependent on MET1 and to a lesser extent on DDM1 (Figure S7B), which is consistent with the function of MET1 and DDM1 in maintenance of DNA methylation (Stroud et al. 2013). Although CMT3 is known to be the DNA methyltransferase that is specifically responsible for CHG methylation (Stroud et al., 2013; Du et al., 2015), the CHG methylation of SUVH1 peaks is reduced to a greater extent in the *nrpd1* mutant than in the *cmt3* mutant (Figure S7B). Unlike RdDM that targets short TEs and edges of long TE, CMT3 is known to mediate CHG methylation in the body regions of long TEs (Zemach et al.

2013). Thus, this analysis further confirmed that DNA methylation of SUVH1 peaks is established and maintained by the RdDM pathway. The maintenance of DNA methylation of SUVH1 peaks is also dependent on the maintenance DNA methyltransferase MET1 and the chromatin-remodeling protein DDM1.

### **The binding of SUVH1/3 to chromatin is dependent on DNA methylation and is interdependent on the binding of SDJ1/2/3 to chromatin**

To assess whether DNA methylation is a prerequisite for SUVH1 binding to chromatin, we treated the *SUVH1-GFP* transgenic plants with the cytosine methylation inhibitor 5-Aza-2-deoxycytidine (5-Aza-dC) and determined whether the binding of SUVH1 to methylated genomic regions is reduced by the treatment. From the SUVH1 peaks identified by the ChIP-seq experiment, we selected four SUVH1 peaks for validation, all of which are located in methylated promoters. As determined by chromatin immunoprecipitation combined with quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR), SUVH1 is significantly enriched in the four methylated promoter sequences (Figure 5A). The 5-Aza-dC treatment reduces the enrichment of SUVH1 at the tested loci (Figure 5A), suggesting that DNA methylation is necessary for the SUVH1 binding. However, the 5-Aza-dC treatment only partially reduced the binding of SUVH1 to chromatin (Figure 5A), suggesting that some other chromatin features may be also required for the binding of SUVH1 to chromatin. Further studies are required to clarify which chromatin features are involved in the recruitment of the SUVH-SDJ complex in addition to DNA methylation.

Considering the binding of SUVH1 to methylated genomic regions and the formation of the SUVH-SDJ complex, we asked whether the integral SUVH-SDJ complex binds to methylated genomic regions. Our ChIP-qPCR result indicated that, like SUVH1, SUVH3, SDJ1, and SDJ3 were enriched in the methylated promoters (Figures 5B, 5C; S8), suggesting that the methylated genomic loci are likely to be bound by the integral SUVH-SDJ complex. We introduced the *SUVH1-FLAG* fusion gene into the *sdj1/2/3* mutant and the wild-type plants to determine whether the SDJ proteins are required for SUVH1 binding to methylated genomic loci. While the SUVH1-FLAG protein was shown to bind methylated genomic loci, the binding of the SUVH1-FLAG protein to the methylated genomic loci was markedly reduced in the *sdj1/2/3* mutant plants (Figure 5B). Similarly, we introduced the *SDJ1-FLAG* fusion gene into the *suvh1/2* double mutant and the wild-type plants to investigate whether SUVH1 and SUVH3 are required for SDJ1 binding to methylated genomic loci. As determined by the ChIP-qPCR experiment, the binding of SDJ1-FLAG to methylated genomic loci was also dependent on SUVH1 and SUVH3 (Figure 5C). Thus, the binding of the SUVH and SDJ proteins to methylated genomic loci is interdependent.

#### **The SUVH-SDJ complex promotes the expression of promoter methylated genes**

To investigate the function of the SUVH and SDJ proteins at the whole-genome level, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to generate transcriptomes for the *suvh1/3*  double mutant, the *sdj1/2/3* triple mutant, the *suvh1/3;sdj1/3* quadruple mutant, and the wild type. Differentially expressed genes are highly overlapped in the three mutants (Figure 6A; Supplemental Data Set 2, 3, 4). It is noteworthy that more differentially expressed genes were identified in the *suvh1/3;sdj1/3* quadruple mutants than in the *suvh1/3* double mutant and the *sdj1/2/3* triple mutant (Figure 6A). While the differentially expressed genes identified in the three mutants are correlated (Figure 6B; Supplemental Figure 9), the effect of *suvh1/3;sdj1/3* on gene expression is clearly stronger than that of *suvh1/3* and *sdj1/2/3* (Figure 6B). These results suggest that the SUVH proteins function synergistically with the SDJ proteins in the regulation of gene expression. Since the *suvh1/3;sdj1/3* mutant shows a stronger effect on gene expression than the *suvh1/3* double mutant and the *sdj1/2/3* triple mutant, we focused our analyses on the differentially expressed genes in the *suvh1/3;sdj1/3* mutant.

Considering that SUVH1 peaks were identified by our ChIP-seq experiment, we asked whether the SUVH-SDJ complex affects the expression of the genes with promoters bound by SUVH1 at the whole-genome level. By comparing the ChIP-seq and RNA-seq data, we found that 110 differentially expressed genes identified in the *suvh1/3;sdj1/3* mutant were bound by SUVH1 at their 1-kb promoters (Figure 6C; Data Set 1, 4). Among the 110 differentially expressed SUVH1-bound genes, down-regulated genes (83) are significantly more than up-regulated genes (27) (Figure 6C), supporting the notion that the SUVH-SDJ complex tends to promote the expression of its target genes. *AT1G52040* and *ESM1*were previously shown to be down-regulated in the *suvh1* mutant (Li et al., 2016). Our RNA-seq analysis indicated that the expression of the two genes was markedly reduced in the *suvh1/3* mutant, the *sdj1/2/3* mutant, and the *suvh1/3;sdj1/3* mutant (Figure 6D). SUVH1 was significantly enriched in the promoters of the genes as determined by our SUVH1 ChIP-seq experiment (Figure 6D). Moreover, reduction of gene expression was also observed in many other genes with promoters bound by SUVH1, including *ESP* and *ROS1* (Figure 6D; Supplemental Data Set 1, 2, 3, 4). Specifically, *ROS1* is a 5-mC glycosylase that is responsible for initiating active DNA demethylation (Gong et al. 2002; Zhang et al. 2018). In the *ROS1* promoter, a methylated sequence was shown to act a methylstat (sensor of DNA methylation) and thereby regulate DNA methylation by affecting the expression of *ROS1* (Williams et al., 2015; Lei et al., 2015). Our SUVH1 ChIP-seq result indicated that SUVH1 specifically binds to the methylstat sequence (Figure 6D). Accordingly, the *ROS1* expression was reduced in the *suvh1/3*, *sdj1/2/3*, and *suvh1/3;sdj1/3* mutants (Figure 6D). Therefore, the SUVH-SDJ complex not only acts as a reader and but also as an effector of the *ROS1*methylstat sequence.

#### **SUVH1/3 and SDJ1/2/3 function as transcriptional activators**

SUVH1 and SUVH3 are two Su(var)3-9 homologs that potentially have histone methyltransferase activities. In the previous study (Li et al., 2016), reduced expression of *AT1G52040* and *ESM1* was shown to be accompanied by reduction of H3K4 trimethylation. Thus, it is possible that SUVH1 and SUVH3 are directly responsible for the histone H3K4 trimethylation. However, our *in vitro* histone methyltransferase activation assay did not detect the histone methyltransferase activity of SUVH1 and SUVH3 (Figure S10).

To investigate how the SUVH-SDJ complex promotes gene expression, we applied a luciferase reporter system, which requires a reporter construct and an effector construct. In the reporter construct, a luciferase reporter gene was driven by a mini *35S* promoter in combination with the GAL4-binding cis-element *5×UAS* (Figure 7A). In the effector construct, a GAL4-DNA binding domain (GAL4-BD) was fused with an effector protein (Figure 7A). The genes encoding the SUVH-SDJ complex components were independently fused with GAL4-BD in the effector construct. We co-transformed the reporter construct and each of the effector constructs into Arabidopsis mesophyll protoplasts to check whether the SUVH-SDJ complex components have transcriptional activation abilities. By detecting luminescence activities, we demonstrated that the expression of the luciferase reporter gene is markedly increased by SUVH1, SDJ1, and SDJ3, moderately increased by SDJ2, and

slightly increased by SUVH3 (Figure 7A). The effects of the different SUVH-SDJ components on the expression of the reporter gene are generally correlated with the effects of their corresponding mutations on the expression of transgenic and endogenous promoter methylated genes (Figure 2A-2C). The result suggests that the function of the SUVH-SDJ components in the expression of promoter methylated genes is attributed to the transcriptional activation ability of the components.

# **SUVH1/3 function synergistically with SDJ1/2/3 in the regulation of growth and development**

Given the finding that the differentially expressed genes identified in the *suvh1/3;sdj1/3* quadruple mutant are significantly more than in the *suvh1/3* double mutant and in the *sdj1/2/3* triple mutant as revealed by RNA-seq (Figure 6A, 6B), we wonder whether the effect can be further enhanced in the *suvh1/3;sdj1/2/3* quintuple mutant. However, we failed to obtain the quintuple mutant in the selfed progenies of the F1 hybrid between *suvh1/3* and *sdj1/2/3* even though we successfully obtained the *suvh1/3;sdj1/3* quadruple mutant from the progenies. We identified 8 *suvh1;sdj1/3;SDJ2+/-* mutant plants in the F2 population, but none of *suvh1;sdj1/2/3*  quadruple mutant plants was found. In the selfed progenies of the 8 *suvh1;sdj1/3;SDJ2+/-* mutant plants, we obtained 160 *suvh1;sdj1/3;SDJ2+/+*mutant plants and 184 *suvh1;sdj1/3;SDJ2+/-* mutant plants but failed to obtain even one *suvh1;sdj1/2/3* quadruple mutant plant (Figure 7B), suggesting that this quadruple mutant is lethal. Surprisingly, we found that all the 184 *suvh1;sdj1/3;SDJ2+/-* mutant plants showed obvious developmental defects including small rosettes and short stature, whereas all the 160 *suvh1;sdj1/3;SDJ2+/+* mutant plants had no such defects (Figure 7C). These genetic results demonstrate that the SUVH-SDJ complex components function synergistically and are essential for plant growth and development. Thus, this study reveals biological significance of the expression of promoter methylated genes mediated by the SUVH-SDJ complex.

### **DISCUSSION**

DNA methylation is an important epigenetic marker that is required for the maintenance of transposon silencing in eukaryotes (Borges and Martienssen, 2013; Law and Jacobsen, 2010). In Arabidopsis genome, transposons and other DNA repeats are enriched in pericentromeric heterochromatin regions and are also dispersed in gene-rich chromosome arms (Ahmed et al. 2011). Hypermethylation of the transposon tends to suppress the expression of the adjacent gene especially when the DNA methylation is present in the promoter, which potentially results in harmful effects on the organism (Zhang et al., 2006; Penterman et al. 2007). Therefore, it is necessary to counteract DNA methylation and transcriptional silencing of promoter methylated genes. Active demethylation initiated by 5-mC DNA glycosylase family proteins in plants is a well-known mechanism that is required for counteracting transcriptional silencing (Zhang et al. 2018). However, since DNA methylation is critical for inhibiting the activity of transposons, this mechanism can only remove DNA methyl groups to a limited extent to suppress transcriptional silencing. Thus, a distinct mechanism is required to maintain the expression of promoter methylated genes without alteration of DNA methylation. Our study identifies a methylated-DNA-binding protein complex and demonstrates that the complex is involved in preventing transcriptional silencing of promoter methylated genes at a downstream step of DNA methylation. Although promoter DNA methylation is known to suppress gene expression, a large number of promoter methylated genes are not subjected to transcriptional silencing (Zhang et al. 2006; Li et al. 2016). This study reveals how promoter methylated genes are protected from the potentially deleterious transcriptional silencing without alteration of DNA methylation (Figure 8).It is worth noting that the expression of only a small subset of genes with promoters bound by the SUVH-SDJ complex is significantly downregulated in the *suvh1/3;sdj1/3* mutants (Figure 6A-6C). As shown in our study, SUVH1/3 and SDJ1/2/3 function synergistically in the regulation of gene expression and development (Figure 6A-6C, 7B, 7C). It is possible that more down-regulated genes may be identified in the *suvh1;sdj1/3;SDJ2+/-* mutant plants that show developmental defects. Alternatively, it is possible that the SUVH-SDJ complex can contribute to transcriptional activation only at a subset of chromatin loci bound by the SUVH-SDJ complex. In future, it is necessary to determine why the SUVH-SDJ complex promotes gene expression only at a subset of its binding sites.

In the Arabidopsis genome, long transposons are predominantly present in pericentromeric heterochromatin regions, while small transposons are mainly dispersed in gene-rich chromosome arms (Zemach et al. 2013). Our study shows that SUVH1 are preferentially located in short TEs and the edge of long TEs (Figure 4B). The locations of the SUVH1 peaks are highly similar with that of RdDM targets (Zhong et al. 2012). The RdDM pathway is responsible for establishment of DNA methylation at CHH and to a lesser extent at CG and CHG sites (Stroud et al. 2013). Our DNA methylation analysis indicates that CHH methylation of SUVH1 peaks is exclusively dependent on NRPD1, the largest subunit of DNA-dependent RNA polymerase IV in the RdDM pathway (Figure 4G). CMT2 is a DNA methyltransferase that is mainly responsible for maintenance of CHH methylation in the body regions of long TEs (Zemach et al. 2013; Stroud et al. 2014). Our DNA methylation analysis shows that CHH methylation of SUVH1 peaks is not significantly reduced in the *cmt2* mutant (Figure 4G).Thus, SUVH1 preferentially binds to short TEs, which are usually located in chromosome arms and are proximal to genes, but does not bind to long TEs, which are mainly located in pericentromeric heterochromatin regions.

Unlike long TEs that are usually far from genes, short TEs are frequently dispersed in gene-rich euchromatin regions especially at 5'- and 3'-ends of genes and need to be silenced to avoid affecting gene expression (Hollister and Gaut 2009). While the RdDM pathway is responsible for methylating gene-proximal TEs and thus silencing the TEs (Zhong et al. 2012), the active DNA demethylation is evolved to counteract DNA methylation and thereby protect the corresponding genes from transcriptional silencing (Tang et al. 2016). Unlike the active DNA demethylation mechanism that requires base excision and potentially causes DNA damage, the SUVH-SDJ complex components have the transcriptional activation ability and are capable of directly promoting the expression of genes adjacent to TEs without alteration of DNA methylation. However, the function of the SUVH-SDJ complex is not completely independent of the active DNA methylation mechanism. Our study shows that the expression of *ROS1* is significantly reduced in the SUVH-SDJ complex mutants (Figure 6D). An independent study shows that several Su(var)3-9 homologs including SUVH1 and SUVH3 are required for the expression of *ROS1*, thereby facilitating DNA demethylation at a subset of ROS1 target genomic loci (Xiao et al. 2018). Thus, the SUVH-SDJ complex is responsible for promoting the expression of promoter methylated genes (Figure 8). As one of promoter methylated genes, *ROS1* is activated by the SUVH-SDJ complex and then mediates DNA demethylation and transcriptional silencing at a subset of ROS1 target genomic loci (Figure 8).In future, it is necessary to clarify how the SUVH-SDJ complex and the active DNA demethylation mechanism are coordinated to counteract DNA methylation and transcriptional in plants.

Our study demonstrates that the SUVH-SDJ complex is essential for plant growth and development (Figure 7B, 7C), suggesting that the maintenance of the expression of some promoter methylated genes may be essential in Arabidopsis. In tomato, DNA demethylation initiated by the 5mC glycosylases is required for fruit ripening (Liu et al. 2015; Lang et al., 2017). However, the DNA demethylation is not only correlated with the activation of ripening-induced genes but also with the repression of ripening-repressed genes (Lang et al., 2017), suggesting that DNA methylation may be responsible for maintenance of gene expression at ripening-repressed genes even though the molecular mechanism is elusive. Here, the finding of the SUVH-SDJ complex in promoting the expression of promoter methylated genes may provide a plausible explanation for promoter DNA methylation–mediated transcriptional activation. Considering DNA methylation is known to be more important in plants with larger genomes than in Arabidopsis (Alleman et al. 2006; Wei et al. 2014; Liu et al. 2015). In future, it is necessary to investigate whether orthologs of the SUVH-SDJ complex components are involved in transcriptional activation of promoter methylated genes and whether and how the SUVH-SDJ complex is involved in regulation of growth, development, and stress responses in plants with larger genomes.

We noted that a similar study was recently published online when we were preparing our paper (Harris et al. 2018). The published study reported that SUVH1 and SUVH3 interact with DNAJ1 and DNAJ2 (which are corresponding to SDJ1 and SDJ2, respectively, in our study) and thus form a complex that enhances gene transcription; the main conclusions reported in the published study are confirmed by our independent study. Moreover, our study reveals several findings that were not reported in their study. 1. We demonstrate that the SUVH-SDJ complex is required for plant growth and development and thereby discovered biological significance of the SUVH-SDJ complex. 2. We show that the conserved PHD finger of the SDJ proteins is responsible for the formation of the SUVH-SDJ complex, thus providing a further insight into the molecular mechanism of the SUVH-SDJ complex. 3. We identify an additional SUVH/3-interaction DNAJ domain-containing protein (SDJ3) involved in the formation of the SUVH-SDJ complex, which was not reported in the published study. In addition to the findings that were not reported in the published study, our study shows that SUVH1/3 and SDJ1/2/3 are mutually dependent for binding to

methylated genomic loci (Figure 5B, 5C), which is different from the published study showing that SUVH1/3 are responsible for the recruitment of SDJ1/2 to methylated genomic loci in one way (Harris et al., 2018). Of note, the effect of *sdj1/2* on the binding of SUVH1 and SUVH3 to methylated DNA was detected by an *in vitro* oligonucleotide pull down assay in the published study (Harris et al., 2018), whereas the effect of *sdj1/2/3* on the binding of SUVH1 to methylated chromatin was determined by an *in vivo*ChIP experiment in our study. It is possible thatthe association of the SUVH-SDJ complex with methylatedchromatin is not only dependent on the binding of SUVH1/3 to DNA methylation but also dependent on the binding of SDJ1/2/3 to some uncharacterized chromatin marks, which are obviously not detectable by the oligonucleotide pull down assay. In future, it is required to investigate which chromatin marks are involved in the association of the SUVH-SDJ complex in addition to DNA methylation.

### **MATERIALS AND METHODS**

#### **Plant materials, constructs, growth conditions, and luminescence imaging**

The Arabidopsis T-DNA insertion mutants used in this study were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC). The T-DNA insertion mutants include *suvh1* (SALK\_003675), *suvh3* (CS878938), *sdj1* (SALK\_021078), *sdj2* (CS831551), and *sdj3* (SALK\_091525).The mutations were crossed intothe *YJ*  luciferase reporter system(Li et al 2016).The CRISPR-CAS9-mediated mutants includethe *suvh3* mutation inthe *suvh3;YJ*and *suvh1/3;YJ*plants and the *sdj2* mutation in the *sdj2*single mutantplants with the Col-0 background (Supplemental Figure 1A-1C)*.* Seedlings were grown on MS (Murashige and Skoog) medium plates at 22℃ under long day (16 h light/8 h dark) conditions. Flower tissue samples were collected from adult plants grown at 22℃ under long day conditions.

Native promoter-driven *SUVH1*, *SUVH3*, *SDJ1*, *SDJ2*, and *SDJ3* genomic sequences were fused with *3xFLAG* and *5xMYC* and cloned into *pCAMBIA1305* or *pRI909* vectors. For overexpression of *SDJ3*, *35S* promoter-driven *SDJ3* was fused with the 5'-terminal 3xFLAG tag and cloned into the *pRI909* vector. The agrobacterium strain GV3101 harboring the constructs indicated above were transfected into Arabidopsis plants by the floral dip method. The *SDJ3* with the point mutation Y658A/P659A was generated using the Mut Express MultiS Fast Mutagenesis Kit (Vazyme, C215-01) and either the wild-type or mutated *SDJ3* construct was introduced into *pCAMBIA1305* vector in fusion with the *3xFLAG* tag. The CRISPR-CAS9-mediated *suvh3* and *sdj2* mutations were introduced into the *YJ* reporter plantsaccordingto the protocol described previously [\(Xing et](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/?term=Xing%20HL%5bAuthor%5d&cauthor=true&cauthor_uid=25432517) al, 2014). The *pHEE401E* constructs producing guide RNAs for *SUVH3* and *SDJ2* were transformed into the *YJ* reporter plants. The primers for generating the constructs are listed in Supplemental Data Set 5.

For luminescene imaging, 10-day-old seedlings grown on MS medium plates were sprayed with 2.5 mg/ml luciferin (Nano light, NF-FF-FA). The luminescence imaging was performed using a CCD camera (Princenton Instruments) and WinView32 software.

#### **Affinity purification, mass spectrometric analysis**

To perform affinity purification, 3 g flower tissue samples from *SUVH3-FLAG*, *SUVH1-MYC*, *SDJ1-FLAG*, *SDJ3-MYC*, and *SDJ3-Y658A/P659A-FLAG* transgenic plants were collected and ground in liquid nitrogen and suspended in 10 ml Lysis buffer (150 mMNaCl, 50 mMTris [pH 7.6], 5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 0.1% NP-40, 0.5 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 1 protease inhibitor cocktail tablet every 50 ml [Roche]). The supernatant was incubated with 80 μl anti-FLAG M1 Agarose (Sigma, A4596) or anti-c-MYC Agarose (Sigma, A7470) at 4°C for 2.5 h after centrifugation at 14000 rpm for 15 min at 4℃. Then the beads were harvested by centrifugation at 800 rpm for 3 min at 4℃ and washed four times with the lysis buffer. Next, the bead-bound proteins were eluted with 3xFLAG peptide (Sigma, F4799) or c-MYC peptide (Sigma, M2435) for 30min at 4 ℃. The eluted proteins were run on 10% SDS-PAGE gel and subjected to silver staining using the ProteoSilver Silver Stain Kit (Sigma, PROT-SIL1).

The mass spectrometric analysis was performed according to the protocol described previously (Zhang et al, 2012). For mass spectrometric analysis, the whole proteins were cut from the gel, de-stained and digested with trypsin at 37°C. The peptides were eluted on a capillary column followed by being sprayed into a LTQ mass spectrometer equipped with a nano-ESI ion source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then the Mapped peptides were searched on the Mascot server (Matrix Science Ltd., London, UK)

#### **Co-immunoprecipitation, and gel filtration**

For co-immunoprecipitation,3 g flower tissue samples were harvested from parental and F1 plants and ground in liquid nitrogen and suspended in 10 ml Lysis buffer. After centrifugation, 100 μl supernatant was used as input and the other was incubated with anti-FLAG M1 Agarose (Sigma, A4596) or anti-c-MYC Agarose (Sigma, A7470). The bead-bound proteins were harvested, washed four times, and then eluted using 3xFLAG peptide (Sigma, F4799) or c-MYC peptide (Sigma, M2435). Both eluted and input proteins and were run on SDS-PAGE gel followed by immunoblotting.

For gel filtration, 0.5 g 12-day-old seedlings were ground in liquid nitrogen and suspended in 2 ml lysis buffer (150 mMNaCl, 50 mMTris [pH 7.6], 5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 0.1% NP-40, 0.5 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 1 protease inhibitor cocktail tablet every 50 ml [Roche]). Then the supernatant was passed through a 0.22-μm filter by centrifugation at 10000 rpm for 10 min at 4℃. Then, 500 μl filtrate was loaded onto a Superose 6 increase (10/300 GL) column (GE Healthcare, 29-0915-96). 500 μl eluate per fraction was harvested and run on SDS–PAGE gel followed by immunoblotting.

#### **Yeast two-hybrid assay**

For yeast two-hybrid assays, the full-length cDNA sequences of *SUVH1*, *SUVH3*, *SDJ1*, *SDJ2*, and *SDJ3* were cloned into the *pGADT7* and *pGBKT7* vectors in frame with the 3'-terminal of *GAL4-AD* and *GAL4-BD*, respectively. A series of truncated *SDJ1*, *SDJ2*, and *SDJ3* cDNA sequences were cloned into the *pGADT7*vector, and a series of truncated *SUVH3* and mutated *SDJ3* cDNA sequences were cloned into *pGBKT7* vector. The primers used for generating the constructs shown above are listed in Supplemental Data Set 5.

The *pGADT7* and *pGBKT7* constructs were transformed into the yeast strains AH109 and Y187, respectively. The strain AH109 grew on synthetic dropout medium lacking Leu (SD-L), and strain Y187 grew on synthetic dropout medium lacking Trp (SD-W). Then the positive clones grown on SD-W mated with the positive clones grown SD-L in YPDA liquid medium for 16-20 h. After that, the mixture was spotted on synthetic dropout medium lacking both Trp and Leu (SD-WL). Finally, the positive clones grown on SD-WL were suspended and spotted on both synthetic dropout medium lacking Trp, Leu, His (SD-WLH) and SD-WL. 3 mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT) was added to SD-LHW to inhibit the background growth.

#### **Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing and data analysis**

For whole-genome bisulfite sequencing, 1 g 10-day-old seedling samples were harvested, and genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN, 69104) and sent to BIONOVA (Beijing, China) for bisulfite treatment, library preparation, and deep sequencing. For data analysis, unique bisulfite sequencing reads were mapped to the TAIR10 reference genome using Bismark, allowing one mismatch. The methylation level of each cytosine site was represented using the percentage of reads number reporting mC relative to the total reads number reporting C and T (Krueger and Andrews, 2011). KaryoploteR package was used for plotting DNA methylation patterns on chromosomes (Gel et al, 2017). Heat maps of DNA methylation were generated using the EnrichedHeatmap package in R (Gu et al, 2018). The raw bisulfite sequencing data of the DNA methylation mutants (*met1*, *ddm1*, *cmt3*, *cmt2*, and *nrpd1*) were from a previous study (Stroud et al., 2013). Our raw bisulfite sequencing data will be deposited in GEO (accession number XXXX).

#### **RNA transcript analysis**

Total RNA was extracted from 12-day-old seedlings using TRIzol Reagent (Ambion, 15596018) according to the manual. To perform qPCR, 1 μg total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the 5X All-In-One Master Mix (with AccuRT Genomic DNA Removal Kit) (amb, 492) according to the manual, then the cDNA was used for RT-qPCR using the KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Master Kit (KAPA, KK4601). For RNA-deep sequencing, total RNA was sent to BerryGenomics (Beijing, China). The clean reads were aligned to the TAIR10 Arabidopsis genome sequence using HISAT2 v2.0.4 (Kim et al, 2015). The reads mapped on the exon were counted using Featurecounts (Liao et al, 2014). The differentially expressed genes were identified using edgeR (|log2(Fold Change)| > 0.5 and P value < 0.01) (Nikolayeva and Robinson*,* 2014). The RNA-seq raw data will be deposited in GEO (accession number XXXX).

#### **Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays**

To perform chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), 2 g 10-day-old seedling samples were ground in liquid nitrogen and suspended in 15 ml Nuclear Extraction Buffer (NEB) (20 mMTris.HCl [PH7.5], 20 mMKCl, 2 mM EDTA [PH8.0], 2.5 mM MgCl2, 25% glycerol, 250 mM sucrose, 5 mM DTT, 1% protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM PMSF) and cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde (Sigma, F8775), 0.125 M glycine was used to stop the reaction. The nucleus was washed 3-5 times using the Nuclear Resuspension Buffer (NRBT) (20 mMTris.HCl [PH7.5], 2.5 mM MgCl2, 25% glycerol, 0.2% Triton X-100) followed by sonication in Sonication Buffer (20 mMTris-HCl [PH 8.0], 2 mM EDTA [PH8.0] , 0.2% NP-40, 1 mM PMSF, 1% PPI). Then the chromatin was diluted with Dilution Buffer (2 mM EDTA, 20 mMTris-HCl [PH8.0], 200 mMNaCl, 1 mM PSMF, 1% protease inhibitor cocktail) and incubated with Anti-FLAG M2 Magnetic Beads (Sigma, M8823) or anti-GFP beads at 4℃ for overnight with rotation after centrifugation. Then the beads were washed 5 times using Wash Buffer (150 mMNaCl, 20 mMTrisHCl [PH 8.0], 2 mM EDTA[PH 8.0], 0.1% Triton X-100, 1 mM PMSF ) followed by TE (10 mMTris-HCl [PH 8.0], 1 mM EDTA [PH 8.0]). After that, the immuno-complexes were eluted from the beads and reverse cross-linked by NaCl and Proteinase K (Sigma, P4850). Finally, DNA was extracted using Phenol/Chloroform/Isoamyl alcohol. The ChIP-DNA was used for qPCR or sequencing. For ChIP-qPCR, the ChIP signals on indicated loci were normalized by *ACTIN2*. The primers used for qPCR are listed in Supplemental Data Set 5. The 5-Aza-2'-deoxycytodine treatment was performed as previously described (Li *et al*, 2016). Briefly, plants were grown on MS medium plates supplemented with 3.5 µg/ml 5-Aza-2'-deoxycytodine (Sigma, A3656) for 2 weeks. One gram of seedling samples were used for ChIP-qPCR.

For ChIP-seq sequencing, the ChIP-DNA was sent to BIONOVA (Beijing, China) or Novogene (Tianjin, China). Clean reads were mapped to the TAIR10 Arabidopsis genome with Bowtie2 v2.2.5 (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). The MACS2 callpeak was used to identify ChIP-enriched peaks (Zhang *et al*, 2008). The MACS2 bdgdiff was used to determine differentially expressed peaks between the *SUVH1-GFP* transgenic plants and the wild-type control plants with default parameters. NGS.plot and DeepTools were used for generating average profiles and heatmaps of SUVH1 ChIP-seq signals, respectively (Shen et al 2014; Ramírez et al. 2014). The ChIP-seq raw data will be deposited in GEO (accession number XXXX).

The full-length *SUVH1*, *SUVH3*, and *SDJ1* sequences were cloned into the *pGEX6P-1* vector to generate fusion proteins with a GST in their N-terminal. The primers used for generating the constructs are listed in Supplemental Data Set 5. The constructs were transformed into the *E. coli* strain Transetta (DE3) (TRAN, M651123). The bacteria was grown in LB medium at 37℃ to OD 600 = 0.6, then shifted to 16℃, and treated with 0.1 mM IPTG overnight at 16 ℃. Then the bacteria was harvested and suspended by GST-lysis buffer (20 mMTris-HCl [PH 7.5], 150 mMNaCl) followed by sonication for 3 min. After centrifugation at 18000 rpm for 1 h at 4 ℃, the supernatant was subjected to protein purification using Glutathione-Sepharose 4B (GE Healthcare, 71024800-EG). The bead-bound proteins were eluted by the elution buffer (50 mMTris-HCl [PH 7.5], 150 mMNaCl, 20 mM GSH) and stored at -80℃ with 5% glycerine.

#### **Histone methyltransferase activity assay**

The histone methyltransferase activity assay was performed as previously described (Zhang et al. 2002 and Suiet al. 2012). The reaction was carried out in 20 μl MAB buffer (50 mMTris-HCl [pH 8.5], 20 mMKCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 250 mM Sucrose, 100 μM ZnCl2, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol), containing 10 mg fusion protein and 5 μg calf thymus histone substrates (Roche, 10223565) and 500nCi [methyl-3H] AdoMet (0.55mCi/ml, NEN, NET155H) as a methyl donor. After incubation at 37℃ for 1-2 h, the reaction was stopped by adding SDS-PAGE loading buffer and boiling at 100℃ for 5 min, and proteins were run on 15% SDS-PAGE gel followed by transferred to a PVC membrane. Finally, the membrane was exposed to X-ray films for 4 weeks at -80℃.

#### **Dual luciferase reporter assay**

An effector vector and a reporter vector were used in the dual luciferase reporter assay. *SUVH1, SUVH3, SDJ1, SDJ2, SDJ3* were fused with 35S promoter-driven GAL4-DNA binding domain (GAL4-BD) in the effector vector to determine the transcriptional activation activity of these proteins. In the reporter, a luciferase reporter gene was drived by a minimal 35S promoter with a GAL4-BD binding site (*5xUAS*) at the upstream of the minimal 35S promoter. VP16 is a viral transcription

activator, which was used as a positive control. This assay was performed in Arabidopsis mesophyll protoplasts as described previously (Yooet al, 2007). The luciferase activity was measured using Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1910) in the Promega GLOMAX 20/20 LUMINOMETER.

### **Figure 8. Model for the function of the SUVH-SDJ complex in transcriptional activation of promoter methylated genes**

RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) pathway mediates DNA methylation of promoters proximal to transposons, resulting in gene silencing. The SUVH-SDJ complex binds to methylated promoters and acts as a transcriptional activator of promoter methylated genes. As a promoter methylated gene, *ROS1* is transcriptionally activated by the SUVH-SDJ complex and then initiates active DNA demethylation to facilitate the expression of promoter methylated genes. "m" stands for 5-methylcytosine.
