### **ABSTRACT**

The OLIGOPEPTIDE TRANSPORTER 3 (OPT3) has recently been identified as a component of the systemic network mediating iron (Fe) deficiency responses in Arabidopsis. Reduced expression of *OPT3* induces an over accumulation of Fe in roots and leaves, due in part by an elevated expression of the IRON-REGULATED TRANSPORTER 1 (IRT1). Here we show however, that *opt3* leaves display a transcriptional program consistent with an Fe overload, suggesting that Fe excess is properly sensed in *opt3* leaves and that the OPT3 mediated shoot-to-root signaling is critical to prevent a systemic Fe overload. We also took advantage of the tissue-specific localization of *OPT3,* together with other Fe-responsive genes, to determine the timing and location of early transcriptional events during Fe limitation and re-supply. Our results show that the leaf vasculature responds more rapidly than roots to both Fe deprivation and re-supply, suggesting that the leaf vasculature is within the first tissues that sense and respond to changes in Fe availability. Our data highlight the importance of the leaf vasculature in Fe homeostasis by sensing changes in apoplastic levels of Fe coming tough the xylem and relaying back this information to roots via the phloem to regulate Fe uptake at the root level.

**Key words:** Iron deficiency response, iron translocation, iron overload, long-distance signaling, iron transcriptomics

### **Summary statement**

Iron (Fe) is an essential micronutrient that can become toxic at high concentrations. Iron uptake is induced under Fe deficiency and repressed when Fe levels are restored to prevent a Fe overload. Here we show that Fe deficiency is rapidly sensed in the leaf vasculature and impaired sensing in leaves results in a systemic Fe overload while triggering opposite transcriptional programs in roots and leaves.

### **INTRODUCTION**

Iron (Fe) is an essential micronutrient across all kingdoms of life, playing critical roles in electron transport reactions. In plants, Fe deficiency has a negative impact on plant development, crop yield and seed quality (Hindt & Guerinot 2012; Khan *et al.* 2014). For humans, Fe is an essential nutrient and plants are the main source of Fe, not only for humans but also for livestock. Iron deficiency in humans has been described by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the most common nutritional deficiency in the world affecting nearly 30% of the world's population (2.2 billion people) (WHO, 2015). Understanding how plants take up, re-distribute and accumulate Fe in different tissues, including seeds, will help developing crops with enhanced nutritional value for human consumption. These *biofortification* efforts can be aided by developing plants more resilient and better equipped to tive under Fe-limiting conditions. To this end, it is necessary to have a mechanistic understanding of how plants sense and communicate the Fe status between tissues and how plants adapt when Fe becomes limiting.

Iron in soils is often found as precipitates and not readily bioavailable. To solubilize Fe, plants have evolved different mechanisms to increase Fe bioavailability, either by reducing Fe3+ -complexes to Fe2+ (Strategy I) or by releasing Fe-binding molecules such as phytosiderophores (Strategy II). Phytosiderophores form stable complexes with Fe in the soil and these Fe-ligand complexes are then taken up by specific root transporters (Hindt & Guerinot 2012; Khan *et al.* 2014). Dicotyledonous plants, such as *Arabidopsis thaliana,* rely on Strategy I to mine Fe from the soil. The main components of this reduction-based strategy are: a plasma membrane H + -ATPase (AHA2), that acidifies the rhizosphere to increase Fe solubility, a FERRIC REDUCTASE OXIDASE2 (FRO2), which reduces Fe3+ -complexes to Fe2+ , and an IRON REGULATED TRANSPORTER1 (IRT1), which takes up Fe2+ from the rhizosphere into root cells (Conte & Walker 2011). These tee components are regulated either at transcriptional level, post-transcriptional level or both depending on the Fe status of the plant (Kerkeb *et al.* 2008; Ivanov *et al.* 2014; Brumbarova *et al.* 2015). The transcriptional activation of *AHA2*, *FRO2* and *IRT1*, depends on the activity of several transcription factors including FER-LIKE Fe-DEFICIENCY INDUCED TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR (FIT, bHLH029), bHLH038/39, bHLH100/101. It has been suggested that some of these bHLH proteins, known as the *FIT network*, form heterodimers and this physical interaction is required to induce the expression of *AHA2*, *FRO2* and *IRT1* (Hindt & Guerinot 2012; Brumbarova *et al.* 2015). Besides bHLH proteins, the transcription factors MYB10 and MYB72 have also been identified as transcriptional regulators within the FIT network. MYB10 and MYB72 are required to induce the expression of NICOTIANAMINE SYNTHASES4 (*NAS4*), an enzyme that mediates the synthesis of nicotianamine (NA). NA is a Fe-binding molecule derived from methionine, which helps mobilizing Fe within plant tissues and failure to induce *NAS4*, as shown in the Arabidopsis mutant *myb10my72,* affects plant survival under Fe-limiting conditions (Palmer *et al.* 2013). The complex regulation of Fe uptake at transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels makes possible the accumulation of sufficient levels of Fe across tissues, while preventing toxicity due to a Fe overload.

An additional transcriptional network, known as POPEYE (PYE), has also been identified and characterized in Arabidopsis. The PYE network plays an important role during Fe-deprivation but also when Fe levels are restored as a result of the FIT network activation (Long *et al.* 2010). PYE is also a bHLH protein and interacts with additional PYE-like proteins induced during Fe-limiting conditions. Furthermore, PYE-like proteins interact with BRUTUS (BTS), which is a Fe-binding E3 ligase also induced during Fe-limiting conditions (Selote et al., 2015; Hindt et al., 2017). In contrast to PYE-like proteins, BTS is a negative regulator of the Fe-deficiency response and targets PYE-like proteins for degradation (Selote et al., 2015). Notably, and despite the significant advances made at understanding how plants respond when Fe is limiting, the mechanistic details of how plants sense Fe are still scarce. For instance, it has been known for more than a decade that Fe uptake in Arabidopsis is regulated by a local signal that requires the presence of Fe in the apoplast and a shoot-borne (systemic) signal that negatively regulates the FIT network at the root level (Vert *et al.* 2003; Mendoza-Cózatl *et al.* 2014; Zhai *et al.* 2014). The chemical identity of this shoot-borne signal is not known but it has been proposed to be Fe2+ or a Fe-ligand complex (García *et al.* 2013; Mendoza-Cózatl *et al.* 2014). Recently, a member of the oligopeptide transporter family (OPT3) was identified as a component of the shoot-to-root Fe signaling network (García *et al.* 2013; Mendoza-Cózatl *et al.* 2014; Zhai *et al.* 2014). OPT3 is a plasma membrane transporter preferentially expressed in companion cells (phloem-loading cells) and a reduced expression of *OPT3* induces a constitutive high expression of *IRT1* and *FRO2* (Stacey *et al.* 2008). OPT3 was originally identified as a transporter essential during embryogenesis and further inspection of necrotic lesions in a knockdown mutant (*opt3-2*) led to the discovery that *opt3-2* over-accumulates Fe in roots and leaves (Stacey *et al.* 2008). Lower expression of *OPT3* reduces Fe loading into the phloem and decreases the Fe concentration in the phloem sap by 50% (Zhai *et al.* 2014). These results, together with the fact that Fe homeostasis in roots can be restored by expressing OPT3 specifically in shoots,

suggest that OPT3 is a key component of the negative feedback mechanism that regulates Fe uptake at the root level.

In this work, we took advantage of the *opt3* phenotypes to explore the transcriptional programs induced by a defective Fe signaling network between shoots and roots. Our data suggest that impaired systemic communication induces both the FIT and PYE networks in roots. Radiotracer analyses using <sup>59</sup>Fe2+ also demonstrate that *opt3-2* translocates twice the amount of Fe to shoots compared to wild-type Col-0. Notably, the transcriptional program in *opt3* shoots is consistent with a Fe overload, suggesting that in contrast to *opt3* roots, *opt3* leaves are capable of sensing Fe levels properly. We also took advantage of the known induction of *OPT3* and other vascular genes under Fe limiting conditions to define the timing of transcriptional induction across tissues when plants experience Fe deprivation and Fe resupply. Our results consistently show that the leaf vasculature respond to Fe deficiency and re-supply more rapidly than mesophyll cells and roots. These data suggest that the leaf vasculature is among the first tissues that respond at the transcriptional level, to changes in Fe availability coming from roots tough the xylem. Rapid sensing of Fe availability in leaves allows an efficient relay of information to the roots, via the phloem, to properly regulate Fe uptake and maintain Fe homeostasis.

## **MATERIALS AND METHODS**

### **Plant material and growth conditions**

*Arabidopsis thaliana* ecotype Columbia (Col-0), *opt3-2* (SALK\_021168C), *opt3-3* (SALK\_058794C, provided by Dr. Olena Vatamaniuk), and At*SUC2p:FLAG-RPL18* (provided by Dr. Julia Bailey-Serres) were sterilized and plated on ¼ MS plates (0.25x Murashige and Skoog salts plus vitamins [Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA], 1 mM MES pH 5.6, and 1% Phytoagar [Duchefa Biochemie, NL]). Alternatively, for phenotypic analysis, Fesufficient (Replete) and Fe-deficient media (-Fe) were prepared by adding the following reagents (final concentration): KNO3 (1.25 mM), KH2PO4 (0.625 mM), MgSO<sup>4</sup> (0.5 mM), Ca(NO3)<sup>2</sup> (0.5 mM), H3BO<sup>3</sup> (17.5 µM), MnCl<sup>2</sup> (5.5 µM), ZnSO<sup>4</sup> (0.5 µM), Na2MoO<sup>4</sup> (0.062 µM), NaCl (2.5 µM), CoCl<sup>2</sup> (4 nM), Fe-NaEDTA (100 µM), and Phytoagar (0.7%). For Fedeficient media (-Fe), Fe-NaEDTA was omitted from the recipe and 100 µM of ferrozine, a Fe chelator, was added to the media to chelate all residual Fe from agar.. Unless otherwise stated, all chemicals were analytical grade purchased from Sigma-Aldrich or Fisher Scientific. For plants cultured hydroponically, seeds were first germinated on ¼ MS plates and after 2 weeks, seedlings of uniform size were transferred to hydroponic cultures. As described elsewhere (Jobe et al., 2012; Nguyen *et al*., 2016), no additional copper was added to the hydroponic solution. During germination, plants first acquired Cu from the MS media (25 nM in the ¼ MS recipe) and later from the hydroponic solution. In the experiments described here, the Cu concentration in the hydroponic solution was 11.5 ± 1.4 nM (mean ± SE, n = 6; measured by ICP-OES). Hydroponic solution was replaced every two days and plants were grown for an additional 2-3 weeks (bolting stage) before treatment or harvesting for downstream analyses.

### **Rhizosphere acidification**

The rhizosphere acidification assays were performed as previously described (Yi & Guerinot 1996) with minor modifications. Briefly, seeds were grown vertically on ¼-strength MS media for 15 days and transferred to 0.8% (w/v) agar plates containing 0.006% (w/v) bromocresol purple and 0.2 mM CaSO<sup>4</sup> (pH 6.4) for 48 h.

### **RNA-Seq Library construction, read maps and differential expression**

Plants grown hydroponically as described elsewhere (Nguyen *et al.* 2016). After a total of 4 weeks (bolting stage), roots and rosette leaves were harvested and used for RNA extraction. RNA was isolated from 3-4 plants per biological replicate with tee biological replicates per genotype. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy plant minikit (Qiagen, USA) and the residual DNA was removed using a DNase kit (Ambion-Life Technologies, USA). Singleend 100bp Illumina sequencing was performed at the DNA Core Facility, University of Missouri, Columbia. Each library (12 total, 3 Col-0 shoots, 3 Col-0 roots, 3 *opt3-2* shoots and 3 *opt3-2* roots) was barcoded, divided into pools and sequenced on tee independent lanes. Sequencing resulted in an approx. 430 million reads, which were subsequently cleaned by trimming bases from the 5' and 3' such that the remaining sequence contains bases with a base-call probability > 0.1. Among these reads those shorter than 25bp were discarded leaving 400 million reads. Reads were aligned to the TAIR10 release of the Arabidopsis genome using Tophat2 using default parameters with the exception that only reads with one valid alignment were reported, resulting in 381 million valid alignments (Kim *et al.* 2013). Gene counts were obtained using the TAIR10 GFF3 and the summarize Overlaps function from the ShortRead BioConductor library. Across all libraries the average mapping efficiency was 95%. Differential expression analysis was carried out using edgeR and the global Biological Coefficient of Variation (Robinson *et al.* 2009). Any gene with mean raw count < 50 was declared to have *No Support* and the corresponding fold change was set to zero. Genes with an absolute fold change of < 0.5 (log2), were not considered for significance tests. Genes with unadjusted p-values > 0.05 were removed and the remainder were subjected to multiple testing correction using the method of Benjamini and Hochberg (Benjamini & Hochberg 1995). Heatmaps were created in *R* using heatmap.2 from the gplots package with a custom color and break function to maintain consistency between plots. Venn diagrams were constructed from the Venn function in gplots and the corresponding figure was assembled using Inkscape.

## **Fe2+ translocation experiments**

Col-0 and *opt3-2* seedlings were grown hydroponically as described earlier. On the day of the experiment, plants were incubated for 30 min in the uptake buffer (5 mM Mes-Tris pH 5.0, 0.2 mM CaSO4, 1 mM ascorbic acid, and 10 µM FeCl3, prepared freshly). After 30 min, plants were transferred to fresh uptake buffer containing <sup>59</sup>Fe2+ (0.1 µCi mL-1 ). After the indicated times (20-90 min), a leaf from each plant was excised, weighed immediately and placed in a scintillation vial. 10 mL of Opti-Fluor liquid scintillator (PerkinElmer) was added to each scintillation vial and kept in the dark for 1 h before the specific activities were determined using a liquid scintillation counting (TriCarb Liquid Scintillation Counter, PerkinElmer). To avoid overlapping signals, vials were separated 2 blocks from each other and a vial with 10 mL of Opti-Fluor was included in the analysis to determine the background activity. For Phosphorimaging experiments, plants were incubated in uptake buffer containing <sup>59</sup>Fe2+ (0.1 µCi mL-1 ) for 90 min before rosettes were dissected for autoradiography using a GE Typhoon FLA 9000 laser scanner as described previously (Mendoza-Cózatl *et al.* 2014).

### **Translating ribosome affinity purification (TRAP)**

During TRAP experiments, polysomes [ribosomes and mRNA] are immunoprecipitated by targeting a FLAG-tag that has been translationally fused to the ribosomal protein RPL18 [HF-RPL18]. The recovered mRNAs, originally attached to ribosomes, can then be used for gene expression analyses. The key determinant to selectively immunoprecipitate polysomes in a tissue- or cell-specific manner is the expression of the Flag-tagged RPL18 subunit in specific cells or tissues using specific promoters. For this experiments we used leaves from Arabidopsis plants expressing HF-RPL18 under the control of the SUC2p promoter, which is specific for companion cells in source leaves (Mustroph et al., 2009). Four-week-old plants (At*SUC2p:FLAG-RPL18)* grown hydroponically were rinsed for 15 min in Milli-Q water and transferred to Fe-deficient media for 2 h. Rosette leaves were then harvested and TRAP was performed as previously described (Reynoso *et al.* 2015; Castro-Guerrero *et al.* 2016) with minor modifications. After the last wash with elution buffers, RNA was released from the EZ-View anti-FLAG® agarose beads (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis) by adding 600 µL TRIzol reagent, vortexed, centrifuged and the supernatant was used for RNA purification using the Direct-zol™ RNA MiniPrep Kit (Zymo Research, USA). Total RNA (DNA-free) was used for cDNA synthesis using the SuperScript® III First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen, USA).

### **Reverse transcription-PCR and quantitative RT-PCR**

For gene expression experiments at different time points during Fe deprivation, plants were grown in hydroponic cultures containing 50 µM of Fe-EDTA. At bolting stage, plants were transferred to hydroponic media without Fe-EDTA after washing the roots for 15 min with water. Plants grown hydroponically were rinsed for 15 min with Milli-Q water before transferring them to Fe-deficient media for the indicated times. Unless otherwise stated, Fe deficiency experiments started in the morning 3 h after the lights were turned on (Zeitgeber 3 or ZT3). Total RNA was isolated from 4 plants per biological replicate using the RNeasy plant mini kit (Qiagen, USA) and cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript III® kit (Invitrogen, USA). RT-PCR was performed using cDNA as the template for the indicated number of cycles. RT-PCR data are representative of at least tee independent experiments. Real-time RT-PCR was conducted using SYBR green master mix and a Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). The ΔΔCt method was used to analyze relative mRNA abundance. The transcript levels were normalized to *ACT2* and data are presented as log2 fold change compared to Col-0. Primers used in this work are listed in Table S5.

## **Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR)**

ddPCR assays were performed using a QX 200 droplet digital PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA) using the same cDNA prepared for RT-PCR experiments. The ddPCR mixture (20 μl) reactions contained EvaGreen ddPCR Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA), gene-specific primers and cDNA as template. Each ddPCR reaction mixture was transferred into a DG8 cartridge (BioRad, USA) and 70 μl of Droplet Generation Oil were added into each of the 8 oil wells. Nano-liter size droplets were formed using a droplet generator (Bio-Rad, USA). Droplets were then transferred very gently to a 96-well polypropylene plate (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), heat-sealed and placed in a thermocycler for standard PCR reactions (C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler, Bio-Rad, USA). After 40 cycles of amplification, the 96-well plate was loaded into a QX200 Droplet Reader (Bio-Rad) and the fluorescence intensity of each individual droplet was measured. The analysis of ddPCR data was performed using the QuantaSoft droplet reader software provided by the manufacturer (Bio-Rad, USA). Quantification of gene expression was originally obtained as copies/μl, which was used to calculate the gene-of-interest/*ACTIN2* ratios. Data presented are the mean ± SE of 3 biological replicates (tissue from 4 plants were pooled together for each biological replicate). The primers used in ddPCR experiments are listed in Table S5.

### **Luciferase imaging**

To assemble the OPT3p::LUC construct in the binary vector pGreen 0179, the luciferase (LUC) coding sequence, containing the omega leader from the tobacco mosaic virus, was amplified from the pPZPXomegaL+ plasmid (Jobe et al., 2012) and cloned into pGreen 0179 using the restriction enzymes BamHI and XbaI. This construct was made Gatewaycompatible by inserting a Gateway cassette at the SmaI site immediately upstream of the BamHI. Next, the 3kb region upstream of the OPT3 start codon (OPT3p) was amplified using the primers listed in Table S5 and cloned into pENTR/D-TOPO following manufacturer's recommendations (Invitrogen, USA). The OPT3p fragment was recombined into pGreen 0179 GW LUC by an LR reaction (Invitrogen, USA) and the resulting plasmid was used to transform Arabidopsis (Col-0) using the Agrobacterium floral-dip method. Several T3

homozygous lines were tested for bioluminescence and line 3-9 was selected for further characterization based on its robust signal. For imaging experiments, OPT3p::LUC seedlings were liquid sterilized and plated on 0.5X Murashige and Skoog medium with 1% Sucrose and 0.8% agar on a sterile 80 µm nylon mesh (Sefar Nylon 6/6 Mesh Lab Pak, Sefar, NY, USA) to facilitate plate-to-plate transfer of seedlings. Seeds were stratified for 2 days at 4 °C and then grown at 22 °C in 12 h light /12 h dark conditions for 7-12 days. Seedlings plates were sprayed with 5 mM luciferin and either imaged every 30 min under 12 h light/12 h dark for two days before transfer to constant light conditions or immediately transferred to constant light conditions as described previously (Huang et al., 2016). For single transfer experiments, seedlings were transferred to replete plates on day two and then transferred to replete or -Fe plates (as described above, *Plant material and growth conditions*) at Zeitgeber 78 (ZT78). For double transfer experiments, seedlings were first transferred to replete or -Fe treatment plates at ZT30. After 2 more days of imaging the seedlings were moved again to different treatment plates (replete, -Fe or excess Fe [400 M]), pre-sprayed with luciferin at ZT78 and imaged for another 3-5 days.

### **Statistical Analysis**

Unless otherwise stated, Student's *t*-test was used to determine whether means were statistically different (P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant). No corrections were made for multiple comparison of means. Statistical analyses were performed using Graphpad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

# **RESULTS**

## **Contrasting Fe phenotypes in** *opt3* **mutants**

The *Arabidopsis opt3-2* mutant has been shown to display a constitutive high expression of *IRT1* and *FRO2* (Stacey *et al.* 2008). In addition to *IRT1* and *FRO2*, Figure 1 shows that *opt3* mutants (*opt3-2* and *opt3-3*) also display a constitutive high activity of the plasma membrane H+-ATPase in roots, shown as a purple-to-yellow change in bromocresol plates (Selote *et al.* 2015). However, a comprehensive genome-wide analysis of gene expression in *opt3* mutants has not been reported. Therefore, we conducted a RNA-seq analysis to identify genes constitutively deregulated in *opt3-2* compared to wildtype plants. For these experiments, we used 4 week-old plants (Col-0 and *opt3-2*) grown in hydroponic conditions containing 12.5 µM Fe (similar to 1/4 MS media). Total RNA was extracted from leaves and roots, 3 independent RNA isolation per tissue (see Methods for a detailed description), and DNA-free samples were used to synthesize libraries suitable for transcriptome analyses using Illumina-sequencing (RNA-seq). After comparing gene expression profiles between *opt3-2* and Col-0, and after performing rigorous statistical filtering, we identified a total of 1143 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), 539 DEGs in roots and 604 DEGs in leaves 539 (≥ 2-fold change, FDR < 0.05; see Table S1 for the full list and description of DEGs). Interestingly, the majority of the DEGs (> 60%) were found to be up-regulated (415 genes in roots and 502 in leaves) rather than down-regulated suggesting an overall activation of transcriptional programs in *opt3-2* compared to Col-0 (Figure 1B). However, it was also evident that there was little overlap between DEGs in roots and leaves. To further determine how many of the DEGs in *opt3-2* were associated with Fe homeostasis, we assessed the expression profile of a specific cluster of Fe-responsive genes known as the *ferrome* (Schmidt & Buckhout 2011), together with genes associated with the GO terms *response to Fe ion* (GO:0010039), *iron ion transport* (GO:0006826), and *iron ion homeostasis* (GO:0055072). This analysis revealed a clear up-regulation of Fe- deficiency responsive genes in roots, including members of the FIT and PYE network, in *opt3-2* roots (Figure 1C; Table S2/Dataset 1). In contrast, *opt3-2* leaves were transcriptionally less different from wildtype Col plants (Figure 1C) and several of the Fe-related genes upregulated in *opt3-2* leaves (e.g. *NAS3, YSL1, FERs*) have in fact been associated to Fe excess rather than Fe deficiency. This contrasting response was unexpected, considering that *opt3-2* over-accumulates Fe in both roots and leaves, and suggests that the Fe overload in *opt3-2* is properly sensed in leaves but not in roots (Figure 1D; Table S2/Dataset 1).

### *opt3-2* **displays opposite transcriptional programs in roots and leaves**

 Fe mobilization toughout the plant requires the activity of Fe transporters and the coordinated synthesis of Fe-binding molecules such as nicotianamine (Conte & Walker 2011). To gain further insight into the transcriptional programs that may be responsible for the altered *ionome* reported in *opt3-2* (Stacey *et al.* 2008; Mendoza-Cózatl *et al.* 2014), we focused our transcriptome analysis on two different types of gene families critical for Fe homeostasis: transcription factors (TFs) and transmembrane proteins (including transporters and putative receptors, Figure 2 A-B). In *opt3-2*, a total of 45 TFs were found to be significantly up-regulated compared to wildtype roots (≥ 2-fold, Figure 2A, Supplemental Table S2/Dataset 2). Notably, the six TFs with the highest expression in *opt3-2* roots are members of the FIT network (bHLH38/39, bHLH100/101) or act within the FIT network (MYB10/72) (Palmer *et al..* 2013). *FIT* was also found to be significantly up-regulated 3.5 times, but it was not in the top 10 up-regulated genes in *opt3-2* roots. In contrast, only 10 TFs were significantly down regulated in *opt3-2* roots (Table S2/Dataset 2) and none of them have previously been associated with Fe homeostasis in Arabidopsis or any other organism. In leaves, 42 TFs were found to be up-regulated in *opt3-2* compared to Col-0 (≥ 2-fold, Figure 2A), and interestingly, 6 TFs within the WRKY family were found among the top 10 most up-regulated TFs in *opt3-2* leaves (Table S2/Dataset 2). Some of these WRKY genes, in particular WRKY48 and WRKY75, have been previously reported to be induced by abiotic stresses such as phosphate deficiency and exposure to hydrogen peroxide (Bakshi & Oelmüller 2014). A similar number of TFs (39 TFs, ≥ 2-fold**)** were down regulated in *opt3-2*

leaves and 6 of them belong to the ERF/AP2 family of transcription factors that have been associated with developmental processes in Arabidopsis (Licausi *et al.* 2013). To facilitate the access and visualization of our transcriptome data, we established a stand-alone version of an electronic Fluorescent Pictograph Browser (eFP browser; available at [http://gene.rnet.missouri.edu/efp/cgi-bin/public\\_html/efpWeb.cgi\)](http://gene.rnet.missouri.edu/efp/cgi-bin/public_html/efpWeb.cgi) (Winter *et al.* 2007) (Figure S1).

Since allocation of Fe within plant tissues requires the coordinated action of Fe and Fe-ligand transporters, we also compared the expression of genes encoding membrane proteins in *opt3-2* and wildtype Col-0. To prevent the loss of information due to the still incomplete annotation of transporters, we included proteins with ≥ 4 predicted transmembrane domains irrespective of their annotation or putative function (Table S3). This analysis revealed that 52 membrane protein genes were up-regulated in *opt3-2* roots compared to Col-0 (≥ 2-fold, Figure 2B, Table S2/Dataset 3) and 11 of these genes have been described to mediate key processes in Fe and Zn homeostasis (e.g. *FRO2, IRT1, FRD3, NRAMP3* and *MTP3*). On the other hand, 22 membrane proteins were significantly down regulated in *opt3-2* roots and some of them have also been associated with Fe and Zn homeostasis (*FRO5, YSL2, ZIP2* and *HMA2*; Table S2/Dataset 3). In *opt3-2* leaves, 62 genes coding for membrane proteins were up-regulated compared to Col-0 (≥ 2-fold, Figure 2B; Table S2/Dataset 3). However, and in contrast to *opt3-2* roots, the majority of these DEGs have not been described as part of any Fe or Zn homeostasis network. In fact, only two of these genes, YSL1 and YSL7, have been associated with the movement of Fe/Zn/Ni-NA complexes and both YSLs genes are known to be induced by Fe excess, rather than Fe deficiency (Klatte *et al.* 2009; Ravet *et al.* 2009). Similarly, only 2 out of the 21 down regulated genes encoding membrane protein in *opt3-2* leaves are known to play roles in Fe or copper homeostasis (YSL2 and FRO4; DiDonato et al., 2004; Bernal et al., 2012). *OPT3* was

the third most down regulated genes encoding membrane protein, but this is due to the fact that *opt3-2* is a knockdown mutant with 28% of residual expression in leaves compared to wild-type Col-0 (Table S2/Dataset 3). Taken together, these results suggest that the constitutive Fe deficiency in *opt3-2* roots includes the up-regulation of the FIT and PYE networks. This in turn leads to the up-regulation of Fe transporters and other membrane proteins that facilitate the solubilization (FRO2) and translocation (FRD3) of Fe. However, *opt3-2* leaves do not show any transcriptional programs associated with a Fe deficiency response. In fact, some of the transcription factors and membrane proteins up-regulated are part of core abiotic stress responses consistent with oxidative stress and Fe overload (Hahn *et al.* 2013).

Next, we investigated whether the opposite expression patterns observed in leaves and roots were the result of the reduced expression of *OPT3* and not an artifact unique to the *opt3-2* allele. To test this, we analyzed the expression of Fe-responsive genes in an independent allele of *opt3* (*opt3-3*) by qRT-PCR. Figure 3 shows that, consistent with the RNA-seq data, *opt3* leaves (*opt3-2* and *opt3-3*) show a clear induction of genes associated with a Fe overload (Figure 3A), while roots display an up-regulation of genes associated with Fe deficiency responses (Figure 3B). These results support the original observation that *opt3* leaves are capable of sensing and responding to the Fe overload but this information is not properly relayed to roots due to the low and insufficient expression of *OPT3* in *opt3* mutants.

### **Fe uptake and translocation is enhanced in** *opt3-2*

Fe uptake in Arabidopsis is controlled at transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels (Kerkeb *et al.* 2008; Ivanov *et al.* 2014). In *opt3-2* roots, there is a constitutive high expression of the different components of the Fe uptake, vacuole release and translocation machinery (i.e. *FRO2, IRT1, IRT2, NRAMP3, FRD3;* Figure 2B; Table S2/Dataset 3). However, the correlation between expression levels of genes mediating Fe uptake and the actual capacity of roots to take up and translocate Fe is often overlooked. To address this question, we performed radiotracer experiments using intact plants, grown in hydroponic cultures and <sup>59</sup>Fe2+ . Uptake experiments were performed in the presence of ascorbic acid to ensure that ferric iron [Fe3+] was reduced to ferrous iron [Fe2+], which is the preferred substrate for IRT1. A total of 2 µCi of <sup>59</sup>Fe2+ (10 µM Fe2+ final concentration) were added to the hydroponic solution and the radioactivity in leaves was measured at different time points (Figure 4A). After a lag period of 30 min, the first counts coming from <sup>59</sup>Fe2+ were detected in leaves and after 60 min it was evident that *opt3-2* plants were translocating significantly more <sup>59</sup>Fe2+ than wild-type Col-0 plants. After 90 min, *opt3-2* leaves were translocating 2.2 times more <sup>59</sup>Fe2+ compared to wild-type Col-0 plants (82 ± 24 pmoles <sup>59</sup>Fe2+/ min / g FW *vs* 36 ± 8 pmoles <sup>59</sup>Fe2+ / min / g FW; mean ± SD, n = 3). In an independent set of experiments, plants were incubated with 2 µCi of <sup>59</sup>Fe2+ for 90 min and then leaves were imaged using a Phosphorimager screen to assess the distribution of <sup>59</sup>Fe2+ within the leaves. Figure 4B shows that the distribution of <sup>59</sup>Fe2+ was even across the entire rosette, which is expected from xylem-driven transport. These observations were further confirmed by measuring the radioactivity present in each detached leaf separately (Figure S2). Altogether, these results suggest that the dramatic up-regulation of the Fe uptake machinery in *opt3-2* (i.e. 44-fold increase in *IRT1* expression) translates into a modest but significant 2-fold increase in the rate of Fe uptake and translocation from roots to leaves.

### **Leaf vasculature responds faster to Fe deficiency and Fe sufficiency than roots**

*OPT3* is a Fe-responsive gene preferentially expressed in the leaf vasculature (Mendoza-Cózatl *et al.* 2014). To further explore the timing of Fe deficiency response at the whole plant level, we first use *OPT3* as a leaf marker for Fe deficiency and compare its induction with *FIT* and *IRT1*, which are well-known Fe deficiency markers in roots. For these experiments, and unless otherwise stated, plants were transferred from replete media to Fedeficient media in the morning 3 h after the lights were turned on (Zeitgeber 3, ZT3). RT-PCR experiments showed that *OPT3* responded faster to Fe deficiency than *FIT* and *IRT1* (Figure 5). *FIT* expression was detected 8 h after plants were transferred to hydroponic medium lacking Fe, while *IRT1* expression was detected after 12 h as previously reported (Vert *et al.* 2003; Long *et al.* 2010). *OPT3* expression however, was up-regulated at the firsttime point chosen in this experiment (4 h), demonstrating that the Fe deficiency response in leaves precedes the transcriptional response in roots. Similar results were obtained using quantitative ddPCR (Figure S3).

Knowing that *OPT3* is preferentially expressed in companion cells (phloem-loading cells), and that its expression remains tissue-specific during Fe deficiency (Mendoza-Cózatl *et al.* 2014), we explored whether additional Fe-inducible vascular markers displayed similar timing responses in leaves when plants experience Fe deprivation. By mining publicly available databases, including Fe deficiency transcriptome data (Baüer & Blondet 2011; Schuler *et al.* 2011; Rodríguez-Celma *et al.* 2012; Urzica *et al.* 2012; Ivanov *et al.* 2012) and later cell-specific expression data (Mustroph et al., 2009) we were able to identify additional Fe-responsive genes preferentially expressed in the vasculature [*AHP4* (At3g16360), *FRO3*, At1g47400, *bHLH100*]. From these, *FRO3* and *bHLH100* have previously, and independently, been localized to the vasculature using promoter::GUS transcriptional fusions (Mukherjee *et al.* 2006; Wang *et al.* 2007) while At3g16360 and At1g47400 were selected based on bioinformatics analyses (Mustroph *et al.* 2009; Rodríguez-Celma *et al.* 2012). In addition, we were also able to identify Fe-responsive genes preferentially localized in mesophyll cells [At5g44580 and *CGLD27* (At5g67370)] but not in the vasculature (Mustroph *et al.* 2009; Rodríguez-Celma *et al.* 2012). Note that, in some cases, the role of these Fe responsive mesophyll or companion-cell marker genes in Fe homeostasis is not known; however, their transcriptional induction during Fe deficiency is what makes them suitable to assess the shoot Fe deficiency response in a cell-specific manner. To further confirm that At3g16360 and At1g47400 were in fact preferentially expressed in companion cells during Fe deprivation, we isolated transcripts preferentially being translated in companion cells using the Translating Ribosome Affinity Purification (TRAP) technique. SUC2p::HF\_AtRPL18 plants grown hydroponically in replete media for 4 weeks were transferred to Fe-deficient media for 2 h and the rosette leaves were used to isolate both total RNA and RNA associated to ribosomes (TRAPed). The isolated RNA was used to synthesize cDNA and the level of gene expression between TRAPed and total RNA preparations was assessed by qRT-PCR. Figure 6A shows that SUC2, a companion cell marker, was enriched in TRAPed RNA, confirming that we had in fact enriched mRNAs associated to ribosomes from companion cells. The mRNAs of At3g16360 and At1g47400 were also enriched (> 2 fold) compared to total RNA suggesting that these genes are preferentially expressed and translated in companion cells. In contrast, the mRNAs of At5g44580 and At5g67370 (mesophyll cell markers) were depleted in TRAPed preparations suggesting that these genes are not preferentially expressed in the vasculature, which is consistent with the transcriptome data (Mustroph et al., 2009), suggesting that these are genes preferentially expressed in mesophyll cells. Note that currently there are not TRAP lines available specific to mesophyll cells to perform the opposite experiment, as the Rubisco promoter, often considered as a mesophyll cell marker, is active in companion cells (Mustroph *et al.* 2009) ( Figure S4). Nevertheless, Figure 6A confirmed that AHP4 (At3g16360) and At1g47400 are *bona fide* Fe responsive genes preferentially expressed and translated in companion cells.

To further increase the time resolution of the Fe deficiency response in leaves, we began sampling rosettes from plants experiencing Fe deprivation at earlier time points (Figure 6B). Interestingly, the companion cell-specific markers for Fe deficiency begin to respond in a relative short period of time (≤ 2 h), while Fe-responsive genes from mesophyll cells responded later (≥ 2h). These results were quantitatively confirmed using ddPCR (Figure S5). Altogether, these results suggest that Fe deficiency is first sensed in leaves, particularly within the vasculature, and this response is faster than the earliest events reported for Fe deficiency responses in roots (Figures 5 and 6).

Proper maintenance of Fe homeostasis requires rapid and efficient sensing of Fe availability and a corresponding transcriptional response. This includes changes in gene expression during Fe deficiency but also transcriptional changes once the Fe levels have been restored (Kerkeb *et al.* 2008; Ivanov *et al.* 2014; Brumbarova *et al.* 2015). Having demonstrated that the leaf vasculature responds to Fe deficiency in a relatively short period of time (< 4 h; Figure 6), we then decided to test the timing of transcriptional responses when Fe levels are restored (Fe re-supply). In these experiments, the expression of the selected marker genes (Figure 6) were assessed in plants transferred from replete media to Fe-deficient media and then moved back to replete media (Figure 7). As previously shown, all Fe-deficiency companion-cell marker genes were induced after 2 h of experiencing Fe deprivation and the induction continued after the transfer to replete media; however, this induction was reversed within 2 h of being transferred to Fe-sufficient conditions (Figure 7). In previous Fe deficiency experiments, we showed that the transcriptional activation of both companion cell and mesophyll cell marker genes continued for more than 6 h after experiencing Fe deprivation (Figure 6). The dampening of the transcriptional activation after 2 h of Fe resupply suggests that the leaf vasculature is capable of sensing the availability of Fe coming from roots and respond accordingly to both Fe deficiency or sufficiency. Interestingly, the induction of Fe deficiency markers in mesophyll cell continued after the plants were resupplied with Fe beyond the 2 h time point, suggesting that the Fe deficiency and sufficiency responses in mesophyll cells is delayed compared to the leaf vasculature.

### **Dynamic imaging of leaf responses to Fe availability.**

Luciferase-based reporter systems have previously been used to track in real-time plant responses to changes in environmental or nutrient conditions (Jobe et al., 2012). To further explore in a dynamic and non-invasive way the response of Arabidopsis leaves to changes in Fe availability, we generated transgenic plants (Col-0) where the expression of the firefly luciferase reporter gene (LUC) was under the control a 3 kb of the upstream regulatory region (promoter) of *OPT3*. As a first step, we characterized the bioluminescence of OPT3p::LUC plants grown in replete conditions. OPT3p::LUC plants show circadian expression under constant light and temperature, with expression peaking near subjective dawn and reaching a trough before subjective dusk (Figure S6). These results are consistent with data from array-based circadian experiments in Arabidopsis (available at DIURNAL, diurnal.mocklerlab.org/diurnal\_data\_finders/new; Mockler et al., 2007). Next, we determined the behavior of the OPT3 promoter by tracking LUC activity in OPT3p::LUC plants during Fe deprivation (Fig. 8A). For these experiments, and to avoid the effects of light/dark transitions on reporter levels, seedlings were first grown on replete media over a nylon mesh and transferred to Fe deficient media at Zeitgeber (ZT) 78 under constant light conditions. Dynamic tracking of LUC activity showed a rapid and consistent increase on luminescence when plants experienced Fe deprivation (Figure 8B). These rapid response is consistent with data obtained by gene expression analyses (Figure 6B, S3). Interestingly, LUC activity during Fe limitation continued to oscillate in a rhythmic pattern; however, the lowest point of LUC activity (i.e. trough) during Fe deprivation was still significantly higher compared to the highest peak of LUC activity in plants grown on replete media, suggesting that the induction of *OPT3* by Fe limitation is independent of the circadian clock.

Our previous gene expression experiments under Fe limitation and re-supply at independent time points (Figure 7) suggested that the leaf vasculature responds rapidly to Fe re-supply by repressing the induction of Fe deficiency marker genes. To explore this repression in more detail, we took advantage of the dynamic nature of LUC imaging to investigate the behavior of OPT3p::LUC plants when Fe levels are restored after a period of Fe deprivation (Figure 8C). For these experiments, OPT3p::LUC plants were first transferred from replete media to Fe deficient media (first transfer at ZT30) and then transferred for a second time to either Fe deficient, replete or media containing excess Fe (at ZT78). Figure 8C shows that OPT3p::LUC plants grown on Fe deficient media display a continuous increase in LUC activity. In contrast, plants transferred from Fe deficient plates to either replete or media containing excess Fe show a sharp decline of LUC activity shortly after the transfer (2 h), which is consistent with the time frame determined by RT-PCR experiments (Figure 7). These results demonstrate that leaves, and more specifically the leaf vasculature, can sense and respond dynamically to changes in Fe availability.

# **DISCUSSION**

OPT3 has recently been identified as a key component of the shoot-to-root Fe signaling network (Mendoza-Cózatl *et al.* 2014). *opt3* mutants are different from other Arabidopsis mutants displaying an active Fe deficiency responses by the fact that *opt3* mutants cannot be rescued by foliar application of Fe (García *et al.* 2013). The phloemspecific localization of OPT3, together with *in vitro* and *in planta* radiotracer experiments, led to the hypothesis that OPT3 mediates the mobilization of Fe, or Fe-ligand complexes, into companion cells and later into the phloem stream for long-distance transport. These Fe-ligand complexes, together with additional yet unidentified players, are proposed to be part of the negative systemic signal that represses Fe uptake in roots under Fe-replete conditions (Mendoza-Cózatl *et al.* 2014). The consequences of having an impaired shoot-to-root communication of the Fe status has proven to be a powerful tool to explore signaling and transport processes regulating the uptake and allocation of Fe in Arabidopsis. Here we show that, a transcriptional up-regulation of Fe uptake in *opt3-2* increases the rate of Fe translocation rates by two-fold (Figure 4), leading to a Fe overload that is properly sensed in shoots but not in roots. Furthermore, by taking advantage of tissue-specific Fe-responsive genes, we further demonstrate that the leaf vasculature responds to Fe deficiency and resupply before mesophyll cells and roots.

### **Roots and leaves have autonomous Fe sensing mechanisms**

*opt3* mutants over accumulate Fe in roots and leaves (Stacey *et al.* 2008; Mendoza-Cózatl *et al.* 2014; Zhai *et al.* 2014) but here we show that the transcriptional responses in *opt3-2* are opposite, displaying an Fe deficiency response in roots but an Fe overload in leaves (Figure 1C). The root transcriptome also suggests that *opt3-2* roots are under severe stress. For instance, 30% of the up-regulated genes in *opt3-2* roots (124 genes) are part of the *Plant Core Environmental Stress Response* (PCER) identified when plants experience either oxidative stress, wounding or salt stress (Table S4; Hahn et al., 2013). However, and despite the oxidative stress occurring in *opt3-2*, markers associated with Fe excess, such as *FER3, FER4, ZIP5*, *YSL1* or *FSD1* remained unchanged or even repressed. Notably, some of these Fe sufficiency markers (*YSL1*, *YSL7*, *FER3* and *FER4*) were induced in *opt3-2* shoots but not in roots (Fig. 1C; Table S1) suggesting that, at least at the transcriptional level, *opt3-2* roots are unable to sense the ongoing Fe overload. The current model for Fe sensing, at the whole plant level, suggests that Fe uptake in roots is negatively regulated by a systemic signal originated in leaves and that OPT3 mediates, at least in part, the movement of this signaling molecule. A reduced expression of *OPT3*, observed in *opt3* mutants, results in a less negative signal mobilized to roots thus triggering a Fe deficiency response even during replete conditions. The precise chemical nature of the mobile signal and the OPT3 substrate are still unclear. Phloem sap measurements in *opt3* mutants show a 50% reduction in Fe content, which led to the suggestion that OPT3 may be mobilizing Fe or an Fe-ligand complex into the phloem. The fact that *opt3* roots are unable to sense their own Fe status despite experiencing a significant Fe overload, suggests that the negative signal is unlikely to be ferrous iron (Fe2+), which is present at high levels in *opt3* roots. Alternatively, the mobile signal could be an Fe-ligand molecule synthesized in leaves and either mobilized into the phloem by OPT3 or formed in the phloem sap after OPT3 has delivered Fe2+ into companion cells and the phloem stream.

Fe sensing in *opt3* leaves on the other hand, seems intact. Markers for Fe excess such as *NAS3, YSL1, YSL7, FER3* and *FER4* are significantly up-regulated, together with 158 genes part of the PCER (Table S4; Hahn et al., 2013). The Fe overload in leaves is the result of the overall activation of Fe reduction, uptake and translocation *opt3-2* roots (Figures 1A, C and 4). Our radiotracer experiments further show that this transcriptional activation leads to a two-fold increase in the Fe root-to-shoot translocation rates, compared wild-type plants (Figure 4). Overall these results also suggest that once roots are committed to take up and translocate more Fe, leaves have no mechanisms in place to reduce the incoming flux of Fe. This is interesting considering that leaves are properly sensing a Fe overload but are unable to rely this information to roots due to the impaired expression of *OPT3* in *opt3* mutants. Altogether, these results suggest that roots have a major role controlling the Fe levels in both roots and shoots, and that the role of leaves is to sense the Fe status of the whole plant and transduce that information to roots, via the phloem.

### **Timing of Fe deficiency responses at tissue- and cell-specific resolution**

Over the last decade, there have been significant advances at identifying patterns of gene expression at tissue and cell-specific resolution (Mustroph *et al.* 2009). In addition, several transcriptome studies have identified Fe-responsive genes specific to leaves and roots

(Rodríguez-Celma *et al.* 2012). By combining the tissue-specific expression of genes and their known response to Fe deprivation, we were able to define the site and timing of the earliest Fe deficiency and sufficiency responses in Arabidopsis. Initially, this was possible by comparing the expression of *OPT3, FIT* and *IRT1* (Figure 5) and determined that leaves are able to sense changes in Fe availability hours before roots. Moreover, by using Fe-responsive genes and their known location in the vasculature or mesophyll cells, we were able to demonstrate that the leaf vasculature respond to Fe deficiency considerably faster than roots (< 4 h). A similar timeline, in the opposite direction, was reported for *HEMA1* (At1g58290; Rodriguez-Celma et al., 2013). *HEMA1* encodes a Glu-tRNA reductase involved in tetrapyrrole biosynthesis; *HEMA1* is expressed in both mesophyll and companion cells (Mustroph et al., 2009) and is rapidly repressed during Fe deficiency (Rodriguez-Celma et al., 2013). *SAT1* (At1g55920) and *GH9C1* (At1g48930) are two additional genes that are induced by Fe deficiency in leaves and are expressed in both mesophyll and companion cells (Mustroph et al., 2009; Trapet et al., 2016). The precise timing of induction however has not been determined but these genes, together with the mesophyll and companion cell markers identified in the present work, may help defining better Fe deficiency responses in leaves at cell-specific resolution. It is unclear at this point whether the Fe signaling pathway is different in the vasculature and mesophyll cells or whether the different response is a direct consequence of the position of companion cells within the vascular bundle. Companion cells (phloem) are in close proximity to xylem vessels; therefore, changes in the apoplastic concentration of solutes, as a result of changes in xylem composition and xylem unloading, will likely be first sensed by cells surrounding the vasculature (i.e. companion cells). Considering that it takes at least 60 min for an atom of Fe in the growth media to reach the leaves (Figure 4), it is reasonable to suggest that it will take a similar amount of time (60+ min) before the Fe concentration in the apoplast near the leaf vasculature begin to decline

when plants experience Fe deprivation. This delay is due in part to the residual Fe already present in cells (symplast) and xylem vessels at the time of the transfer. Moreover, the vacuole is a major reservoir of Fe, critical to maintain Fe homeostasis in plant cells (Lanquar *et al.* 2005). In mesophyll cells, the large central vacuole occupies as much as 90% of the cell by volume, while in companion cells vacuoles are small and fragmented (Froelich *et al.* 2011). Thus, vacuoles in companion cells are likely to have less buffer capacity to maintain a steady concentration of solutes compared to mesophyll cells. The proximity to the vasculature and the different cell morphology of companion cells compared to other leaf cells may explain the rapid transcriptional response of companion cells in response to changes in Fe levels (Figures 6-8). In fact, the up-regulation of *OPT3* during Fe deprivation is perhaps among the earliest transcriptional events in Fe signaling at the whole plant level. From the physiological perspective, sensing Fe in companion cells will be advantageous as they can relay faster information about the Fe status in leaves to roots via the phloem. It remains to be determined however, whether companion cells, mesophyll cells and roots share a common Fe sensing mechanism. Nevertheless, the rapid response of the leaf vasculature to Fe deficiency and the key role of shoot-to-root signaling to maintain Fe homeostasis at the whole plant level make companion cells an ideal cell type to study Fe deficiency responses at cell-specific resolution and the integration of signals leading to the activation or repression of the Fe uptake machinery in roots.
