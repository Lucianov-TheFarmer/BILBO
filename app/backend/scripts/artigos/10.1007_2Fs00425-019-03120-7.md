### **Abstract**

# *Main conclusion* **Modulation of gene expression in roots of** *Brassica napus* **by silicon (Si) supply could allow plants to cope with future stresses.**

The origin of the benefcial efects of silicon (Si) in plants, especially when they are subject to stress, remains poorly understood. Some authors have shown that Si alleviates plant stress and consider that this is mainly due to a mechanical efect on the cell wall. In addition, the other studies have shown that Si can also afect gene expression and modulate a number of metabolic pathways, especially in plants cultivated under stress conditions. Previously, Haddad et al. (Front Plant Sci 9:5–16, [2018](#page-6-0)) showed that a pretreatment of *Brassica napus* plants with Si (1.7 mM) for 1 week alleviated the stress induced by N privation. These results suggest that this improved resistance in Si-treated plants might be due to the establishment of defense mechanisms prior to exposure to the N stress. The aim of the current work was to test this assumption in *Brassica napus* roots (where Si is mainly stored) using a transcriptomic approach via the RNA sequencing. Our results indicated that the Si supply leads to a modulation of the expression of genes in *Brassica napus* roots. Functional categorization of the diferentially expressed genes demonstrated that numerous genes are involved in diferent metabolic pathways and especially in cell wall synthesis, phytohormone metabolism, and stress responses. All these results show that Si modifes the root metabolism of *B. napus*, which could allow a better adaptation to future stresses.

# **Introduction**

Silicon (Si) is a major constituent of the earth's crust, comprising 28% of the lithosphere (Epstein [1994](#page-6-1)). In recent years, the role of silicon in plants has been investigated extensively through numerous studies (Coskun et al. [2019](#page-6-2)). Yet, to date, Si is considered as a benefcial but non-essential element for plant growth and development (Guntzer et al. [2012\)](#page-6-3). The benefcial efects of Si supply have been observed, especially in plants subjected to either biotic or abiotic stresses (Keeping and Reynolds [2009;](#page-6-4) Liang et al. [2015](#page-6-5). For example, Si is effective at reducing diseases caused by both fungi and bacteria in diferent plant species (Ma and Yamaji [2006](#page-6-6)), to increase plant resistance to drought (Eneji et al. [2008\)](#page-6-7) and to alleviate salt stress (Azeem et al. [2015](#page-5-0)). In a recent study, Haddad et al. ([2018\)](#page-6-0) showed that a pretreatment of *Brassica napus* plant with Si (1.7 mM, for 1 week) enhances growth and alleviates stress induced by N privation. This study suggests that a Si supply could promote defense mechanisms in plants prior to exposure to an N stress. The main known mechanism attributed to Sibenefts is the mechanical barrier formed by accumulation and polymerization of Si as Si(OH)4 in cell walls (Guerriero et al. [2016\)](#page-6-8), which promotes the strengthening of the extracellular matrix and increases mechanical support in the plant. However, the role of Si still remains inconclusive (Epstein [1994;](#page-6-1) Guntzer et al. [2012;](#page-6-3) Pontigo et al. [2015](#page-6-9)), because Si does not seem to act as a direct signal or second messenger (Vivancos et al. [2015](#page-6-10); Coskun et al. [2019](#page-6-2)). Indeed, Si supplies only modulate a few genes, especially when plants are cultivated under non-stressed conditions (Fawe et al. [2001;](#page-6-11) Watanabe et al. [2004](#page-6-12); Fauteux et al. [2006](#page-6-13); Chain et al. [2009;](#page-5-1) Rasoolizadeh et al. [2018](#page-6-14)).

To elucidate the role of Si in priming defense mechanisms, we performed a wide-ranging analysis of the infuence of Si on the root transcriptome of *B. napus* due to the major role that this compartment plays in both Si uptake and storage (Lux et al. [2003;](#page-6-15) Haddad et al. [2018](#page-6-0)). Thus, a comparative analysis of the root gene expression between plants supplied with (1.7 mM) or without Si has been performed using RNA sequencing (RNAseq) technology to detect diferentially expressed genes (DEGs) and to identify the functional categories and metabolic pathways in which they could be involved.

## **Materials and methods**

### **Seedling growth and Si treatment**

*Brassica napus* L. (var. "Citizzen") seeds were germinated on perlite over deionized water for 4 days in darkness. Seedlings were then transferred to natural light conditions and supplied with the nutrient solution previously described by Haddad et al. [\(2018\)](#page-6-0). After emergence of the frst leaf (after 2 weeks), seedlings were transferred to hydroponic tanks (20 L) containing the same nutrient solution as before. The natural light was supplemented by high-pressure sodium lamps (MASTER Greenpower T400 W, Philips) providing photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) of 450 μmol of photons m−2 s−1 at the canopy level for 16 h per day. The thermoperiod was 20 °C (day) and 15 °C (night). After the emergence of the fourth leaf, plants were split into two batches: one (+Si) supplemented with 1.7 mM sodium silicate (Na2SiO3, 9H2O) and the other one (−Si) supplemented with 1.7 mM sodium chloride. The pH of the nutrient solutions was bufered to 5.6. After 1 week, roots of plants were rinsed threefold with nutrient solution without Si (pH 5.6) then shoot and root samples were separated and frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for further analysis. For all analyses, three biological replicates (*n*=3) corresponding to a pool of three plants were used.

### **Silicon analysis**

Silicon content in plants was determined from 100 mg of dried and crushed plant tissues (shoots and roots), using the colorimetric method previously described by Haddad et al. ([2018\)](#page-6-0).

## **RNA extraction and RNA sequencing (RNAseq) technology**

Total RNAs were extracted from 200 mg of root samples using the hot phenol method as previously described by Haddad et al. ([2018\)](#page-6-0). Purifcation of RNAs including a step of DNA digestion by DNAse treatment was performed using an RNeasy mini kit according to the manufacturer's protocol (Qiagen, Courtaboeuf, France).

Sequencing was undertaken with an Illumina Nex-Seq 500 sequencer (IPS2 POPS platform). RNAseq libraries were generated by the TruSeq Stranded mRNA protocol (Illumina®, California, USA) in paired-ends (PE), 260 bp in size and a read length of 75 bases. Twelve samples per lane NextSeq 500 using the individual barcoded adapters and giving approximately 25 million PE reads per sample were generated. Classical trimming (Qscore >20, read length >30) was performed to remove bad-quality sequences and the STAR\_2.5.2a mapper was used to align reads against the *B. napus* transcriptome (with local option and other default parameters). The abundance of each of 52,962 genes (annotation V5 from Genoscope: [http://www.genoscope.cns.fr/](http://www.genoscope.cns.fr/brassicanapus/data/) [brassicanapus/data/\)](http://www.genoscope.cns.fr/brassicanapus/data/) was calculated by counts PE reads mapping unambiguously one gene. According to these rules, 25% of the PE reads were unmapped, 10% of the PE reads with multi-hits were removed, and 65% of PE reads were associated with a gene without ambiguity.

### **RNAseq bioinformatic analysis**

Diferential analysis followed the procedure described in Rigaill et al. ([2016\)](#page-6-16). Library size was normalized using the trimmed mean of *M* value (TMM) method and count distribution was modeled with a negative binomial generalized linear model. Dispersion was estimated by the edgeR method (V1.12.0, McCarthy et al. [2012](#page-6-17)). Expression diferences were compared between + Si and −Si plants using the likelihood ratio test and *p values* were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control False Discovery Rate (FDR, *p* value <0.05). Considering our experimental context (Si supply tended to have a priming efect on unstressed plants), we chose to consider a gene as being diferentially expressed for an adjusted *p* value ≤0.05 irrespective of the absolute value of the "Log2 fold change". Fragments Per Kilobase Million (FPKMs) were calculated for visual analysis only, and were obtained by dividing normalized counts by gene length. For all diferentially expressed genes (DEGs), the gene ontology and the MapMan bank identifcation number (BIN) ontology were assigned using *B. napus* GO annotations from Genoscope (2015-04-27) and the Mercator Automated Sequence Annotation pipeline (V3), respectively. In addition, each *B. napus* CDS (from Genoscope V5) was blasted against the NCBI *Viridiplantae* nucleotide database to fnd the best *B. napus* RNA match. All these data are provided in supplemental table S1.

RNAseq expression data were validated using four DEGs with contrasting fold changes for RT-qPCR analysis. RT and qPCR were performed following the protocol described by Haddad et al. ([2018](#page-6-0)). The primers used and the results of the RT-qPCRs are presented in table S2.

Gene ontology enrichment analysis for Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) was performed with gene ontology (GO) information slimmed to plants using the BiNGO plug-in (V3.0.3) of the Cytoscape platform (V3.7.0). Hypergeometric test was applied to identify enriched GO terms (a *p* value ≤0.05 was considered as signifcant). In addition, pathway analysis of DEGs was carried out using MapMan (V3.6.0) with the *B. napus* CDS annotation (Genoscope V5), the biotic stress pathway overview layout and a *p value* cut-of of 0.05.

### **RNAseq data management**

All steps of the experiment, from growth conditions to bioinformatic analyses, were managed in a CATdb database (Gagnot et al. [2007](#page-6-18); ProjectID: NGS2017\_08\_Brasilice). The RNAseq project was submitted to the GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus; Edgar et al. [2002;](#page-6-19) [http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) [geo](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo); GEO accession: GSE122968) international repository.

### **Statistical analysis**

Statistical analyses of silicon contents were performed with Microsoft® Excel 2010/XLStat® 2016 software. Analysis of variance was conducted using Student's test after verifying compliance of normality of the data and homogeneity of variance using the Shapiro–Wilk and Bartlett tests, respectively.

# **Results and discussion**

The present study aims to provide a comprehensive survey of the root transcriptome to identify metabolic pathways responsive to external silicon supply in *B. napus* cultivated under non-stressed conditions. For this, plants were cultivated in hydroponic conditions for 7 days and supplemented (+Si; 1.7 mM) or not (−Si) with silicon.

Despite *B. napus* being a non-Si accumulator species (Guntzer et al. [2012](#page-6-3)), Si was efciently taken up by *B. napus* (3.89±0.19 versus 6.34±0.05 mg g−1 DW in −Si and +Si plants, respectively) and mainly stored in the roots (0.173±0.02 versus 0.384±0.02 mg g−1 DW in −Si and +Si plants, respectively). These results are in agreement with those reported by Farshidi et al. ([2012\)](#page-6-20) and more recently by Haddad et al. [\(2018\)](#page-6-0). This storage of Si mainly in the roots is an argument for focusing on the efect of Si on gene regulation and subsequent metabolic pathway modulations in this compartment.

This study shows that the number of DEGs that responded to Si was quite low in roots (74 DEGs; Fig. [1a](#page-3-0)) when the log2Fold change threshold was arbitrarily set to 1.5, a classical value for DEG analysis with RNAseq. This result is in agreement with numerous studies, showing that Si supply on unstressed plants led to only 50 DEGs in roots of soybean (Rasoolizadeh et al. [2018](#page-6-14)) or 2, 20, and 47 DEGs in leaves of *Arabidopsis,* rice, and wheat, respectively (Watanabe et al. [2004](#page-6-12); Chain et al. [2009;](#page-5-1) Fauteux et al. [2006](#page-6-13)). Nevertheless, considering the assumption that Si could play an indirect role in a priming efect in unstressed *B. napus* plants (Haddad et al. [2018\)](#page-6-0), it seemed relevant to also consider genes with lower log2Fold changes. Thus, by applying this criterion (all log2fold changes for an FDR adjusted *p* value ≤0.05), the number of DEGs in the roots reached 1040 (Fig. [1](#page-3-0)a). Among them, 323 (31%) genes were signifcantly upregulated and 717 (69%) were downregulated (Fig. [1a](#page-3-0) and Supplementary Table 1). Upon functional categorization (BIN Groups) of these DEGs, and excluding the "not assigned" (21% of DEGs) and "Misc" (10.4% of DEGs) categories, the most representative categories were "RNA", "Protein", "Signaling", "Hormone metabolism", "stress", Transport", "Development", and "Cell wall" (54% of DEGs were grouped in these eight categories out of a total of 32 Fig. [1b](#page-3-0)). This indicates that rapeseed initiates broad molecular modifcations in response to Si supply, but it is interesting to note that DEGs were not randomly distributed, but belong to a limited number of functional categories. RNA, signaling, hormone metabolism, stress, and cell wall have been reported frequently among studies on silica (Manivannan et al. [2017](#page-6-21)). This strengthens the hypothesis on the putative physiological roles of Si in alleviating stresses in plants (Pontigo et al. [2015;](#page-6-9) Etesami and Jeong [2018;](#page-6-22) Coskun et al. [2019](#page-6-2)).

In agreement with this role in avoiding the negative consequences of a stress, GO enrichment and MapMan classifcation revealed a modulation by Si of numerous DEGs directly or indirectly associated with biotic and abiotic stress responses (Fig. [2](#page-4-0)a, b). Their functional annotation (Table [1](#page-5-2); Supplementary Table 1) indicated that 30 of the DEGs are related to biotic stress (BIN 20.1) and 21 to abiotic stress (BIN 20.2). Most of these genes were downregulated (41) by Si under our conditions. # **Conclusion**

A high-throughput transcriptome analysis, performed, for the frst time, in roots of *B. napus* provided a survey of the genes related to the responses to Si of *B. napus* cultivated under non-stressed conditions. Taking into account the modulated gene-network in roots, this study provides evidence that even under non-stressed conditions, Si modulates a large range of genes especially involved in cell wall remodeling.
