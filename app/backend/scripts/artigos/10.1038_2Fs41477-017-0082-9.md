# *Physcomitrella* MADS-box genes regulate water supply and sperm movement for fertilization

Shizuka Koshimizu<sup>1,2</sup>, Rumiko Kofuji<sup>1,3</sup>, Yuko Sasaki-Sekimoto<sup>4,5</sup>, Masahide Kikkawa<sup>6</sup>, Mie Shimojima<sup>4</sup>, Hiroyuki Ohta<sup>0,4,5,7</sup>, Shuji Shigenobu<sup>2,8</sup>, Yukiko Kabeya<sup>1</sup>, Yuji Hiwatashi<sup>0,1,2,9</sup>, Yosuke Tamada<sup>1,2</sup>, Takashi Murata<sup>1,2</sup> and Mitsuyasu Hasebe<sup>1,2\*</sup>

MIKC classic (MIKC<sup>c</sup>)-type MADS-box genes encode transcription factors that function in various developmental processes, including angiosperm floral organ identity. Phylogenetic analyses of the MIKC<sup>c</sup>-type MADS-box family, including genes from non-flowering plants, suggest that the increased numbers of these genes in flowering plants is related to their functional divergence; however, their precise functions in non-flowering plants and their evolution throughout land plant diversification are unknown. Here, we show that MIKC<sup>c</sup>-type MADS-box genes in the moss *Physcomitrella patens* function in two ways to enable fertilization. Analyses of protein localization, deletion mutants and overexpression lines of all six genes indicate that three MIKC<sup>c</sup>-type MADS-box genes redundantly regulate cell division and growth in the stems for appropriate external water conduction, as well as the formation of sperm with motile flagella. The former function appears to be maintained in the flowering plant lineage, while the latter was lost in accordance with the loss of sperm.

ADS-box genes are conserved in green plants, metazoans and fungi<sup>1,2</sup>, and encode proteins containing the MADS domain, which comprises approximately 60 amino acid residues with DNA binding activity for transcriptional regulation<sup>3,4</sup>. In addition to the MADS-domain, MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes<sup>5</sup> encode I, K and C domains for protein interactions. MIKC<sup>C</sup>type MADS-domain proteins interact with distinct sets of other MADS-domain proteins, other transcription factors and chromatin remodelling factors<sup>6,7</sup>. These interactions enable MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-domain proteins to specifically regulate different sets of genes, including those encoding various transcription factors and proteins involved in hormone biosynthesis, signalling and growth regulation<sup>8</sup>. As a result, MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes function in a range of developmental processes in gametophytes, seeds, embryos, roots, leaves, inflorescences and fruits<sup>9</sup>.

Although approximately 40 MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes are present in angiosperm species, single MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes have been found in species of the Zygnematophyceae, Charales and Coleochaetales algae<sup>10</sup>, of which Zygnematophyceae is inferred to be the most closely related lineage to extant land plants<sup>11</sup>. The moss *Physcomitrella patens* is a member of a basal lineage of land plants, and possesses six MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes<sup>12</sup>, which diverged in the moss lineage<sup>13</sup>. These results suggest that the common ancestor of land plants had a single MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box gene, and that the increase in the number of genes through gene and genome duplications and subsequent neo- and sub-functionalization has corresponded to the evolution of body organization in the land plants<sup>14,15</sup>.

Because MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes have diverged remarkably in land plants and are involved in various developmental

processes in angiosperms, their functions have been characterized in the green algae and bryophytes to infer their original role in the common land plant ancestor, as well as their subsequent functional evolution. Based on expression analyses, the MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADSbox genes are speculated to regulate gamete and gametangium formation in the green algae and bryophytes<sup>10, 16-18</sup>; however, lossand gain-of-function experiments have not been reported in the green algae, and three mutant lines lacking single *Physcomitrella* genes are indistinguishable from wild type, which is probably because of genetic redundancy<sup>17</sup>. In addition, downregulation of the *Physcomitrella* MADS-box gene *PPM1* using antisense RNA causes variable defects in the gametangia and sporophytes, although the *ppm1* deletion mutant resembles the wild type<sup>17</sup>. Therefore, the functions of MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes in green algae and *Physcomitrella* remain unclear.

In this study, to elucidate the precise functions of the MIKC<sup>C</sup>type MADS-box genes in *Physcomitrella* and infer their evolution in the land plants, we analysed the spatiotemporal protein localization patterns of all six *Physcomitrella* MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes and characterized the morphology and development of deletion and overexpression mutant lines. We found that the MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes are necessary for proper fertilization in two ways: the regulation of the length of the gametophore internodes affects external water uptake, which enables the sperm to swim to the egg, and the regulation of motile flagellum formation in sperm. The former function appears to have been maintained in the angiosperms, since internode length is determined by the regulation of cell division and growth and certain angiosperm MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes regulate cell division and growth<sup>19–21</sup>. The latter function was lost, consistent with the loss of sperm in the angiosperms.

The first three genes form one clade, and the last three genes constitute another[22.](#page-9-4) To analyse the localizations of the corresponding proteins, we inserted a DNA fragment containing *uidA* or a modified yellow fluorescent protein gene, *Citrine*, just before the stop codon of each MIKCC-type MADS-box gene by homologous recombination (Supplementary Figs. 2–13), and β-glucuronidase (GUS) or Citrine fusion lines were generated. We observed at least five tissues in each of the two independent GUS or Citrine fusion lines for each MIKCC-type MADS-box gene and the experiments were repeated at least three times. Morphology of the fusion lines was not distinguished from that of wild type. **MIKC<sup>c</sup>-type MADS-box genes negatively regulate cell divisions** and growth in the gametophore stem. To analyse the roles of the six MIKC<sup>c</sup>-type MADS-box genes, we produced single deletion mutant lines for each gene using homologous recombination (Supplementary Figs. 16–22). As previously reported<sup>17</sup>, single deletion mutants *ppm1*, *ppm2* and *ppmads1* were indistinguishable from the wild type (Supplementary Fig. 23). Similarly, we did not find any phenotypic differences between single deletion mutants of the other three genes and the wild type throughout the life cycle (Supplementary Fig. 23). These observations were confirmed in two independent transgenic lines for each gene. Since the protein localization of the six genes was found to partially overlap in time and space, we deleted all six MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes to form sextuple deletion mutant lines using homologous recombination (Supplementary Figs. 16–22).

We analysed two sextuple deletion mutant lines independently generated from different quintuple deletion mutant lines (ppm1 ppm2 ppmads1 ppmc5 ppmc6 ppmads-s no. 109 and ppm1 ppm2 ppmads1 ppmc5 ppmc6 ppmads-s no. 305), and confirmed that both lines showed the same phenotypes. Following observations and experiments were repeated at least three times. We observed the wild type and the sextuple deletion mutants throughout the life cycle (Fig. 2a-e). Whereas the chloronemata and caulonemata of the sextuple deletion mutant lines were indistinguishable from those of the wild type (Fig. 2a,b), the gametophores of the sextuple deletion mutant lines were taller and had longer internodes than the wild type (Fig. 2c,f). We found that the number and length of cells in each internode were significantly increased in the sextuple mutant lines compared with the wild type (Fig 2g,h), indicating that MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes negatively regulate cell division and growth in gametophore internodes. To investigate genes targeted by the MADS-box genes, we compared the gametophore transcriptomes of the wild type and sextuple deletion mutant line no. 109. The transcript levels of 322 and 152 genes were increased and decreased in the mutant, respectively, compared with those of the wild type (Supplementary Table 1). Since external water is transmitted along the stems and gametophore leaf edges (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Video 3), the changes to the cuticle in the centre of the gametophore leaf blades are unlikely to be a major cause of the defects in external water conduction.

Another possibility is that the defect in capillary water transmission between gametophore leaves is caused by the elongated gametophore internodes in the sextuple deletion mutant lines. Water accumulates at a gametophore leaf axil until the droplet is large enough to touch the axil immediately above, then it is sucked to the upper axil by capillary action, driven by the narrow space between the adaxial leaf surface and the stem surface facing it (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Video 3). To investigate the effect of internode length on external water conduction in a single genetic background, wild-type gametophores cultivated under regular conditions were moved to red-light or submerged conditions to elongate the stems<sup>34</sup> (Fig. 4g). These results indicate that the defect in external water conduction is caused by elongated internodes even in the absence of visible changes to the cuticle.

Sperm entry into archegonia is less efficient in the deletion mutant lines. When gametophores of the sextuple deletion mutant lines were grown under short-day conditions at 15°C for 3 weeks<sup>23</sup>, antheridia and archegonia were formed at the tops of gametophores, and sporophytes formed after fertilization. The outer morphology of the antheridia, archegonia, sporophytes and spores in the mutant lines were indistinguishable from those in the wild type (Fig. 2d,e and Supplementary Fig. 28); however, the percentage of gametophores forming sporophytes significantly decreased in the mutants (Fig. 5a). Since bryophytes need water for sperm to swim to the archegonia for fertilization, we hypothesized that the shortage of water around the gametangia caused by the defect in external water conduction impaired fertilization. The number of archegonia into which sperm entered was decreased in the sextuple deletion mutant lines compared with the wild type (Fig. 5b-d). Furthermore, when we supplied water to the gametangia by submerging them at the time of fertilization, the number of sporophytes increased (Fig. 5a). These findings indicate that the lack of water for fertilization is one of the reasons for the reduced number of sporophytes produced in the sextuple deletion mutants.

#### Discussion

Our analyses of protein localization, functional deletions and overexpression of six *Physcomitrella* MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes revealed their involvement in the regulation of gametophore internode length, influencing external water conductions to supply water for sperm cells to swim to the eggs (Fig. 4), and the regulation of motile sperm flagella formation (Fig. 6), both of which are necessary for fertilization. The external water conduction also functions to supply water to the surface of the gametophores, which otherwise easily dry out because the gametophore leaves comprise a single cell layer and the stems comprise only a few cell thicknesses. The *Physcomitrella* MIKC<sup>C</sup>-type MADS-box genes therefore effectively function in reproduction and vegetative growth, regulating internode length.

The difference in internode length is determined by cell divisions and cell growth, reflected in the numbers and length of the cells, respectively (Fig. [2\)](#page-2-0), suggesting that *Physcomitrella* MIKCCtype MADS-box proteins regulate target genes involved in these processes. Some members of the angiosperm MIKCC-type MADSbox genes function in organ growth in the late stages of flower development, regulating genes involved in growth regulation in concert with other transcription factor[s8](#page-8-7) . In addition, *XAL1* regulates cell proliferation in the *Arabidopsis* root meristem[19,](#page-9-2) and the overexpression of *Arabidopsis SOC1* and *AGL24* genes results in elongated carpel[s20,](#page-9-22)[21](#page-9-3). Furthermore, MIKCC-type MADS-box genes in the monilophyte *Ceratopteris richardii* are expressed in tissues with high levels of cell division, and their involvement in organ growth has been suggested[40](#page-9-23). Together, these findings suggest that MIKCC-type MADS-box genes in land plants, including bryophytes, monilophytes and angiosperms, have similar functions in growth regulation. Dozens of putative orthologues were commonly regulated by *Physcomitrella* and *Arabidopsis* MIKCC-type MADSbox genes (Supplementary Table 2), and these overlapping DEGs are good candidates for the future investigation of the evolution of genetic networks regulated by MIKCC-type MADS-box genes.

In terms of flagellum movement, we found that the expression levels of 23 genes putatively orthologous to *Chlamydomonas* flagellum-related genes[37](#page-9-19) were decreased in the antheridia of the sextuple and triple mutants, where sperm differentiate (Supplementary Table 5). Seven genes out of the 23 flagellum-related genes encode dynein heavy chains, central pair apparatus proteins, and components of dynein regulatory complex, which have roles in regulation of flagella motility and bending in both Chlamydomonas and amimal[s41,](#page-9-24) suggesting the involvement of these genes in flagellum motility in *Physcomitrella*. Despite this evidence, we could not detect visible alterations in flagellum structures of the mutant sperm, such as those that were previously seen in sperm defective in flagellum movement using TEM in Chlamydomonas[42](#page-9-25) and mouse[43](#page-9-26)[,44.](#page-9-27) Following the development of methods to collect enough sperm for analysis, higher resolution studies including cryo-electron tomography could be an appropriate approach[45](#page-9-28) to reveal the structural basis of the immobile flagella.

MIKCC-type MADS-box genes in the charophycean green algae *Chara globularis* are expressed during flagellate sperm differentiation, suggesting that the regulation of the flagellum-related genes by MIKCC-type MADS-box proteins dates back to the common ancestor of the charophycean green algae and land plants. MIKCCtype MADS-box genes in *C. richardii* are expressed in gametophytes with antheridia[40,](#page-9-23) and future studies will reveal whether the regulation of flagellum-related genes by MIKCC-type MADS-box genes is conserved in the monilophyte lineage. Orthologues of nine flagellum-related genes including genes encoding dynein motor proteins regulated by the *Physcomitrella* MIKCC-type MADS-box genes were not found in *Arabidopsis* genome (Supplementary Table 5), suggesting that they were lost during the evolutionary transition from the use of flagellate sperm to pollen tubes for fertilization, while orthologues of a further 14 genes probably function in other processes in *Arabidopsis*. In addition to the increased number of MIKCC-type MADS-box genes in the angiosperm lineage, it is possible that neofunctionalization of these genes originally involved in the regulation of flagellum formation contributed to the divergence of their functions in angiosperms.

Each MIKCC-type MADS-domain protein is localized in a different but partially overlapping pattern throughout the life cycle of *Physcomitrella* (Fig. [1](#page-1-0) and Supplementary Fig. 14). At least one MIKCC-type MADS-domain protein was localized in most cells that we examined; however, we observed phenotypic differences only between the sextuple deletion mutant lines and the wild type in the gametophore stems and the sperm, where *PPM1*, *PPM2* and *PPMC6* are co-localized. MIKCC-type MADS-

domain proteins function in protein complexes in *Arabidopsis*[9](#page-8-8) , and appropriate combinations are necessary for their functions. MIKCC-type MADS-domain proteins were also localized in locations where no phenotype was observed in their gene deletion mutants under controlled laboratory conditions. Future localization changes of the MIKCC-type MADS-domain protein and new combinations of localization patterns may lead to adaptive phenotypic changes.

Together, our results give insights into both the commonalities and diversity of the MADS-box genes throughout land plant evolution. MADS-box genes are involved in cell division and growth in both *Physcomitrella* and the flowering plants, suggesting that the MIKCC-type MADS-box genes of the common land plant ancestor also had such functions. By contrast, the regulation of flagellumrelated genes was lost in the flowering plant lineage in accordance with the loss of sperm. This study also shows that the most conspicuous functions of MIKCC-type MADS-box genes as homeotic selectors in floral organ development evolved in the vascular plant lineage after the divergence of the mosses. Further, this study reports theregulation of flagellum formation by transcription factors in land plants.

#### **Methods**

**Plant materials and culture conditions.** Te *P. patens*[46](#page-9-29) Bruch and Schimp Cove-NIBB[47](#page-9-30) line was cultured on BCDAT medium with 0.8% (w/v) agar at 25 °C under continuous light conditions[47](#page-9-30) to generate protonema materials. Protonemata were transplanted into sterile peat pots (Jify-7; Jify Products International AS), half immersed in water and cultured for a month at 25 °C under continuous light to generate gametophores. To obtain gametangia and sporophytes, the peat pots containing gametophores were moved to 15 °C under short-day (8h light and 16h dark) condition[s23.](#page-9-5) To obtain gametophores with elongated internodes, the pots containing young gametophores were cultured in a red box (red-light condition) or submerged in water (submergence condition) for 2–3weeks.

**Transformation.** The primer sequences, plasmids and constructs used for the production of the GUS and Citrine fusions, deletion lines and overexpression lines are shown in Supplementary Table 6 and Supplementary Figs. 2–13,16–22,25. Transformation was performed as described previousl[y47.](#page-9-30) Stable GUS and Citrine fusion and deletion lines were screened using a DNA gel blot analysis to confirm single integrations. Overexpression lines were screened using PCR and quantitative RT–PCR analyses. All transgenic lines were examined to be haploid with flow cytometry as previously described[48.](#page-9-31) The sextuple deletion mutant line no. 305 was made as follows: *PPM2* was deleted from the *ppm1* deletion mutant line no. 1, *PpMADS1* and *PPMC6* were simultaneously deleted from the *ppm1 ppm2* mutant line no. 1, and *PPMC5* and *PpMADS-S* were simultaneously deleted from *ppm1 ppm2 ppmads1 ppmc6* no. 90. The sextuple deletion mutant line no. 109 was made as follows: *PPMC5* was deleted from *ppm1 ppm2 ppmads1 ppmc6* no. 90, then *PpMADS-S* was deleted from *ppm1 ppm2 ppmads1 ppmc5 ppmc6* no. 11. The triple deletion mutant lines no. 14 and no. 80 were made by deleting *PPMC6* from *ppm1 ppm2* mutant line no. 1.

**DNA gel blot analysis.** Genomic DNA was digested with restriction enzymes, run on 0.7% (w/v) SeaKem GTG agarose (Lonza), and transferred to a Hybond N+nylon membrane (GE Healthcare). Probe labelling, hybridization and detection were performed using the AlkPhos direct labelling and detection system with CDP-Star (GE Healthcare).

**Microscopy analysis.** Images and videos were taken using fluorescence microscopes SZX16 and BX51 (Olympus) equipped with colour cameras DP71 (Olympus) and DS-Fi1c (Nikon), respectively. Citrine fluorescent images were taken using a confocal laser microscope (SP8; Leica Microsystems), with a detecting fluorescence emission of 515–550nm under excitation at 514nm. For archegonia with sperm, mature archegonia (stage [949](#page-9-32)) were fixed with 8% glutaraldehyde, stained using DAPI (1μgml<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ), and observed using a confocal laser microscope (A1R; Nikon) with a detecting fluorescence emission of 423–478nm under excitation at 405nm. For sperm movement, images and videos were taken using a dark-field microscope (BX60; Olympus) equipped with a CCD camera (ORCA-R2; Hamamatsu Photonics), and the videos for 5 s were used for quantification of movement. Sperm were extracted by pressing the antheridia between a glass slide and a cover slip, and only isolated and mature sperm with spiral shape were observed.

**Protein expression using the oestrogen inducing system.** Gametophores were cultured on sterile peat pots for 2weeks before being treated with 2.0μM

β-oestradiol (Wako) dissolved in water. The gametophores were cultured for 2weeks under continuous light conditions at 25 °C.

**Quantitative RT–PCR analysis.** RNA was extracted and purified using an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) and DNase I (Qiagen). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using ReverTraAce qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo), and qRT–PCR was performed using an ABI 7500 RealTimePCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a QuantiTect SYBR Green PCR Kit (Qiagen). Results were analysed using 7500 software version 2.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The quantification of each sample was performed in triplicate.

**Transcriptome analyses.** For the gametophores, RNA was extracted using PureLink Plant RNA Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and purified with a RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) and DNase I (Qiagen). RNA-seq libraries were prepared using a TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina). For the antheridia transcriptome, frozen antheridia from three shoot apices were ground in 50μl of RLT buffer (Qiagen) using 5mm and 0.5mm zirconia beads, then 10μl of supernatant was used for a single-cell RNA-seq method (Quartz-Se[q50\)](#page-9-33). Sequencing was conducted using a HiSeq 2500 (Illumina) and 50bp reads were obtained. For the reads of both the gametophore and antheridia transcriptomes, adapter and low-quality sequences were removed from the reads using cutadapt (version 1.9.1). The reads were mapped to transcript sequences of *P. patens* version 3.0 (Ppatens\_251\_v3.0.transcript.fa) obtained via Phytozome (DOE-JGI; [https://](https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov/) [phytozome.jgi.doe.gov/\)](https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov/) and calculated using RSEM (version 1.2.25) with Bowtie. DEGs between the wild type and the mutant lines with *q*<0.05 were extracted with an R package, TCC[51](#page-9-34), and then DEGs with a fold change >1.5 were selected. RNA-seq analyses for the gametophores were performed in biological triplicate and Quartz-Seq analyses for the antheridia were in biological quadruplicate. Published microarray data for each *Arabidopsis* MIKCC-type MADS-box gene (*AP1*[24,](#page-9-6) *AP3*[25,](#page-9-7) *PI*[25](#page-9-7), *AG*[26,](#page-9-8) *SOC1*[27,](#page-9-9)[28](#page-9-10) and *SVP*[29](#page-9-11)) were compared with the *Physcomitrella* DEGs present between the wild type and the deletion mutant lines. Flagellum-related genes were identified from the antheridia DEGs by comparison with the 360 and 292 high and moderate confidence genes, respectively, identified in the flagella of the green alga *C. reinhardtii*[37.](#page-9-19)

**Observation by TEM.** To visualize the cuticle, gametophore leaves and stems were fixed overnight at 4 °C in a 0.05M PIPES buffer (pH 7.0) containing 1.5% glutaraldehyde and 1.5% formaldehyde. Samples were washed three times with 0.05M PIPES buffer for 20 min each, and osmium fixation was performed by placing the samples in 2% OsO4 in 0.05M PIPES buffer (pH 7.0) for 2 h in the dark at 4 °C, followed by washing them three times with distilled water for 30min each. Dehydration was performed using a graded ethanol series (30, 50, 70, 90 and 100%) for 30 min at each stage, with two final rinses of 100% ethanol for 15 min each[52](#page-9-35). The fixed tissues were embedded in LR White resin (London Resin Company Ltd) and polymerized at 60 °C for 1 day. Sectioning was performed using an Ultracut UCT Ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems) at 70nm. The sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and observed using a JEM1010 (JEOL) TEM at 80 kV. For TEM observations of flagella, we used the previously described method[53](#page-9-36) with modifications. Flagella were fixed in 10% tannic acid and 2% glutaraldehyde for 1 h on ice. Samples were washed three times with 0.05M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) for 15 min each, and were fixed in 2% OsO4 in 0.05M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) for 1 h on ice. The samples were then washed three times with distilled water for 15min each. Dehydration was performed using a graded ethanol series (30, 50, 70, 90 and 100%) for 30 min at each stage, with two final rinses of 100% ethanol for 15 min each. After this step, tissues were embedded in Epon resin (TAAB Laboratories Equipment) and polymerized at 60 °C for 1 day. Sectioning was performed using an Ultracut UCT Ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems) at 50 nm. The sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and observed using a JEM1010 (JEOL Ltd.) TEM at 80 kV.

**Extracellular lipid identification and quantification.** The extracellular lipids of *P. patens* were identified and quantified using previously described methods with a minor modification[54](#page-9-37)[,55](#page-9-38). The total extracellular lipids were extracted by immersing the gametophores into chloroform for 30 s at room temperature, and *n*-tetracosane (C24 alkane) and behenyl oleate (C40 wax ester) were added as internal standards. The solution was dried under a stream of nitrogen gas, and the residues were incubated with 50μl of BSTFA-TMCS and 50μl of pyridine for 1h at 80 °C. The BSTFA and pyridine were then removed under a stream of nitrogen gas. The samples were re-dissolved in chloroform and analysed using a gas chromatography-mass spectrometer (GC-MS; GCMSTQ-8030; Shimadzu) equipped with a Rxi-5HT column (length, 15m; internal diameter 0.25mm; film thickness 0.1μm; Restek) for the identification of the wax components. The samples were also analysed using gas chromatography-flame ionization detector (GC-FID; GC2014; Shimadzu) equipped with a Rxi-5HT column (length, 15m; internal diameter 0.25mm; film thickness 0.1μm; Restek) for the quantification of the wax components. For the GC-MS and GC-FID, the carrier gas was helium, the injection port temperature was 320 °C, and the column temperature was programmed as follows: 50 °C for 5min, increasing to 200 °C at a rate of 40 °C per min and held for 2min, then raising to 380 °C at a rate of 3 °C per min and held for 10min. The extracellular lipids were quantified by comparing the FID peak areas against the internal standards.

**Reactivation experiment for sperm.** Sperm were extracted from the antheridia by pressing them between a glass slide and a cover slip with a 0.3-mm spacer. After waiting for the sperm bodies to adhere to the glass slide, they were treated with 0.1% NP-40 substitute (Wako) in 30mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 5mM MgSO4, 1mM DTT, 1mM EGTA and 50 mM potassium acetate to induce de-membranation. A reactivation solution (30mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 5mM MgSO4, 1mM DTT, 1mM EGTA, 50 mM K-acetate, 1% polyethylene glycol (6,000mol wt) and 1mM ATP) was added to the de-membraned flagella using a modified metho[d56](#page-9-39), and the sperm with reactivated flagella were counted.
