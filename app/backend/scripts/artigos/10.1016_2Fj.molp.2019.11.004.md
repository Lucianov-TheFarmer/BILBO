# **ABSTRACT**

Precursor mRNA (pre-mRNA) splicing is essential for gene expression in most eukaryotic organisms. Previous studies from mammals, *Drosophila* and yeast show that the majority of splicing events occurs co-transcriptionally. In plants, however, the nature of co-transcriptional splicing (CTS) and its regulation are still largely unknown. Here, we used chromatin-bound RNA sequencing (CB-RNA-seq), to study CTS in *Arabidopsis thaliana*. CTS was widespread in *Arabidopsis* seedlings, with a large proportion of alternative splicing events determined co-transcriptionally. CTS efficiency correlated with gene expression level, the chromatin landscape and, most surprisingly, the number of introns and exons of individual genes, but was independent of gene length. In combination with eCLIP-seq analysis, our results showed that the hnRNP-like proteins RZ-1B and RZ-1C promote efficient CTS globally through direct binding, frequently to exonic sequences. Notably, this general effect of RZ-1B/1C on splicing promotion was mainly at the chromatin level, not at the mRNA level. RZ-1C promotes CTS of multiple exons genes in association with its binding to the regions both proximal and distal to the regulated introns. We propose that RZ-1C promotes efficient CTS of genes with multiple exons involving cooperative interactions with many exons, introns and splicing factors. Our work thus reveals important features of CTS in plants and provides methodologies for the investigation of CTS and RNA-binding proteins in plants.

## **Introduction:**

Pre-mRNA splicing is carried out by the spliceosome, a megadalton complex that triggers the precise excision of introns from the pre-mRNA (Jangi and Sharp, 2014; Reddy et al., 2013). Early studies of individual genes implied that splicing could already occur during transcription, a process termed CTS (Bauren and Wieslander, 1994; Beyer and Osheim, 1988; Osheim et al., 1985; Zhang et al., 1994). The potential for CTS is not surprising in organisms with relatively long genes (e.g. Human). Since the time needed for splicing (a few seconds to 3 min) is much shorter than the time needed for transcribing a gene (often more than 10mins for human genes)(Alexander et al., 2010; Bentley, 2014; Beyer and Osheim, 1988; Huranova et al., 2010; Singh and Padgett, 2009). However, recent genome-wide profiling of nascent RNA has revealed that CTS is the predominant mode of splicing in different species including budding yeast (Ameur et al., 2011; Bhatt et al., 2012; Khodor et al., 2011; Khodor et al., 2012; Tilgner et al., 2012; Oesterreich et al., 2016), an organism with average gene length of 1-2kb, indicating rapid CTS. Indeed, in budding yeast, the catalysis of splicing occurs on average 45bp after the Pol II finish transcribing the 3' intron acceptor site, much faster than previously anticipated (Oesterreich et al., 2016). Interestingly, in contrast, two recent reports showed the catalysis of splicing in human cell often occurs when Pol II travels to more than a kilobases downstream of intron acceptor site (Drexler et al., 2019; Nojima et

al., 2018), indicating significantly differed regulations between species.

Despite their tight coupling in vivo, transcription and splicing can both happen independent of each other in vitro. How and why these two processes are so tightly coupled in-vivo has been the focus of much effort in yeast and mammalian cells. The extent of CTS correlates with different gene features and varies between organisms, tissues and individual genes, indicating great regulatory potentials (Brugiolo et al., 2013; Khodor et al., 2012). In particular, the transcription elongation rate, which is regulated by trans factors and chromatin structure, can influence splicing site choice (Bentley, 2014; de la Mata et al., 2003; Kornblihtt, 2007; Naftelberg et al., 2015; Roberts et al., 1998). Faster elongation rate could reduce the chance of weak, promoter proximal splicing sites being utilized, influencing the production of certain mRNA isoforms (Bentley, 2014; de la Mata et al., 2003; Kornblihtt, 2007; Naftelberg et al., 2015; Roberts et al., 1998). This is known as "kinetic coupling", a mechanism has been demonstrated in several different organisms (Bentley, 2014; de la Mata et al., 2003; Kornblihtt, 2007; Naftelberg et al., 2015; Roberts et al., 1998), and is involved in flowering time control (Marquardt et al., 2014) and light response (Godoy Herz et al., 2019) in *Arabidopsis*. In addition, a study in budding yeast showed that slower or faster elongation could also affect the fidelity of splicing in additional to its efficiency, indicating that an optimal elongation rate has likely been selected for in-vivo (Aslanzadeh et al., 2018). Notably, recent works in mammalian cells showed the spliceosome is complexed with Pol II CTD phosphorylated on the serine 5 (S5P) but not Pol II CTD phosphorylated on the serine 2 (S2P), suggesting a physical link between splicing and transcription through phosphorylation of Pol II CTD (Nojima et al., 2015, Nojima et al., 2018). In addition, splicing and transcription can also be coupled by splicing factors that in turn regulate transcription (Das et al., 2007; de la Mata and Kornblihtt, 2006; Ji et al., 2013; Monsalve et al., 2000). The coupling between splicing and transcription is likely also important for RNA quality control. For example, the deposition of exon-junction complex, a complex important for none sense

mediated decay of RNA, is largely splicing dependent and co-transcriptional (Alexandrov et al., 2012; Barbosa et al., 2012; Singh et al., 2012; Steckelberg et al., 2012).

Despite its importance, relatively little is known about the coupling between transcription and splicing in plants. In *Arabidopsis*, CTS has been observed at individual loci such as *FLC* and *DOG1* (Dolata et al., 2015; Rosa et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2016a). For example, single molecule RNA FISH (smFISH) results show only two dots for unspliced *FLC* RNA within the nucleus, and these overlap with the *FLC* DNA FISH signal, indicating efficient co-transcriptional splicing (Rosa et al., 2016). In addition, pNET-seq analysis in *Arabidopsis* also found enrichment of reads with 3'end mapped at exon 3' end (most likely splicing intermediate) specifically for the Pol II CTD S5P (Zhu et al., 2018), consistent with the idea of CTS. Notably, typical plant (e.g. *Arabidopsis thaliana*) genes are structurally similar to yeast genes with an average length of 2.4 kb (Reddy, 2007), much shorter than those of mammals, which should theoretically reduce the chance for CTS. Moreover, the majority of alternative splicing events in plants are intron retention, in sharp contrast to the situation in mammals (Reddy, 2007). To date, the extent of CTS as well as its features and regulations at whole genome-level is unknown in plants.

RNA-binding proteins represent major regulators in determine the fate of RNAs including splicing, either co- or post-transcriptionally. *Arabidopsis* genome encode at least 500 putative RNA-binding proteins, the majority of which are functionally unknown (Marondedze et al., 2016; Reichel et al., 2016). Indeed, methodology for study RNA-binding proteins such as CLIP-seq was only available recently and remains technically challenging for plant samples (Meyer et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2015). A relatively well studied class of RNA-binding

proteins in plant are SR proteins, a group of proteins that were extensively linked with alternative splicing (Reddy and Shad Ali, 2011), presumably through their interactions with sequence motifs that located in exons or introns (Jeong, 2017). Mammalian SR proteins also regulate Pol II 5' end pausing and elongation (Ji et al., 2013; Lin et al., 2008; Xiao et al., 2019), suggesting their important roles at co-transcriptional level. We reported previously that a small group of proteins are in close association with SR proteins in *Arabidopsis*, namely RZ-1B and RZ-1C (Wu et al., 2016b). Both proteins feature a N-terminal RRM domain, a C-terminal low complexity region and a zinc-finger motif in the middle (Lorkovic and Barta, 2002). RZ-1s represent hnRNPs (Hanano et al., 1996; Lorkovic and Barta, 2002) and have potential to interact with multiple SR proteins (Wu et al., 2016b). RZ-1b and RZ-1c have redundant and essential function in plant development and regulate alternative splicing of more than 100 genes (Wu et al., 2016b). Notably, RZ-1C tightly associates with chromatin and could affect transcription in addition to splicing at individual genes such as *FLC*, suggesting a possible role in co-transcriptional regulation (Wu et al., 2016b).

Here we studied the features and regulation of CTS in plants through adapting chromatin-bound RNA-seq in *Arabidopsis*. CTS is widespread in *Arabidopsis* seedlings. The efficiency of CTS correlate with gene expression level, the distance from intron 3'end to gene 3'end and also histone modifications, consistent with previous reports in yeast and animals, and support the idea of kinetic coupling. As a novel feature of CTS in plant, we found the average CTS efficiency of genes correlated with their intron and or exon numbers while largely independent of gene length. We further explored roles of RZ-1B and RZ-1C in CTS regulation. RZ-1B and RZ-1C function to enhance CTS globally. The CTS promotion by RZ-1C is in association with its direct binding to RNA targets, as revealed through eCLIP-seq, an efficient and powerful technique we adapted for studying the direct RNA-binding of proteins in plants. Our work uncovers general features of CTS in plants and highlights the role exon/intron numbers as well as RZ-1B/1C in CTS regulation.

**Results** 

# **Widespread CTS as revealed by CB-RNA-seq in** *Arabidopsis* **seedlings.**

To test the universality of CTS in plants, we adapted the CB-RNA-seq method for use in *Arabidopsis*. CB-RNA was extracted from isolated nuclei followed by a stringent urea wash to release non-chromatin associated protein and RNAs, a method that has been widely used in both mammalian and yeast systems (Bhatt et al., 2012; Khodor et al., 2011; Oesterreich et al., 2016; Wuarin and Schibler, 1994) (Fig.1A; Supplementary Fig. 1A and B). We used the ratio of unspliced RNA to spliced RNA at several different genes as an estimate of enrichment of nascent RNA in our assay. This include *FLC*, a gene that showed tight co-transcriptional splicing (Rosa et al., 2016). Given efficient removal of intron at *FLC* chromatin, one would expect the highest enrichment of unspliced RNA at chromatin compared with nucleus and cytosol. Indeed, this is the case at *FLC* and other two genes we tested, indicating the successful enrichment of chromatin-bound RNA (Supplementary Fig. 1C to E). The CB-RNA fraction was treated to remove the ribosomal RNA and any contaminating poly (A) RNA and then used for Illumina RNA-seq with strand specificity.

For a given locus, the CB-RNA reflects the sum of two proportions of RNA molecules (Fig. 1B) (Ietswaart et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016a): RNAs being transcribing at the locus (RNAe, elongating RNA) and RNAs that have been fully transcribed but not yet released from the chromatin (RNAf, full-length RNA). The latter fraction exists as RNA polymerase II (Pol II)

generally pauses at the 3ʹ end of the gene before completion of termination(Zhu et al., 2018). Assuming that RNAe dominates the CB-RNA fraction, we expect a 5ʹ to 3ʹ decrease in the amount of CB-RNA (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, assuming that introns are removed from the pre-mRNA co-transcriptionally, the reads coverage at the intronic region should be significantly lower than at the adjacent exonic regions, resulting in a sawtooth-like pattern (Fig. 1C).

As expected, we observed a declining pattern from the 5ʹ to the 3ʹ end at many individual loci in our CB-RNA-seq data, but not in the mRNA-seq data (Fig.1D and E; Supplementary Fig. 1F), indicating successful isolation of CB-RNA. We note that this declining pattern was observed more often in long genes compared with short ones, possibly due to the longer time needed by Pol II for elongation at these genes, resulting in the RNAe dominating the locus over the RNAf. Moreover, the sawtooth pattern of CB-RNA was observed at individual genes as well as globally, indicating general CTS (Fig.1D to F; Supplementary Fig. 1F). Given good read coverage of introns as well as exons, and also good correlation between biological repeats (Supplementary Fig. 1G, Supplementary Table 1), we then quantified the CTS efficiency throughout the *Arabidopsis* genome using widely accepted standards (Herzel and Neugebauer, 2015; Khodor et al., 2011; Khodor et al., 2012). At each intron–exon boundary, we calculated the ratio of intronic reads over the adjacent exonic reads to yield a 5ʹ splicing site (5ʹSS) ratio and a 3ʹ splicing site (3ʹSS) ratio (Fig. 1G). The median value for both 5ʹSS (88,364 introns) and 3ʹSS (89,231 introns) ratios was around 0.2 (Fig. 1H), similar to that found in *Drosophila* (Khodor et al., 2011), but lower than that in mouse (Khodor et al., 2012). Therefore, we conclude that *Arabidopsis* pre-mRNAs are generally co-transcriptionally spliced, as in yeast and animals.

## **CTS efficiency correlated with gene expression level.**

Accumulating evidence indicates that transcription and splicing are coupled, i.e., Pol II transcription can help determine splice-site choice and efficient assembly of an active spliceosome can change the extent of CTS (Godoy Herz et al., 2019; Naftelberg et al., 2015). We therefore determined whether CTS efficiency was related to the level of gene expression. For high-confidence analysis, we excluded data with a 5ʹSS or 3ʹSS ratio equal to or more than one. In these cases, reads were often indistinguishable between an intron or alternative exon in the same region. The rest of the data, i.e., 5ʹSS and 3ʹSS ratios distributed between 0 and 1, were plotted against the corresponding gene expression level (as determined by the mRNA-seq data). Both the 5ʹSS and 3ʹSS ratios negatively correlated with the level of gene expression (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. 2), suggesting that the pre-mRNAs of highly expressed genes were processed at a higher rate than those of weakly expressed genes.

## **CTS efficiency correlated with exon and or intron numbers.**

In mouse and *Drosophila*, gene structure influences splicing efficiency (Khodor et al., 2011; Khodor et al., 2012). Therefore, we plotted the 5ʹSS and 3ʹSS ratios together with different gene features. The first and last introns were often less efficiently removed at the co-transcriptional level compared with the internal introns (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Furthermore, the longer the distance between the intron 3ʹ end and the gene 3ʹ end, the more efficient the CTS (Supplementary Fig. 3B). This suggests sufficient co-transcriptional time is beneficial to efficient CTS, consistent with previously reported in other organisms (Khodor et al., 2011; Khodor et al., 2012). We observed that both gene length and number of introns/exons negatively correlated with the average 5ʹSS and 3ʹSS ratios (Fig. 2B and C; Supplementary Fig. 4A). Since intron number and gene length correlate with each other (Fig. 2D), we wondered which one was more relevant to the regulation of CTS efficiency.

Surprisingly, the negative correlation between intron number and 5ʹSS and 3ʹSS ratio remained, even for gene groups with fixed lengths (Fig. 2E; Supplementary Fig. 4B). By contrast, no obvious tendency was found between gene length and 5ʹSS or 3ʹSS ratio among genes with a fixed number of introns (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Fig. 4C and D ). We also used a general linear model to statistically assess the effect of intron number or gene length on CTS efficiency (see Methods). The ANOVA test indicated that intron number was a significant factor (*p* < 2.2e-16), rather than gene length (*p* = 0.88), or the interaction between intron number and gene length (*p* = 0.84). Furthermore, intron number did not correlate with gene expression level (Supplementary Fig. 4E), excluding the involvement of intron number in the regulation of CTS via effects on gene expression. Unlike the case in animals, there was no obvious tendency between exon or intron length with the splicing efficiency of its own or its adjacent exon (Fig. 2G and H). Overall, we conclude that the number of introns and or exons was an important contributor to efficient CTS independent of the gene length.

## **CTS efficiency negatively correlated with certain histone marks.**

Transcription and CTS both occur within the context of chromatin in vivo and therefore are interlinked (Aslanzadeh et al., 2018; de Almeida and Carmo-Fonseca, 2014; Ullah et al., 2018). We determined whether CTS efficiency is related to the level of certain histone marks within the chromatin, i.e. H3K4me3, H3K9ac and H3K27me3, for which data from the same seedling stage are available in public databases (see Methods). There was clear correlated trend between the 5ʹSS or 3ʹSS ratio and the levels of the H3K4me3- or H3K9ac-marked histones, respectively (Fig. 3). But none correlated trend between CTS and the level of H3K27me3-marked histones was found. As previously reported and in consistent with "kinetic coupling" model, higher H3K4me3 or H3K9ac levels may indicate faster elongation (Church and Fleming, 2018 ; Nagai et al., 2017), which could in turn reduce the time Pol II spends on the chromatin, therefore reducing the efficiency of CTS.

## **Decision of alternative splicing are often made co-transcriptionally.**

Alternative splicing is important regulatory step in gene expression in higher eukaryotes (Jangi and Sharp, 2014; Reddy et al., 2013; Staiger and Brown, 2013). Examples in mammalian cells showed that splicing of an alternative exon can happen either co or post-transcriptionally (Pandya-Jones and Black, 2009; Saldi et al., 2016; Vargas et al., 2011). Furthermore, it was proposed that splicing commitment but not necessarily catalysis occurs at the chromatin (de la Mata et al., 2010). We therefore wondered to what extent alternative splicing is determined co-transcriptionally in plant. We focused on exon-skipping (ES), alternative 5ʹ splice site (A5SS) and alternative 3ʹ splice site (A3SS) events (Fig. 4A) which were annotated in TAIR10 genome and can be assessed in our Col-0 mRNA-seq data (see method). In these events, featured reads spanning an exon-exon junction were used to determine the relative ratio of different isoforms from the same locus, reflected as a PSI value (Percentage Spliced In value; see Methods). Unlike intron retention (IR), the abundance of isoforms in these events can be calculated without the confounding effects of unprocessed introns in the CB-RNA. Comparing the mRNA-seq and CB-RNA-seq data, we found that the PSI values from the two samples were generally correlated (Fig. 4B), indicating the decision of alternative splicing are often made at chromatin. It worth to note that decision made post-transcriptionally does exist at some individual loci (Fig. 4C). In addition, compared to constitutive removed introns at the same locus, introns involved in alternatively splicing were generally less efficiently removed co-transcriptionally, although they were often not the least efficient (Fig. 4D). Therefore, our data indicated that for alternative splicing events including ES, A5SS and A3SS in *Arabidopsis* seedlings, the decision of alternative splicing is often made at chromatin.

## **RZ-1B and RZ-1C enhance the efficiency of CTS globally.**

We next tested how CTS is regulated by trans-factors. Among many candidates, we choose to test RZ-1B and RZ-1C as a starting point, given they associate with chromatin and interact with a group of SR proteins (see introduction, Wu et al., 2016b), the later are important splicing regulators across different species. The effect of RZ-1B and RZ-1C on CTS was tested through CB-RNA-seq. Surprisingly, the distribution of the 5ʹSS and 3ʹSS ratios of the mutant shifted towards a higher value and the means of the ratios increased, suggesting that the RZ-1 proteins positively regulate the CTS efficiency of a large number of genes (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. 5A and B). Indeed, we found that 6,119 exon-intron-exon units from 3,046 genes were significantly differentially spliced at co-transcriptional level in the *rz-1b rz-1c* mutant compared with Col-0 (false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.001; see Methods, Supplementary Table 2; Fig. 5B). Among them, 98% of units (5971 of 6119) were less efficiently spliced in *rz-1b rz-1c*. This general effects of RZ-1 on CTS efficiency contrast to its relatively subtle effects on splicing based on mRNA-seq. The 5'SS or 3'SS ratio, of either all introns or those that were differentially spliced at chromatin, remains unchanged between Col-0 and *rz-1b rz-1c*, based on mRNA-seq data (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Fig. 5C). Indeed, by using same criteria as in CB-RNA, we found only 502 exon-intron-exon units that were less efficiently spliced in *rz-1b rz-1c* compared with Col-0 based on mRNA-seq data (Supplementary Fig. 5D, yellow cycle). These results suggest although co-transcriptional splicing rate is slower in *rz-1b rz-1c*, the introns were generally been removed effectively at mature mRNA. Taken together, the above results indicate RZ-1B and RZ-1C are generally required for the efficient CTS.

# **RZ-1C promotes CTS in association with its direct RNA-binding**

We then searched for RNA targets bound by the RZ-1 proteins in vivo to further assess how these proteins regulate CTS. Crosslinking and immunoprecipitation (CLIP) analysis has been shown to be the most reliable method in faithfully detecting RNA-protein interactions, however it is extremely challenging in plants (Meyer et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2015). We combined the individual-nucleotide resolution CLIP (iCLIP) protocol (Konig et al., 2010) with the recently developed enhanced CLIP (eCLIP) protocol (Van Nostrand et al., 2016) and adapted it for our research (see Methods, Fig. 5D). We kept the step of radio isotope labeling of RNA as in the original iCLIP protocol to ensures the faithful detection of RNA that bound by the RZ-1C (Fig. 5E). We then followed the eCLIP protocol for generation of sequencing library due to its higher efficiency and reliability (Van Nostrand et al., 2016). RZ-1C fused with GFP and expressed in its genomic context (*RZ-1C:GFP-RZ-1C*) was able to complement the *rz-1b rz-1c* mutants (Wu et al., 2016b). This transgenic line and a *35S:GFP* construct (as negative control) were used in our eCLIP analysis (Fig. 5E). Peak calling and subtraction of any site that appeared in the *35S:GFP* data was performed over biological replicates (Supplementary Fig. 6A, Supplementary Table 1), resulting in the identification of 2,295,498 RZ-1C cross-linked sites (XLS) in the *Arabidopsis* genome. 1,980,453 (86.3%), 90,257 (3.9 %), 130,762 (5.7 %) and 94,026 (4.1 %) of these sites were located in exons, introns, 5ʹUTRs and 3ʹUTRs, respectively (Fig. 5F). These XLS were distributed over 10,937 genes (Supplementary Table 3), suggesting that RZ-1C binds to a broad range of RNAs in vivo. Furthermore, among the above identified 3,046 genes that differentially spliced on chromatin (Fig. 5B) in the *rz-1b rz-1c* mutant, 84 % had RZ-1C eCLIP XLS (Fig. 5G). This is a significantly higher number than the percentage of total genes bound by RZ-1C (38 %, hypergeometric test, *p* < 2.2e-16) or the percentage of expressed genes (fpkm>1) at the same

stage (55%, hypergeometric test, *p* < 2.2e-16). This suggests a direct link between RZ-1C and the promotion of CTS at those genes. The binding of the RZ-1C protein with RNA as well as its effect on CTS were further validated on selected genes through RNA immunoprecipitation-qPCR (RIP-qPCR) and CB-RNA or mRNA extraction followed by qPCR analysis of splicing efficiency (Fig. 5H and I; Supplementary Fig. 6B and C). Notably, in consistent with the sequencing results, the difference of splicing efficiency between WT and *rz-1b rz-1c* was not observed in mRNA samples in qPCR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7). Overall, our eCLIP data indicated that the RZ-1C protein promote efficient CTS of several thousand genes involving its direct binding with their RNAs.

# **RZ-1C binds to both introns and exons with different strength.**

We then monitored the distribution of RZ-1C XLS across loci to further dissect the mechanism of RZ-1C-regulated CTS. RZ-1C XLS was located well within the predicted transcription start sites (TSS) and polyadenylation sites (PAS) (Fig. 6A). As shown in Fig. 5F, the apparent enrichment of RZ-1C XLS at exons over introns lead us to ask if this could be due to the more presence of exons over introns at steady state within cell. Since RZ-1C locates exclusively within nucleus and associates with chromatin (Wu et al., 2016b), the CB-RNA-seq data could serve as a good input control for our eCLIP analysis. In order to judge the binding strength of RZ-1C with different RNAs, we normalized the eCLIP data to the sequencing reads density calculated from CB-RNA-seq data. In general, RZ-1C still favors exons over introns after this normalization (Fig. 6B, Green line). Among 10937 genes bound by RZ-1C, RZ-1C XLS was detected at solely exons, at both exons and introns, and at solely introns at 7496, 3370 and 71 genes respectively (Fig. 6C). Importantly, RZ-1C bound and unbound introns are at similar abundance in CB-RNA-seq data, indicating the absence of RZ-1C XLS from 7496 genes is not due to different abundance of their introns (Fig. 6D).

Interestingly, in cases where RZ-1C binds to both intron and adjacent exon, the intronic binding was substantially stronger than the exonic binding when taking into account their steady state levels at chromatin (Fig. 6B, Black line). Therefore, RZ-1C bind only to exons at the majority of its targets, while in cases where it does binds to intron, the binding strength is often stronger, compared with the adjacent exons.

# **RZ-1C binds to unspliced exons stronger than spliced exons.**

Given RZ-1C binds only to exons at majority of its targets, we further wondered if its binding had any preference towards spliced or unspliced exons. We focused on exon located XLSs from which their corresponding sequencing reads are either spanning an exon-intron junction or an exon-exon junction. In these two cases, the splicing state of the corresponding RNA that bound by RZ-1C can be determined faithfully. After normalize to the reads density calculated from CB-RNA-seq data at each corresponding locations, exon-intron spanning RNAs bound by RZ-1C are at higher levels than exon-exon spanning RNAs (Fig. 6E). This result was further supported by RIP-qPCR at individual loci. We designed primers that are specific for different forms of RNA and quantified the strength of binding as a function of input (chromatin RNA before the IP) level, and found that the RZ-1C binding (as a percentage of input value) was significantly higher at the exon-intron junction than at either the exon only or the exon–exon junction (Supplementary Fig. 8), suggesting stronger binding with unspliced exons.

# **U-rich and GA-rich motifs were enriched in sequences surrounding RZ-1C XLS.**

Next, we searched for specific motifs to which RZ-1C could preferentially bind. Multiple EM for Motif Elicitation (MEME, see method) analysis revealed several degenerative motifs (Fig. 6F). Enrichment of U-rich motifs was most likely due to the preference of UV

crosslinking at such sequences. Importantly, GA-rich motifs were also enriched, similar to those previously identified in RZ-1C systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX) experiments (Wu et al., 2016b), indicating that GA-rich sequences are likely to be bound by RZ-1C with high affinity. Notably, GA-rich motifs are also bound by other splicing regulators such as SR proteins (Clery et al., 2011; Xing et al., 2015) and are important for splicing regulation across different species. Therefore, in case of RZ-1C, plants take advantage of similar *cis*-elements as in mammals to regulate co-transcriptional splicing.

## **RZ-1C promotes CTS through both local and global mode.**

The above data suggest that RZ-1C plays a critical role in enhancing the efficiency of CTS, often through binding with exonic sequences, but does not fully elucidate its mode of action. We propose a model that summarizes two alternative, but not mutually exclusive, modes of action for RZ-1C in regulating CTS. In the first "local" mode, RZ-1C would act as a canonical splicing factor, binding *cis*-elements at the exon or intron to define and facilitate the removal of the adjacent intron (Fig. 6J, blue arrow). In the second "global" mode, RZ-1C would facilitate removal of an intron through binding to exons or introns distal to it (Fig.6J, black arrow). We have evidence for both modes based on our CB-RNA-seq and eCLIP-seq data. There were a total of 2572 genes that showed both differential CTS efficiency and RZ-1C binding (Fig. 5G). Within the 2572 genes, 5408 splicing-affected exon-intron-exon units were identified. Compared with the RZ-1C binding in splicing-affected units to the random units taken from the same gene, we found a higher percentage of RZ-1C binding to splicing-affected units, supporting the "local" mode of action (Fig. 6G). Consistent with our previous results, RZ-1C bound higher percentage of exons than introns within the splicing affected units (Fig. 6G). In the meantime, for intronic binding, significant difference between splicing affected units and the random units was also observed (Fig. 6G). Suggest the intronic

binding is also important for the CTS promotion of RZ-1C. Further, 27 % of the 5408 units did not have any RZ-1C binding within the unit itself but still showed defective splicing (Fig. 6H, "Total"). In these cases, RZ-1C binding distal to the affected intron would be important for its efficient removal at co-transcriptional level, supporting the "global" mode. Thus, we propose a working model of RZ-1B and RZ-1C in the regulation of CTS, in which RZ-1C binds to exons, and in less cases, introns, and can promote the co-transcriptional removal of introns that both proximal and distal to it (Fig. 6J).

## **Discussion**

Numerous work including structural studies showed that splicing is a generally conserved process from yeast to human, which can happen both during or post-transcription. With our data added, current evidence indicate that co-transcriptional splicing is a predominant mode in all eukaryotes tested so far. In addition, several lines of our data is in consistent with the kinetic coupling model between transcription and splicing as proposed previously (see introduction). The CTS efficiency correlated with the length between intron 3'end and gene 3'end, while it negatively correlated with active histone marks (H3K4me3 and acetylation) related to rapid transcriptional elongation. Notably, recent evidence showed the Pol II and splicing machinery could be also coupled through Pol II CTD phosphorylated on the serine 5 (S5P). Nascent elongating transcripts sequencing (NET-seq) analysis with antibody against Pol II CTD S5P in mammalian cells showed enrichment of sequencing reads with their 3' end mapped precisely to the exon 3'end, indicating such reads could be derived from splicing intermediates which were not from the Pol II active center but from spliceosome that associate with Pol II CTD S5P (Nojima et al., 2015; Nojima et al., 2018).

How could kinetic coupling comply with the physical coupling regulated by dynamic phosphorylations of Pol II CTD is an interesting question to be addressed in the future. Related to this, we looked at how CTS efficiency was related to the Pol II levels as judged by the high resolution pNET-seq data that published previously (Zhu et al., 2018). Interestingly, a positively correlated trend was observed between 5'SS or 3' SS ratio and the steady state levels of Pol II that carries CTD S5P and or CTD S2P modifications (Supplementary Fig. 9). This data is consistent with the observed correlation between SS ratio and active histone marks, given H3K4me3 and acetylation also indicate frequent Pol II initiation in addition to fast elongation (Wozniak and Strahl, 2014). Therefore, fast Pol II initiation and elongation could result in both higher Pol II level and relatively low CTS efficiency (due to fast elongation), a similar scenario that has been described for *FLC* locus (Wu et al., 2016a). In addition, we also noted a significant peak that associated with Pol II CTD S5P but not Pol II CTD S2P at the junction of exon 3' end and intron 5' end ( Supplementary Fig. 9), most likely representing splicing intermediates (Nojima et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2018). Consistent with this explanation, such a peak is more pronounce in exon-intron-exon groups with low SS ratios, a scenario that would produce such splicing intermediates more frequently during transcription.

We found in this study that RZ-1C is a relatively specific regulator of CTS instead of an essential factor for splicing in general. Indeed, most defects of CTS that observed in the *rz-1b rz-1c* were not associated with intron retention in the steady state mRNA level (Fig 5B, C; Supplementary Fig. 5B to D; Supplementary Fig. 7). In these cases, despite slower rate of splicing at chromatin in *rz-1b rz-1c*, effective splicing seems occurs at post-transcriptional level, ensuring successful intron removal before mRNA maturation. In addition, frequent intron retentions may activate surveillance pathways that selectively degrade such transcripts

(e.g. non-sense mediated decay). Future work will be needed to distinguish between these possibilities. Nevertheless, RZ-1B and RZ-1C likely work at the interface between transcription and mRNA maturation, while the efficient CTS governed by the RZ-1B/1C may be part of the pipeline that ensures successful mRNA maturation or fate determination.

An intriguing aspect to explore in the future is whether RZ-1B/1C also affect transcriptional elongation rate, like in case of some mammalian SR proteins (Ji et al., 2013; Lin et al., 2008; Xiao et al., 2019). Our preliminary analysis support this hypothesis. We observed a steeper 5' to 3' slope of CB-RNA in *rz-1b rz-1c* double mutant compared to Col-0 (Supplementary Fig. 10). As we explained in Fig. 1B, the shape of 5' to 3' slope is tuned by Pol II firing rate, elongation rate and also termination rate. Notably, a change of initiation rate along won't affect the shape of 5' to 3' slope. Therefore, our data is consistent with a hypothesis that RZ-1B/1C affect Pol II dynamics globally. Notably, Pol II travels at uneven speed across a gene (e.g. Pausing at the exon-intron boundary), therefore techniques like pNET-seq would be required in the future to reveal the role of RZ-1B/1C in regulating Pol II activity at high spatial resolution and may further links to its RNA-binding and the roles in CTS regulation.

In mammals, intron are often one magnitude longer than exons, therefore, it was considered that splicing happens through initially recognizing an exon and its adjacent splice site, a mechanism known as "exon definition" (De Conti et al., 2013). In contrast, plant gene splicing is generally considered to occur by intron definition, given the abundant retention of introns, and their shorter length, compared with mammals (Reddy, 2007). According to intron definition model, introns and their 5'splice sites and 3' spliced sites can be determined by spliceosome. Apart from the apparent difference of intron length, what contribute and determine the intron or exon definition is a yet unsolved question. In mammals, splicing

factors such as SR proteins often bind with exon splicing enhancers (ESE) that located at exons and therefore contribute to the exon-definition. Interestingly, as we reported in this study, RZ-1C binds to mainly exons at the majority of its targets, although both its exonic and intronic bindings are important for the regulation of CTS. Currently, it is unclear if intron definition in plant would accompany with more frequent presence of regulatory motifs in introns. Systematic analysis of RNA-binding features of splicing factors through eCLIP-seq, a reliable and powerful method as we adapted in this study, would likely provide an unbiased answer to this question. Notably, a recent study in yeast showed E complex of spliceosome can adapt both intron and exon definition mode without significant conformational change and showed evidence that exon definition can also happen in yeast (Li et al., 2019), a species confers intron-definition as previous considered. Therefore, it would be interesting to test if exon-definition and intron definition co-exist in plant as well, in connection with the RNA-binding properties of splicing factors.

An unexpected discovery from our study is that exon and or intron number correlated with efficient CTS. Consistent with our finding, a recent study based on long reads sequencing of chromatin-bound RNA reveals a significant proportion of yeast nascent RNA are either fully spliced or fully unspliced (Herzel et al., 2018). The removal of individual introns are likely to be coordinated instead of following a simple 'first come, first served' rule (Fededa et al., 2005; Tilgner et al., 2018). Although contribution of introns does exist, our work highlights the role of exons in this mechanism. Exactly how multiple exons and introns promote CTS is an exciting next question to address. RZ-1B/1C seems be part of this mechanism, as RZ-1B/1C-regulated exon-introns-exon units were more enriched in genes with a higher number of introns or exons (Fig.6I). Besides RZ-1B/1C, other factors likely are also involved in this mechanism as the trend between CTS efficiency and intron numbers remains when

comparing RZ-1C bound and unbound genes (Supplementary Fig. 11A), despite the CTS efficiency was generally higher for RZ-1C bound genes (Supplementary Fig. 11B). Previous research showed higher order interactions exist among splicing factors and RNAs (Singh et al., 2012). This is consistent with the role of the RZ-1 proteins, which could promote CTS by being situated at exons and introns, even when distal to the affected intron. RZ-1C could form nuclear speckles and interact with multiple SR proteins (Wu et al., 2016b). Such interactions were confirmed in vivo through GFP-RZ-1C-IP-MS, in addition, multiple other splicing factors were also detected (Supplementary Table 4). An interesting hypothesis to test is whether there are multivalent interactions among exons, introns and splicing regulators such as RZ-1C and SRs, and if such interactions could contribute to the more efficient CTS for gene with higher numbers of introns (Fig. 6I). What's the role of Pol II during this process is another yet unanswered question waiting for future study. Overall, our work has revealed the co-transcriptional nature of plant gene splicing and highlighted the role of exon/intron numbers, as well as the role of RZ-1 proteins, in this process.

# **Methods**

*Plant materials and growth conditions* 

The Col-0 ecotype of *Arabidopsis thaliana* was used as the wild-type control in this study.

The *rz-1b rz-1c* double mutant and *RZ-1C:GFP-RZ-1C* in *rz-1b rz-1c* have been described

- previously (Wu et al., 2016b). All experiments used seedlings grown on 1/2 Murashige and
- Skoog (MS) medium for 10 d at 22 °C under 16h light/8h dark cycles.

# *CB-RNA extraction and sequencing library construction*

- CB-RNA extraction in *Arabidopsis* seedlings was performed as described previously (Bhatt
- et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2016a; Wuarin and Schibler, 1994) (Supplementary Fig. 1A) and

followed the guidelines previously proposed (Herzel and Neugebauer, 2015). In brief, nuclei from 2 g of ground seedlings were prepared using a Honda buffer supplement with the addition of RNase inhibitor and yeast tRNA, to protect the integrity of RNA. The nuclei pellet was first resuspended in an equal volume of resuspension buffer (50 % glycerol, 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 25 mM Tris-HCL pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 100ng/µl tRNA, 0.01U/µl RNasin® ), followed by brief washing with two volumes of UREA wash buffer (25 mM Tris-HCL pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 1 M urea, 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 1% Tween-20). The chromatin was pelleted by centrifugation at 8000 g for 1 min, resuspended with an equal volume of resuspension buffer, followed by washing by using one volume of UREA wash buffer. The resulting chromatin pellet after centrifugation was resuspended in 1 mL Trizol and followed by RNA extraction in combination with the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). The integrity of the resulting CB-RNA was checked by gel electrophoresis by comparing to the pattern of total RNA. DNA copurified with CB-RNA was removed by two sequential digestions with TURBO DNase (Thermo). The resulting RNA was then used for further analysis.

For constructing the CB-RNA-seq library, the above CB-RNA was treated with Ribominus (Thermo) to remove the rRNA following the kit instructions. Any contaminated polyadenylated RNAs were removed using Oligo d(T) cellulose beads (NEB). A strand-specific RNA-seq library was then constructed using the dUTP method. The resulting library was pair-end sequenced on the Illumina platform. A summary of all sequencing results is included in Supplementary Table 1 .

*CB-RNA and mRNA data analysis* 

The raw data of CB-RNA were mapped to the *Arabidopsis* genome (TAIR10) with HISAT2 v2.0.5 (Pertea et al., 2016), and the Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) of the genes were calculated by StringTie v1.3.4d (Pertea et al., 2016) with default parameters. Genes with a FPKM < 1 were filtered following the 5ʹSS or 3ʹSS ratio calculation for exon-intron-exon features to determine the co-transcriptional splicing frequency (Fig. 1G). Reads were counted from CB-RNA-seq mapped to 25 bp upstream (5ʹ exonic reads) or downstream (5ʹ intronic reads) of the exon-intron junction as well as 25 bp downstream (3ʹ exonic reads) or upstream (3ʹ intronic reads) of the intron-exon junction. The ratio of 5ʹ intronic reads number to 5ʹ exonic reads number was calculated as the 5ʹSS ratio (Fig. 1G). The 3ʹSS ratio was calculated in a similar way. The differentially splicing introns between the wild type (WT) and the double mutant were identified as previously reported by using Fisher's test (Deng et al., 2010). Briefly, Fisher's test was performed to identify differential representation of intron over two flanking exons. The test was performed by using normalized CB-RNA-seq or mRNA-seq read counts of each intron and its two flanking exons of the WT and *rz-1b rz-1c*, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg multiple comparison correction. The intron with an FDR less than 0.001 was considered as the differentially splicing intron. The ChIP-seq data of histone modifications from GSE28398 (Luo et al., 2013) were mapped by Bowtie v1.1.2 (Langmead et al., 2009) and only reads mapped to unique position of genome were kept in following analysis. All histone modifications data were normalized by total number of reads. The raw data from mRNA-seq were mapped with TopHat v2.1.1 (Trapnell et al., 2012). Cufflinks v2.2.1 (Trapnell et al., 2012) was used to calculate the FPKM of genes. Cuffdiff v2.2.1 was used to identify significant differentially expressed genes with the following criteria: FPKM > 1, fold change > 2 and FDR < 0.05.

The alternative splicing isoforms A3SS, A5SS, IR and ES were identified based on TAIR10 gene annotation (Fig. 4). Individual AS event was kept for further analysis in case it is

supported by sequencing reads from our mRNA-seq data. "Percentage spliced in" (PSI) was used to denote the fraction of alternative splicing isoforms, a popularly used method (Katz et al., 2010). Briefly, PSI indicates the ratio of two isoforms expressed simultaneously in a tissue. A PSI towards 1 or 0 suggests a trend that one isoform of an alternatively spliced gene was expressed. A PSI of 0.5 indicates equal expression of two isoforms. PSI values were calculated using MISO v0.5.4 (Katz et al., 2010) in the CB-RNA and mRNA datasets. Reads coverage in this study was calculated as reads number per million total sequencing reads per base pair.

To analyse the variance of CTS caused by intron number and gene length, a general linear model (GLM) was fitted as follows: CTS efficiency (calculated as the mean of 5ʹ and 3ʹSS values in a gene) ~intron number of the gene × gene length of the gene, followed by an ANOVA test. Only genes (10,382 of 12,910 genes with available SS values) between 1–4kb in length were used in the GLM analysis.

*eCLIP and sequencing library construction* 

eCLIP was performed as previously described with modifications (Konig et al., 2010; Meyer et al., 2017; Van Nostrand et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2015). In brief, plants growing on petri dishes for 10 d were gently harvested and immersed in an shallow layer (0.5 cm) of ice-cold 1xPBS and cross-linked with UV (245 nM) light at 600 mJ/cm<sup>2</sup> on ice, a dose proven to be sufficient for *Arabidopsis* seedlings (Meyer et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2015). 2 g of liquid nitrogen-ground plant powder was lysed with lysis buffer, DNase I/RNase I treated and sonicated to release the RNA/proteins. The resulting lysate were cleared by centrifugation, then subjected to immunoprecipitation with an anti-GFP antibody (Ab290, Abcam) and protein A beads (Dynabeads, Thermo). The RNA/protein complex after IP was washed extensively including several washes with high salt wash buffer (1M NaCl), treated with

polynucleotide kinase and alkaline phosphatase to remove the terminal phosphate, ligated to an RNA adaptor at the 3ʹ end and end-labeled with <sup>32</sup> P. Ninety percent of the resulting complex was eluted using LDS sample buffer (Thermo) and resolved on a 4–12 % NuPAGE (Thermo) gel and then transferred to nitrocellulose membrane for autoradiography (~3hrs). The remaining 10 % of the complex was run in parallel to check the enrichment of the target protein. The region above the size of proteins (from 70kD to ~140kD) was cut out based on the autoradiograph and RNA was released by proteinase K digestion. The resulting RNA was further purified and reverse transcribed using SuperScript III (Thermo) with a primer complementary to the RNA adaptor. The resulting cDNA was purified and subjected to DNA adaptor ligation to its 3ʹ end. The final library was amplified from the cDNA by using primers specific to both end adaptors (FP-F and FP-R) using Q5 Hot-Start DNA polymerase. For GFP-RZ-1C-eCLIP sample, typically 12 cycles were sufficient for final library amplification from cDNA. The resulting PCR products were purified and resolved on a 3 % low melting point agarose gel and fragments from 175–400 bp were recovered from the gel and used for pair-end Illumina (PE150) sequencing. A summary of all sequencing results is shown in Supplementary Table 1. All oligos and adaptor sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 5.

## *eCLIP data analysis*

eCLIP data were processed using iCount v2.0.1.dev (T. Curk, 2016). Given the principle of eCLIP experiment, only R2 reads were used in binding site identification although pair-end sequencing results were generated. In brief, we used the "demultiplex" function, removed the adapter sequence from reads and extracted 10 bp random sequences, which were used to mark the PCR duplicates. The clean reads were then aligned with the "mapstar" function. All binding sites were identified and quantified using "xlsites" function (parameters: --group\_by

start--quant cDNA). Finally, the "peaks" function was used to identify significant binding sites (FDR < 0.05), and binding clusters were generated with the "clusters" function. For the five replicates generated by eCLIP, the clusters were identified in each replicate separately, and in a pool of the five replicates. Only clusters from the pooled replicates that were also identified in all five replicates were retained as binding clusters. Only binding sites found in the binding clusters were kept for the subsequent analyses. MEME-ChIP (Machanick and Bailey, 2011) was used to find motifs enriched within the binding sites.

# *qPCR validation of CB-RNA-seq data and mRNA-seq data*

CB-RNA was extracted and purified as described above. mRNA was purified from total RNA by using Dynabeads mRNA Purification Kit (Thermo). In brief, 500 ng of chromatin-bound RNA was treated twice with TURBO DNase (Thermo) and used for cDNA synthesis with gene-specific primers and SuperScript III (Thermo) at 52 ºC for 1 h. In case of mRNA, 50ng of mRNA was used for cDNA synthesis. Two primer pairs were designed for measuring splicing efficiency for each exon-intron-exon structure: one primer pair spanned the exon-exon junction for amplification of the spliced transcripts only, and another primer pair spanned the intron-exon junction for amplification of unspliced transcripts only. All the reverse primers were included in the reverse transcription reaction to ensure gene and strand specific detection of RNA. The resulting cDNA was diluted 30 times with water and used for quantitative PCR using a qTOWER<sup>3</sup> 84 (Jena) and SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche). For data normalization, the value obtained by the spliced primer was normalized to the value obtained by the unspliced primer. The values from three independent samples were normalized to the mean WT level. Final values are ± S.E.M. from three independent samples. A 'no reverse transcriptase' control (- RT) was used to ensure the values reflected the level of RNA and not DNA contamination. Primers used for cDNA synthesis and quantitative PCR are described in Supplementary Table 5.

## *RNA immunoprecipitation*

Nuclei from 2 g of ground seedlings were prepared with Honda buffer as described in *CB-RNA extraction and sequencing library construction.* Nuclei pellets were resuspended in 2.5 volumes of Nuclei Lysis Buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1 % SDS, 1x protease inhibitor cocktail, 50 ng/µl tRNA) and sonicated in a Bioruptor (Diagenode) 15 times (30s on/30s off). Immunoprecipitation was performed by incubating 50 µL Dynabeads protein A (Thermo), 5 µg antibody (Anti-GFP, AB290, Abcam), and 1.2 mL diluted chromatin (containing 100 µL sonicated chromatin) at 4 ºC for 1.5 h. After the IP, the beads were washed four times in washing buffer (167 mM Nacl, 16.7 mM Tris pH 7.5, 1.2 mM EDTA, 0.8 % Triton X-100, 1x protease inhibitor, 20 U/mL RNase inhibitor). The RNA-protein complex was eluted and reverse cross-linked by adding 200 µL elution buffer (2 mM EDTA, 0.2 % SDS, Proteinase K)) to the washed beads and incubating at 55 ºC overnight. The RNA was precipitated, dissolved, DNase treated, and then used as a template for reverse transcription with gene/strand-specific primers. The values presented in the figure are ± S.E.M. from three independent samples and shown as IP/1 % of input (RNA). All the primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 5.

# *Immunoprecipitation and Mass Spectrometry*

Immunoprecipitation and Mass Spectrometry analysis were done as previously described with modifications (Questa et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2014). For immunoprecipitation, 5 g of seedling of RZ-1C:GFP-RZ-1C and 35S:GFP were harvested, grinded to fine powder with liquid nitrogen. Two volumes of extraction buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, 150 mM NaCl,

2.5 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100 and 2x Proteinase inhibitor (Roche)) were added into the grinded powder, followed by gentle rotation at 4°C for 10mins. The supernatant was recovered after three sequential centrifugation at 14000rpm for 5mins. Pre-washed GFP-Trap agarose beads (Chromotek) were incubated with supernatant for 2hrs at 4°C with rotation, followed by washing with 1ml extraction buffer for 5 times. The resulting beads were boiled in 1x LDS sample buffer(Thermo). 5% of the resulting samples were loaded on 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to PVDF membrane, followed by western blot by using anti-GFP antibody (AB290, Abcam).

For Mass Spectrometry analysis, protein samples from above steps were loaded into SDS-PAGE gel (10% stacking gels) and run until all the loading dye migrate into the gel. The band was cut out as a whole, washed, reduced and alkylated, followed by trypsin digestion as previously reported (Shevchenko et al., 2006). Peptides extraction was done by using 5% formic acid/50% acetonitrile, dried down and re-dissolved in 0.1% TFA. LC-MS/MS analysis was done by using Synapt G2Si mass spectrometer (Waters) combined with a nanoAcquityTM UPLCTM-system (Waters) running a BEH C18 column (1.7 µm, 75 µm x 250mm, Waters). Raw files from the Synapt G2Si were processed with the Protein Lynx Global Server (PLGS 3.0.2) software (Waters), and search against TAIR10\_pep\_20101214 database (www.arabidopsis.org). Results were exported as excel files using the Ion Accounting Output (IdentityE).
