In this study, we cloned *Dek33* by positional cloning and found that it encodes the pyrimidine reductase in riboflavin biosynthesis. ## INTRODUCTION

Maize (Zea mays) is one of the most important crops that provide resources for food, feed, and biofuel (Godfray et al., 2010). It is also widely considered as the model plant for studies regarding diverse biological phenomena, such as, heterosis, imprinting, transposons and genetic diversity in monocots (Bennetzen and Hake, 2009). The seed is a key organ of maize that comprises three compartments: embryo, endosperm and seed coat (Reiser and Fischer, 1993; West and Harada, 1993). The study of maize seed development has been facilitated by the characterization of mutants. In maize, the normal product of fertilization displays as a mature dormant seed with a well-developed embryo and starchy endosperm. Variations from this norm with significantly reduced embryo or endosperm development are considered as defective kernel (dek) types (Lowe and Nelson, 1946). Although a large number of classic *dek* mutants have been described (Neuffer et al., 1978; Neuffer and Sheridan, 1980; Sheridan and Neuffer, 1980), only dek1, dek2 and dek10 have been identified and characterized so far (Lid et al., 2002; Qi et al., 2017b; Qi et al., 2017a). In recent years, the collection of *dek* mutants was expanding, with more genes cloned from mutants isolated from different resources (Qi et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2017; Garcia et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017; Dai et al., 2018; Li et al., 2018). A subclass of dek mutants is represented by the empty pericarp (*emp*) mutants (Scanlon et al., 1994), which display more severe endosperm and embryo defects, have also been studied (Gutiérrez-Marcos et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2015; Xiu et al., 2016; Cai et al., 2017; Ren et al., 2017). Interestingly, the majority of these *dek/emp* mutants belong to the PPR family proteins, except *dek1* (Lid et al., 2002), *dek*\* (Qi et al., 2016) and dek38 (Garcia et al., 2017). Therefore, more genes associated with other molecular mechanisms that underlie the well-known *dek/emp* mutants will be needed to characterize to investigate many basic biological processes during maize seed development.

Riboflavin is the precursor of flavin mononucleotide and flavin adenine dinucleotide which are essential cofactors involved in numerous critical cellular processes (Frenkel et al., 1979; Gastaldi et al., 1999). Riboflavin biosynthesis proceeds by the same reaction steps in higher plants as in eubacteria, except that fungi use a different pathway (Roje, 2007; Fischer and Bacher, 2008). In *E. coli*, the deamination and reduction steps are catalyzed by a single bifunctional enzyme named RibD (Richter et al., 1997; Magalhaes et al., 2008). In higher plants, there are two

distinct RibD homologues: PyrD (Fischer et al., 2004) and PyrR (Hasnain et al., 2013). One mystery surrounding plant PyrR is the containing of an extra C-terminal domain COG3236, which was characterized with experimental support in only one study (Frelin et al., 2015). Despite this progress, naturally occurring regulation mechanism of the PyrR and the COG3236 domain remains elusive in higher plants.

In this study, we reported the map-based cloning of maize classic kernel mutant *dek33*, which encodes the pyrimidine reductase in riboflavin biosynthesis. Our results indicated that DEK33 is essential for oilbody formation, endoreduplication and ABA biosynthesis during seed development. We also found that the COG3236 domain might be an essential regulatory structure for DEK33 stability. In addition, several proteins that interact with DEK33 were identified by yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay and confirmed by luciferase complementation image (LCI) assay, including RGLG2 and SnRK1, suggesting possible post-translational regulation to DEK33 stability.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### **Plant materials**

The maize *dek33-N1299*, *dek33-N1145A* and *UFMu05381* mutants were obtained from the Maize Genetics Cooperation stock center. *dek33-N1299* and *dek33-N1145A* were crossed into the W22 and W64A genetic background to generate F1 population, respectively. The F1 population was then self-crossed to generate the segregated F2 ears that displayed a 1:3 segregation of *dek (dek33/dek33)* and wild type (WT) (+/+ and *dek33/*+) phenotypes. Homozygous kernels from segregated F2 ears of *dek33-N1299* (named as *dek33-ref*) were used as mapping population. The heterozygous kernels from segregated F2 ears of *dek33-N1145A* and *UFMu-05381* were used in allelism tests with *dek33-N1299*.

Kernels of *dek33-N1299* and WT were harvested for phenotypic observation, paraffin and resin sections preparation, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation, RNA and protein extraction, riboflavin content analysis, riboflavin feeding assay, oilbody isolation, flow cytometric analysis, carotenoid and ABA content analysis, zeaxanthin epoxidase (ZEP) and aldehyde oxidase (AO) activity examination. For RNA-seq, three biological replicates were from three different segregated F2 ears of *dek33-N1299* that harvested at 15 DAP. One ear was harvested in the summer of 2015, and the other two ears were harvested in the summer of 2017. For *Dek33* expression pattern analysis, kernels at 3, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 30, 36 days

after pollination (DAP) were harvested from the same B73 ear for RNA and protein extraction. Other tissues including root, stem, leaf, tassel, silk, husk and ear were harvested from the same B73 plant for RNA and protein extraction.

*N. benthamiana* plants were grown in a growth chamber at 22°C and 70% relative humidity under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod for about 1-1.5 months before infiltration. After infiltration, plants were kept under the same growth conditions.

#### Measurements of starch, protein and fatty acid

For starch and protein measurement, the protocol follows the method as described previously (Qi et al., 2016). For fatty acid measurement, the protocol follows the method as described before (Feng et al., 2018). All the measurements were performed at least three biological replicates.

### Paraffin, resin and TEM sections

For paraffin, resin and TEM sections, samples were prepared as described before (Dai et al., 2018). Bright-field photographs of paraffin and resin sections were taken using a Leica microscope at Shanghai University. Sample sections of TEM were observed using "FEI Tecnai Spirit G2 BioTWIN".

### Map-based cloning

For map-based cloning of *Dek33* gene, 1173 homozygous *dek33-N1299* kernels from the segregated F2 ears were used as mapping population. Molecular simple sequence repeats (SSR) markers AC218-6, AC191-16, AC210-12, AC184-3, AC194-1, AC208-2, AC211-5 and Indel (insertion-deletion) marker InD7 were developed to map the *Dek33* locus on maize chromosome. Fine mapping of *Dek33* indicated that the *Dek33* gene was localized to a 631,730 bp genomic interval between AC194-1 and AC211-5 on chromosome 5.

### **Polyclonal antibodies**

For antibody production, the full-length DEK33 open reading frames (ORF) sequence was cloned into the pGEX-4T-1 vector (Amersham Biosciences). Primers were listed in Table S4. Protein expression was followed by established procedures in *E. coli* BL21 strain. Protein purification and production of antibody in rabbits were performed according to standard protocols of Abclonal of China. The antibodies

against GFP and GST were obtained from Clontech. The antibodies against FLAG and  $\alpha$ -Tubulin were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. The antibodies against D1, D2 and CP43 were obtained from Agrisera.

#### Protein extraction and immunoblot analysis

Extraction of protein from *dek33*, WT and B73 tissues was performed according to the method described before (Bernard et al., 1994). Extraction of protein from *N*. *benthamiana* leaves was performed as described previously (Liu et al., 2010).

Separated protein samples were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (0.45  $\mu$ m; Millipore, Bedford, MA). The membrane with a protein sample attached on it was incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. Using the Super Signal West Pico chemiluminescent substrate kit (Pierce), the signal was visualized according to the manufacturer's instructions. Antibodies against DEK33 and CP43 were used at 1:2,000, antibodies against FLAG, GFP, GST, D1, D2 and  $\alpha$ -Tubulin were used at 1:5,000.

### Subcellular localization of DEK33

For subcellular localization of DEK33, the full-length DEK33 ORF sequence was cloned into pSAT6-EYFP-N1 (Dai et al., 2018). The recombinant plasmid (~1  $\mu$ g) was introduced into *N. benthamiana* leaf epidermal cells through transient transformation using the Bio-Rad PDS-1000/HeTM biolistic particle delivery system. The fluorescence signals were detected using LSM710 (Occult International).

### **RNA extraction, RT-PCR and quantitative RT-PCR**

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Tiangen). Using ReverTra Ace reverse transcriptase (Toyobo), RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the attached random primers. RT-PCR was performed for each individual fragment that was transiently expressed in *N. benthamiana* with *Actin1* as an internal control. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green Real-Time PCR Master Mix (Toyobo) using a Mastercycler ep realplex 2 (Eppendorf) according to the standard protocol (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). All primers were listed in Table S4.

### Bacterial strain *DE3AribD* and functional complementation experiment

An E. coli ribD deletant (DE3AribD) strain containing a chromosomal copy of the

orynebacterium glutamicum riboflavin transporter gene *ribM* was constructed from strain BL21 (DE3) using the CRISPR-Cas9 system (Mathes et al., 2009; Jiang et al., 2015). Correct replacement of the *ribD* gene was confirmed by sequencing (Fig. S4).  $DE3\Delta ribD$  was maintained on LB medium containing 50  $\mu$ M riboflavin.

For functional complementation experiment, maize DEK33 and PyrD were cloned into the modified pET-32a vector (Novagen) without their predicted N-terminal targeting peptides using ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). *E. coli* RibD was cloned into the same vector as a positive control. DEK33-i, DEK33-ii and DEK33-iii were also cloned into the modified pET-32a. The positions at which the various truncations were made are shown in Figure S11. All constructs were verified by sequencing. Functional complementation experiment was performed as described previously (Hasnain et al., 2013). All primers were listed in Table S4.

## Preparation and quantification of riboflavin

Riboflavin was extracted from *dek33* and WT mature kernels as described previously (Langer and Lodge, 2014). Quantification of riboflavin was examined using HPLC by standard protocols in Shanghai Institute of Material Medica, Shanghai.

### **Riboflavin feeding assay**

Mature kernels of *dek33* and WT were surface-sterilized 30 min in 10%  $H_2O_2$ , followed by saturated CaSO<sub>4</sub> for 3 hours. Kernels were rinsed three times in sterile water and sown on a piece of sterile filter paper (Whatman Grade No. 3 MM filter paper) wetted with sterile water. After germination for 3 days, seedlings were transferred onto half-strength MS culture medium with or without riboflavin and placed in a growth chamber with a photoperiod of 16-h light and 8-h dark at 25°C.

## **PSII** activity (*Fv/Fm*) analysis

Seedlings of *dek33* and WT were cut off and put on a piece of wet paper. After dark adaption for 15 min, chlorophyll fluorescence was measured with an Imaging PAM 101 (Watzs, Germany), and PSII activity (Fv/Fm) values were calculated according to the manufacturer's instructions.

## **RNA-seq analysis**

For RNA-seq, three biological replicates were performed. For each replicate, kernels

from the F2 ears were genotyped for *dek33* or WT by molecular markers AC194-1 and AC211-5, and at least 5 individual genotyped endosperms of dek33 or WT were mixed for total RNA (20 µg) extraction. Library construction was performed according to Illumina standard instructions by Shanghai Biotechnology. Reads were aligned to the maize B73 genome using TopHat2.0.6 (Langmead et al., 2009). Data were normalized as fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped. Significant differentially expressed genes were identified as genes with a fold change>=2 and q-value $\leq$ =0.05. GO enrichment was performed with the accession of agriGO Singular Enrichment numbers significant DEGs by (http://bioinfo.cau.edu.cn/agriGO/). RNA-seq raw data are available from the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) under the series entry GSE80250.

### **Isolation of oilbodies**

Ten kernels of *dek33* and WT were ground in liquid nitrogen, and dried to a constant weight for subsequent analysis. 150 mg of each sample meal was used for isolation of oilbodies. The following steps were performed according to an established protocol (Siloto et al., 2006). Oilbody-associated proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE using standard protocols (Sambrook et al., 1989) and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R 250.

### **Flow Cytometry Detection**

For extraction of nuclei, the protocol follows the method as described previously (Wang et al., 2014). Each flow cytometric histogram was saved using PARTEC CA3 software and analyzed with WinMDI 2.8 software.

### Preparation and quantification of carotenoids

The carotenoids were extracted from *dek33* and WT mature kernels as described previously (Kurilich and Juvik, 1999) and the carotenoids content was determined using HPLC according to a described protocol (Shutu et al., 2012) at China Agricultural University.

## Preparation and quantification of ABA, ZEP and AO

ABA was isolated from *dek33* and WT mature endosperms and embryos using the protocol described before (Wang et al., 2012). ABA content was detected by ELISA analysis according to Zhao et al., 2006. The ABA detection ELISA kit was obtained from the Center of Crop Chemical Control, China Agricultural University, China.

Plant ZEP (XY-00768) and AO (SY-G0070) ELISA kits were obtained from Shanghai Shuangying biotechnology. The content of ZEP and AO was determined following standard protocols in ELISA kits.

### Protein overexpression in N. benthamiana

For protein overexpression in *N. benthamiana*, full-length and truncated DEK33 were cloned into a pHB vector under the CaMV 35S promoter. DEK33, DEK33<sup>\*</sup>, COG3236 and COG3236<sup>\*</sup> were cloned into a modified pR1101-AN (Takara). For modification of pR1101-AN, a 6×FLAG fragment was cloned into *EcoRI* and *SacI* sites. A GFP fragment was cloned into *NdeI* and *SalI* sites, *EcoRI* and *SacI* sites, respectively. A GST fragment was cloned into *NdeI* and *SalI* sites. DEK33 and DEK33<sup>\*</sup> were cloned into pR1101-AN with FLAG tag. COG and COG<sup>\*</sup> were cloned into pR1101-AN with GFP tag. The positions at which the various truncations were made are shown in Figure S11. All primers were listed in Table S4. Vectors were transformed into *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* strain GV3101. The agro-infiltration procedure was performed as previously described (Liu et al., 2010).

## Y2H assay

For the DEK33 bait, the full-length DEK33 ORF was fused downstream from the GAL4 BD domain in pGBKT7 at *EcoRI* and *SalI* sites. This bait was used to screen a maize developing kernel cDNA library fused downstream of the GAL4 AD domain in pGADT7. For the pGADT7-preys, all ORFs were fused to pGADT7 at *EcoRI* and *BamHI* sites. All primers were listed in Table S4.

## LCI assay

The ORFs of DEK33 and six selected DEK33-interacting proteins identified from Y2H screening were cloned into both JW771 (NLUC) and JW772 (CLUC) using ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). All primers were listed in Table S4. The following experiment was performed according to Feng et al., 2018. The resulting luciferase signals were collected using the Tanon-5200 image system.

## RESULTS

# The *dek33* mutation produces defective kernels with reduced storage components and delayed development

The *dek33* mutant stock (*dek33-N1299*) obtained from the Maize Genetics Cooperation Stock Center was crossed into the W22 genetic background to generate F1 population. F2 ears from self-crossed F1 displayed a 1:3 segregation of *dek* (*dek33/dek33*) and WT (+/+ or *dek33/*+) phenotypes, indicating that *dek33* is a single recessive mutant (Fig. S1A). The homozygous *dek33* kernels can be distinguished from F2 ears as early as 12 DAP, as *dek33* kernels exhibited smaller white endosperm compared with the WT (Fig. S1B). At maturity, *dek33* kernels contained smaller and opaque endosperms (Fig. 1A-C), and smaller and defective embryos which were occasionally viviparous (Fig. 1B-C). The 100-kernel weight of *dek33* was 63.7% that of the WT (Fig. 1D).

To further determine the underlying biochemical basis for the *dek* phenotype of the mutant, the major storage components were examined in *dek33* and WT mature kernels. Total starch, total fatty acid, and protein (total protein, zein and non-zein) contents were analyzed. There was no significant difference in total starch content per weight between *dek33* and WT (Fig. 1E). However, total fatty acid and total protein content per weight was generally reduced. GC analysis showed that total fatty acid content in *dek33* was only 53.2% of that in the WT (Fig. 1F; Fig. S1C-D). SDS-PAGE analysis indicated significant reduction of total protein in *dek33* (Fig. S1E). Quantitative analysis showed that the content of total protein and zein in *dek33* was 95.6% and 86.2% of that in the WT, respectively (Fig. 1G). However, there was no significant change for the content of total fatty acid, total protein and zein was reduced in *dek33*, while total starch and nonzein content showed no significant changes.

The seed development was observed through histological sections of kernels collected at different DAPs. Longitudinal sections of the whole kernel showed affected seed development in *dek33*. At 12 and 18 DAP, *dek33* embryos are much smaller compared with the WT (Fig. 1H). Light microscopy observations clearly indicated that *dek33* endosperm cells filled with fewer starch granules compared with the WT at 18 DAP (Fig. 1I). Aleurone layer cells of *dek33* showed smaller size with <sup>10</sup>

abnormal cellular morphology compared with the WT (Fig. 1I). Furthermore, TEM sections revealed that the number and size of protein bodies were drastically reduced in *dek33* endosperms (Fig. S1F). In addition, fewer oilbodies were observed in both *dek33* embryo (Fig. 1J) and aleurone layer cells (Fig. 1K) compared with the WT. These results indicated that the seed development was severely affected in *dek33*.

### Positional cloning of *Dek33*

Genetic mapping of the *Dek33* gene was carried out with the F2 mapping population of *dek33-N1299* (named as *dek33-ref*). After characterizing a population of 1173 homozygous *dek33-ref* individuals using molecular markers (Table S4), *Dek33* was placed between molecular markers AC194-1 and AC211-5, with an interval of 631,730 bp in physical distance (Fig. 2A). Nucleotide sequence analysis within this interval identified nine predicted ORFs with gene models (Table S1). Genomic sequence analysis revealed that only *GRMZM2G090068* has a single base mutation (G-to-A) in exon 7 (Fig. 2B; Fig. S2A), resulting in a premature stop codon TGA. Thus, *GRMZM2G090068* was considered as the candidate gene for *Dek33*.

To confirm that *GRMZM2G090068* was the *Dek33* gene, another *dek* mutant (*dek33-N1145A*) was discovered that was found to be allelic to *dek33-N1299* (Neuffer and England, 1995; Dong et al., 2018), which was also confirmed in our study (Fig. S2B). Genomic sequence analysis of *GRMZM2G090068* in *dek33-N1145A* indicated the same mutation with *dek33-N1299* (Fig. S2A). In addition, a *UniformMu* mutant stock (*UFMu05381*) carrying a *Mutator* insertion in the first exon of *GRMZM2G090068* (named as *dek33-mu*) was obtained from the Maize Genetics Stock Center (Fig. 2B), and was used for an allelism test. In *dek33-mu*, homozygous mutant kernels abort shortly after fertilization (Hasnain et al., 2013). An allelism test between *dek33-ref* and *dek33-mu* revealed a 1:3 segregation of *dek* and WT kernel phenotype on the F2 ear, indicating that *dek33-mu* cannot complement *dek33-ref* (Fig. S2C-E). Further genotyping analysis indicated that all *dek* kernels contained both the *Mutator* insertion and single base mutation, while WT kernels contained either of two alleles or nothing (Fig. S2F-G). Therefore, *GRMZM2G090068* was indeed the *Dek33* gene.

### Dek33 encodes the pyrimidine reductase in riboflavin biosynthesis

Conserved domain analysis indicated that DEK33 encodes the pyrimidine reductase in

riboflavin biosynthesis (Fig. 3A). This pyrimidine reductase is a functional homologue in plants to the bacterial RibD protein (Fischer et al., 2004; Chatwell et al., 2006; Chen et al., 2006).

To examine whether the *dek33* mutation affected *Dek33* transcriptional expression, quantitative RT-PCR was performed with total RNA extracted from *dek33* and WT kernels at 15 DAP. The result showed no significant change of *Dek33* expression between *dek33* and WT (Fig. 3B), suggesting that the *dek33* mutation did not affect the transcriptional level of *Dek33*.

To further investigate whether the *dek33* mutation affected DEK33 protein level, immunoblot analysis was carried out with DEK33 specific antibody in total protein extracted from *dek33* and WT kernels at 15 DAP. An expected band with molecular mass of ~68 kD was detected in the WT, whereas this band was not detected in *dek33* (Fig. 3C; Fig. S3A). However, a ~53 kD band corresponding to the truncated fragment of DEK33 was detected in *dek33*, with much lower protein level compared with the WT (Fig. 3C; Fig. S3A). We subsequently confirmed this result in *N. benthamiana* by transient expression of full-length DEK33 and truncated DEK33 as in *dek33* with pHB vector under the CaMV 35S promoter. Western blot analysis with the same antibody detected the ~68 kD and the ~53 kD bands in samples expressed for the full-length DEK33 and the truncated DEK33, respectively (Fig. S3B). These results indicated that the *dek33* mutation caused lower protein level of truncated DEK33.

## The dek33 mutation does not affect the DEK33 reductase activity

Previous study indicated that the COG3236 domain is not essential for DEK33 reductase activity in *E. coli* (Hasnain et al., 2013). Because the *dek33* mutation caused truncated DEK33, we therefore tested whether the truncated protein affected the DEK33 reductase activity using an *E. coli* strain  $DE3\Delta ribD$  (Fig. S4). Three truncated proteins were tested for complementing activity: DEK33-i identifies the truncated DEK33, DEK33-ii lacks the C-terminal COG3236 domain and DEK33-iii lacks both COG3236 and deaminase domains (Fig. 3D). The result showed that DEK33-i remained active (Fig. 3E; Fig. S5), suggesting that the *dek33* mutation did not affect the DEK33 reductase activity.

### Riboflavin feeding partially rescues the phenotype of *dek33* seedlings

To identify effects caused by the *dek33* mutation on riboflavin biosynthesis, riboflavin content was examined in *dek33* and WT mature kernels using HPLC analysis. The result showed that the riboflavin content decreased by 48% in *dek33* (Fig. 3F), indicating that the *dek33* mutation greatly affected the riboflavin biosynthesis.

To analyze effects caused by reduced riboflavin, a riboflavin feeding assay was performed to kernels and seedlings of *dek33* and WT. On basic growth medium (half-strength MS medium without riboflavin), dek33 kernels showed low level of germination rate (~15%) (Fig. S6A). At seedling stage (7 days after germination), WT seedlings showed a normal phenotype, while *dek33* seedlings exhibited lethal germination stage (Fig. 3G). On the growth medium supplemented with 0.3 mM riboflavin, the germination rate of *dek33* kernels showed no significant change (Fig. S6A). However, after 7 days cultivation in medium supplemented with riboflavin, dek33 seedlings were able to grow partially green leaves with yellow-and-white variegation. These *dek33* seedlings showed delayed development, and would not survive the third leaf stage (Fig. 3G; Fig. S6B). The result indicated that *dek33* seedlings supplemented with 0.3 mM riboflavin partially reverted the mutant phenotype.

Because mutant seedlings can be recovered, we therefore analyzed various photosynthetic parameters in *dek33* and WT seedlings. The result showed that PSII activity (Fv/Fm) was reduced in *dek33* (Fig. S6C). In addition, the ultrastructure of chloroplasts showed that thylakoid membrane systems of *dek33* were disturbed and the membrane spacing was not as clear as the WT (Fig. S6D). Moreover, immunoblot analysis using specific antibodies against the subunits of photosynthetic protein complexes showed that the level of photosystem II proteins (D1, D2 and CP43) was almost abolished in *dek33* (Fig. S6E). These results suggested that the *dek33* mutation severely affected chloroplast development and photosynthesis.

### DEK33 expresses highly in leaves and kernels, and is localized in chloroplasts

Homologous sequence searched using DEK33 amino acid sequence by BLASTP identified homologous proteins from other species. Sequence alignment of these homologous showed high similarity in reductase and COG3236 domains (Fig. S7). Subsequently, a neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was constructed (Fig. S8A). The result indicated that DEK33 homologs are highly conserved in angiosperms.

Quantitative RT-PCR revealed that *Dek33* is constitutively expressed in all tested 13

tissues (Fig. S8B). During seed development, expression of *Dek33* occurs before 3 DAP and continues later than 30 DAP (Fig. S8B). An immunoblot analysis with the DEK33 specific antibody showed that DEK33 is highly accumulated in kernel and leaf (Fig. S8C). DEK33 was first detectable at 3 DAP and accumulated in abundance (Fig. S8D).

To experimentally determine the subcellular localization of DEK33, full-length DEK33 was fused with yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) and transiently expressed in *N. benthamiana* leaf epidermal cells. Confocal laser scanning microscopy showed co-localization of the YFP signal with chloroplasts autofluorescence (Fig. S8E). The result indicated that DEK33 targets chloroplasts.

## The dek33 mutation alters transcriptome

To investigate impacts of the *dek33* mutation on gene expression, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was carried out for *dek33* and WT endosperm from 15 DAP kernels. Among 33,284 transcripts detected by RNA-seq, significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified as those with a threshold fold change >=2 and q-value  $\leq$ =0.05. Based on this criterion, 855 genes showed significantly altered expression between *dek33* and WT.

Within the 855 DEGs, 645 genes could be functionally annotated. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis indicated that 645 genes were mainly classified into eight GO terms (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Data Set 1). GO classifications such as GO: 0044284 (cellular catabolic process), GO: 0006091 (generation of precursor metabolites and energy), GO: 0006520 (cellular amino acid metabolic process), GO: 0006082 (organic acid metabolic process) and GO: 0006412 (translation) exhibited extensively up-regulated expression, indicating that the metabolic process was greatly affected in *dek33*. Genes related to GO: 0006334 (nucleosome assembly) also exhibited up-regulated expression, revealing that the function of endoduplication was impacted in *dek33*. However, genes related to GO: 0012511 (monolayer-surrounded lipid storage body) were expressed with significantly decreased regulation, which was related to the reduced total fatty acid content in *dek33*. In addition, genes classified to GO: 0045735 (nutrient reservoir activity) showed significant down-regulation, which was related to the reduced zein content in *dek33*. These results indicated that the metabolic process, endoduplication, lipid storage body formation and zein synthesis

were altered in *dek33*, suggesting a critical role for the riboflavin biosynthesis during seed development.

### The *dek33* mutation results in fewer oleosins

Transcriptome analysis indicated significant down-regulation of monolayersurrounded lipid storage body related genes in *dek33*. To validate the differences observed by RNA-seq, quantitative RT-PCR was performed on seven most significant lipid storage body related genes from GO category (Fig. 4B). The result confirmed that the expression level of these genes was reduced in *dek33*.

Oleosins are the major proteins associated with oilbody and are usually present as two or more isoforms (Tzen et al., 1990). Because down-regulation of oilbody-related genes was detected at RNA level, oleosins were then prepared from the total homogenates of mature *dek33* and WT kernels. SDS-PAGE analysis of isolated oilbody-related proteins indicated significant reduction of oleosins in *dek33* (Fig. 4C). These results indicated that the *dek33* mutation resulted in dramatic decrease of oleosins.

### The dek33 mutation affects endoduplication

Endoreduplication is a general feature of endosperm development in maize, involving replication of the nuclear genome without cell division and leading to elevated nucleic acid content (Qi et al., 2016). Endoreduplication is a variant of the mitotic cell cycle (G1-S-G2-M) with only G1 and S phase. RNA-seq data showed up-regulation of nucleosome assembly-related genes (39 genes) and DNA replication-related genes (5 genes) in *dek33* (Supplementary Data Set 1). The expression level of ten selected genes was further confirmed by quantitative RT-PCR (Fig. 4D; Table S2).

Because obvious developmental delay was observed in *dek33*, flow cytometric analysis was therefore performed to determine if there is an aberrant endoreduplication in *dek33* endosperm. The result showed that endoreduplicated nuclei with C-values of 12 C or greater, accounting for 29.11% of the DNA in 15 DAP endosperm of *dek33*, but 34.40% of the DNA in 15 DAP WT endosperm (Fig. 4E; Fig. S9). The result indicated that the endoreduplication was suppressed in *dek33* endosperm.

### The dek33 mutation disrupts ABA biosynthesis

Similar to the group of maize viviparous mutants (McCarty, 1995), *dek33* kernels exhibited viviparous phenotype (Fig. 1B). In several maize viviparous mutants, carotenogenesis and ABA biosynthesis were affected at various stages (Robertson, 1955; Robertson et al., 1978). To explore whether the *dek33* mutation impacted ABA biosynthesis, the content of carotenoids was determined by HPLC in *dek33* and WT mature kernels. The result showed significant reduced content of lutein, zeaxanthin,  $\beta$ -cryptoxanthin,  $\beta$ -carotene and antheraxanthin in *dek33* (Fig. 5A). To further determine if the *dek33* mutation affected ABA content, ELISA analysis was used for examining ABA content in embryo, endosperm and seedlings of *dek33* and WT. The results showed reduced ABA content in both *dek33* kernels and seedlings (Fig. 5B; Fig. S10).

Several enzymes were identified as the key role in ABA biosynthesis, e.g., ZEP (Marin et al., 1996) and AO (Marin and Marion-Poll, 1997). To eliminate whether the *dek33* mutation affected ZEP and AO activity, ZEP and AO activity was detected by ELISA analysis in *dek33* and WT kernels at 21 DAP. Significant decreased ZEP and AO activity was observed in *dek33* compared with that in the WT (Fig. 5C). In addition, the expression of genes involved in ABA biosynthesis (Vp5, Vp7, Vp10 and Vp14) was also examined by quantitative RT-PCR in kernels at 21 DAP (Hable et al., 1998; Burbidge et al., 1999; Singh et al., 2003; Porch et al., 2006). The result showed up-regulation of Vp5, Vp7 and Vp10 in *dek33*, while Vp14 showed no significant changes of expression (Fig. 5D). These results suggested that the *dek33* mutation significantly disrupted ABA biosynthesis.

## The COG3236 domain might be important for DEK33 stability

Because the *dek33* mutation caused lower protein level of truncated DEK33, we suspected that COG3236 domain might play possible stability function for DEK33. To explore the possible stability function of COG3236, DEK33-FLAG and DEK33<sup>\*</sup>-FLAG were then transiently expressed in *N. benthamiana* leaves (Fig. S11). RT-PCR was performed for each individual fragment that was transiently expressed to avoid experimental error in parallel experiments. Western blot analysis using antibody against FLAG revealed a single ~60kD band of DEK33<sup>\*</sup>-FLAG with lower protein level compared with the ~75kD band of DEK33-FLAG (Fig. 6A; Fig. S12A).

To further experimentally determine the stability function of the COG3236 domain, GFP-COG and GFP-COG<sup>\*</sup> were transiently expressed in in *N. benthamiana* 

leaves (Fig. S11). As a single domain that fused to the C-terminus of GFP, GFP-COG expressed normally with clearly detected band, while GFP-COG<sup>\*</sup> band was detected with lower protein level (Fig. 6B; Fig. S12B). Because the conserved COG3236 domain was fused to both C-terminus of DEK33 in plants and N-terminus of RibA in bacteria such as *Vibrio vulnificus* (Frelin et al., 2015), we subsequently detected the expression of fused protein COG-GFP and COG<sup>\*</sup>-GFP in *N. benthamiana* leaves. Interestingly, COG<sup>\*</sup>-GFP was not detectable, while COG-GFP expressed normally with clearly detected band (Fig. 6C; S12C). These results suggested that the COG3236 domain might be important for the protein stability of DEK33.

# DEK33 interacts with RGLG2 and SnRK1, which might have possible post-translational regulation to DEK33 stability

The Y2H screening assay was performed to identify proteins that interact with DEK33. After screening approximately  $6 \times 10^6$  recombinant cDNA clones, 40 putative DEK33-interacting proteins were identified (Table S3). Subsequently, six proteins with independent positive clones from the Y2H screening (Table S3), including RGLG2 and SnRK1, were further analyzed by Y2H (Fig. 7A-B) and LCI (Fig. 7C) assays. The result indicated that DEK33 has protein-protein interactions with all these proteins in yeast and *N. benthamiana*. Because the *dek33* mutation caused truncated DEK33 in maize, we therefore tested the interaction between truncated DEK33 and G1-G5. Interestingly, no interaction between truncated DEK33 and these proteins was observed in yeast (Fig. S13).

### DISCUSSION

In this study, we identified and characterized the maize classic kernel mutant *dek33*. *Dek33* encodes the pyrimidine reductase in riboflavin biosynthesis. A premature stop codon in the COG3236 domain produced a weak mutant allele with significant reduction of truncated DEK33 protein and riboflavin content, leading to abnormal oilbody formation, endoreduplication and ABA biosynthesis during seed development (Fig. 8).

# *dek33-ref* is a weak mutant allele that functions with reduced riboflavin content during seed development

Riboflavin is essential for the basic metabolism and numerous critical cellular

processes. Complete abolishment of the riboflavin pathway would produce pleiotrophic phenotypes and affect the survival of plants (Xiao et al., 2004). Although seven distinct enzymes are known to be involved in the riboflavin pathway in plants (Herz et al., 2000; Schramek et al., 2003; Fischer et al., 2004; Sandoval et al., 2008), only *Arabidopsis cos1* and *psh1* have been characterized (Xiao et al., 2004; Ouyang et al., 2010). To our knowledge, the characterization of *dek33* provided the first description of riboflavin deficiency during maize seed development. This will be useful to understand the essential role for the riboflavin biosynthesis in maize.

Compared with *dek1*, which creates severe effects on seed development (Becraft et al., 2002; Lid et al., 2002), *dek33* is a weak mutant allele that is responsible for *dek* phenotype with delayed seed development (Fig. 1). The homozygous *dek33-mu* allele displayed lethal phenotype (Hasnain et al., 2013), indicating that DEK33 is evidently required for seed development. In *dek33*, the G-to-A transition caused the presence of a premature stop codon, leading to lower protein level of truncated DEK33 (Fig. 2, 3). Therefore, it is possible that the lower protein level of truncated DEK33 in *dek33* still retains reductase activity, and thus riboflavin can still be synthesized, but with less content, to maintain seed development (Fig. 8).

During seed development, the embryonic development of *dek33* was severely affected and embryonic cells were less cytoplasmic dense with fewer oilbodies (Fig. 1). Maize embryo only represents 11% of the mass of the seed but accumulates 90% of the oil (Watson et al., 2003), and about 90% of the seed oil is accumulated in oilbodies (Murphy, 1993). Previous studies showed that riboflavin deficiency produces changes in fatty acid composition (Taniguchi and Nakamura, 1976; Taniguchi et al., 1978), and probably affects lipid metabolism by interfering with  $\beta$ -oxidation of fatty acids (Olpin and Bates, 1982). In  $\beta$ -oxidation, acyl-CoA dehydrogenases (ACADs) are FAD-dependent enzymes that catalyze the alpha, beta-dehydrogenation of acyl-CoA esters (Thorpe and Kim, 1995; Arent et al., 2008; Swigonova et al., 2009). Although the expression of some ACADs genes was altered (Fig. S14), however, we supposed that the dek33 mutation might not affect FAD-dependent enzymes directly. It is possible that reduced FAD content affects the FAD-dependent  $\beta$ -oxidation in *dek33*, leading to altered gene expression and significant reduced oilbodies, which might be one important reason for the defective embryo (Fig. 8).

Seed dormancy and germination are controlled by multiple phytohormones, with

ABA and gibberellins acting in opposing roles (McCarty, 1995; Kucera et al., 2005). After maturation, *dek33* kernels displayed occasionally viviparous phenotype (Fig. 1). Previous studies indicated that carotenogenesis and ABA biosynthesis are affected at various stages in several maize viviparous mutants (Robertson, 1955; Robertson et al., 1978; Neill et al., 1986). In plants, carotenoids provide not only the pigments, but also the precursors for plant hormone ABA synthesis (Bartley and Scolnik, 1995). In dek33, carotenoids and ABA content showed significant reduction (Fig. 5). Therefore, it is reasonable to propose that the *dek33* mutation impacts carotenogenesis and ABA biosynthesis, resulting in less ABA content, which might be responsible for viviparous embryo (Fig. 8). However, we observed that the expression of some FAD-dependent Vp genes was up-regulated in dek33 (Fig. 5). A previous study reported a similar up-regulation of Arabidopsis AAO3 gene, which encodes an enzyme active on abscisic aldehyde in ABA biosynthesis (Seo et al., 2000). The mutation of AAO3 gene resulted in complete loss of AO protein and activity, leading to reduced ABA content in rosettes. However, a more intense expression of AAO3 was observed in the mutants. We speculated that a feedback regulator mechanism whereby an initial decrease in carotenoids under low-riboflavin stress may result in an immediate speedup of transcriptional compensation.

### Possible post-translational regulators of DEK33 protein stability

Previous study indicated that the COG3236 domain is not required for DEK33 reductase activity (Hasnain et al., 2013). However, the maize *dek33* mutation in this domain resulted in defective kernels with delayed development (Fig. 1). In addition, the *Arabidopsis phs1* mutation in this domain also caused severe photooxidative damage (Ouyang et al., 2010), suggesting essential roles for the COG3236 domain in higher plants.

If the COG3236 domain is not required for DEK33 reductase activity, it is possible that this domain maintain the stability of DEK33. The speculation comes from two observations: loss of part COG3236 domain caused lower level of truncated DEK33 in *dek33* (Fig. 2), and less DEK33<sup>\*</sup>-FLAG that was transiently expressed in *N*. *benthamiana* leaves (Fig. 6). A previous study indicated that the enzymatic activity of this domain was an *N*-glycosidase to prevent accumulation of toxic, reactive intermediates during riboflavin biosynthesis (Frelin et al., 2015). Thus, it is possible that the accumulation of reactive intermediates may be expected to cause protein 19 turnover. However, the COG3236 family is diverse and probably encompasses functions unrelated to riboflavin (de Souza and Aravind, 2012). As a single protein fused to C-terminus of GFP, the expression of  $COG^*$  was not as stable as the full length COG domain (Fig. 6). Interestingly, as a single protein fused to N-terminal of GFP, the fused protein  $COG^*$ -GFP was not detectable (Fig. 6). These evidences led us to speculate that the COG3236 is also important for DEK33 stability besides its *N*-glycosidase activity.

Recent studies have indicated that ubiquitination and phosphorylation are involved in protein stability regulation (Choi et al., 2016; Piattoni et al., 2011; Tsai and Gazzarrini, 2012; Yang et al., 2018). In our study, Y2H and LCI assays demonstrated that DEK33 can interact with RGLG2 (Yin et al., 2007) and SnRK1 (Halford et al., 2003), suggesting possible post-translational regulation for DEK33 stability. Therefore, it is possible that the truncation of DEK33 resulted in the loss of interaction between DEK33 with RGLG2/SnRK1 (Fig. S13), which might be responsible for unstable truncated DEK33 in *dek33*. It is also plausible that the denaturation of the C-terminus might result in unstable truncated DEK33 directly, as the COG3236 domain was indicated as a possible stability structure, which is independent of association with RGLG2/SnRK1. A recent study of maize *o10* reported a similar observation (Yao et al., 2016). The G-to-A transition caused a premature stop codon, leading to reduced protein level in *o10* kernels. Further experimental evidences will be needed to determine which mechanism regulates the DEK33 protein stability during maize seed development.
