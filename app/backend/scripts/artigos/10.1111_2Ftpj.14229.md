# The HDA19 histone deacetylase complex is involved in the regulation of flowering time in a photoperiod-dependent manner

Received 4 August 2018; revised 8 December 2018; accepted 19 December 2018. \*For correspondence (e-mail hexinjian@nibs.ac.cn).

#### SUMMARY

Chromatin modifications are known to affect flowering time in plants, but little is known about how these modifications regulate flowering time in response to environmental signals like photoperiod. In *Arabidopsis thaliana*, HDC1, a conserved subunit of the RPD3-like histone deacetylase (HDAC) complex, was previously reported to regulate flowering time via the same mechanism as does the HDAC HDA6. Here, we demonstrate that HDC1, SNLs and MSI1 are shared subunits of the HDA6 and HDA19 HDAC complexes. While the late-flowering phenotype of the *hda6* mutant is independent of photoperiod, the *hda19, hdc1* and *snl2/3/4* mutants flower later than or at a similar time to the wild-type in long-day conditions but flower earlier than the wild-type in short-day conditions. Our genome-wide analyses indicate that the effect of *hdc1* on histone acetylation and transcription is comparable with that of *hda19* but is different from that of *hda6*. Especially, we demonstrate that the HDA19 complex directly regulates the expression of two flowering repressor genes related to the gibberellin signaling pathway. Thus, the study reveals a photoperiod-dependent role of the HDA19 HDAC complex in the regulation of flowering time.

Keywords: histone deacetylase complex, flowering time, photoperiod, HDC1, HDA19, Arabidopsis thaliana.

## INTRODUCTION

Flowering is the developmental transition from vegetative growth to reproductive growth during the plant life cycle. In Arabidopsis, the time of flowering is regulated by several pathways, including the autonomous, vernalization and photoperiod pathways (Simpson and Dean, 2002; Imaizumi and Kay, 2006; Amasino, 2010). The autonomous pathway senses endogenous signals rather than environmental cues; the vernalization pathway can accelerate flowering after the plants undergo a prolonged period of cold (e.g. winter). These two pathways are integrated and together repress the transcription of the central flowering repressor gene FLC (Michaels and Amasino, 2001; Putterill et al., 2004). The photoperiod pathway can sense day length; the core mechanism of this pathway is the circadian regulation of the degradation of CONSTANS (CO), a major flowering promoter (Valverde et al., 2004; Imaizumi et al., 2005). In long-day conditions (LD), the protein level of CO reaches its peak at dusk, and is then degraded throughout the night, facilitating the expression of floral integrator genes; in short-day conditions (SD), the protein level of CO is low and does not have major effects on floral integrator genes, resulting in delayed flowering (Samach *et al.*, 2000; Suarez-Lopez *et al.*, 2001; Yanovsky and Kay, 2002). Mutations in components of the photoperiod pathway result in delayed flowering in LD but do not affect flowering time in SD (Koornneef *et al.*, 1991; Song *et al.*, 2012). The convergence of the aforementioned three flowering pathways results in activation of the floral integrator genes, *FT*, *SOC1* and *LFY*. This activation then upregulates the expression of floral identity genes, ultimately stimulating flowering.

Histone modifications including methylation, acetylation, ubiquitination and phosphorylation are all known to be involved in regulating the expression of flowering regulatory genes in Arabidopsis (He and Amasino, 2005; Li *et al.*, 2007; He, 2012; Su *et al.*, 2017). Histone acetylation, which is correlated with transcriptional activation, is added by histone acetyltransferases and is removed by histone deacetylases (HDACs). In Arabidopsis, the conserved RPD3-like HDACs consist of 12 putative members (Pandey *et al.*, 2002; Hollender and Liu, 2008; Peng *et al.*, 2017), in which HDA5, HDA6, HDA9 and HDA19 have been well studied. HDA5 promotes flowering time by repressing the

expression of the flowering repressor genes FLC and MAF1 (Luo et al., 2015). HDA9 is involved in the regulation of both leaf senescence and flowering time (Kang et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2016). HDA9 delays flowering time by repressing the expression of AGL19 and thus promoting the expression of FT especially in SD (Kang et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2016). HDA6 is required for DNA methylation and heterochromatin silencing (Murfett et al., 2001; Aufsatz et al., 2002; Gu et al., 2011; Pontvianne et al., 2013; Yu et al., 2017). Moreover, HDA6 is involved in phytohormone signaling, circadian transcription, stress responses and the regulation of flowering time (Wu et al., 2008; Chen et al., 2010; Yu et al., 2011; Zhu et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2017). In the hda6 mutant, the FLC expression is de-repressed, and flowering time is delayed under both LD and SD; double-mutant analysis has shown that the delayed flowering time phenotype of the hda6 mutant can be restored to the wild-type level in plants that harbor both the hda6 and flc mutations (Wu et al., 2008; Yu et al., 2011). This finding clearly demonstrates that HDA6 represses the expression of FLC and thereby regulates flowering time. HDA19 functions either redundantly with, or independently of, HDA6 in various biological processes, including embryogenesis, germination, floral development, phytohormone signaling and stress responses (Tian et al., 2005; Zhou et al., 2005; Tanaka et al., 2008; Chen and Wu, 2010; Chen et al., 2010; Krogan et al., 2012). Whereas HDA6 is known to regulate flowering time via its repression of FLC expression, very little is known about the possible function of HDA19 in the regulation of flowering time.

In Arabidopsis, the SIN3-like proteins SNL1–SNL6, the RbAp48 homolog MSI1, and the Rxt3 homolog HDC1 have been shown to interact with HDA19 and/or HDA6 (Perrella et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2013; Mehdi et al., 2016). SNL3, also known as ATSIN3, participates in abscisic acid (ABA) responses by interacting with the APETALA2/EREBP-type transcription factor AtERF7 (Song et al., 2005). SNL1 and SNL2 regulate seed dormancy by decreasing histone acetylation of key genes involved in the ethylene and ABA signaling pathways (Wang et al., 2013). MSI1 is not only a subunit of the Arabidopsis RPD3 HDAC complex, but is also a subunit of the PRC2 histone H3K27 methyltransferase complex and the CAF1 chromatin assembly complex (Kaya et al., 2001; Kohler et al., 2003; Mehdi et al., 2016). Loss-of-function of MSI1 causes a late-flowering phenotype (Steinbach and Hennig, 2014). However, considering that MSI1 is known to be a component of at least three protein complexes, it is not yet known which complex is responsible for the role of MSI1 in the regulation of flowering time. HDC1 was shown to regulate flowering time, plant growth and ABA sensitivity, and the late-flowering phenotype of the hdc1 mutant was shown to be caused by de-repression of FLC (Perrella et al., 2013). Although both MSI1 and HDC1 are known to regulate flowering time, we do not know how these two subunits—both of which shared by the HDA6 and HDA19 HDAC complexes—are functionally coordinated to regulate flowering time.

Here, we demonstrate that HDA6 and HDA19 can independently interact with SIN3-like proteins (SNLs), with MSI1, and with HDC1, and thereby form RPD3-type HDAC complexes in Arabidopsis. While the hda6 mutant plants flower later than the wild-type plants in both LD and SD, the hdc1 mutant plants flower later than the wild-type in LD and flower earlier than the wild-type in SD. In LD, the late-flowering phenotype is dependent on FLC in the hda6 mutant but is independent of FLC in the hdc1 mutant. This observation contradicts a previous study that reported that the hdc1 mutant has increased FLC expression and thereby causes a late-flowering phenotype in LD (Perrella et al., 2013). Our results support a photoperiod-dependent role for HDC1 in the regulation of flowering time. HDC1 is a shared component of the HDA6 and HDA19 HDAC complexes, and its role in the photoperiod-dependent regulation of flowering time is shared by HDA19 and SNL2/3/4, but not by HDA6. Our study reveals that an epigenetic mechanism controls photoperiod-dependent flowering time, thus deepening our understanding of how chromatin modifications differentially regulate flowering time in response to photoperiodic signals.

# RESULTS

# HDC1 and HDA6 regulate flowering time via distinct mechanisms

In Arabidopsis, the HDAC HDA6 is known to repress the expression of FLC, a flowering repressor gene, thereby promoting flowering (Wu et al., 2008; Yu et al., 2011). HDC1 was reported to function as a subunit of the HDA6 HDAC complex and to regulate flowering time via the same mechanism as HDA6 (Perrella et al., 2013). To determine whether or not HDA6 and HDC1 regulate flowering time via the same mechanism, we examined the flowering time of wild-type, hda6 and hdc1 plants in LD (16 h light and 8 h dark), and found that the hda6 and hdc1 mutants have more rosette leaves and flower later than the wild-type (Figure 1a–c). To confirm the late-flowering phenotype of the hdc1 mutant, we transformed a pHDC1: HDC1-Flag construct into the hdc1 mutant for complementation testing. Transgenic hdc1 mutant plants that expressed the HDC1-Flag fusion transgene had the same flowering time as wild-type plants (Figure S1a–c), confirming that the hdc1 mutation causes the late-flowering phenotype.

To investigate the molecular mechanism of the late-flowering phenotype of the *hdc1* mutant, we used quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) to evaluate the diurnal expression levels of *FLC* and *FT* in the wild-type, *hda6* and *hdc1* mutant plants. During

the LD photoperiod, the expression of both *FLC* and *FT* in the wild-type is gradually increased during the day and then decreased during the night (Figure 1d). As expected, as compared with the wild-type, the *hda6* mutant had increased expression of *FLC* and decreased expression of

FT (Figure 1d). If HDC1 functions together with HDA6 in the regulation of flowering time, then we would expect that the hdc1 mutant should also have increased FLC expression and decreased FT expression. However, our qPCR results showed that FLC expression increased in the hda6 mutant but not in the hdc1 mutant (Figures 1d and S1). The result contradicts the previous study reporting that the hdc1 mutant has an increased level of FLC expression (Perrella et al., 2013). Although FLC expression was not increased in the hdc1 mutant, the expression of FT was decreased in these plants, and to an even greater extent than in the hda6 mutant (Figure 1d); this decreased level of FT expression in the hdc1 mutant is consistent with its late-flowering phenotype (Figure 1a–c).

To further determine whether FLC is required for the late-flowering phenotype of the hda6 and hdc1 mutants, we introduced an flc knockout mutation into the hda6 and hdc1 mutants by genetic crossing, and then evaluated the flowering phenotypes of the flc hda6 double-mutant and the flc hdc1 double-mutant. The flc mutation significantly rescues the late-flowering phenotype in the hda6 mutant but not in the hdc1 mutant (Figure 1e). The results demonstrate that the increased level of FLC expression is required for the late-flowering phenotype of the hda6 mutant but not for the phenotype of the hdc1 mutant, suggesting that HDC1 regulates flowering time via a mechanism that is distinct from that of HDA6.

To confirm our finding that HDC1 and HDA6 regulate flowering time via distinct mechanisms, we crossed the hda6 mutant with the hdc1 mutant and thereby generated hda6 hdc1 double-mutants. If HDC1 and HDA6 regulate flowering time via the same mechanism, then the flowering time of the hda6 hdc1 double-mutant should not be later than the flowering time of either the hda6 or hdc1 single-mutants. To the contrary, our results actually indicated that the hda6 hdc1 double-mutant flowered much later than either the hda6 or hdc1 single-mutants (Figure 1f–h), thus supporting our earlier finding that HDC1 promotes flowering via a mechanism distinct from that of HDA6. Given that the effect of hda6 hdc1 on flowering time was even stronger than the additive effect of hda6 and hdc1, HDA6 and HDC1 may act synergistically in the regulation of flowering time.

## HDC1 regulates flowering time in a photoperioddependent manner

To investigate whether or not the function of HDC1 in the regulation of flowering time is independent of photoperiod, we compared the effect of the hda6 and hdc1 mutations on flowering time in SD (8 h day and 16 h dark). Strikingly, the hda6 mutant flowers later than the wild-type, whereas the hdc1 mutant flowers earlier than the wild-type (Figure 2a,b). Combined with the late-flowering phenotype of the hdc1 mutant in LD (Figure 1a–c), these SD results indicated that HDC1, unlike HDA6, regulates flowering time in a photoperiod-dependent manner. Further, we grew the wild-type and hdc1 plants in SD for 10, 20, 30 and 40 days, and then transferred the plants to LD. As the number of days spent in the SD treatment increased, the flowering time became increasingly delayed in the wild-type, but is only slightly delayed or not delayed in the hdc1 mutant (Figure 2c). This result confirms the photoperiod-dependent role of HDC1 in the regulation of flowering time.

To explore the molecular mechanism of the early-flowering phenotype of the hdc1 mutant in SD, we performed qPCR to evaluate the diurnal expression of FLC and FT. In SD, the expression of FLC in the wild-type was slightly increased during the day and decreased during the night. The diurnal expression of FLC was markedly increased in the hda6 mutant but was unaltered in the hdc1 mutant (Figure 2d). In the wild-type, the expression of FT increased gradually throughout the day starting shortly after dawn and then decreased gradually during the night (Figure 2e); this FT expression pattern is consistent with previous reports (Valverde et al., 2004). FT expression increased to a much lesser extent in the hda6 mutant than in the wild-type after dawn (Figure 2e), which is consistent with the lateflowering phenotype of the hda6 mutant (Figure 2a). In the hdc1 mutant, FT expression during the day did not exceed the level of the wild-type, but was much higher than in the wild-type at night (Figure 2e). This finding suggests that the early-flowering phenotype of the hdc1 mutant may be caused by the persistently high expression of FT. To confirm whether the FT expression is required for the earlyflowering phenotype of the hdc1 mutant, we introduced an ft-11 mutation into the hdc1 mutant by crossing and then determined whether the ft-11 mutation affects the earlyflowering phenotype in the hdc1 mutant background. While the hdc1 mutant flowers much earlier than the wild-type, the ft-11 hdc1 double-mutant flowers not earlier than the wild-type (Figure 2f), strongly supporting the conclusion that the early-flowering phenotype of the hdc1 mutant in SD is dependent on FT expression.

These results establish that HDC1 regulates flowering time in a photoperiod-dependent manner. Thus, we predict that a photoperiod-dependent flowering-time regulator must affect how HDC1 regulates flowering time in LD versus SD. The transcription of FT is activated by CO, a core photoperiod-dependent flowering-time regulator (Onouchi et al., 2000). The CO protein is stable in light and is degraded in dark; the degradation of CO is responsible for the late-flowering phenotype in SD relative to LD (Valverde et al., 2004). ## HDA6 and HDA19 separately interact with HDC1, SNLs and MSI1 to form multi-subunit complexes

To investigate whether HDC1 interacts with any other proteins to regulate flowering time, we generated transgenic Arabidopsis plants carrying a native promoter-driven HDC1 transgene tagged by a Myc epitope (pHDC1:HDC1- Myc) and purified HDC1-Myc interacting proteins using anti-Myc antibody. Mass spectrometry analysis indicated that HDC1-Myc co-purified with HDA19, SIN3-like proteins (SNL1–SNL6) and MSI1 (Table 1). These proteins are homologs of the conserved subunits of the RPD3-containing HDAC complex (Hayakawa and Nakayama, 2011; Peng et al., 2017). To confirm these protein–protein interactions, we generated pHDA19:HDA19-Flag and pSNL3:SNL3-Myc transgenic plants and performed affinity purification in combination with mass spectrometry. We found that HDA19-Flag co-purified with HDC1, SNL1–SNL6 and MSI1, and also found that SNL3-Myc co-purified with HDA19, HDC1 and MSI1 (Table 1). To confirm these protein–protein interactions, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments, demonstrating that HDA19 interacts with HDC1 and SNL3, and indicating that HDC1 interacts with SNL3 (Figure 3a,b). These results suggest that HDC1, SNLs and MSI1 interact with HDA19, and thus form a multi-subunit HDAC complex. This conclusion is consistent with the previous study reporting the identification of the HDA19-containing HDAC complex in Arabidopsis (Mehdi et al., 2016).

Previous studies indicated that HDC1 and SNLs interact not only with HDA19 but also with HDA6 (Perrella et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2013). However, our affinity purification experiment indicated that HDC1 and SNL3 co-purified with HDA19 but not with HDA6 as determined by mass spectrometry (Table 1), suggesting that HDC1 and SNL3 interact primarily with HDA19 rather than with HDA6 in Arabidopsis. To examine whether HDA6 also forms a HDAC complex in vivo, we generated HDA6-Flag transgenic plants and identified HDA6 co-purified proteins by affinity purification in combination with mass spectrometry, indicating that HDC1, SNLs (SNL1, SNL2, SNL3 and SNL4) and MSI1 were identified among the HDA6 co-purified proteins (Table 1). Our co-IP experiment confirmed the interaction of HDA6 with HDC1 and SNL3 (Figure 3a). These results are consistent with the previous studies reporting that HDC1 and SNL3 can interact not only with HDA19 but also with HDA6 (Perrella et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2013; Mehdi et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2017).

# The histone deacetylase complex components HDC1, HDA19 and SNL proteins regulate flowering time in a photoperiod-dependent manner

Having demonstrated that HDC1 is a subunit of both the HDA6- and HDA19-containing HDAC complexes, and given that HDC1 was shown to regulate flowering time via a mechanism distinct from that of HDA6, we predicted that HDC1 may function in the HDA19-containing HDAC complex to regulate flowering time. Therefore, we investigated whether HDA19 and other subunits of the HDA19-containing HDAC complex are required for the function of HDC1 in the photoperiod-dependent regulation of flowering time. The flowering time of the *hda19* and *snl2/3/4* mutants was evaluated in both LD and SD. Given that the *hda19* mutant plants produce many secondary leaves at the seedling stage, it is hard to differentiate the primary rosette leaves from secondary leaves. Thus, we used days to bolting

rather than numbers of rosette leaves to assess the flowering time of the *hda19* mutant. While the *hda19* and *snl2/3/4* mutants flower either later than or at a similar time to the wild-type in LD (Figure 4a–c), they flower much earlier than the wild-type in SD (Figure 4d–f), demonstrating that, like HDC1, HDA19 and SNL2/3/4 also have photoperiod-dependent roles in the regulation of flowering time.

# Comparing the transcriptomes of the hdc1, hda19 and hda6 mutant plants grown in long-day conditions

To investigate how HDC1, HDA6 and HDA19 regulate gene transcription and flowering time in LD, we performed RNA deep-sequencing (RNA-seq) to compare the transcriptomes of wild-type, hdc1, hda6 and hda19 mutants grown in LD. Our RNA-seq analysis identified hundreds of genes that were either up- or downregulated in the mutants as compared with the wild-type (Figure 5a; Data S1). The differentially expressed genes in the hdc1 mutant are significantly overlapped with the differentially expressed genes in the hda19 mutant (P = 1.682E-138 for upregulated genes and P = 1.205E-249 for downregulated genes, hypergeometric test), and to a lesser extent in the hda6 mutant (P = 9.741E-44 for upregulated genes and P = 1.089E-58 for downregulated genes, hypergeometric test), which supports the inference that HDC1 is a shared subunit of the HDA19 and HDA6 HDAC complexes.

As shown by heat map (Figure 5b), the differentially expressed genes identified in the hdc1 mutant have a similar expression pattern between the hdc1 and hda19 mutants, but show clearly different patterns between the hdc1 and hda6 mutants (Figure 5b). Further, we examined whether the differentially expressed genes in the hdc1 mutant are differentially expressed in the hda19 and hda6 mutants by scatter plotting and correlation analysis, indicating that the differentially expressed genes in the hdc1 mutants are more correlated with the differentially expressed genes in the hda19 mutant (r = 0.719) than in the hda6 mutant (r = 0.484; Figures 5c and S5). These analyses highlight that the function of HDC1 in the regulation of gene expression is more closely related to HDA19 than HDA6. However, there are a number of differentially expressed genes that are not overlapped between the hdc1 and hda19 mutants (Figure 5a), which is consistent with the different developmental phenotypes between the two mutants. We predict that HDC1 and

HDA19 may also independently function in the regulation of gene transcription.

As shown by previous studies (Aufsatz *et al.*, 2002; Earley *et al.*, 2010; To *et al.*, 2011), silencing of transposable elements (TEs) and repetitive DNA sequences is released in the *hda6* mutant. Consistently, our RNA-seq experiment identified a large number of TEs that were significantly upregulated in the *hda6* mutant relative to the wild-type (Figure 5d; Data S2). However, most of these TEs were not significantly upregulated in the *hdc1* and *hda19* mutants (Figure 5d; Data S2). The results imply that, unlike HDA6, HDC1 and HDA19 are dispensable for TE silencing. Considering that HDC1 is a conserved subunit of the SIN3-like HDAC complex, we suggest that HDA6 functions in TE silencing independently of forming a SIN3-type HDAC complex.

# The effect of hdc1 and hda19 on transcription is accompanied by alteration of histone acetylation at the whole genome level

We have identified hundreds of genes that are differentially expressed in the hdc1 mutant compared with the wild-type (Figure 6a; Data S4). If the expression of a flowering-time-related gene is directly targeted by the HDA19 HDAC complex, both the expression and histone acetylation levels of the gene are supposed to be increased in the hdc1 and hda19 mutants. We carried out ChIP-seq to examine histone H3 acetylation at the whole-genome level. The ChIP-seq data indicated that the histone H3 acetylation levels of the upregulated genes identified in the hdc1 and hda19 mutants are significantly increased in both the hdc1 and hda19 mutants but not in the hda6 mutant (Figures 6c and S9; Data S5), which is consistent with the fact that the expression of many upregulated genes identified in the hdc1 mutant is also upregulated in the hda19 mutant but not in the hda6 mutant (Figure 6a,b). The results imply that HDC1 and HDA19 co-regulate the expression of many genes through histone deacetylation.

To confirm the function of HDC1 and HDA19 in histone deacetylation and transcriptional repression, we selected four genes that showed both increased H3 acetylation and increased expression in the hdc1 and hda19 mutants for validation (Data S4 and S5). The histone acetylation and expression levels of the genes were confirmed by ChIPqPCR and RT-qPCR experiments, respectively (Figure 6d,e). Further, we generated HDC1-Flag and HDA19-Flag transgenic plants to examine whether HDC1 and HDA19 are enriched in the four selected genes. Our ChIP-qPCR experiment indicated that both HDC1 and HDA19 were significantly enriched in the genes (Figure 6f). The results demonstrate that HDC1 and HDA19 are directly responsible for histone deacetylation and transcriptional repression at their common target genes.

# HDC1 and HDA19 indirectly regulate the expression of FT in short-day conditions

We have demonstrated that the expression of the core floral integrator gene FT is significantly increased in the hdc1 and hda19 mutants but not in the hda6 mutant in SD (Figures 2e and 4g). The increased expression of FT in the hdc1 and hda19 mutants provides a plausible explanation for the early-flowering phenotype of the mutants in SD. It is possible that, in SD, HDC1 and HDA19 may regulate flowering time by facilitating histone deacetylation of FT. However, although the expression of FT is increased in the hdc1 and hda19 mutants (Figure 4g), the H3 acetylation of FT is not significantly affected in the hdc1 and hda19 mutants as compared with the wild-type (Figure 6g), implying that the histone deacetylation activity of the HDA19 HDAC complex is not directly involved in the regulation of the FT expression, and thus implying that the FT is not a direct target of the HDA19 HDAC complex.

To investigate the cause of the early-flowering phenotype of the hdc1 mutant in SD, we examined whether the expression of known flowering-time-related genes is regulated in the hdc1 mutant as determined by our RNA-seq data. From the list of known flowering-time-related genes (Bouche et al., 2016), our RNA-seq analysis identified nine genes (FDP, FLC, GASA5, MAF5, PRR9, SNZ, SUS4, TFL1 and AGL14) that are differentially expressed in the hdc1 mutant [log2(fold change) > 1 or < 1; P < 0.01]. Considering the role of the HDA19 HDAC complex in histone deacetylation and transcriptional repression, we expected that the early-flowering phenotype of the hdc1 mutant in SD may be directly caused by histone hyperacetylation and increased expression of some positive flowering-time regulator genes. However, none of the positive floweringtime regulators was confirmed to be significantly increased in both the hdc1 and hda19 mutants as determined by our RNA-seq and RT-qPCR experiments (Figure S10; Data S6). Thus, the HDA19 HDAC complex may directly regulate the expression of a gene that has not yet been characterized as a flowering-time regulator in SD. It is necessary to identify which gene is directly responsible for the regulation of flowering time in the mutants of the HDA19 complex in future.

# DISCUSSION

RPD3 HDACs typically function in multi-subunit complexes in eukaryotes (Hayakawa and Nakayama, 2011). In Arabidopsis, the closely related RPD3 HDACs HDA6 and HDA19 were shown to interact with conserved subunits of RPD3 HDAC complexes (Mehdi *et al.*, 2016; Yu *et al.*, 2017). However, it is unknown the extent to which HDA6 and HDA19 function in HDAC complexes. It is also unknown how the conserved subunits of the HDAC complexes are shared by the HDA6 and HDA19 HDAC complexes and thereby regulate distinct biological processes. In this study, we demonstrate that HDC1, SNLs and MSI1 are shared subunits of the HDA6 and HDA19 HDAC complexes. Further, we carried out gel filtration assay to evaluate the extent to which HDA6 and HDA19 function in HDAC complexes. We demonstrate that, while HDA6 and HDA19 can function in SIN3-type HDAC complexes, a large portion of the HDA6 and HDA19 protein populations do not form the HDAC complexes. The result is consistent with our observation that a large number of genes are regulated in the *hda6* or *hda19* mutants but not in the *hdc1* mutant, confirming that HDA6 and HDA19 not only function in the SIN3-type HDAC complexes but also function independently of the complexes. Moreover, our gel filtration results also suggest that HDA19 HDAC complex is more stable in SD than in LD. In SD, the *hda19* mutant as well as the *hdc1* and *snl2/3/4* mutants show an early-flowering phenotype relative to the wild-type (Figures 2a–c and 4d–f), while the *hda6* mutant shows a late-flowering phenotype (Figure 2a,b). We propose that, in SD, the formation

of a stable HDA19 HDAC complex is likely to prevent HDC1 and SNL2/3/4 from forming a complex with HDA6 to mediate opposite roles in the regulation of flowering time.

In a previous study, HDC1 was reported to affect the expression of FLC (Perrella et al., 2013), suggesting that HDC1 regulates flowering time via the same mechanism as does HDA6. Our results indicated that, unlike the hda6 mutant that has a late-flowering phenotype in both LD and SD (Wu et al., 2008), the hdc1 mutant shows a late-flowering phenotype in LD but not in SD (Figures 1a–c and 2a–c). As previously reported (Wu et al., 2008; Yu et al., 2011), the late-flowering phenotype of the hda6 mutant was caused by increased expression of FLC, a key flowering repressor gene. Our RT-qPCR analysis indicated that, in LD, the expression of FLC was increased in the hda6 mutant but not in the hdc1 mutant (Figures 1d and S1d). Further, the hdc1 mutation causes a late-flowering phenotype, even in the flc mutant background in LD (Figure 1e), indicating that FLC is dispensable for the late-flowering phenotype of the hdc1 mutant. Thus, different from the previous report (Perrella et al., 2013), our present study clearly demonstrates that HDC1 and HDA6 regulate flowering time via separate mechanisms. HDC1 regulates flowering in a photoperiod-dependent manner, whereas HDA6 functions in the autonomous flowering regulation pathway. The expression of FLC is gradually reduced during vegetative growth and reaches the lowest level before flowering (Luo et al., 2013; Shen et al., 2014). Perrella et al. (2013) detected FLC expression when plants were about to bolt. A low expression level of FLC during detection may cause the error in their result.

We demonstrate that HDC1 and HDA19 are directly responsible for HDAC and transcriptional repression of GASA5 and GA2OX6, two flowering repressor genes in the gibberellin signaling pathway. Gibberellin is an important plant hormone that is required for promoting flowering (Conti, 2017). However, little is known about whether and how the gibberellin signaling pathway is related to chromatin modifications during flowering. Our study reveals how the HDA19 HDAC is involved in the regulation of flowering time through the gibberellin signaling pathway. As determined by our RNA-seq experiment, the expression of GASA5 and GA2OX6 increases in the hdc1 and hda19 mutants not only in LD but also in SD (Data S3 and S6). In LD, overexpression of GASA5 was shown to delay flowering by reducing FT expression (Zhang et al., 2009a). Thus, it is reasonable to observe that the hdc1 and hda19 mutants showed late flowering. The result supports the inference that overexpression of GASA5 is responsible for the late-flowering phenotype of the hdc1 and hda19 mutants in LD. In SD, however, the expression of FT already remains a very low level in the wild-type. Thus, the effect of the overexpression of GASA5 and GA2OX6 on the reduction of FT expression is negligible in the hdc1 and hda19 mutants. Moreover, the effect of hdc1 and hda19 on the expression of some other uncharacterized floweringtime regulator genes becomes dominant in SD, resulting in an early-flowering phenotype of the mutants.

SIN3-like proteins were previously shown to interact with the HDAC HDA19 in vivo (Wang et al., 2013). Our results demonstrate that SIN3-like proteins interact with both HDA19 and HDA6, indicating that these SIN3-like proteins are shared subunits of the HDA6 and HDA19 HDAC complexes. The SIN3-like proteins SNL1 and SNL2 have been reported to affect seed germination as well as ABA and auxin responses (Wang et al., 2013, 2016). However, it is unknown whether the SIN3-like proteins regulate flowering time. We generated a snl2/3/4 mutant by crossing, and demonstrated that the triple-mutant plants flower later than the wild-type in LD and flower earlier than the wildtype in SD (Figure 4a–f), indicating that like HDC1, SNL2/3/ 4 also have a photoperiod-dependent role in the regulation of flowering time. Given that the photoperiod-dependent role of HDC1 and SNL2/3/4 is not shared by HDA6, we predict that HDC1 and SNL2/3/4 may function in the HDA19 HDAC complex to regulate flowering time. Consistent with the prediction, the flowering time of the hda19 mutant is slightly delayed or not affected in LD, but is much earlier than that of the wild-type in SD (Figure 4a,c,d,f). These results strongly suggest that HDC1 and SNL2/3/4 function in the HDA19 HDAC complex to regulate flowering time in a photoperiod-dependent manner.

In Arabidopsis, MSI1–MSI5 are homologs of the retinoblastoma-associated proteins RbAp46 and RbAp48 (Hennig et al., 2005). A recent study indicated that heterozygous plants of the msi1-1 mutant and plants harboring a newly-reported msi1-5 allele both flower later than the wild-type in LD but not in SD; this study also reported that the photoperiod-dependent role of MSI1 is independent of FLC (Steinbach and Hennig, 2014). MSI1 is a subunit in several types of protein complexes, including the HDA6 and HDA19 HDAC complexes (Table 1), the CAF-1 nucleosome assembly complex, and the PRC2 histone methyltransferase complex (Kohler et al., 2003; Hennig et al., 2005; De Lucia et al., 2008; Mehdi et al., 2016). Our results indicated that, like MSI1, the HDAC complex components HDA19, HDC1 and SNL2/3/4 have a photoperioddependent role in the regulation of flowering time, suggesting that the photoperiod-dependent role of MSI1 is at least partially attributable to the HDA19 HDAC complex.

Although the photoperiod-dependent roles of HDC1, SNLs and MSI1 are attributable to the HDA19 complex but not the HDA6 complex, we cannot exclude the possibility that HDC1, SNLs and MSI1 function in the HDA6 complex to regulate other biological processes. HDA6, HDC1, SNLs and MSI1 were independently reported to regulate ABA signaling and stress responses by different groups (Chen et al., 2010; Perrella et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2013; Mehdi et al., 2016). In future, it will be interesting to clarify whether the involvement of these components in ABA signaling and stress responses is dependent on the formation of the HDA6 HDAC complex.

In conclusion, this study reveals the mechanism of how the HDA19 complex functions in maintaining vegetative growth in SD, and facilitates the transition from vegetative growth to flowering in LD (Figure S11). This mechanism ensures that plants maintain proper vegetative or reproductive growth in different seasons, thus enabling plants to survive and reproduce. Further studies will be required to precisely characterize how the HDA19 HDAC complex senses different photoperiods and then regulates flowering time in a photoperiod-dependent manner.

#### EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

#### Plant materials and growth conditions

In this study, the hda6 mutant (axe1-5, CS66153) was obtained from the Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Centre ([http://arabidopsis.](http://arabidopsis.info/) [info/](http://arabidopsis.info/)). The hdc1 (Salk\_043645), snl2 (Salk\_078781), snl3 (Salk\_020633), snl4 (Salk\_042565), hda19 (Salk\_139445), flc (Salk\_140021), maf4 (SAIL\_1213\_A08), ft-11 (GABI-290E08) and co-11 (SAIL\_24\_H04) mutants were obtained from the Arabidopsis Information Resource Center [\(http://www.arabidopsis.org/](http://www.arabidopsis.org/)). The mutant maf5 was generated by inserting a base in the exon of MAF5 genome with CRISPR-Cas9 system. All of the mutants were in the Columbia (Col) background. The primers used for genotyping of the mutants are listed in Data S7.

Full-length genomic sequences of HDA6, HDC1, SNL3, HDA19, as driven by their native promoters, were cloned into the pCAM-BIA1305 and/or pRI909 vectors. Flag- or Myc-tag was fused to the C-terminals of the genes in the pCAMBIA1305 vector and was fused to the N-terminals of the genes in the pRI909 vector. The primers used in the amplification of HDA6, HDC1, SNL3 and HDA19 are shown in Data S7. All the constructs were transformed into the Agrobacterium strain GV3101 and then introduced into Arabidopsis by the floral-dip method. The T1 seedlings were grown on MS medium supplemented with antibiotics. The F1 generation of two different transgenic plants acquired by crossing was used for co-IP. Arabidopsis seedlings were grown on MS medium at 23°C in LD (16 h light/8 h dark) or in SD (8 h light/16 h dark). The seedlings were transplanted into soil and grown in the same conditions for examination of flowering time. For gibberellic acid (GA3) treatment, 5-day-old seedlings were transplanted into soil and were sprayed with 100 l<sup>M</sup> GA3 twice a week until flowering (Zhang et al., 2009a).

## Affinity purification, mass spectrometry analysis and coimmunoprecipitation

For affinity purification, 2 g flowers from 1-month-old plants and 2 g seedlings from 2-week-old plants were collected for isolating protein extracts. Protein extraction was performed according to a previously described method (Zhang et al., 2012) and extracts were subjected to affinity purification. For HDA6/HDA19-Flag affinity purification, anti-Flag M2 Affinity Gel (A2220; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to the protein extract and incubated in lysis buffer at 4°C for 2 h. The agarose-bound proteins were precipitated by centrifugation and washed four times, followed by an elution with the Flag peptide (F4799; Sigma-Aldrich), whereas for the HDC1/SNL3-Myc affinity purification, anti-c-Myc Agarose Affinity Gel (A7470; Sigma-Aldrich) was used for incubation and 0.1 <sup>M</sup> ammonium hydroxide at pH 11–12 was used for elution. The eluted proteins were analyzed via mass spectrometry, as described previously (Ning et al., 2015). For co-IP, 0.5 g samples of 2-week-old progeny seedlings (acquired by crossing Flagand Myc-tagged transgenic plants) were used for isolating protein extracts. The method used here was the same as in the aforementioned affinity purification. The eluted proteins were boiled in 1 9 sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer and separated on an SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gel for Western blotting with anti-Flag (M20008L, Abmart, Arlington, MA, USA) and anti-Myc antibodies (M20002L, Abmart).

#### Gel filtration

HDA6/HDC1/SNL3/HDA19-Myc transgenic plants were used for gel filtration, and 0.5 g flower samples from 1-month-old plants were collected for isolating protein extracts. Aliquots (0.3 ml) of the protein extracts were loaded onto a Superose 6 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare, 17–5172–01) and fractions were collected (500 ll each). The fractions were separated on a 10% SDS–PAGE gel and subjected to Western blotting.

#### RNA deep sequencing and data analysis

For genome-wide expression analysis, total RNA was isolated with TRIzol reagent (15596018, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) from 3 g samples of 2-week-old seedlings at ZT16 in LD and 18-day-old seedlings at ZT24 in SD. The sequencing libraries were built by Vazyme Biotech, and sequenced with an Illumina HiSeqXTen instrument using a paired-end scheme (2 9 150 bp). After the adaptor sequences were removed, reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis genome (TAIR10) using the TopHat v2.0.6 program (Trapnell et al., 2009). Genes with at least a twofold change in expression combined with Fisher's exact test (P < 0.01) were considered to be differentially expressed. The Gplots package in R was used to generate a heat map of the differentially expressed genes. The raw RNA deep sequencing data were deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession number [GSE124499\)](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/GSE124499).

## Analyses of RNA transcripts by quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA from 2-week-old seedlings in LD or 18-day-old seedlings in SD grown on MS medium plates was isolated with the TRIzol reagent. The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using 5 9 All-In-One RT MasterMix (with an AccuRT Genomic DNA Removal Kit) (G492; ABM, Richmond, BC, Canada). qPCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System and Kapa SYBR Fast qPCR master mix (KM4101; Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA) was used. The expression of each gene that was tested in this study was normalized to the expression of ACT2. The primers that were used for the qPCR are listed in Data S7.

#### Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed as previously described (Zhang et al., 2009b) with minor modifications. Three gram samples of 2-week-old seedlings in LD or 18-day-old seedlings in SD grown on MS medium plates were subjected to cross-linking in 1% formaldehyde under vacuum conditions. The histone H3 acetylation levels were detected by anti-acetylated H3 antibody (06-599; Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), and immunoprecipitated DNA was analyzed by qPCR or deep sequencing.

For analyses of HDC1 and HDA19 on chromatin, HDC1-Flag and HDA19-Flag transgenic plants were generated, and the enrichment of HDC1 and HDA19 on chromatin was examined by anti-Flag antibody (F1804; Sigma-Aldrich). In the ChIP-qPCR experiment, the enrichment of H3Ac, HDC1 and HDA19 on genes that were tested in this study was normalized to the enrichment on the ACT2 locus. The primers that were used for the qPCR are listed in Data S7. For the H3Ac ChIP-seq analysis, uniquely matched reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis genome (TAIR10). The H3Ac levels on genes and TEs were represented by RPKM (reads per kilobase per million). The raw H3Ac ChIP-seq data were deposited in the GEO database (accession number [GSE124499](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/GSE124499)).
