# In Brief

Stomata, small valves on the plant epidermis, are made of two guard cells surrounding a pore. Han et al. show that the transcription factor MUTE orchestrates gene regulatory circuits to switch cells to a differentiation state, then ensures that only a single symmetric division occurs to create a functional stoma.

#### SUMMARY

Precise cell division control is critical for developmental patterning. For the differentiation of a functional stoma, a cellular valve for efficient gas exchange, the single symmetric division of an immediate precursor is absolutely essential. Yet, the mechanism governing this event remains unclear. Here we report comprehensive inventories of gene expression by the Arabidopsis bHLH protein MUTE, a potent inducer of stomatal differentiation. MUTE switches the gene expression program initiated by SPEECHLESS. MUTE directly induces a suite of cell-cycle genes, including CYCD5;1, in which introduced expression triggers the symmetric divisions of arrested precursor cells in mute, and their transcriptional repressors, FAMA and FOUR LIPS. The regulatory network initiated by MUTE represents an incoherent type 1 feed-forward loop. Our mathematical modeling and experimental perturbations support a notion that MUTE orchestrates a transcriptional cascade leading to a tightly restricted pulse of cell-cycle gene expression, thereby ensuring the single cell division to create functional stomata.

#### INTRODUCTION

Mirroring the physiological importance of stomatal movement for plant photosynthetic growth, survival, and fitness, a stoma in nearly all land plant species is constituted with a pore surrounded by paired guard cells (GCs), which, upon sensing environmental cues, undergo regulated opening and closure [\(Scarth,](#page-13-0) [1932\)](#page-13-0). The physiological and evolutionary constraints for forming functional stomata have likely led to a mechanism that strictly enforces a single symmetric cell division to generate stomata with mirror-symmetric, paired GCs [\(Chater et al., 2017; Peterson](#page-12-0) [et al., 2010\)](#page-12-0). However, the underlying mechanism enabling the only one division event remains unclear.

Stomatal differentiation occurs through stereotypical cell division and differentiation events. In *Arabidopsis*, an asymmetric entry division of a protodermal cell produces a meristemoid, a self-renewing stomatal precursor, which reiterates asymmetric amplifying divisions but eventually differentiates into a guard mother cell (GMC). Stomatal differentiation completes following the single symmetric cell division (SCD) of the GMC [\(Han and](#page-12-1) [Torii, 2016](#page-12-1)). Three basic-helix-loop-helix (bHLH) proteins, SPEECHLESS (SPCH), MUTE, and FAMA, drive the sequential steps of stomatal differentiation. They are expressed transiently in a respective developmental window, and their loss-of-function mutations result in a failure to initiate stomatal cell lineages (*spch*), arrested meristemoids (*mute*), and multinumeral GMClike tumors (*fama*), respectively [\(MacAlister et al., 2007; Oha](#page-13-1)[shi-Ito and Bergmann, 2006; Pillitteri et al., 2007\)](#page-13-1). SPCH, MUTE, and FAMA form a heterodimer with bHLH proteins, SCREAM (SCRM, also known as ICE1) and SCRM2 ([Kanaoka](#page-12-2) [et al., 2008\)](#page-12-2). In addition, Myb genes *FOUR LIPS* (*FLP*) and *Myb88* restrict GMC-divisions, and *flp myb88* mutants exhibit multinumeral GCs ([Lai et al., 2005\)](#page-12-3).

Extrinsic cell-cell signaling enforces proper stomatal patterning through inhibition of these stomatal bHLH proteins. A small secreted peptide EPIDERMAL PATTERNING FACTOR2 (EPF2) is perceived by the receptor kinase ERECTA and its partner TOO MANY MOUTHS (TMM), which in turn inhibits SPCH [\(Hara et al., 2009; Horst et al., 2015; Hunt and Gray,](#page-12-4) [2009; Lee et al., 2012](#page-12-4)). SPCH and SCRM directly induce *EPF2* and *TMM* expression, thereby constituting a negative feedback loop [\(Horst et al., 2015; Lau et al., 2014\)](#page-12-5). During the meristemoid-to-GMC transition, EPF1 perceived by ERECTA-LIKE1 (ERL1) enforces stomatal spacing [\(Hara et al., 2007; Lee](#page-12-6) [et al., 2012\)](#page-12-6). Here, MUTE directly induces *ERL1*, which mediates an autocrine signaling for proper GMC differentiation ([Qi](#page-13-2) [et al., 2017](#page-13-2)).

Plants possess a number of cell-cycle genes [\(De Veylder et al.,](#page-12-7) [2007\)](#page-12-7). Among them, D-type cyclins (CYCDs) associate with

<span id="page-2-0"></span>*(legend on next page)*

CYCLIN-DEPENDENT KINASE A1; 1 (CDKA1;1) to drive G1/ S-phase transition, whereas A-type cyclins (CYCAs) complex with CDKA for S/G2 transition as well as with CDKB1;1 to suppress endoreduplication ([Boudolf et al., 2009; De Veylder](#page-12-8) [et al., 2007](#page-12-8)). Specific roles of core-cell cycle regulators have been associated with stomatal development. For example, CYCD4 is involved in stomatal-lineage divisions in the hypocotyls [\(Kono et al., 2007\)](#page-12-9). Dominant-negative forms of CDKB1;1 and CDKA1;1, and higher-order mutants of CYCAs, inhibit GMC division ([Boudolf et al., 2004; Yang et al., 2014;](#page-12-10) [Vanneste et al., 2011](#page-12-10)). It was reported that FLP directly represses *CDKB1;1* and *CDKA1;1* ([Xie et al., 2010; Yang et al., 2014\)](#page-13-3), and FAMA binds to the promoter region of *CDKB1;1* ([Hachez](#page-12-11) [et al., 2011\)](#page-12-11). Although these studies demonstrated the repressive roles for FAMA and FLP in the GMC division, it is not clear how the single symmetric division event is initiated and ensured.

Among the stomatal transcription factors, *MUTE* is the only one whose overexpression confers constitutive differentiation of stomata [\(Pillitteri et al., 2007, 2008; Trivino et al., 2013\)](#page-13-4). Overexpression of *SPCH* and *FAMA* confers highly divided stomatal-lineage cells and singular GCs, respectively [\(MacAlister](#page-13-1) [et al., 2007; Ohashi-Ito and Bergmann, 2006\)](#page-13-1). We thus hypothesized that MUTE, as a positive regulator of stomatal differentiation, activates gene regulatory networks to create stomata. To test this, we performed a genome-wide profiling of early MUTE-responsive genes. Comparison of SPCH and MUTE target genes revealed how MUTE disconnects stomatal-lineage cells from extrinsic inhibitory signals, thus ''locks in'' the differentiation program. Further, our study revealed that MUTE directly binds to the promoters and upregulates cell-cycle regulators of the GMC symmetric division event. At the same time, MUTE directly binds to the promoters and upregulates *FAMA* and *FLP*, which repress the cell-cycle regulators. Our mathematical modeling predictions and experimental perturbations demonstrate that an incoherent feed-forward loop mediated by MUTE, cell-cycle regulators, and FAMA/FLP defines a core regulatory circuit ensuring the single symmetric division. Our study establishes the role of MUTE in orchestrating a transcriptional cascade leading to stomatal differentiation and defines a core regulatory circuit for the single symmetric division.

#### RESULTS

#### MUTE Induces and Represses Specific Sets of Transcriptomes

To obtain the most complete inventories of transcriptional changes driven by MUTE, we employed an estradiol-inducible *MUTE* overexpressor (*iMUTE*) line, which, upon estradiol treatment, triggered a rapid, >700-fold increase in *MUTE* transcripts within 2 hr, reaching to 2000-fold increase in 8 hr (Figure S1A). The seedlings eventually differentiated epidermis solely composed of stomata, in which 86.4% (538/623) of the GCs expressed the mature GC marker E994 [\(Figures 1A](#page-2-0)–1D). GC differentiation is governed by the sister gene *FAMA* ([Hachez et al., 2011](#page-12-11)). To identify the direct MUTE targets and not those governed by FAMA, we examined the transcriptional changes 8 hr after induction, before *FAMA* expression peaks (see Figure S3). We performed paired-end sequencing of RNA from *iMUTE* and un-induced controls (see [STAR Methods](#page-14-0) and Table S1). The three biological replicates showed high reproducibility, with the Pearson's correlations between log RPKM (reads per kilobase exon per million) > 0.980 among samples of the same condition (Figure S1B).

Four-hundred and seventy-two *iMUTE* upregulated genes and 818 downregulated genes were extracted after q value = 0.05 cutoff and log2 fold change (FC) > 0.5 ([Figure 1](#page-2-0)F, Table S1). The Gene Ontology (GO) categories for the *iMUTE* upregulated genes are overwhelmingly in the cell cycle, cell division, and mitosis (CC + CD + Mitotic) categories. Highly enriched categories also include stomatal complex morphogenesis and GC differentiation ([Figures 1](#page-2-0)G and S2; Table S2). The GO enriched categories for *iMUTE* downregulated genes include auxin-related [\(Figures 1](#page-2-0)F and S2; Table S2). A comparison of *iMUTE* differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with published fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) stomatal-lineage transcriptomes ([Adrian et al., 2015](#page-12-12)) showed that 21% and 15% of *iMUTE* up- and downregulated genes, respectively, overlap with FACS\_MUTE up- and downregulated genes [\(Figures 1](#page-2-0)F and S1E). Low reproducibility among the FACS-sorted samples (Figure S1D), however, limited the reliability of comparisons (see [STAR](#page-14-0) [Methods\)](#page-14-0).

#### MUTE Acts as a Transcriptional Switch for the Stomatal Patterning Ligand-Receptor Systems

The closest paralog of *MUTE*, *SPCH* drives the initial entry into stomatal cell lineages, and its induced overexpression (*iSPCH*) results in epidermis with excessive asymmetric divisions ([MacAlister et al., 2007; Pillitteri et al., 2007\)](#page-13-1) ([Figure 1](#page-2-0)E). To understand the extent of their shared and specific functions, we next compared their target genes. An induced SPCH (*iSPCH*) RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was performed essentially at the same condition (8 hr of induction using 4-day-old seedlings) ([Lau et al., 2014](#page-12-13)), thus we re-analyzed the published *iSPCH* data for direct comparison. Twenty-four percent (113/472) of *iMUTE* upregulated genes are shared with *iSPCH* [\(Figures 1](#page-2-0)F and 1H; Table S1). To further correlate their shared transcriptional response to physical genome-wide SPCH binding locations ([Lau](#page-12-13) [et al., 2014](#page-12-13)), we analyzed the extent by which the promoters of the co-regulated genes are occupied by SPCH. Fifty-five percent (62/113) of SPCH and MUTE co-upregulated genes are SPCH bound, whereas only 22% (87/392) of SPCH-specific upregulated genes are SPCH bound ([Figures 1F](#page-2-0) and 1H; Table S1). Thus, extracting the transcriptional response shared by SPCH and MUTE highly enriches the selected set of SPCH targets.

The highest enriched GO-category for *iSPCH* and *iMUTE* coupregulated genes is stomatal complex development, where 74% (14/19) of genes are SPCH bound [\(Figures 1G](#page-2-0), 1H and S2, Table S3). All eight *iMUTE*-up/*iSPCH*-up/SPCH-bound genes are known players of stomatal development: *TMM*, *ERL1*, *ERL2*, *BASL*, *POLAR*, *POLAR-LIKE*, *SCREAM* (*SCRM*), and *HDG2* ([Figures 1G](#page-2-0)–1I and Table S3). *SCRM2* was also coupregulated (log2 = 0.43, qVal = 3.94E-14) ([Figure 1](#page-2-0)I, Table S1). A subsequent qRT-PCR analysis confirmed their induction (Figure S3). On the other hand, *CARBONIC ANHYDRASE1* (*CA1*), which mediates inhibition of stomatal development at elevated CO2 levels ([Engineer et al., 2014\)](#page-12-14) and *STOMAGEN* were repressed by both *iSPCH* and *iMUTE* [\(Figure 1](#page-2-0)I, Table S1).

To rule out the possibility that *iMUTE* causes a non-specific, promiscuous induction of SPCH targets, we further tested whether the promoters of these genes are indeed occupied by the functional MUTE protein expressed transiently during the meristemoids-to-GMC transition. For this purpose, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed using *Arabidopsis mute* plants complemented by the functional MUTE-GFP protein driven by its own promoter (*proMUTE::MUTE-GFP*) using the *scrm-D*-enabled background [\(Horst et al., 2015; Qi et al.,](#page-12-5) [2017](#page-12-5)). Indeed, direct associations of MUTE with the promoters of *TMM*, *SCRM*, as well as *BASL* and *POLAR* were detected, indicating that they are the *bona fide*, direct MUTE targets [\(Fig](#page-2-0)[ures 1J](#page-2-0) and S4). Strong MUTE-GFP association was detected within the location of known SPCH binding sites ([Figures 1](#page-2-0)K and S4; Table S3).

*EPF2* is a known direct target of SPCH ([Horst et al., 2015; Lau](#page-12-5) [et al., 2014](#page-12-5)). Although *iSPCH* triggered >30-fold increase in *EPF2* expression, *iMUTE* directly repressed *EPF2* (0.47, qVal = 1.50E-02) [\(Figures 1](#page-2-0)I and S3). ChIP assays detected direct MUTE binding to the SPCH binding site within the *EPF2* promoter ([Figures 1J](#page-2-0), 1K, and S4). On the other hand, both *iSPCH* and *iMUTE* directly upregulate the receptors *ERLs* and *TMM* ([Figures 1](#page-2-0)I–1K, S3, and S4) ([Horst et al., 2015; Lau et al., 2014;](#page-12-5) [Qi et al., 2017\)](#page-12-5). It is worth noting that EPF1, the sister peptide of EPF2, is perceived by ERL1 and TMM to regulate MUTE activity during GMC differentiation ([Qi et al., 2017](#page-13-2)). Together, our findings suggest that MUTE acts as a transcriptional switch for the stomatal patterning ligand-receptor system, eliminating the earlier signal EPF2 induced by SPCH while maintaining the expression of shared receptors to perceive the later signal, EPF1 ([Figure 1](#page-2-0)L).

#### MUTE Directly Upregulates Cell-Cycle Genes Driving the Symmetric Division of Stomata

MUTE terminates asymmetric division of meristemoids [\(Pillitteri](#page-13-4) [et al., 2007](#page-13-4)). Unexpectedly, the most significantly *iMUTE*-upregulated genes belong to the combined GO categories CC + CD + Mitotic, suggesting that MUTE is a potent inducer of cell division ([Figures 1](#page-2-0)F, 1G, and S2; Table S2). Indeed, 46 genes belonging to the CC + CD + Mitotic categories are up by *iMUTE*, whereas only two out of six genes are specifically up by *iSPCH* [\(Figures 2](#page-5-0)A and 2B, Table S3). Among them, *iMUTE* strongly induced B-type cyclin-dependent kinase genes, *CDKB1;1* and *CDKB2;1*, and A-type cyclins, most notably *CYCA1;1*, *CYCA2;2*, and *CYCA2;3* ([Figures 2](#page-5-0)B and S5). A subsequent ChIP analysis demonstrated that functional MUTE protein associates with the promoter regions of these cell-cycle regulator genes, indicating that they are direct MUTE targets [\(Figures](#page-5-0) [2](#page-5-0)C, 2D, and S4). qRT-PCR time course analyses confirmed the increase of *CDKB1;1*, *CYCA2;2*, and *CYCA2;3* transcript levels 4 hr after *iMUTE* induction (Figure S5). CDKB1;1 and CYCA2s are known to promote the GMC symmetric division [\(Boudolf](#page-12-10) [et al., 2004; Xie et al., 2010\)](#page-12-10) and, consistently, they are enriched in *MUTE* expressing transcriptome [\(Adrian et al., 2015\)](#page-12-12) [\(Fig](#page-5-0)[ure 2B](#page-5-0)). Our results suggest that MUTE promotes the SCD of GMCs through direct upregulation of *CDKB1;1* and *CYCA2s*.

#### CYCD5;1, a Direct MUTE Target, Accumulates Transiently Prior to the GMC Symmetric Division

A previous stomatal-lineage transcriptome study reported *CYCD7;1* as a GMC-specific D-type cyclin [\(Adrian et al., 2015](#page-12-12)). However, our RNA-seq and time course transcript analyses showed that *MUTE* induction has no effects on the expression of *CYCD7;1* (log2 FC = 0.07, qVal = 1.00E+00; [Figures 2B](#page-5-0) and S5; Table S1). Therefore, MUTE does not activate *CYCD7;1* expression. In contrast, *iMUTE* strongly induces *CYCD5;1*, which has not been associated with stomatal development previously [\(Figure 2](#page-5-0)B). The functional MUTE protein binds to the *CYCD5;1* promoter [\(Figures 2](#page-5-0)C and 2D), and *iMUTE* rapidly induces *CYCD5;1* transcripts [\(Figure 2E](#page-5-0)), demonstrating that *CYCD5;1* is a MUTE target. Consistently, *CYCD5;1* transcript levels were substantially reduced in the *mute* mutant background [\(Figures 2](#page-5-0)F and S4).

To understand the role of *CYCD5;1* in stomatal development, we generated *Arabidopsis* plants expressing CYCD5;1-GFP driven by its own promoter (*CYCD5;1pro::CYCD5;1-GFP*). A strong signal was detected within the nuclei of a GMC [\(Figures](#page-5-0) [2](#page-5-0)G and 2I). Consistently, in addition to the transcript levels, *iMUTE* increased the cells accumulating CYCD5;1-GFP [\(Fig](#page-5-0)[ure 2](#page-5-0)H). The *CYCD5;1* promoter possesses five E-boxes, three of which are situated where the *in vivo* MUTE binding is detected [\(Figures 2C](#page-5-0) and 2D; amplicon a). CYCD5;1-GFP accumulates in the nucleus of a meristemoid within 3–4 hr (3.3 ± 1.4 hr, n = 25) after the last asymmetric division, reaches maximum 10 hr (10.6 ± 4.1 hr, n = 26), and disappears 8 hr (7.9 ± 1.1 hr, n = 28) prior to the symmetric division [\(Figure 3](#page-6-0)A and Video S1). #### CYCD5;1 Is Sufficient to Trigger Symmetric Division-like Divisions of Arrested Mute Meristemoids

To address whether *CYCD5;1* expressed in the meristemoids is sufficient for the symmetric division in the absence of *MUTE*, we

<span id="page-6-0"></span>expressed *CYCD5;1* driven by the *MUTE* promoter into *mute* null mutant background ([Figures 3](#page-6-0)D–3I). The *mute* meristemoids undergo several rounds of spiral asymmetric divisions and arrest ([Pillitteri et al., 2007](#page-13-4)) ([Figures 3](#page-6-0)D and 3E). Strikingly, *MUTEpro:: CYCD5;1* triggered aberrant division of arrested *mute* meristemoid [\(Figures 3](#page-6-0)F–3J). These aberrant divisions are in parallel or perpendicular orientations [\(Figures 3](#page-6-0)G–3I, pink arrowheads) resembling the GMC tumors seen in *fama* [\(Ohashi-Ito and](#page-13-5) [Bergmann, 2006](#page-13-5)). Of the arrested meristemoids from 10 independent lines of *MUTEpro::CYCD5;1* in *mute*, 55.6% (168/302) exhibited the SCD-like divisions, whereas none (0/80) of the *mute* meristemoids did [\(Figure 3](#page-6-0)J). Therefore, the expression of *MUTEpro::CYCD5;1* alone could drive SCD-like divisions of arrested *mute* meristemoids. None of these aberrantly divided tumors differentiated into stomata, consistent with the notion that other MUTE target genes are necessary for GC differentiation programs.

### FAMA and FLP, Which Restrict the Symmetric Division, Are Also Direct MUTE Targets

FAMA and FLP restrict the SCD of a GMC through direct binding to the promoters of *CDKB1;1* ([Hachez et al., 2011; Xie et al.,](#page-12-11) [2010](#page-12-11)). In addition, FLP directly suppresses *CDKA1;1* expression [\(Xie et al.,](#page-13-3) [2010; Yang et al., 2014\)](#page-13-3). It remains unknown, however, what triggers their expressions. To address this question, we

investigated the regulatory relationships of MUTE with FLP and FAMA. Our RNA-seq analysis identified both *FAMA* and *FLP* as *iMUTE*-specific upregulated genes, not upregulated by *iSPCH* [\(Figure 1I](#page-2-0) and Table S1). Both *FAMA* and *FLP* expressions are induced at around 8 hr after the *iMUTE* induction [\(Fig](#page-7-0)[ures 4](#page-7-0)A and S3), slightly delayed from early stomatal genes and cell-cycle genes (Figures S3 and S5). The *FAMA-GFP* signal was not detected in the *mute* epidermis ([Figure 4](#page-7-0)B). On the other hand, the previously reported *FLP* reporter, *FLPpro::GUS-GFP* [\(Lai et al., 2005\)](#page-12-3), exhibited a basal expression throughout leaf epidermis with stronger signals in GMCs [\(Figure 4](#page-7-0)B). The basal *FLP* reporter signal persisted in arrested *mute* meristemoids [\(Figure 4](#page-7-0)B). Consistently, in *mute*, *FAMA* transcripts are at a detection limit, whereas *FLP* transcripts were substantially reduced yet detectable ([Figures 4](#page-7-0)C and S4). Thus, MUTE is absolutely required for *FAMA* expression, while MUTE boosts *FLP* expression during the GMC transition.

#### Incoherent Type 1 Feed-Forward Loop Ensuring the Single SCD

Our study revealed that MUTE directly upregulates both cellcycle regulators that positively drive SCD and transcription factors that negatively regulate SCD via direct repression of the cell-cycle genes. In the field of network dynamics and behaviors, the MUTE-driven transcriptional network motif represents a typical incoherent type I feed-forward loop (I1-FFL) constituted by the three nodes, X, Y, and Z [\(Mangan and Alon, 2003\)](#page-13-6). Here, MUTE (X) is situated at the top of the network, which activates both *FAMA*/*FLP* (Y) as well as *CDKs/CYCs* (Z), whereas

Mathematical modeling of MUTE, FAMA/FLP, and CDKs/ CYCs faithfully reproduced the generation of a single pulse of CDKs/CYCs observed *in vivo* [\(Figure 5](#page-8-0)B). We aimed for a minimal component modeling, which is qualitatively equivalent to three-component I1-FFL and provides qualitative, not quantitative, properties of the gene regulatory network. All the interactions are implemented by Hill kinetics as described in the original model with modifications ([Mangan and Alon,](#page-13-6) [2003\)](#page-13-6). Details of the model are described in Supplemental Information.

To address the importance of the I1-FFL, we sought to break up the I1-FFL *in silico* and experimentally verify the stomatal phenotype as an outcome. If a node Y turns on simultaneously as X, the modeling predicts the output peak Z would decline to a sub-threshold level ([Figure 5](#page-8-0)C). Here, the amplitude difference of CDKs/CYCs can be modified by one parameter, which corresponds to the strength of FAMA/FLP induction under the *MUTE* promoter. To test this prediction experimentally, *FAMA* as well as *FLP* were expressed precociously during the meristemoid-to-GMC transition driven by the *MUTE* promoter [\(Figures 5](#page-8-0)E–5G). #### Saturation and Noise in Ectopic iMUTE Could Flip the Outcome of I1-FFL

Our mathematical and experimental analyses revealed that regulatory motif controlled by MUTE enables a fast response time and transient upregulation of cell-cycle regulator gene expression. Previous studies reported that *MUTE* overexpression confers constitutive stomatal differentiation in the cotyledon/leaf epidermis [\(MacAlister et al., 2007; Pillitteri et al.,](#page-13-1) [2007; Trivino et al., 2013](#page-13-1)). However, through characterization of our model parameters, we found that MUTE has to regulate *FAMA/FLP* much more tightly to ensure the single SCD under *iMUTE* overexpression [\(Figures 6A](#page-10-0) and 6B). Here, sustained *iMUTE in silico* limits the possible range of strong CDKs/CYCs activation by MUTE, since strong direct activation leads to CDKs/CYCs level reaching steady-state high expression under prolonged *MUTE* expression, corresponding to sustained CDKs/CYCs activation. On the other hand, *iMUTE* overexpression causes stronger activation of *FAMA/FLP*. In the parameter sets we employed, this could lead to a faster decline of CDKs/CYCs, diminishing the peak below a threshold to trigger the SCD. Taking into account the modeling results that predict the dysregulation of the MUTEorchestrated I1-FFL, we sought to revisit the *MUTE* overexpression phenotype.

Indeed, careful observations of *iMUTE* epidermis revealed that, within the sheet of stomata-only epidermis, occasionally formed are singular GCs, *fama*-like GMC tumors, and stomata made with a trio or quartet of GCs surrounding a pore [\(Figures](#page-10-0) [6C](#page-10-0)–6G). The singular GCs [\(Figures 6](#page-10-0)C, 6F, and 6G, pink asterisks) are the hallmark of *FAMA* overexpression ([Ohashi-Ito and](#page-13-5) [Bergmann, 2006\)](#page-13-5), whereas the excessive symmetric divisions [\(Figures 6C](#page-10-0) and 6G, orange and white brackets) imply the loss of *FAMA* or *FLP* activities. Conversely, three- to four-celled stomata [\(Figures 6C](#page-10-0) and 6D, white arrowheads) are the signature of ectopic activities of cell-cycle genes in GMCs [\(Adrian et al.,](#page-12-12) [2015; Yang et al., 2014\)](#page-12-12). We further found that *CYCD5;1* overexpression can confer extra division of GCs in addition to excessive divisions of the epidermis [\(Figures 6H](#page-10-0)–6J), suggesting that the extra GC divisions in sustained *iMUTE* could be attributed to elevated *CYCD5;1* expression. Mature GC GFP marker was expressed in a subset of GCs in three- to four-celled stomata [\(Figure 6E](#page-10-0), cyan arrowheads) and likewise in a subset of singular GCs [\(Figure 6F](#page-10-0), white asterisks). Thus, regardless of developmental outcome as singular GCs or three- to four-celled stomata, *iMUTE* can trigger eventual GC differentiation. The supernumerary GMCs expressed stomatal-lineage GFP marker, *TMMpro::GUS-GFP* ([Figure 6G](#page-10-0), white bracket), corroborating their identity.

#### DISCUSSION

This study identifies comprehensive inventories of early MUTEresponsive genes. A comparison of SPCH and MUTE targets revealed how MUTE switches cell-cell signaling from stomatallineage initiation to commitment. We further unraveled that MUTE directly induces the expression of both cell-cycle regulators and their transcriptional repressors, thereby orchestrating the I1-FFL to generate the single symmetric division event necessary to create functional stomata.

#### Logic of Cell-State Transition by Sequential Actions of bHLH Proteins

How could later-acting stomatal bHLHs switch the precursor state from their earlier-acting sisters? Our study revealed that MUTE binds to the SPCH binding sites of the shared target genes and takes over their lineage-specific expressions, while repressing the earlier-acting gene *EPF2* to switch cell-cell signaling circuits [\(Figure 1L](#page-2-0)). The regulatory modules of stomatal differentiation resemble that of myogenesis in animals, where myogenic bHLHs Myf5, MyoD, Myogen, and MRF4 sequentially direct lineage specification, proliferation, and differentiation ([Putarjunan and Torii, 2016; Tapscott, 2005\)](#page-13-7). Extensive ChIP sequencing (ChIP-seq) studies of Myf5 and MyoD have shown that these two myogenic bHLHs bind to the nearly identical target sites genome-wide. However, unlike Myf5, MyoD promotes strong transcriptional activation via Pol II recruitment, suggesting that their functional specificities lie in their transcriptional activities [\(Conerly et al., 2016](#page-12-15)). Each stomatal bHLH possesses a unique motif signifying its function. Interestingly, overexpression of SPCH without the MAP kinase target domain or truncated FAMA lacking the N-terminal activation domain phenocopies MUTE activities [\(Lampard et al.,](#page-12-16) [2008; Ohashi-Ito and Bergmann, 2006](#page-12-16)), suggesting that these additional modules prevent the functional interference among the three bHLHs.

It is known that *SPCH*, *MUTE*, and *FAMA* expressions are tightly regulated by the epigenetic mechanisms ([Lee et al.,](#page-12-17) [2014; Matos et al., 2014\)](#page-12-17). The local chromatin state may explain why some targets (e.g., *TMM*, *SCRM*) are immediately induced by *MUTE* while others (e.g., *FAMA*) delay for 8 hr. It could also explain the previous report that the ability for *MUTE* to induce stomatal differentiation becomes restricted as plants age [\(Trivino](#page-13-8) [et al., 2013\)](#page-13-8). #### MUTE as a Potent Inducer of Cell Division

Our study establishes that MUTE is a potent inducer of cell-cycle genes [\(Figure 2](#page-5-0)). MUTE strongly upregulates *CDKBs* and *CYCA2s* that promote GMC symmetric divisions [\(Boudolf](#page-12-10) [et al., 2004; Xie et al., 2010\)](#page-12-10). CDKBs-CYCA2s complexes are known to regulate S/G2 phase but do not drive the cell-cycle entry. Our work further identified CYCD5;1 as a D1-cyclin promoting the symmetric division. *CYCD5;1* is known to partner with CDKA1;1 [\(Boruc et al., 2010\)](#page-12-19), which is not likely a MUTE target [\(Figures 2](#page-5-0) and S5). Because G1/S transition is a ratelimiting step, once *CYCD5;1* expression is induced, basal levels of CDKs and G2/M cyclins in *mute* may be sufficient to execute the symmetric-division-like cell division. Time-lapse imaging shows that CYCD5;1 peaks and disappears 8 hr before the symmetric division prior to the accumulation of CDKB1;1, which pairs with CYCAs to drive G2/M phase (Figure S7). The sequential peaks of CYCD5;1 followed by CDKB1;1 are consistent with their roles in G1/S and G2/M transitions, respectively.

While this manuscript was under revision, [Weimer et al. \(2018\)](#page-13-9) reported *CYCD7;1*. While both *CYCD5;1* and *CYCD7;1* are expressed transiently in GMC, there are several marked differences. First, MUTE does not induce or repress *CYCD7;1* expression (Figure S5), indicating that *CYCD7;1* is not a direct MUTE target. Second, introduced expression of *CYCD5;1* in the *mute* arrested meristemoids triggers SCD-like divisions [\(Figures](#page-6-0) [3F](#page-6-0)–3J), whereas *CYCD7;1* is not sufficient to override the cell division arrest in *mute* ([Weimer et al., 2018\)](#page-13-9). Third, CYCD5;1 disappears before the GMC division [\(Figure 3A](#page-6-0) and Video S1), whereas CYCD7; 1 remains in immature GCs [\(Weimer et al.,](#page-13-9) [2018\)](#page-13-9). These differences suggest a mechanism by which CYCD5;1 initiates the GMC symmetric division driven by MUTE, whereas CYCD7; 1 promotes the GMC division in a MUTE-independent manner. This scenario explains the weak phenotypes of *cycD7;1* ([Weimer et al., 2018\)](#page-13-9) and *cycD5;*1 (Figures S6B–S6D).

It is worth noting that modest enrichments of *CDKB1;1* and *CYCA2;3* were reported in *scrm-D mute* ([Pillitteri et al., 2011\)](#page-13-10). Because CDKB1;1 and CYCA2s suppress endocycles ([Boudolf](#page-12-8) [et al., 2009](#page-12-8)), it is possible that these cell-cycle genes exhibit background-level expressions in the MUTE-independent manner, which may be crucial for preventing the endoreduplication of stomatal-lineage cells. In this scenario, the primary role of MUTE is to boost their timely expressions above the threshold level to drive the symmetric division. CYCD5;1 was reported as a candidate quantitative trait gene for endoreduplication [\(Sterken et al., 2012](#page-13-11)). Consistently, CYCD5;1-GFP is detected broadly beyond stomatal cell lineages (Figure S6). In any event, *Arabidopsis* MUTE as a potent inducer of cell division accords with the role for its mobile *Brachypodium* ortholog, BdMUTE, in promoting the subsidiary cell division ([Raissig et al., 2017\)](#page-13-12). Whether BdMUTE (or other grass MUTE orthologs) directly drives the symmetric division of grass stomata is a future question.

#### I1-FFL Orchestrated by MUTE Drives the Single Symmetric Division to Create Stomata

Our study unraveled that MUTE directly activates the expressions of cell-cycle genes and the direct repressors of the cellcycle genes. Furthermore, our modeling showed that the I1-FFL orchestrated by MUTE can trigger a single pulse of gene expression, in this case the cell-cycle genes, within the narrow developmental windows encompassed by MUTE and FAMA/FLP [\(Figure 5\)](#page-8-0). The I1-FFL is known to function as a pulse generator ([Basu et al., 2004; Mangan and Alon, 2003](#page-12-20)): the circuit can generate a pulse output even under sustained input. This explains why sustained *iMUTE* overexpression still largely produces ''normal'' stomata with paired GCs. In our modeling framework, the strength of direct *CDKs/CYCs* activation and the decay rate set up the *CDKs/CYCs* peak level. This intuitively explains the extra GC divisions occasionally observed in the strong ectopic *iMUTE* background, which likely reflects high sustained expression of *CDKs/CYCs*. Indeed, we found that *CYCD5;1* overexpression could lead to the extra GC divisions [\(Figure 6](#page-10-0)). Since *MUTE* expression window is limited in the wild-type, theoretically, the simple linear circuit could be implemented for a pulse output. However, the I1-FFL would hold advantages for this biological context. The I1-FFL can accelerate the response time ([Mangan and Alon, 2003](#page-13-6)), thus allowing MUTE to achieve the single division event concomitantly with stomatal differentiation. Our model is consistent with a previous report for a step input [\(Goentoro et al., 2009\)](#page-12-21) such that a delay in the response of *FAMA/FLP* to MUTE enabled the amplitude and duration of *CDKs/CYCs* activation to be increased, which contributes to the sharp and high peak. This emphasizes the importance of the direct control of *FAMA/FLP* by MUTE to achieve such coordination. The I1-FFL was implicated in the transcriptional control of root Casparian strip differentiation [\(Fernandez-Marcos et al., 2017](#page-12-22)), root apical meristem identity [\(Grigg et al., 2009\)](#page-12-23), and root vascular patterning ([De Rybel](#page-12-24) [et al., 2014](#page-12-24)). Thus, plants may use I1-FFL for critical cell-fate decision-making processes in broader contexts. The single division event creating stomata, a process orchestrated by MUTE, could be an excellent system to study the quantitative dynamics of I1-FFL in the future.

#### <span id="page-16-1"></span>METHOD DETAILS

#### Plasmid Construction and Transgenic Plants Generation

The following constructs were generated for the study: pLJP245 (*Est::MUTE [iMUTE]*), pLJP246 (*Est::SPCH [iSPCH]),* pSKH198 *(CYCD5;1pro::gCYCD5;1-GFP),* pSKH238 *(CYCD5:1pro\_*D*3E::gCYCD5;1-GFP),* pSKH128 *(MUTEpro::gCYCD5;1),* pSKH133 *(MUTEpro::gFLP),* pSKH137 *(MUTEpro::gFAMA),* pJT164 *(FAMApro::FAMA-RFP)*, and pXQ21 (*CaMV35S::CYCD5;1*). For detailed information of each plasmid, see Table S4. For generating transgenic Arabidopsis, plasmid constructs were electroporated into Agrobacterium (GV3101/pMP90) and subsequently transformed by floral dipping. At least three lines were subjected to detailed characterizations.

#### Estradiol-Induction, RNA Preparation, and RNA Sequencing

Homozygous transgenic lines for *iMUTE* or *iSPCH* were grown on ½ MS agar media for 4 days. 40-50 seedlings for each replicate were bathed in a ½ MS liquid media containing 10 mM estradiol (Sigma-Aldric, E2758) or DMSO (mock). For the RNA-seq samples, three replicates of *iMUTE* seedlings per treatment (mock or estradiol) were collected 8hours after treatment for total RNA isolation. Three different batches of seedlings were harvested at the same time of the day to minimize the effects of circadian clock. Total RNA was isolated from the seedlings using RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74904) and DNase I digestion (Qiagen, 79254) was performed on column during the RNA extraction according to the instructions of the manufacturer. The RNA samples were sent to the New York Genome Center (NYGC) facility for library construction and sequencing. RNA samples were run on the Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer to access the RNA quality. mRNA libraries were prepared by using Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA and read paired-end 50bp in length with 30M coverage per sample on Illumina Hiseq 2500 system.

#### Bioinformatics and Data Analysis

Sequences of RNA-seq experiments, those performed in this study and those obtained from a public database (GSE57953, GSE57497 and GSE58856, NCBI GEO), were mapped on TAIR 10 genome using TopHat2-PE (ver. 2.0.13, [https://github.com/](https://github.com/infphilo/tophat) [infphilo/tophat](https://github.com/infphilo/tophat)) with default options, and the Illumina reads were mapped and the read counts were calculated using our in-house programs with the gene location GFF file distributed at TAIR [\(ftp://ftp.arabidopsis.org/Genes/TAIR10\\_genome\\_release/TAIR10\\_](ftp://ftp.arabidopsis.org/Genes/TAIR10_genome_release/TAIR10_gff3/TAIR10_GFF3_genes.gff) [gff3/TAIR10\\_GFF3\\_genes.gff](ftp://ftp.arabidopsis.org/Genes/TAIR10_genome_release/TAIR10_gff3/TAIR10_GFF3_genes.gff)). Differentially expressed genes and their statistical significance were evaluated using DESeq2 R package ([http://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html\)](http://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html). Upregulated and downregulated genes in RNA-seq experiments were defined as log2 (treated/control) more than 0.5 or less than 0.5, and q-values less than 0.05. Qualities and reproducibility of our *iMUTE* induced and mock control triplicate samples were verified by evaluating correlations between logarithms of RPKM (read per kilobase exon per million mapped reads). Values of Pearson's correlation were more than 0.980 between samples of the same condition (control vs control or treated vs treated of the three biological replicates) and more than 0.946 between different conditions (Figure S1B). The published FACS-sorted stomatal cell-lineage transcriptome samples ([Adrian et al., 2015\)](#page-12-12) showed low reproducibility, and none of the biological replicates of stomatal-lineage specific populations clustered correctly (Figure S1D). Likewise, the published inducible SPCH (*iSPCH*) and control wild-type RNA-seq data ([Lau et al., 2014\)](#page-12-13) showed low reproducibility: the *iSPCH* 6-hour treatment Rep1 clustered together with control wild-type 6-hour treatment rather than with the *iSPCH* 6-hour treatment Rep2, and so on (Figure S1C). While our data are triplicated pair-end experiments and the others were duplicated/triplicated single-end experiments ([Adrian et al., 2015; Lau et al., 2014\)](#page-12-12), we assumed that the difference did not affect much for the scope of this study.

A SPCH ChIP-seq data (GSE57497) was also downloaded from NCBI GEO and aligned on TAIR 10 genome using bowtie2 program (version 2.1.0, <http://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/bowtie2/>) with default parameters. Genes were classified for some functional groups using the annotation obtained from the Gene Ontology database (<http://geneontology.org>, validation date 9/1/2016) and literatures. Stomatal genes were defined as genes having ''stomatal complex morphogenesis (GO:0010103)'' and/or ''stomatal complex development (GO:0010374)'' and curated using the literature ([Horst et al., 2015; Lau et al., 2014; Peterson et al., 2013; Pillitteri](#page-12-5) [et al., 2011](#page-12-5)). Cell cycle, cell division, mitotic and auxin-related genes were defined as all genes having GO annotations that contained ''cell cycle,'' ''cell division," ''mitotic'' and ''auxin'' such as GO:0007049 (cell cycle), GO:0051726 (regulation of cell cycle), GO:0051301 (cell division), GO:0051302 (regulation of cell division), GO:0007067 (mitotic nuclear division), GO:0006312 (mitotic recombination), GO:0009733 (response to auxin), and GO:0009734 (auxin-activated signaling pathway).

To cluster the expression patterns, we calculated logarithm of odds score (LOD) of contingency tables for upregulated genes between two experiments in order to minimize biologically-irrelevant clustering based on the type of experimental procedures (e.g. minimize clustering on the basis of an upregulation of stress signatures due to protoplasting followed by a FACS sorting [\[Adrian et al.,](#page-12-12) [2015\]](#page-12-12)). The experiments were clustered using a hierarchal approach, in which the pairs having most score were clustered into a new virtual node until no pairs exist. Heat maps and Venn diagrams were generated using in house python scripts.

#### cDNA Preparation and qRT-PCR

For time-course induction, estradiol-inducible *MUTE* seeds were sown on 1/2 media, and subjected to stratification at 4C for two days then grown at the regular long-day condition for three or four days. Subsequently, 40-50 seedlings were transplanted to 1/2 MS media containing DMSO (mock) or 10 mM estradiol (*iMUTE*) and sequentially harvested after 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 hours after induction. To reduce the effects of circadian clock, seedlings were transferred at the same time of the day. 1 mg of RNA was converted to cDNA using iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad) according to the instructions of the manufacturer. First-strand cDNA was diluted to seven fold in double distilled water and used as template for Real-time qPCR. qRT-PCR was performed as described in [\(Qi et al., 2017\)](#page-13-2). At least four 10-fold dilution series were amplified by the same primers to set standard curve and calculate the quantity of the gene expression. For each experiment, three technical replicates were performed, and for all experiments at least 3 biological replicates were performed. Relative expression was calculated by dividing *ACTIN2* gene expression over the specific-gene expression. See Table S4 for primer sequences used for qRT-PCR.

#### Chromatin Immunoprecipitation

Total 160 mg (2 plates) of seeds were sown on ½ MS agar media, and seedlings at 4248 hrs after germination were collected and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen. Frozen tissue was ground to fine powder and re-suspended in 20ml of ChIP extraction buffer 1 ([Bowler](#page-12-28) [et al., 2004](#page-12-28)) containing 1% formaldehyde (Sigma, F1635), and was incubated at 4C for 10 min for DNA-protein cross-linking. 2 M glycine was added to quench the reaction, then samples were incubated at 4C for 5 min. To shear DNA, the samples were subjected to sonication using Bioruptor Plus UCD-300 sonicator (Diagenode), 30 sec on/off cycles for 15 times at High power setting. Afterward, samples were diluted up to 1 ml with ChIP dilution buffer with Triton and spun at full speed. Dynabeads protein G (Invitrogen, 10004D) was used for pre-clearing of the samples for 1 hour at 4C, which were then subjected to overnight immunoprecipitation at 4C. 2 ml of anti-GFP antibody (Ab290, Lot: GR278073-1) was added in 1 ml pre-cleared chromatin sample. Subsequent steps of capturing, washing, reverse cross-linking of immune-complex and DNA purification were performed according to the protocol by [Yamaguchi et al. \(2014\).](#page-13-18) Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was carried out using gene specific primers (Table S4). The qPCR was run using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix on CFX96 real-time system (Bio-Rad) as decribed previously ([Qi et al., 2017\)](#page-13-2). At least three technical replicates were performed for each experiment. For each experiment, at least three biological replicates were performed. Dilution series of input was amplified simultaneously by the same primers to calculate the quantity of the ChIPed DNA as % input [\(Yamaguchi et al., 2014](#page-13-18)).

#### Microscopy

For confocal microscopy, cell peripheries of seedlings were visualized with either propidium iodide (Molecular Probes). Images were taken using LSM700 (Zeiss) or SP5 (Leica) with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 500-515 nm for GFP; and excitation 619 nm and emission at 642 nm for propidium iodide. For SP-5, a HyD detector was used. The confocal images were false colored, and brightness/contrast were uniformly adjusted using Photoshop CS6 (Adobe). For time-lapse live imaging, Arabidopsis germinating cotyledons expressing dual reporters (plasma-membrane RFP with either *CYCD5;1pro::CYCD5;1-GFP*, *MUTEpro::MUTE-GFP*, or *FAMApro::FAMA-GFP*) were generated by genetic crosses and subjected to time-lapse imaging with 30-min intervals using a motorized stage on LSM700 (Zeiss) under continuous light condition [\(Peterson and Torii, 2012; Qi et al., 2017](#page-13-19)).

#### Mathematical Modeling

We formulated the interaction of MUTE, FAMA/FLP, and CDKs/CYCs as type 1 incoherent feed-forward loop model ([Mangan and](#page-13-6) [Alon, 2003\)](#page-13-6). In this model, time course of MUTE expression level ([*MUTE*]) is given, and the resulting FAMA/FLP and CDKs/CYCs expression ([*FAMA/FLP*] and [*CDKs/CYCs*], respectively) are under the control of MUTE expression. For minimal component, we hereby combine CDKs/CYCs as one parameter. Thus, the model does not account for specific cell cycle phase. Likewise, both FAMA and FLP are simplified as one parameter. We also introduced MUTE promoter activity as [*MUTEpro*].

We define wild-type *MUTE* promoter activity as

$$\begin{bmatrix} \textit{MUTEpro} \end{bmatrix} (t) = \begin{cases} 1 & (0 < t < 8) \\ 0 & (otherwise) \end{cases}$$

In the iMUTE mutant, the promoter activity pattern is defined as follows:

½*MUTEpro*ð*t*Þ = 2 ð0*<t*Þ 0 ðotherwiseÞ

:

We assume *MUTE* expression is controlled by *MUTE* promoter activity as follows:

$$\frac{d[MUTE]}{dt}(t) = \beta_{MUTE}[MUTEpro](t) - \alpha_{MUTE}[MUTE](t).$$

b*MUTE* represents the strength of the effect of *MUTE* promoter over *MUTE* expression. a*MUTE* is the decay rate of MUTE. *FAMA/FLP* expression is assumed to be controlled by MUTE as follows:

$$\frac{d[FAMA/FLP]}{dt}(t) = \beta_{FAMA/FLP} f_a([MUTE](t-\tau), K) - \alpha_{FAMA/FLP} [FAMA/FLP](t).$$

b*FAMA*/*FLP* represents the strength of the effect of MUTE over *FAMA/FLP* expression. a*FAMA*/*FLP* is the decay rate of FAMA/FLP. *f* (*u*, *K*) represents the effect of a gene on another gene expression. Activation reaction is defined by

$$f_a(u,K) = \frac{u}{K+u},$$

and repression reaction is defined by

$$f_r(u,K) = \frac{K}{K+u}.$$

t is the delay of the effect of MUTE over *FAMA/FLP*. This delay is introduced based on the induction kinetics of *FAMA* transcripts upon estradiol induction of *iMUTE*.

*CDKs/CYCs* expression is controlled both by MUTE and FAMA/FLP as follows:

$$\frac{d[CDKs/CYCs]}{dt}(t) = B_{CDKs/CYCs} + \beta_{CDKs/CYCs-MUTE} f_{a}([MUTE](t), K_{MUTE}) + \beta_{CDKs/CYCs-FAMA/FLP} f_{r}([FAMA/FLP](t), K_{FAMA/FLP}) - \alpha_{CDKs/CYCs}[CDKs/CYCs](t).$$

*BCDKs*/*CYCs* represents the basal production or reduction of *CDKs/CYCs*. b*CDKs*/*CYCsMUTE* and b*CDKs*/*CYCsFAMA*/*FLP* are the strength of the effect of MUTE and FAMA/FLP over *CDKs/CYCs* expression. a*CDKs*/*CYCs* is the decay rate of CDKs/CYCs. We incorporate the effects of MUTE and FAMA/FLP over CDKs/CYCs additively, not synergistically. If there is the basal reduction of CDKs/CYCs, i.e. *BCDKs*/*CYCs* < 0, we also assumed [*CDKs*/*CYCs*](*t*) as 0 if it became less than 0.

For *MUTEpro::FLP* and *MUTEpro::FAMA*, we incorporated direct induction of *FAMA/FLP* by *MUTE* promoter as follows:

$$\frac{d[FAMA/FLP]}{dt}(t) = \beta_{FAMA/FLP} f_a([MUTE](t-\tau), K) + \beta_{MUTEpro-FAMA/FLP}[MUTEpro](t) - \alpha_{FAMA/FLP}[FAMA/FLP](t).$$

b*MUTEproFAMA*/*FLP* represents the strength of the direct induction of FAMA/FLP by MUTE promoter. For *MUTEpro::CYCD5;1*, we incorporated direct induction of CDKs/CYCs by MUTE promoter as follows:

$$\frac{d[CDKs/CYCs]}{dt}(t) = B_{CDKs/CYCs} + \beta_{CDKs/CYCs-MUTE} f_{a}([MUTE](t), K_{MUTE}) + \beta_{CDKs/CYCs-FAMA/FLP} f_{r}([FAMA/FLP](t), K_{FAMA/FLP}) + \beta_{MUTEpro-CDKs/CYCs}[MUTEpro](t) - \alpha_{CDKs/CYCs}[CDKs/CYCs](t).$$

We reduced b*FAMA*/*FLP* for *fama* mutant background.

For the analysis of parametric dependence, we calculated *d*, the duration of the first continuous period satisfying [*CDKs*/ *CYCs*](*t*) > 3. We assumed the single cell division is achieved when *d* satisfied 8 < *d* < 16.

Numerical simulation was done with explicit Euler scheme with *Mathematica* (Wolfram, Inc., Champaign, IN). Complete source code for the numerical simulation is provided as Data S1.
