#### 35 **Abstract**

36 The end of the reproductive phase in monocarpic plants is determined by a coordinated arrest of all 37 active meristems, a process known as "Global Proliferative Arrest (GPA)". GPA is linked to the 38 correlative control exerted by developing seeds and possibly, the establishment of strong source-39 sink relationships. It has been proposed that the meristems that undergo arrest at the end of the 40 reproductive phase behave at the transcriptomic level as dormant meristems, with low mitotic 41 activity and high expression of abscisic acid (ABA) response genes. Meristem arrest is also 42 controlled genetically. In *Arabidopsis thaliana*, the MADS-box transcription factor FRUITFULL 43 induces GPA by directly repressing genes of the *APETALA2 (AP2)* clade. The *AP2* genes maintain 44 shoot apical meristem (SAM) activity in part by keeping *WUSCHEL* expression active, but the 45 mechanisms downstream of this pathway remain elusive. To identify target genes, we performed a 46 transcriptomic analysis, inducing AP2 activity in meristems close to arrest. Our results suggest that 47 AP2 controls meristem arrest by repressing genes related to axillary bud dormancy in the SAM and 48 negative regulators of cytokinin signalling. In addition, our analysis indicates that genes involved in 49 the response to environmental signals also respond to AP2, suggesting that it could modulate the 50 end of flowering by controlling responses to both endogenous and exogenous signals. Our results 51 support the previous observation that at the end of the reproductive phase the arrested SAM behaves 52 as a dormant meristem, and strongly support AP2 as a master regulator of this process. 53

#### 56 **Introduction**

57 Flower, fruit and seed production are basic events in plant reproduction. In Arabidopsis 58 (*Arabidopsis thaliana*) and most of the species with indeterminate inflorescences where the shoot 59 apical meristem (SAM) never differentiates, the number of flowers that an inflorescence is able to 60 produce, and therefore, the number of fruits and seeds, depends on the activity of the SAM and the 61 length of the flowering period. Thus, the flowering period comprises from the floral transition until 62 the meristem arrest at the end of flowering. However, while we have a clear understanding of the 63 mechanisms acting at the floral transition, knowledge about the processes that governs the end of 64 flowering at the meristem is still scarce.

65 In monocarpic plants, the end of flowering is associated with a senescent process named Global 66 Proliferative Arrest (GPA). GPA has been well characterized in Arabidopsis (Hensel et al., 1994; 67 Walker and Bennet, 2018), starting with the arrest of SAM in the main inflorescence after the 68 production of a certain number of flowers. At this point, all the active meristems of the plant also 69 arrest more or less coordinately, responding to both local and systemic cues (Ware et al., 2020). 70 After meristem arrest, the plant completes the filling of the last seeds produced and enters a global 71 senescent program that ends with the death of the plant. The initiation of the GPA is clearly affected 72 by seed production and development (correlative control) (Hensel et al., 1994; Wuest et al., 2016; 73 Walker and Bennett, 2018). Sterile mutants or plants where flowers are removed manually show a 74 delayed GPA, which leads to an increase in the flowers produced by the SAM (Hensel et al., 1994; 75 Balanzà et al., 2018; Balanzà et al., 2019). To explain the effect of fertility on GPA, different 76 hypotheses have been proposed. For example, GPA could respond to strong source-sink relations 77 between developing seeds and the SAM (Sinclair and de Wit, 1975; Kelly et al., 1988). The 78 possible existence of a mobile seed-derived signal that could mediate the meristem arrest was also 79 proposed (Leopold et al., 1959; Engvild, 1989; Noodén et al., 2004). Recently, it has been shown 80 that auxin supplied from the last developing fruits proximal to the shoot apex could modify the 81 auxin fluxes in the stem, inducing the SAM arrest (Ware et al., 2020). Despite all the information 82 available, little is known about the molecular control of this process.

83 To assess the molecular responses of the SAM to seed production, Wuest et al. (2016) performed a 84 transcriptomic study providing new evidence regarding the nature of this process. The authors 85 showed that the arrested meristem at the end of flowering retains its identity and functionality, but it 86 has reduced mitotic capacity. Wuest et al. also proposed that arrested meristems resemble dormant 87 meristems, as they present high expression of abscisic acid (ABA) response genes. In agreement 88 with this, the arrested meristems at the moment of GPA are able to recover activity after fruit 89 removal, further supporting the role of seed contribution to meristem arrest (Hensel et al., 1994;

90 Wuest et al., 2016). Although our knowledge of this process is increasing, the molecular events that 91 control the trigger of meristem arrest are still unknown. Recently, the first genetic pathway involved 92 in the control of the length of the flowering phase was described. In Arabidopsis, the end of 93 flowering is controlled by the negative regulation of *APETALA2-like* (*AP2-like*) genes by 94 FRUITFULL (FUL) and the miR172 in the SAM (Balanzà et al., 2018). Both the *ful* loss-of-95 function mutants and the *ap2-170* mutant, an allele of *AP2* that is partially resistant to miR172 96 action, are able to delay GPA, keeping meristems active longer. In addition, both mutants develop 97 more flowers in the main shoot than wild-type plants when fruit and seed production is completely 98 prevented, pointing to a direct effect on SAM activity independent of signals coming from fruits. 99 AP2 has been implicated in the regulation of meristem identity and maintenance through the control 100 of *WUSCHEL* (*WUS*) expression (Wurschum et al., 2006; Zhao et al., 2007), although the 101 underlying mechanism is not well understood. 102 The aim of this work was to identify genes working downstream of the FUL-AP2 pathway to

103 control inflorescence meristem arrest. Our results, combined with previously reported data by 104 Wuest et al (2016), indicate that AP2 controls meristem activity and GPA via two main hormonal 105 responses, ABA and cytokinins (CK), as well as by regulating genes that respond to different 106 environmental signals, such as light quality and temperature. Our results confirm previous 107 observations that arrested meristems at GPA behave as dormant meristems, and uncover a direct 108 role for AP2 in the induction of this dormant state.

#### 110 **Results**

111 To understand how AP2 controls SAM activity and represses untimely meristem arrest, we analysed 112 the transcriptomic response to elevated levels of AP2 in the inflorescence. *AP2* has a complex and 113 fine-tuned regulation both at the transcriptional, post-transcriptional and translational level 114 (Aukerman and Sakai, 2003; Chen, 2004). To simplify this regulation, we decided to take advantage 115 of an inducible system to overexpress an allele of *AP2* completely resistant to miR172, *AP2m3* 116 (35S::LhGR>>AP2m3) (Fig.1A) (Chen, 2004). We previously showed that ectopic expression of 117 *AP2* miR172-resistant alleles in the SAM is able to restore SAM activity and the production of new 118 flowers when they are induced in an arrested SAM (GPA stage) (Balanzà et al., 2018) (Fig.1B). As 119 we were interested in how AP2 modulates the end of flowering and not the process of reversion of 120 arrested meristems, we designed our experiment on meristems that were still active, but close to 121 GPA. Two weeks after bolting, inflorescences were treated with dexamethasone or mock-solution, 122 and SAMs were collected for RNA extraction 6-8 hours post-treatment by manual dissection to 123 remove floral buds and, as much as possible, floral primordia (Suppl. Fig. 1).

151 As AP2 has been described as a repressor of GPA that maintains SAM activity (Balanzà et al., 2018), we 152 hypothesized that genes repressed by AP2 could mediate the entrance into GPA and accumulate at the end of 153 flowering (Up in arrested meristems), and also that the genes activated by AP2 could work in the opposite 154 direction, repressing the entrance into GPA (Down in arrested meristem). In accordance with this hypothesis, 155 when we compared our results with the expression data produced by Wuest et al.(2016), comparing 156 "growing meristems versus arrested meristems" ("active vs GPA") or "reactivated meristems after fruit 157 removal versus arrested meristems" ("reactivated vs GPA"), we observed a high correspondence of the 158 results. Approximately 60% of D.E.G upon AP2 induction showed a similar up- or down-regulated 159 expression in the equivalent conditions in the two Wuest experiments, while 75% of D.E.G by AP2 induction 160 behaved similarly in at least one of the Wuest experiments (Fig. 3 A and B). As expected, the observed 161 changes in gene expression upon AP2 induction associate with the changes observed for genes that are 162 important not only for physiological meristem arrest (mostly dependent on seed production), but also for 163 meristem reactivation after fruit removal, suggesting that AP2 could be a key factor integrating age-related 164 signals (Balanzà et al., 2018), as well as the fertility input (Wuest et al., 2016).

165 We decided to perform a new GO analysis with the reduced list of the common D.E.G. that behaved 166 similarly upon AP2 induction and in at least one of the Wuest experiments. In the group of genes down-167 regulated by AP2 and genes up-regulated at GPA (557 out of the 667 genes down-regulated by AP2), 114 168 categories were identified, all of them were included in the original GO analysis (115 categories; Suppl. table 169 S4, Suppl. table S5). In the group of genes up-regulated by AP2 and down-regulated at GPA (277 out of 435 170 genes), we observed a substantial reduction in the number of enriched categories, obtaining only 9 of the 171 original 49 categories found in AP2-activated genes (Suppl. table S4, Suppl. table S5). This analysis

172 indicates a clear association between the repressed responses by AP2 induction and the pathways that 173 become activated at the end of the reproductive phase. However, this association is not clear for genes 174 positively regulated by AP2 induction, as mentioned above.

175 To understand the differences between the transcriptomic changes described by Wuest et al (2016) under 176 physiological conditions and those that responded to AP2 induction, we performed an additional GO analysis 177 for the genes that behaved differently after AP2 induction with respect to the described changes under 178 physiological conditions (25% of genes of D.E.G. by AP2 induction). This analysis suggests that many of 187 the genes included in the categories obtained from the up-regulated genes in the initial GO analysis (Suppl. 188 table S3) are also up-regulated when GPA occurs under physiological conditions, and therefore may not have 189 a direct function in the control of meristem arrest. Altogether, this analysis suggested that AP2 function in

190 the control of SAM arrest is mainly associated with the repression of genes that tend to accumulate at the end

191 of flowering, which are likely to be responsible for meristem arrest.

#### 192 **Direct targets of AP2 in the inflorescence meristem.**

193 Our RNA-seq analysis was done with samples collected at a relatively short time after Dex-treatment (6-8 194 hours), given that the two-component system for induction that we used implies transcription and translation 195 of AP2. We observed that AP2 induction elicits a response contrary to that observed during meristem arrest 196 under physiological conditions (Wuest et al., 2016). Thus, we wondered if AP2 could directly regulate part 197 of this response and therefore, be a key control point. To assess this, we determined how many of the genes 198 differentially expressed in response to AP2 induction (Suppl. table S3) had been described as putative targets 199 of AP2 in the ChIP-seq data experiments reported by Yant et al. (2010). 26.5% of the D.E.G. (292 genes) 200 were putative targets of AP2 (Suppl. table S3); 102 and 186 genes in the up- and down-regulated gene 201 categories, respectively. To determine if these target genes associated preferentially with any of the 202 previously identified GO categories, we performed a new GO analysis using the list of D.E. target genes. 203 Within this subgroup, GO analyses rendered only 9 and 35 categories over-represented in the genes up- and 204 down-regulated by AP2 induction, respectively (Suppl. table S6). As expected, we obtained a clear reduction 205 in the number of categories obtained in the first analysis (Suppl. table S4), recovering those with a high 206 number of putative direct targets. Among the 9 categories enriched in the up-regulated target genes, we again 207 found the "response to stimulus" ("response to chemical", "abiotic" and "endogenous stimulus" and "to 208 organic substance"), as well as "anatomical structure formation involved in morphogenesis" (Fig. 2 C, Suppl. 209 table S6), and the "response to jasmonic acid stimulus" previously highlighted in the initial GO analysis with 210 the D.E.G (5 out of 10 genes (50%)) (Suppl. table S7). Among the direct targets included in the "jasmonic

- 215 *PROTEIN 1 (JAZ1)* and *JAZ5* (Peng et al., 1997; Chini et al., 2007; Thines et al., 2007).

## 247 **AP2 modulates meristem arrest by repressing the ABA response and the same regulatory network** 248 **that controls axillary meristem dormancy.**

249 It has been suggested that meristems in GPA are similar to dormant meristems (Wuest et al., 2016). 250 ABA has been described to play a crucial role in the maintenance of meristems in the dormant state 251 (Yao and Finlayson, 2015; González-Grandio et al., 2017), and, accordingly, ABA signalling is one 252 of the enriched categories in the GO analysis of D.E.G. (Suppl. table S4, S5 and S7). There are 36 253 D.E.G. related to ABA and dormancy among the genes down-regulated upon *AP2* induction (Suppl. 254 table S11). Most of them are also up-regulated in arrested meristems at GPA (Wuest et al. 2016) 255 (Suppl. Table S11). Interestingly, most of the genes present in this group were previously described 256 by González-Grandio et al. (2017) in the genetic regulatory network controlling Arabidopsis lateral 257 bud dormancy downstream of *BRANCHED1* (*BRC1*) (Gonzalez-Grandio et al., 2013; Gonzalez-258 Grandio et al., 2017). More specifically, the common genes include *HOMEOBOX PROTEIN 21* 259 *(HB21)* and *HB53,* which are direct targets of BRC1, *NINE-CIS-EPOXYCAROTENOID*  260 *DIOXYGENASE3* (*NCED3*), which acts downstream HB21 and HB53 to direct ABA synthesis 261 (Iuchi et al., 2001) and *G-BOX BINDING FACTOR (GBF3), NAC-LIKE, ACTIVATED BY AP3/PI*  262 (*NAP*)*, ABSCISIC ACID RESPONSIVE ELEMENT-BINDING FACTOR3 (ABF3)* and *HISTONE*  263 *H1-3*, which are ABA responsive genes (Ascenzi and Gantt, 1997; Sablowski and Meyerowitz, 264 1998; Uno et al., 2000; Garcia et al., 2008; Yang et al., 2014). From the genetic regulatory network 265 described by González-Grandio et al. (2017) downstream of BRC1, we also identified *ABNORMAL*  266 *SHOOT2/ NGATHA-LIKE1 (ABS/NGAL1), AT1G79520* and *AT1G11210* as being differentially 267 regulated upon AP2 induction, although *a priori* these genes are not related to ABA. We checked 268 *HB21* expression in Wuest's data and observed that the HB21 transcript accumulates in arrested 269 meristems (Wuest et al., 2016).

270 HB21 is an upstream regulator of the bud dormancy regulatory network (González-Grandio et al., 271 2017) and it is a putative direct target of AP2 (Yant et al. 2010). Moreover, in our experiment it was 272 repressed by *AP2* induction. Therefore, we decided to explore its possible contribution to GPA. 273 First, we analysed its expression in the SAM during inflorescence development of wild-type plants. 274 We observed that *HB21* expression increased dramatically around 3 weeks after bolting, when 275 meristem arrest occurs (Fig. 4 A). Interestingly, in a *ful* mutant background, where *AP2* gene 276 expression is maintained in the SAM, delaying meristem arrest (Balanzà et al., 2018), we did not 277 observe *HB21* transcript accumulation in the shoot apex (Fig. 4 A).

278 The "response to ABA" category also included *BLH1*. This protein has been related to ABA-279 mediated seed dormancy (Kim et al., 2013) and interacts physically with KNOX proteins 280 (Hackbusch et al., 2005; Smaczniak et al., 2012), which are important for meristem activity. 281 Interestingly, *BLH1* was also present in the "response to light" category. In fact, *BLH1* accumulates 282 under far-red light and regulates part of the response mediated by phytochrome A (Staneloni et al., 283 2009). As mentioned before, other direct targets of AP2 in the "response to light" category were 284 *PHYA*, *PAR1*, *PAR2* and *HFR1,* all of them involved in the far-red perception and response, 285 suggesting a role for the red/far-red light signalling in the control of meristem arrest. As *BLH1* 286 responds to both ABA and light, we decided to analyse its expression in the SAM during 287 inflorescence development of wild-type and *ful* mutant plants. As with *HB21*, we observed a clear 288 accumulation of the transcript 3 weeks after bolting in the wild type, while no transcript 289 accumulation was detected in the *ful* mutant (Fig. 4 B). These results suggest that the accumulation

- 290 of both genes in the meristem could participate in establishing the meristem arrest observed in wild-
- 291 type plants, as well as reinforcing our hypothesis that AP2 could control meristem arrest, in part, by
- 292 the direct repression of genes in the SAM that modulate ABA and light responses.
- 293 If *HB21* and *BLH1* play a role in promoting meristem arrest, the corresponding mutants may be expected to
- 294 present altered GPA timing. *blh1-2* and *hb21-2* mutants, obtained from NASC, did not develop any obvious
- 295 alterations in vegetative or inflorescence development when compared to wild-type plants, but both showed a
- 296 delayed GPA (Fig. 4 C). When we scored the number of fruits produced in the main shoot by the mutants,
- 297 both of them presented an increased number (*blh1*: 60,8 ± 4,9; *hb21*: 63,1 ± 7,8) with respect to the wild-type
- 298 (54,8 ±4,5) (Fig. 4 D), indicating that these genes play a role in the meristem arrest at the end of flowering.

#### 299 **AP2 regulates cytokinin responsiveness at the GPA.**

300 CKs have been described to promote SAM activity and dormancy break in most plant species. Our GO 301 analysis indicates that the negative regulation of CK signalling was repressed by *AP2* induction. In this 302 category, we recovered 3 out of 4 genes from a small F-box protein family, *KMD1*, *KMD2* and *KMD4*, 303 involved in the proteasomal degradation of the type B-ARR transcription factors that mediate CK signalling 304 (Kim et al., 2013). Two of the identified genes, *KMD2* and *KMD4,* are putative direct targets of AP2 and 305 have a direct role in the control of meristem activity (Kim *et al*., 2013). For this reason, we decided to 306 analyse their expression in the shoot apex meristem during inflorescence development. As for *BLH1* and 307 *HB21*, we observed a clear accumulation of KMD transcripts at the end of flowering in wild-type plants, 308 while in *ful* mutants the expression of these genes remained low (Fig. 5 A, B, C). We also checked if *KMD2* 309 and *4* (putative direct targets of AP2) had a role in the meristem arrest by characterizing GPA timing in the 310 corresponding mutants. The single *kmd2* and *kmd4* mutants were similar to the wild type plants, and 311 produced 56,3 ± 4,4 and 57,1 ± 4,0 fruits respectively, showing no significant differences with respect to the 312 wild-type (54,5 ± 3,8) (Fig. 5D, E). As redundant roles for these gene have been reported (Kim et al., 2013), 313 we also tested the triple mutant *kmd1/2/4*, where all the *KMD* genes detected in our RNA-seq analysis are 314 inactive. Triple mutants presented a clear delay of meristem arrest, (Fig. 5D). Compared to the wild-type and 315 the single mutants analysed, the triple mutant produced more flowers in the main shoot (62,4 ± 3,7), 316 indicating that these genes were also redundant in GPA control (Fig. 5E). Conversely, *35S::KMD2* lines 317 produced a lower number of flowers than wild-type plants (42,7 ± 4,7 vs 54,5 ± 3,8 respectively) (Fig. 5D, 318 E), indicating that high levels of this protein are able to accelerate meristem arrest. Our results confirmed that 319 *KMD* genes participate in the control of meristem activity, and that they accumulate at the end of the 320 reproductive phase. Thus, AP2 could maintain meristem activity, at least in part, by repressing the *KMD*  321 genes.

#### 323 **Discussion**

324 The genetic and molecular control of the end of flowering and the establishment of GPA has been a forgotten 325 topic in plant science. However, in the last years, detailed transcriptomic landscapes of arrested meristems at 326 this developmental stage, and the first evidence of the involvement of a genetic pathway likely controlling 327 age-dependent cues, the FUL-AP2 pathway, have been published (Wuest et al., 2016; Balanzà et al., 2018; 328 Balanzà et al., 2019). These publications also show that the proliferative arrest of meristems is reversible, 329 and that flower production can resume either by removing mature fruits and seeds or by inducing AP2 330 expression in the meristems. In this study, our aim was to better understand how these two cues, those 331 coming from seed production under normal physiological conditions, and those provided by the regulation of 332 AP2 levels in the meristem, interact to control the end of the reproductive phase.

333 The combination of previously published data on AP2 binding sites (Yant et al., 2010) with our 334 transcriptomic analysis in response to AP2 induction in meristems close to arrest has allowed us to identify 335 putative molecular mechanisms that function downstream of the FUL-AP2 pathway at the shoot apex, 336 linking AP2 action with the control of hormonal regulation in the SAM and of several genes that respond to 337 environmental factors. In this scenario, *AP2* (together, probably, with other *AP2-like* genes) represses the 338 ABA response and promotes CK responsiveness to stimulate SAM activity. In parallel, AP2 likely mediates, 339 at least to some extent, the effect of environmental signals, such as temperature and light, that also likely 340 impact the regulation of the end of the reproductive phase.

#### 341 **AP2 promotes SAM activity allowing a high CK responsiveness.**

342 Previous reports have implicated AP2 in the maintenance of *WUS* expression (Wurschum et al., 2006; Zhao 343 et al., 2007), but the mechanism of this regulation has not yet been fully elucidated. In addition, we have 344 previously shown that meristem arrest associates with the absence of *WUS* expression in the SAM (Balanzà 345 et al., 2018). The results of the present study indicate that AP2 could control SAM activity through the 346 regulation of the CK responsiveness in the meristem by repressing the *KMD* genes, which are negative 347 regulators of CK responses. CK are key factors for SAM formation and maintenance (Werner et al., 2003; 348 Gordon et al., 2009; Bartrina et al., 2011; Bartrina et al., 2017), activating the expression of the *B-type ARR* 349 genes that are positive direct regulators of *WUS* (Meng et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017). 3 out of 4 genes in 350 the *KMD* clade were negatively regulated by AP2 and two of them, *KMD2* and *KMD4*, are putative direct 351 targets of AP2. All three transcripts accumulate at the end of the flowering phase when meristem arrest 352 occurs, and mutant analysis reveals a role for these proteins in the control of meristem arrest. As KMD 353 proteins are involved in the proteasomal degradation of the B-type ARR factors that mediate CK responses 354 (Kim et al., 2013), our results suggest a clear link between AP2 action and *WUS* regulation. In this scenario, 355 low levels of AP2 at the end of flowering could allow for the accumulation of the KMD proteins, avoiding 356 CK signalling and turning off the expression of *WUS* in the SAM, which associates with meristem arrest 357 (Fig.6).

#### 358 **The arrested SAM at the end of flowering resembles a dormant meristem.**

359 Wuest et al. (2016) showed that the arrested meristems at the end of flowering behave as dormant meristems. 360 Corroborating this, if seeds, which are key factors to inducing meristem arrest (Hensel et al., 1994; Balanzà 361 et al., 2018; Balanzà et al., 2019), are removed by fruit pruning after GPA takes place, reactivation of the 362 arrested meristem is forced, and flowers are produced again. This response clearly indicates that the arrested 363 SAM is still alive and able to respond to signals. Under physiological conditions, Wuest et al. (2016) showed 364 that ABA responsive genes accumulated in the SAM at the moment of the arrest, but the levels of those same 365 genes were reduced after meristem reactivation. As we previously showed, *AP2* induction after meristem 366 arrest is also able to reactivate flower production (Fig. 1 B) (Balanzà et al., 2018). Interestingly, here we 367 show that the same groups of genes that are up-regulated at GPA are repressed after *AP2* induction in the

- 368 SAM, indicating that AP2 could delay meristem arrest, at least partially, by repressing their expression.
- 369 Our GO analysis showed that several putative direct targets of AP2 are included in the "response to ABA"
- 370 category, suggesting a direct relationship between the ABA response and AP2 action (Suppl. Table S11). We
- 371 observed that *AP2* induction represses the expression of ABA biosynthesis (*ABA1, NCED3*), perception
- 372 (*PYL7*), signalling (*SNRK2.3, ABI2*) and response (*RD20, RD22, RD29A*) genes. In addition, several genes
- 373 included in the genetic regulatory network described for the control of axillary bud dormancy (Gonzalez-

14

374 Grandio et al., 2017), including the AP2 direct target *HB21*, as well as *HB53,* are also found in the D.E.G. in 375 response to AP2 induction in the inflorescence meristem. These HB proteins have been shown to act 376 downstream of BRC1 to direct ABA accumulation and response in axillary dormant buds, but no information 377 about their role in SAM was previously reported. Interestingly, BRC1, or even its close relative BRC2, are 378 not expressed in SAM, not even at the moment of proliferative arrest (Wuest et al., 2016, this work), 379 suggesting that the acquisition of the dormant stage of the SAM at the end of the reproductive phase is not 380 dependent on BRC1. Since AP2 appears to act by repressing dormancy, we can speculate that AP2 would be 381 either repressing the factor(s) activating the HB pathway or maintaining HB21/53 repression directly in the 382 active SAM, and that this repression would disappear at the end of flowering to trigger dormancy and 383 therefore induce meristem arrest. It has been described that *AP2* decreases its activity in the SAM 384 progressively through the direct repression by FUL (Balanzà et al., 2018). The decrease in AP2, which 385 down-regulates *HB21*, would explain the *HB21* accumulation at the end of flowering, which will trigger the 386 accumulation of ABA and ABA responses (Fig. 6). Taken together, our results suggest that the combination 387 of high ABA response and low CK responsiveness could mediate meristem arrest at the end of the 388 reproductive phase inducing the dormant stage, as has been proposed for many species for the establishment 389 and maintenance of bud dormancy (Corot et al., 2017; Tylewicz et al., 2018; Liu and Sherif, 2019; 390 Vayssieres et al., 2020).

#### 391 **Possible role of environmental factors in the control of meristem arrest.**

392 The different developmental phases that take place in plants are processes that are highly controlled 393 and coordinated by both genetic and environmental factors in order to ensure the reproductive 394 success and fitness of the species. Accordingly, the formation of dormant buds in many perennial 395 plants occurs before the start of winter in order to endure the ensuing adverse conditions. Thus, the 396 environmental conditions (day length, temperature, …) are key factors to determine the precise 397 moment to initiate the dormant stage (Heide and Prestrud, 2005; Maurya and Bhalerao, 2017). Our 398 analysis has revealed a putative function for AP2 as a regulator of genes involved in the responses 399 to temperature and light. Interestingly, most of the genes identified in the response to temperature 400 are related to the response to cold. All the identified genes are repressed by AP2, and at the same 401 time, accumulate in arrested SAM (Wuest et al., 2016), suggesting that the expression of genes that 402 respond to low temperatures could induce meristem arrest. In fact, among the D.E.G.s we found 403 upon AP2 induction that are putatively direct AP2 targets is the *C-REPEAT BINDING FACTOR3*  404 *(CBF3)* gene that is a key regulator of the cold response, repressing plant growth (Liu et al., 1998; 405 Gilmour et al., 2000; Gilmour et al., 2004; Chinnusamy et al., 2007; Zhou et al., 2017). 406 Interestingly, one of the few categories recovered from the up-regulated targets genes of AP2 was 407 JA response, including the response repressors JAZ1 and JAZ5 (Chini et al., 2007). JAZ proteins 408 act as negative regulators of CBFs (Hu et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2017). While more evidence is 409 required, our results suggest that AP2 could regulate the same group of genes that are controlled in 410 the cold response.

411 Another important factor controlling dormancy in many plants is light. Shortening of the 412 photoperiod in autumn is an important signal for bud dormancy initiation and growth cessation in 413 trees (Eriksson and Moritz, 2002; Molmann et al., 2006; Olsen, 2010). The day length is usually 414 measured by plants as peaks of far-red light at the beginning and end of the day (Johnson et al., 415 1994; Olsen et al., 1997; Yanovsky and Kay, 2003; Imaizumi, 2010). As observed for the 416 temperature response, AP2 also could regulate many genes involved in light signalling. Among the 417 D.E.G.s identified after AP2 induction that are putatively its direct targets, we have identified core 418 elements in the genetic routes involved in the perception of light quality, such as *PHOT1* (blue 419 photoreceptor) and *PHYA* (red/far-red photoreceptor), as well as in the red/far-red response. 420 Interestingly, *HFR1, PAR1, PAR2* are activated by low red/far-red ratios to negatively regulate the 421 shade-avoidance elongation response in a process mediated by PHYA (Johnson *et al.*, 422 1994; Yanovsky *et al.*, 1995; Devlin *et al.*, 2003). These genes also accumulate in arrested 423 meristems (Wuest et al., 2016) and are negatively regulated by AP2. Thus, AP2 could regulate a 424 subset of genes in the SAM that are involved in the response to light, suggesting that the red/far-red 425 ratio could modulate SAM activity. We have analysed the expression of *BLH1*, one of the far-red 426 responsive genes. Similarly to other genes in this category, *BLH1* accumulates in arrested 427 meristems and is repressed by AP2, and *blh1* mutants show delayed meristem arrest, suggesting a 428 role for *BLH1* in the control of the end of flowering. *BLH1* regulates part of the phytochrome A 429 response and accumulates under far-red light (Staneloni et al., 2009). In addition, *BLH1* is also 430 involved in ABA-mediated seed dormancy and, together with KNAT3, modulates ABA response 431 genes, such as *ABI5* (which accumulates in arrested meristems and is down-regulated by AP2 432 induction) (Kim et al., 2013). Clear examples of crosstalk between light signalling and the ABA 433 response in the control of seed dormancy have been previously proposed. For example, in the 434 establishment of primary seed dormancy, far-red light has been reported to promote dormancy by 435 up-regulating the levels of PIF1 (which also accumulates in arrested meristem and is down-436 regulated by AP2 induction). This, in turn, promotes ABA accumulation, leading to an increase in 437 *ABI5* transcripts (Oh et al., 2004; Oh et al., 2006; Kim et al., 2013; Vaistij et al., 2018). Taken 438 together, these data suggest that *BLH1* could work as a link between ABA and light responses, in 439 addition to being regulated by AP2 in the SAM.

440 We have observed that AP2 regulates many genes involved in the response to light and temperature. 441 It remains to be established if AP2 could mediate the response to these environmental factors by 442 regulating these genes or if the same subset of genes has been recruited to control growth and 443 meristem activity by AP2 independently. However, it is tempting to speculate that environmental 444 factors (temperature and light) and endogenous factors (ABA, CK, age and even seed-derived 445 signals) could converge in the regulation of meristem activity and that AP2 would be a hub in the 446 regulation of these different pathways. It would be very interesting to carry out systematic studies in 447 order to determine how environmental conditions affect meristem arrest at the end of the 448 reproductive phase, and how seasonal variations affect the process.

449 Moreover, our results reinforce the idea, previously proposed by Wuest et al. (2016), that at the end 450 of flowering, the arrested meristem behaves as a dormant meristem. Our work indicates that genetic 451 pathways that operate in other dormancy processes, such as axillary bud dormancy or seed 452 dormancy, could control the arrest of the SAM. We also propose that environmental factors could 453 be important in the control of the meristem arrest, identifying genes involved in both cold and 454 red/far-red responses as important players in this process (Fig. 6). Recently, auxin transport from 455 fruit to the stem has been proposed as a determinant for meristem arrest when the plant has acquired 456 the "competence to stop" (Ware et al., 2020). In the initial GO analysis of genes up-regulated upon 457 AP2 induction, we identified the "auxin response" category (Suppl. Table S4), but those genes were 458 also up-regulated under physiological conditions at the moment of meristem arrest (Suppl. Table 459 S5), suggesting that this set of genes is unlikely to mediate AP2 function in the control of SAM 460 activity. Thus, we can speculate that AP2-mediated control of meristem arrest could work in 461 parallel to auxins, as no D.E.G. related to auxins were identified in the list of direct AP2 targets. On 462 the other hand, our data fit perfectly with the acquisition of the "SAM competence" proposed by 463 Ware et al (2020): the progressive repression of AP2 genes in the SAM would allow for the 464 activation of ABA, light and cold response genes that could provide the competence to stop, 465 rendering the SAM able to respond to the change in auxin fluxes that have been proposed by Ware 466 et al (2020) to be a cue provided from fruits proximal to the inflorescence meristem. Interestingly, 467 the similar expression changes observed between *AP2* induction and the changes observed under 468 physiological conditions in fertile plants and, more importantly with reactivated meristems after 469 fruit (seed) removal, point to AP2 as a key factor integrating not only age-related signals (Balanzà 470 et al., 2018), but also the fertility input in the control of SAM activity (Hensel et al., 1996; Wuest et 471 al., 2016). As changes in auxin fluxes have been identified as part of the fertility input that controls 472 meristem arrest, future studies of the interaction between auxin fluxes and AP2 function could 473 contribute to a better understanding of this developmental process.

## 475 **Plant materials and growth conditions**

476 For all the analyses described, *Arabidopsis thaliana* seeds were stratified for 2 days at 4°C after 477 sowing. Plants were grown at 21°C under long day conditions (16 h light, 8h night), in a 2:1:1 by 478 vol. mixture of peat:perlite:vermiculite. All mutant plants and marker lines used in this study were 479 in the Columbia background. All mutants and overexpression lines used have been described 480 previously: *ful-2* (Gu et al., 1998), *blh1-2* (Pagnussat et al., 2007), *kmd2-1, kmd4-1, kmd1-1 kmd2-1*  481 *kmd4-1, 35S::KMD2* (Kim et al., 2013) except *hb21-2*, which is a new allele obtained from NASC 482 (N835325). 483 The 35S::LhG4:GR»AP2m3 construct was generated by cloning the AP2m3 allele CDS (Chen, 484 2004) via a Gateway LR clonase II recombination reaction downstream of the artificial pOp6 485 promoter into the pOpOn2.1 binary vector derived from the pOpOff2 vector (Moore et al., 2006). 486 Primer sequences used are detailed in Supplemental Table S12. Arabidopsis was transformed with 487 Agrobacterium strain C58 pM090 using the floral dip protocol (Clough and Bent, 1998) and 488 transgenic lines carrying a single transgene insertion were selected. The homozygous lines selected 489 were classified by their GUS expression level. For this study, a T3 homozygous line for the 490 transgene with high and consistent GUS expression was used.

491

#### 492 **DEX treatment**

493 Plants were grown in soil until two weeks after bolting. The induction of 35S::LhG4:GR»AP2m3 in 494 the shoot apex of transgenic plants was carried out dipping the apices in a Dex solution (Dex 5 µM 495 and 0.015% (v/v) Silwet L-77), or control solution with an equivalent concentration of Silwet L-77 496 (mock). At 6 hours after induction, inflorescence apices were harvested and dissected to eliminate 497 older buds. Three biological replicates were sampled, each containing about 20 inflorescence 498 meristems.

## 526 **RT-qPCR analyses**

527 RNA-Seq validation: cDNAs were synthesized from 1.5 μg of total RNA using Oligo (dT24V) 528 primers and Superscript III (Invitrogen). Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer 3 529 software (Rozen and Skaletsky, 2000). Polymerase chain reactions were performed in the 7500 Fast 530 Real-Time PCR detection system (Applied Biosystems) and used SYBR®Green to monitor dsDNA 531 synthesis. The presence of spurious amplification products caused by genomic DNA contamination 532 was verified by the qPCR dissociation profiles. The PCR efficiency and the optimal quantification 533 cycle threshold (Cq value), were obtained by the online program Real time PCR Miner (Zhao and 534 Fernald, 2005). The relative expression ratio between control and treated groups were calculated 535 based on the mean of Cq values and a subsequent statistical test using a Pair-Wise Fixed 536 Reallocation Randomization Test (Pfaffl et al., 2002). The significance of the performed 537 randomization was tested by the p-value (SE <0.005 at p = 0.05). Three independent biological 538 replicates of each experimental condition (control or treated samples), were evaluated using triplet 539 technical replicates. The reference genes used to normalize the qPCR data have been discussed 540 previously (Czechowski et al., 2005). Primer sequences used are detailed in Supplementary Table 541 S12.

542 Expression levels: Inflorescence apex were harvested and trimmed to eliminate older buds. RNA 543 was extracted using E.Z.N.A.® Plant RNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek) and DNase treated with Turbo 544 DNase (Ambion AM1907). The RT-qPCR was performed in the 7500 547 Fast Real-Time PCR detection system (Applied Biosystems) and used EVAGreen to monitor 548 dsDNA synthesis. CT value was obtained from automatic threshold. Results were normalized to the expression of the ACT reference gene. The 2 *-ΔCt* 549 was shown as relative expression level. Three 550 biological replicates were performed for each sample. Two-tailed Student's t-test was performed to 551 determine statistical significance (P < 0.05). Primer sequences used are detailed in Supplementary
