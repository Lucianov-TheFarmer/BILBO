For the Editors of Journal of Genetics and Genomics,

We are submitting a research article, "Magnitude of modulation of gene expression in aneuploid maize depends on the extent of genomic imbalance," for consideration for JGG.

This research investigates the effects of different levels of aneuploidy on gene expression, for genes located in both the dosage-varied chromosome region and across the genome. Using genetic tools uniquely available in maize, we compared gene expression in leaf tissue from diploids and haploids with that of 4 different levels of aneuploidy of a specific chromosome region.

The results described in the manuscript include a number of effects which we hypothesize are characteristic of genomic imbalance. One such effect is the inverse trans effect, in which expression of genes that are not varied in dosage are nevertheless affected and in the opposite direction of the genomic imbalance. This effect is found in both directions of dosage variation (monosomic and trisomic), which this experiment is uniquely able to demonstrate as it includes both hyperploid and hypoploid materials. Also demonstrated here is the progressive nature of expression changes caused by genomic imbalance. For example, tetrasomic plants show changes similar to trisomic plants, but as the imbalance is greater, the effects are proportionally stronger.

We believe this research will further an understanding of the molecular effects of aneuploidy, showing the direct and indirect impacts of genomic imbalance on gene expression. The article will appeal to readers with an interest in the basic mechanisms of gene dosage effects, as well as readers working with any of the numerous genetic conditions in which aneuploidy plays a role.

### **Abstract**

Aneuploidy has profound effects on an organism, typically more so than polyploidy, and the basis of this contrast is not fully understood. A dosage series of the maize long arm of chromosome 1 (1L) was used to compare relative global gene expression in different types and degrees of aneuploidy to gain insights into how the magnitude of genomic imbalance as well as hypoploidy affects global gene expression. While previously available methods require a selective examination of specific genes, RNA sequencing provides a whole-genome view of gene expression in aneuploids. Most studies of global aneuploid effects have concentrated on individual types of aneuploids because multiple dose aneuploids of the same genomic region are difficult to produce in most model genetic organisms. The genetic toolkit of maize allows the examination of multiple ploidies and 1‒4 doses of chromosome arms. Thus, a detailed examination of expression changes both on the varied chromosome arms and elsewhere in the genome is possible, in both hypoploids and hyperploids, compared with euploid controls. Previous studies observed the inverse *trans* effect, in which genes not varied in DNA dosage are expressed in a negative relationship to the varied chromosomal region. This response was also the major type of changes found globally in this study. Many genes varied in dosage showed proportional expression changes, though some were seen to be partly or fully dosage compensated. It was also found that the effects of aneuploidy are progressive, with more severe aneuploids producing effects of greater magnitude.

Keywords: aneuploidy, polyploidy, inverse effect, gene regulation, dosage compensation, Gene Balance Hypothesis

### 1 **1. Introduction**

2 Nearly a century ago, Alfred Blakeslee (1921) recovered isolates of the flowering plant Jimson weed 3 (*Datura stramonium*) that had an extra copy of each of the 12 chromosomes, as well as triploid and 4 tetraploid derivatives of the normal diploid. He noted that each of these trisomic plants had 5 morphological differences from the diploid that were more prominent than the differences among the 6 diploid, triploid and tetraploid plants. Furthermore, he mapped a gene with allelic differences for the 7 presence or absence of purple pigment using altered segregation ratios from a trisomy, but noted that 8 every one of the other trisomies modulated the amount of pigment (darker or lighter) when the purple 9 allele was homozygous. In contrast, the pigment phenotypes of diploid, triploid, and tetraploid were 10 similar. Therewith was born the concept of genetic balance that has been recapitulated in many 11 eukaryotic organisms, e.g., *Drosophila* (Lindsley et al., 1972) and human (Schinzel, 2001). As 12 foreshadowed by the observation of modulation of pigment levels by all trisomies, molecular studies of 13 selected genes found parallels to those classical phenotypic observations (Birchler, 1979; Birchler and 14 Newton, 1981; Birchler et al., 2001, 2005; Birchler and Veitia, 2007; Guo and Birchler, 1994; Rabinow 15 et al., 1991). RNA sequencing studies on gene expression in trisomies of *Drosophila* and *Arabidopsis* 16 *versus* balanced genotypes illustrate the generalized nature of these alterations on a global scale (Hou et 17 al., 2018; Sun et al., 2013a,b). Here, the question of the impact of increasingly imbalanced genotypes 18 for the same chromosomal segment of the genome is addressed.

19 Nondisjunction of the supernumerary B chromosome in *Zea mays* has proven to be very useful for 20 the study of aneuploidy and dosage effects. Maize B chromosomes are non-vital chromosomes 21 consisting of a centromere with a single long arm; the short arm is vanishingly small (Randolph, 1941). 22 They are selfish elements: in male flowers B chromosomes tend to nondisjoin during the second pollen 23 mitosis (Roman, 1947), and those sperm that have two copies of the B chromosomes tend to fertilize 24 the egg as opposed to the polar nuclei in the process of double fertilization (Roman, 1948). B 25 chromosomes occur naturally in some maize lines, and do not cause any noticeable impairment to the 26 plant unless their number approaches that of the A chromosomes (meaning the regularly numbered 27 chromosomes) (Randolph, 1941). An A chromosome segment can be translocated onto a B centromere 28 (Roman, 1947). Such a B-A chromosome can itself participate in crossing over with homologous A 29 chromosome regions, and researchers have taken advantage of this fact to add marker genes to the B-A 30 chromosome (Birchler and Alfenito, 1993). If a marker for kernel color has been added, for example, 31 this allows easy identification of seeds that have the B-A chromosome. A dominantly marked embryo 32 will have been generated by the joining of the egg and a sperm with two B-A chromosomes; the

33 endosperm resulting from fertilization of the polar nuclei by the counterpart sperm will be missing the 34 dominant marker (Birchler and Alfenito, 1993). A line of maize with B-A chromosomes may be 35 aneuploid for that A chromosome region, which allows for the creation of a dosage series for any 36 chromosome arm so desired. Fifteen unique B-A chromosomes with dominant kernel color markers 37 have been developed and introgressed into the maize W22 line (Birchler and Alfenito, 1993). These 38 stocks can be used to study the effects of both hypoploidy (missing chromosomes) and hyperploidy 39 (extra chromosomes) for most of the maize genome.

40 The effects of aneuploidy on gene expression levels can be widespread. A B-A chromosome dosage 41 series in maize showed extensive differences in the quantities of selected proteins depending on how 42 many doses of the long arm of chromosome 1 (referred to as 1L) were present (Birchler, 1979; Birchler 43 and Newton, 1981). No detectable effect of B chromosomes alone was found. The gene loci of the 44 affected proteins were not on 1L, but instead were scattered around the genome. This response has 45 come to be defined as a *trans*-effect, as opposed to a *cis*-effect that affects genes of the varied region 46 itself. Many of the *trans*-affected genes showed an inverse relationship to the dosage of 1L (Birchler, 47 1979; Birchler and Newton, 1981). Given three copies of 1L, affected proteins were found at levels 48 lower than in a normal diploid, and, given one copy, their levels were increased. Another finding of this 49 study was that a protein encoded on the varied portion of 1L was not affected by the dosage of its own 50 locus – it was dosage compensated (Birchler, 1979, 1981; Birchler and Newton, 1981). The results also 51 showed that the inverse effect occurs for most of the other tested chromosomal segments (Birchler and 52 Newton, 1981). This response was initially found with protein levels; mRNA transcripts were later 53 shown to be similarly impacted (Guo and Birchler, 1994). These results suggest that the inverse effect 54 is a general outcome of chromosomal dosage variation, and it has been proposed to be responsible for 55 many aspects of aneuploidy in general (Birchler and Newton, 1981).

56 In a previous study of all five trisomies of *Arabidopsis*, which cover the whole genome, as well as a 57 ploidy series of diploid, triploid and tetraploid, trisomy produced much greater modulations of global 58 gene expression than the change of whole-genome copy number (Hou et al., 2018). Although both 59 direct and inverse effects were found in trisomy, the latter was the most prominent. In the present study, 60 ratio distribution plots were used to visualize expression changes at the genome scale, with the ratio of 61 expression (e.g., expression in trisomy compared with balanced diploid) represented on the x-axis and 62 the number of genes in each ratio bin represented on the y-axis. The question of how global patterns of 63 expression are modulated in more extreme imbalanced genotypes that are possible to construct in maize 64 was examined.

65 Using a set of genetic tools uniquely available in maize, a dosage series for a maize chromosome 66 arm was produced to analyze the effects of the magnitude of genomic imbalance on gene expression. 67 Maize plants were produced that were variable in dosage for chromosome arm 1L. Two separate sets of 68 plants were grown to compare 6 distinct ploidy genotypes: diploids with 1, 2, 3, or 4 copies of 1L and 69 haploids with 1 or 2 copies of 1L. RNA sequencing was used to determine gene expression in mature 70 maize leaf tissue from plants at each dosage level. This approach provided a view of the effects of 71 aneuploidy on the entire transcriptome by comparing different levels of aneuploidy for the same 72 chromosome arm against euploid controls and comparing the same dosage magnitude change for 73 whole-genome ploidy.

### 74 **2. Results**

### 75 **2.1. Gene expression ratio distributions**

76 The results of RNA sequencing for plants at various chromosomal dosage levels were used to 77 compare the expression of genes in the aneuploid to that in the euploid controls. Genes expressed in the 78 euploid but not in the aneuploid were removed from the set; the numbers were variable in the different 79 dosage groups, which resulted in different sizes of compared gene sets. For each gene in the set, mean 80 RNA expression in the dosage-varied plant was divided by that of the control balanced genotype, 81 diploid or haploid, producing a single value for each gene, with a ratio of 1.00 representing no 82 difference between the two sets. These values were plotted in ratio distributions, which has the 83 advantage for examining global trends of modulation, especially close in magnitude to the control, 84 because all genes serve as data points permitting greater statistical power to show narrow range 85 modulations. As shown in Fig. 1 (ratio distribution plots), genes to the left of 1.00 on the x-axis have a 86 decrease of expression compared to normal, and genes to the right have an increase. Data shown in Fig. 87 1 are comprised of both *cis* gene ratios (genes located on chromosome arm 1L, as determined by DNA 88 sequencing of a 1L trisomy compared to a diploid) and *trans* gene ratios (genes located elsewhere in 89 the genome). The 1L and non-1L gene sets show a large difference of characteristic effects.

90 In all graphs comparing aneuploids to normal (Fig. 1), a large shift of the mode of the distribution 91 from 1.00 or a great deviation of the sample distribution from a normal distribution can be identified. 92 Starting at the low end of the spectrum, some ratio distributions contain a spike in the bin ranging from 93 0 to 0.05. This bin represents genes that are expressed at the lowest detectable levels in both diploid 94 and aneuploid but show at least a 20-fold decrease of expression in the aneuploid compared to the 95 diploid. Another spike may occur at the high end of the spectrum, indicating a greater-than-6-fold 96 increase in expression in the aneuploid. The genes in these outlying peaks are almost entirely found to

97 have low read counts (less than 0.25 reads per kilobase of transcript, per million mapped reads; 98 RPKM), in which case small changes to the numerator or denominator have a larger impact on the 99 ratio. They represent a small fraction of the assayed genes.

100 A general trend observed in Fig. 1 is that in *cis*, gene expression changes trend toward correlation 101 with gene dosage. A large peak can be seen near the guide corresponding to dosage increases, 1.50 in 102 trisomy/diploid (Fig. 1A, B and F) and 2.00 in tetrasomy/diploid (Fig. 1C) and disomy/haploid (Fig. 103 1D). In the monosomy/diploid comparison (Fig. 1E), the peak appears near 0.50, corresponding to a 104 dosage decrease. Though the peaks are broad, more genes tend to fall to the left of the guide in each 105 hyperploid, and to the right in the hypoploid. This indicates that the dosage effect in *cis* is partly 106 compensated for different genes to different degrees.

107 Gene expression ratios in *trans* do not resemble those observed in *cis*. Instead, in hyperploids smaller 108 peaks appear below 1.00, most prominently in the tetrasomy/diploid (Fig. 1C) and disomy/haploid (Fig. 109 1D), towards the inverse guides at 0.50. In the monosomy/diploid (Fig. 1E), the main peak is above 110 1.00; the peak becomes fairly broad, exceeding 1.00 into the range of increasing gene expression. 111 Although inverse changes in gene expression are distinct to *trans*, a substantial number of genes are not 112 affected, or are seen to change in the same direction as the varied chromosome. Among genes that are 113 inversely affected, their typical ranges lie between the inverse ratio and 1.00. Table S1 displays the 114 outcome of Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) tests comparing ratio sets of different aneuploid conditions; 115 ratio sets for each dosage are shown to be significantly different from each other. Principal component 116 analysis (PCA) was used to confirm that gene expression levels in biological replicates of the same 117 genotype were similar to each other (Fig. S1).

118 Then, ratio distributions comparing tetrasomy and trisomy were analyzed (Fig. S2). In the *cis* 119 comparison, expression is affected up to a 1.33 ratio, consistent with 1L gene dosage; in the *trans* 120 comparison, most genes are not dramatically different between the two aneuploids, but when they are 121 different, movement trends to the left. This result indicates that some non-1L genes in tetrasomies have 122 lower expression levels than in trisomies. Another conclusion that can be made from this plot is that 123 many of the same genes have altered expression in the two aneuploids in the same direction from the 124 diploid. If a non-1L gene shows a reduced expression in trisomy but not in tetrasomy, the gene would 125 have a high ratio in this comparison. The rarity of this effect compared to low ratios suggests that most 126 genes sensitive to trisomy are also sensitive to tetrasomy. In other words, the ratio distributions show 127 the range of all genes that are modulated even to a slight degree, so the tetrasomy/trisomy distribution 128 illustrates that most genes do not switch direction of modulation compared to the diploid.

### 129 **2.2. Differential expression**

130 Next, an edgeR test was performed to analyze the significant expression changes in each of the 131 comparisons shown in Fig. 1 (Table 1). The haploid/diploid comparison has no meaningful distinction 132 between *cis* and *trans* genes, and both gene sets show similar proportions of affected genes, with some 133 favoring increased expression compared with decreased. For *cis* genes in all five aneuploid/normal 134 comparisons, a large number show significant expression changes, but the vast majority are directly 135 proportional to gene dosage. In the first dosage series (set 1), the number of significantly affected genes 136 is greater in the tetrasomy/diploid and disomy/haploid than in the trisomy/diploid, correlating with their 137 more imbalanced genotypes. In the same comparisons in *trans*, the trisomy/diploid distribution has a 138 larger proportion in the decreased-expression category compared to the ploidy distribution of 139 haploid/diploid. In both the tetrasomy/diploid and disomy/haploid comparisons in *trans*, the number of 140 affected genes is substantially larger than the trisomy/diploid, and the proportion showing decreased 141 expression is greatly increased. In the second dosage series (set 2) in *trans*, the trisomy/diploid 142 comparison favors decreased expression compared to the haploid/diploid, while the monosomy/diploid 143 comparison shows many more genes with a significant increase than a decrease, in both cases 144 contrasting with their dosage relationship. The results of the edgeR significance tests were displayed as 145 volcano plots in Fig. 2.

## 146 **2.3. RT-qPCR verification**

147 In order to validate the results obtained by RNA sequencing, RT-qPCR was performed for a selection 148 of genes, using RNA extracted from materials of the same dosage series. Primers were selected to bind 149 to opposite sides of exon-exon junctions, in order to eliminate the effects of any remaining genomic 150 DNA. External RNA Controls Consortium (ERCC) spike-in was used as an exogenous control, rather 151 than a set of endogenous genes. If overall levels of mRNAs were affected by various aneuploid 152 conditions, housekeeping genes would be subject to changes as well and would not be a reliable control 153 for expression differences in the genome (Birchler, 2014).

154 Five genes were selected to represent each of five important dosage relationships: direct *cis* dosage 155 effect, *cis* dosage compensation, inverse *trans* effect, direct *trans* effect, and no *trans* effect. 156 Relationships between dosage and expression are consistent between RNA sequencing and RT-qPCR, 157 comparing disomy to haploid as well as both trisomy and tetrasomy to diploid (Fig. 3 and Table S2). 158 However, there are two cases of note. One relates to the 1L gene representing dosage compensation; 159 specifically, the compensation is partial in the RT-qPCR results, but complete in the RNA sequencing 160 results. The other is in the non-1L gene representing no change of expression: in the RT-qPCR results,

161 diploid expression was distinctly lower than in the trisomy and tetrasomy. Haploid and disomic haploid 162 for the same genes matched closely. Expression changes were progressively more severe with 163 increasing aneuploidy for the genes representing the direct *cis* dosage effect and the inverse *trans* 164 effect. Overall, the two approaches to quantify gene expression support each other.

### 165 **2.4. Functional group analysis**

166 In a previous study of *Arabidopsis* trisomies, different functional categories of genes were examined 167 individually with different groups being distinct (Hou et al., 2018). As a means of comparison, some 168 functional groups were examined separately for the maize data. Eight functional categories were 169 viewed in terms of both *cis* and *trans* impacts of each level of aneuploidy (Figs. 4, 5, and S3‒S8). K-S 170 tests for comparing functional groups show distinct patterns among them (Table S3). In *cis*, 171 chloroplast-targeted genes (Fig. 4) could be distinguished from the set of all 1L genes at all dosage 172 levels and exhibit a stronger inverse trend compared to all genes. The chloroplast-targeted genes also 173 have the most strongly differentiated response in *trans*. Non-1L mitochondria-targeted and ribosomal 174 genes are also strongly differentiated from the set of all genes in *trans* (Figs. 5 and S3). Proteasome and 175 transcription factor genes are differentiated to a lesser degree (Figs. S5 and S8), while peroxisome-176 targeted, signaling-related, and stress-related genes are not consistently different (Figs. S4, S6 and S7).

177 As with the *Arabidopsis* study, the functional groups with the strongest inverse effects in *trans* are 178 chloroplast- and mitochondria-targeted (Figs. 4 and S3). Also, in parallel, the genes encoding the 179 components of the ribosome tend to show a positive effect in *trans* for trisomies (Fig. 5). In this study, 180 it was possible to examine a tetrasomy for which this trend continues. Interestingly, however, in the 181 disomic haploid distribution, there is a shift downward for ribosomal genes even though other 182 functional classes of genes do not switch direction in this genotype.

### 183 **3. Discussion**

184 Based upon a comparison of various aneuploid conditions, a central observation from this work is 185 that the effects of dosage appear to be progressive in the sense that increasing levels of aneuploidy 186 correlate with increasing alterations of gene expression. In other words, the greater the genomic 187 imbalance, the greater the modulation of gene expression in both *cis* and *trans*. This applies both to the 188 number of genes affected and the typical degree of change. Both disomic haploids and tetrasomic 189 diploids have a greater level of imbalance (the varied chromosome occurs at 2× the copy number of the 190 remainder of the genome) than trisomic diploids (1.5×), and the number of genes with significant 191 expression change is higher in the disomy and tetrasomy than the trisomy. Based on the ratio 192 distribution plots, the range of expression changes in *cis* moves from near a peak at 1.50 in the trisomy

193 set to near 2.00 in the tetrasomy and disomy sets, proportional with gene dosage. In *trans*, the broad 194 peak comprising the majority of genes moves further to the left in the tetrasomy and disomy compared 195 with the trisomy, illustrating the greater inverse effect by increasing levels of genomic imbalance. 196 Further, these data suggest that the inverse effect is not a general suppression of transcription due to 197 aneuploidy-induced stress given that the stress-related genes have a distribution similar to all genes. 198 This conclusion is most directly demonstrated by the presence of inverse effects in *trans* in hypoploid 199 plants, which in that condition equates to an increase in expression. Furthermore, if a general decrease 200 of expression were present, the positively modulated *trans* effects seen in the trisomic set would be 201 diminished relative to the inverse effect in the more extreme hyperploids. In fact, the number of these 202 positively modulated *trans* effects also increases with increasing imbalance, implying that increasing 203 disruption of the regulatory network triggers a cascade of more and larger effects on individual genes 204 rather than the entire set of genes as a whole.

205 At each dosage level, genes with a structural locus on chromosome arm 1L were much more likely to 206 be positively correlated with gene dosage. Nevertheless, the ratio distributions span a range from 207 dosage compensation (no change from diploid) to direct dosage effect (a proportional change matching 208 the gene dosage) with most being intermediate, and there are minor shoulder peaks at compensation 209 and even at the inverse level for *cis* encoded genes. Few genes are affected beyond the ratio of their 210 dosage change.

211 A second phenomenon that can be seen in the comparisons of aneuploid to euploid is the prevalence 212 of inverse effects in *trans*. This directional change of gene expression, opposite to the dosage of the 213 varied chromosome, is in contrast to the haploid/diploid ploidy comparison in which there are few such 214 relative expression changes. In aneuploids, the inverse effect can be seen both holistically (in the ratio 215 distribution plots) and statistically (in the volcano plots). The inverse *trans* effect is often a partial one, 216 and positive *trans* effects also occur (although they are also typically partial). In most cases, a smaller 217 respective portion of *trans* genes than *cis* genes can be statistically confirmed to have altered 218 expression. According to the ratio distribution plots, fully inverse (or direct) effects in *trans* are 219 relatively rare compared with partial effects. The copy number change of structural genes is likely to 220 have a more intimate impact on expression level than *trans*-acting effects mediated through multiple 221 steps and interactions. Although molecular kinetics may account for observed effects in both *cis* and 222 *trans*, the *trans* effects are mediated by relationships among genes such as various transcription factors, 223 which have differential rates of synthesis and degradation of both mRNAs and proteins that could

224 affect the magnitude of the regulatory interactions. Expression ratios more extreme than inverse are 225 rare but present.

226 The modulation of gene expression in aneuploidy has been hypothesized to result from an altered 227 stoichiometry of the subunits of regulatory machinery (Birchler et al., 2001; Birchler and Newton, 228 1981; Birchler and Veitia, 2012; Guo and Birchler, 1994). In this study, it is possible to vary the extent 229 of altered genomic stoichiometry. The modulations in *trans* in general become more extreme with an 230 increasing imbalance of chromosomes.

231 In the interesting case of ribosomal genes, trisomies and tetrasomies at the diploid level have a 232 positive *trans* effect on expression but the disomic haploid flips to an inverse trend. In a theoretical 233 study of how altered regulatory stoichiometry could produce both positive and negative modulations 234 depending on the relative concentrations of interacting regulatory subunits of a multisubunit complex 235 (Birchler et al., 2016), it was illustrated that a positive effect might be conditioned by a low 236 concentration of a critical subunit but at a higher concentration the impact could become dominant 237 negative. The modulation of the ribosomal genes might illustrate such a case.

238 Previous studies of gene imbalance in maize similarly found inverse *trans* effects (Guo and Birchler, 239 1994), although these were conducted with kernel tissue rather than leaf tissue. The similarity of the 240 phenomena observed suggests that despite the developmental differences between the two tissue types 241 and stages, a common mechanism is responsible for the effects seen in both. Phenotypically, aneuploids 242 are affected more strongly than polyploids (Bastiaanse et al., 2019; Birchler and Veitia, 2012; Brunelle 243 and Sheridan, 2014; Lee et al., 1996a, b; Sheridan and Auger, 2008; Zuo et al., 2016), and modulations 244 of gene expression observed here correlate with that observation. These changes in gene expression 245 may be hypothesized to be the cause of the phenotypic effects seen in aneuploidy. However, this 246 relationship is likely to be complicated and might be the result of different sets of genes. For example, 247 1L monosomies are phenotypically less robust than 1L trisomies and both less robust than the diploid, 248 although the *trans* effects in general are in opposite directions. One possibility to consider is that 249 different effects become rate limiting on the phenotype (Birchler and Newton, 1981). Dosage affected 250 genes in monosomies are reduced to lower levels (0.50), as a general rule, than *trans* affected genes in 251 trisomy (0.67). The phenotype might be affected by whichever genes become limiting under the 252 respective circumstances. The more severe effects in monosomy than in trisomy correlate with the 253 greatest reductions in gene expression.

254 In general, the results of this study illustrate that the greater the genomic imbalance, the greater are 255 the modulations of gene expression across the genome. Classical studies at the phenotypic level have 256 shown greater effects with increasing genomic imbalance. Our study illustrates that modulation of gene 257 expression follows the same principles.

258 **4. Materials and methods** 

### 259 **4.1. Plant material and tissue collection**

260 To determine RNA levels of all expressed genes, leaf tissue was collected from plants at each dosage 261 level. The first set included diploid plants with 2, 3, and 4 doses of chromosome arm 1L, and haploid 262 plants with 1 and 2 doses. Leaf tissue was harvested at 45‒46 days post-germination, so as to avoid 263 differential expression of genes based on varying stages of development. The second set included 264 diploid plants with 1, 2, and 3 doses, and leaf tissue was harvested at 55‒57 days post-germination. 265 Plants were greenhouse-grown to minimize environmental variation, and leaf tissue was collected at the 266 same time of day (late mornings) to minimize circadian rhythm variation. The leaf tissue collected was 267 the region approximately 1.5‒15 cm above the auricle. Only the leaf blade was kept, and the midrib 268 was discarded. The tissue was collected from the lowest adult-phase leaves, typically leaves 5 and 6 in 269 the W22 inbred line.

270 The two sets of plants for analysis were phenotyped using different methods, each of which was later 271 verified by checking the relative consistency of *cis* gene expression in leaf tissue. In the first set, 272 dosage of chromosome 1L was confirmed by FISH karyotyping (Albert et al., 2010; Kato et al., 2004). 273 This dosage series was produced by self- or sibling-crosses of segmental trisomy (hyperploid 274 heterozygotes of TB-1La) parents, which had been phenotyped using the dominant color marker gene 275 *Bronze2* on the B-A chromosome (i.e., those kernels with purple embryos and bronze endosperm in a 276 background with *R1-scm2* that pigments both the embryo and endosperm). The offspring diploid 277 embryos carried various combinations of 1 or 2 complete copies of chromosome 1, plus 0, 1, or 2 B-1L 278 chromosomes, resulting in euploid, trisomic, or tetrasomic plants (Figs. S9 and S10). No monosomies 279 were recovered from this series. Kernel color in this generation no longer correlates with phenotype, 280 necessitating FISH karyotyping. Haploids and disomic haploids were produced by crossing trisomic 281 females (hyperploid heterozygotes of TB-1La) with males of the haploid inducer RWS (Röber et al., 282 2005). The haploid inducer line is homozygous for the dominant inhibitor of color, *c1-I*; thus maternal 283 haploids have colored embryos and colorless endosperm on ears that are mostly colorless. Haploids and 284 disomic haploids were distinguished by cytological examination of root tip metaphase chromosomes.

285 In the second set, dosages were limited to 1, 2, and 3 copies of 1L in a diploid background, which 286 allowed for phenotyping of kernels according to the presence of a dominant color gene (*Bronze2*) 287 carried by the B-1L chromosome in the embryo or endosperm (Birchler and Alfenito, 1993). Crossing a

288 trisomic plant (hyperploid heterozygote, as a male) onto a recessive *bz2; R-scm2* tester, if all copies of 289 B-1L were inherited by the embryo (making a trisomy), it had the purple color while the endosperm 290 was bronze. If all copies were inherited by the endosperm (leaving a monosomic embryo), the 291 endosperm carried the purple color while the embryo was bronze. If both embryo and endosperm 292 carried the purple color, normal disjunction had occurred in the preceding second pollen mitosis and 293 the embryo was a balanced diploid.

294 Though differing methods of dosage verification do not interfere with comparability of results from 295 the two sets, there were some distinctive environmental factors that suggest biological replicates from 296 the two sets are ideally compared within the same set, rather than between the two sets. The age of 297 tissue collection in the second set was adjusted to accommodate the slow growth habit of the 298 monosomies. Regarding adult plant phenotype, diploids were typically taller and more vigorous than 299 trisomies. Tetrasomies were less vigorous than trisomies, and monosomies were the least healthy in a 300 diploid background. Haploids were shorter and less robust than diploids; disomic haploids were 301 severely stunted (Birchler and Veitia, 2012).

302 Different numbers of biological replicates could be validated for each dosage as outlined above by 303 *cis* expression. In the first set, four diploid (euploid B-A translocation heterozygotes), five trisomic, and 304 three tetrasomic plants were validated, with the diploid plants serving as the control. Also in the first set 305 were three haploid and two disomic haploid plants, with the haploid plants used as the control. In the 306 second set, four monosomic, three diploid, and three trisomic plants were available, with the diploid 307 plants used as the control.

### 308 **4.2. RNA and DNA sequencing and data analysis**

309 RNA was extracted using a TRIzol-based protocol (Rio et al., 2010). RNA sequencing was 310 performed by the University of Missouri DNA Core, using Illumina technology (Bentley et al., 2008). 311 TruSeq (Illumina) was used for library preparation, involving poly-A enrichment of mRNA using 312 oligo-dT magnetic beads, first-strand cDNA synthesis using reverse transcriptase, second-strand cDNA 313 synthesis using DNA Polymerase I, ligation of adaptors, and finally PCR amplification. The first set 314 was sequenced on a HiSeq platform, with single-end 100 bp reads, and the second set was sequenced 315 on a NextSeq flow cell, with single-end 75 bp reads. Data output from Illumina RNA sequencing was 316 processed via RNAMiner pipeline (Li et al., 2015). Specifically, low quality reads were removed, 317 followed by mapping filtered reads to the maize genome (maize W22) using Tophat2 (Trapnell et al., 318 2009). Uniquely mapped reads were quantified as raw counts and differentially expressed genes were 319 analyzed using R/Bioconductor package edgeR (Robinson et al., 2010). Sequencing and alignment

320 details, including numbers and proportions of uniquely mapped reads, are provided in Table S4. Lists 321 of differentially expressed genes in each comparison are provided in Table S5, including expression 322 data for each gene at each dosage level; Linux command line tools were sufficient to sort *cis* and *trans* 323 genes for separate analysis, based on gene locus. The maize W22 reference genome (Springer et al., 324 2018), including gene locus information, was provided by database MaizeGDB. Statistical analysis, 325 ratio distribution histograms, and volcano plots were produced in R.

326 DNA sequencing was performed for a trisomy from the second set of plants and a normal diploid 327 (control), using a NextSeq flow cell with single-end 75 bp reads. After mapping to the W22 genome 328 using Bowtie2 (Langmead et al., 2009), gene counts were calculated by customized perl scripts. Then, 329 RPKM-normalized counts were used to generate gene ratios and to determine directly the dosage of all 330 genes in a 1L trisomy compared with a diploid control; genes in the trisomic portion of chromosome 1 331 had expression ratios up to 1.50, while all other genes had ratios approaching 1.00. The result suggests 332 a break point at position 164 Mb on chromosome 1, with all genes beyond that position number being 333 part of chromosome arm 1L, defined as *cis* (between genes *Zm00004b002705* and *Zm00004b005868*). 334 RNA and DNA sequencing data are available in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) from NCBI, 335 accession number GSE134936.

336 Statistical tests were used to determine whether expression of a gene was significantly impacted by 337 1L aneuploidy (Table 1). Read count data from sequencing experiments were normalized by the RPKM 338 method. Genes with total RPKM less than 3 were excluded from analysis. Then, for each gene, a test 339 for differential expression was performed using edgeR (Robinson et al., 2010) to compare mean gene 340 expression levels of the aneuploid group and the euploid group. False discovery rate (FDR) was 341 computed to adjust multiplicity in hypothesis testing. We chose 0.05 as the cutoff for controlling FDR 342 in testing genome-wide differential expression. In addition, the K-S test was used to determine whether 343 there is a substantial change in expression distribution between two dosages, such as trisomy *versus* 344 diploid and tetrasomy *versus* diploid (Table S1). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to 345 determine whether biological replicates of the same genotype shared similar expression patterns (Fig. 346 S1). A statistical test based on the PCA was used to identify possible outliers from each genotype 347 group. If any sample was found to be greater than two times the standard deviation from the group 348 mean, it would be considered as an outlier. For all genotypes, values of principal components 1 and 2 349 (PC1 and PC2) for each biological replicate were within two standard deviations of the group mean 350 indicating that no biological replicate could be considered an outlier. Differential expression tests, K-S 351 tests, and PCA were performed using R version 3.4.0.

### 352 **4.3. Generation of gene lists**

353 Gene lists used for the generation of ratio distribution plots were collected from various resources. 354 The list of transcription factors was obtained from PlantTFDB 4.0 (Jin et al., 2017). The lists of 355 chloroplast-, mitochondria-, and peroxisome-targeted genes were generated based on their subcellular 356 localizations predicted or gathered by cropPAL (Hooper et al., 2016). The list of chloroplast-targeted 357 genes contains genes predicted to be chloroplast-targeted by no less than 5 subcellular localization 358 prediction tools, or experimentally confirmed to be chloroplast-located. Genes predicted to be 359 mitochondria-targeted by no less than 4 prediction tools, or experimentally confirmed were classified 360 as mitochondria-targeted genes. Peroxisome-targeted genes were defined as genes predicted to be 361 transported to the peroxisome by no less than one prediction tool. All gene model names from the B73 362 genome were translated to W22 using the Translate Gene Model IDs tool from MaizeGDB (Andorf et 363 al., 2016).

364 To generate the list of genes related to stress response, signal transduction, and structural 365 components of the ribosome and proteasome, we predicted the function terms of proteins by Gene 366 Ontology based on protein sequences provided by the maize W22v2 genome annotation (Springer et 367 al., 2018; Wang et al., 2013). In brief, this method integrated profile-sequence alignment, profile-368 profile alignment, and domain co-occurrence networks to predict protein function at different levels of 369 complexity (Wang et al., 2013). Genes of interest were selected by their predicted GO terms using 370 customized perl scripts.

### 371 **4.4. RT-qPCR analysis**

372 Reverse transcription was performed using the RNA-to-cDNA kit (4387406; Applied Biosystems, 373 USA) following the manufacturer's instructions, using random octamers and oligo dT-16 as primers, 374 500 ng of total RNA as template, and 1 µL 100× diluted ERCC (External RNA Controls Consortium) 375 RNA Spike-In Mix (4456740; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) as an internal control. RT-qPCR was 376 performed using the PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (A25776; Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a 377 StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). PCR conditions were as follows: 378 50°C for 2 min, 94°C for 2 min, 40 cycles of 94°C for 15 s, 55°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 1 min, 379 followed by melt curve stage 95°C for 15 s, and 60°C for 1 min with ramp temperature 0.3‒95°C. Five 380 different dosage effect categories were identified based on RNA sequencing results, and from each of 381 these, one gene was randomly chosen for RT-qPCR analysis. *Zm00004b005358* (direct *cis* dosage 382 effect) and *Zm00004b003241* (*cis* dosage compensation) are TB-1La *cis* genes. *Zm00004b019722* (no 383 *trans* effect), *Zm00004b018088* (inverse *trans* effect), and *Zm00004b013771* (direct *trans* effect) are 384 TB-1La *trans* genes. ERCC00042 was used as an internal control to calculate relative transcript amount using the 2<sup>−</sup>∆∆CT 385 method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Three technical replicates were carried out for 386 each sample.
