# Cytosolic acetyl-CoA promotes histone acetylation predominantly at H3K27 in *Arabidopsis*

Chen Chen<sup>1,2</sup>, Chenlong Li<sup>1,3</sup>, Ying Wang<sup>1</sup>, Justin Renaud<sup>1</sup>, Gang Tian<sup>1</sup>, Shrikaar Kambhampati<sup>1,2</sup>, Behnaz Saatian<sup>1,2</sup>, Vi Nguyen<sup>1</sup>, Abdelali Hannoufa<sup>1,2</sup>, Frédéric Marsolais<sup>1,2</sup>, Ze-Chun Yuan<sup>1</sup>, Kangfu Yu<sup>4</sup>, Ryan S. Austin<sup>1,2</sup>, Jun Liu<sup>1,5</sup>, Susanne E. Kohalmi<sup>1,2</sup>, Keqiang Wu<sup>1,6</sup>, Shangzhi Huang<sup>3</sup> and Yuhai Cui<sup>1,2\*</sup>

Acetyl-coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA) is a central metabolite and the acetyl source for protein acetylation, particularly histone acetylation that promotes gene expression. However, the effect of acetyl-CoA levels on histone acetylation status in plants remains unknown. Here, we show that malfunctioned cytosolic acetyl-CoA carboxylase1 (ACC1) in *Arabidopsis* leads to elevated levels of acetyl-CoA and promotes histone hyperacetylation predominantly at lysine 27 of histone H3 (H3K27). The increase of H3K27 acetylation (H3K27ac) is dependent on adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-citrate lyase which cleaves citrate to acetyl-CoA in the cytoplasm, and requires histone acetyltransferase GCN5. A comprehensive analysis of the transcriptome and metabolome in combination with the genome-wide H3K27ac profiles of *acc1* mutants demonstrate the dynamic changes in H3K27ac, gene transcripts and metabolites occurring in the cell by the increased levels of acetyl-CoA. This study suggests that H3K27ac is an important link between cytosolic acetyl-CoA level and gene expression in response to the dynamic metabolic environments in plants.

hromatin states are regulated through dynamic modification of histones, such as acetylation and methylation<sup>1</sup>. Histone acetylation has critical roles in global regulation of chromatin architecture and gene expression<sup>2,3</sup>. The level of histone acetylation is dynamically regulated through the antagonistic actions between histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs)<sup>4</sup>, and also affected by energy and metabolic states of cells<sup>5,6</sup>. Fluctuation of cellular acetyl-CoA levels affects histone acetylation in yeast and mammalian cells at multiple lysine residues of histone (H3 and H4) tails<sup>7–11</sup>. In yeast, acetyl-CoA levels change periodically in association with growth phase switching<sup>9</sup>. In mammalian cells, the nuclear-cytosolic acetyl-CoA pool is fuelled by at least three pathways: cleavage of citrate, dehydration of pyruvate and conversion of acetylcarnitine<sup>8,12,13</sup>.

In Arabidopsis, cytosolic acetyl-CoA is mainly produced by ATP-citrate lyase (ACL), a heteromeric enzyme composed by two subunits, ACLA and ACLB<sup>14</sup>. Reduction of ACL activity leads to complex bonsai phenotypes<sup>15</sup>. Cytosolic acetyl-CoA can either be converted to malonyl-CoA by acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACCase) to synthesize very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs), flavonoids and malonyl derivatives or condensed to acetoacetyl-CoA for synthesiz-ing precursors of isoprenoids<sup>16,17</sup>. Two forms of ACCase have been found to date, the heteromeric ACCase located in the plastid that is responsible for de novo fatty acids synthesis and the homomeric ACCase in the cytosol that carboxylates acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA for elongating the plastid-produced fatty acids. Two tandem genes, *ACC1* and *ACC2*, encode two homomeric ACCase isoforms in *Arabidopsis*<sup>18</sup>. Although both *ACC1* and *ACC2* show ubiquitous

expression, no visible phenotype has been observed in *acc2* mutants and depleting *ACC1* causes embryo development arrest<sup>19,20</sup>.

Here we report a new *acc1* allele identified from a previous genetic screening<sup>21-23</sup>. We detected increased levels of acetyl-CoA in the *acc1* mutant. Immunoblotting and genome-wide profiling of histone acetylation show that histone hyperacetylation in *acc1* mutants occur predominantly at H3K27. The increased deposition of H3K27ac is dependent on ACL sourced acetyl-CoA and the activity of histone acetyltransferase GCN5. Further transcriptome and metabolome analyses reveal the dynamic changes of H3K27ac, gene transcripts and metabolites occurring in the cell by the increased level of acetyl-CoA. Taken together, these results indicate that acetylation at H3K27 serves as an important link between the dynamic metabolic environments and gene expression in plants.

**Identification of a new** *acc1* **mutant allele.** Visual screens of an *Arabidopsis* EMS-mutagenized population uncovered a mutant *p1045* that shows defective cotyledons, deformed leaves and short primary roots after germination (Fig 1a–k). The *p1045* mutation is a recessive mutation and mapped to the 31st exon of *ACC1* by a whole genome sequencing-based approach<sup>24</sup> (Supplementary Fig. 1). The mutation was predicted to cause a single amino acid substitution from aspartic acid to asparagine (Supplementary Fig. 2a). To confirm that *p1045* is allelic to *ACC1*, we first checked the phenotypes of two other *acc1* mutants: *gk101* (renamed hereafter as *acc1-4*), an EMS mutant of *Ler* ecotype<sup>20</sup>, and SALK\_055428 (renamed as *acc1-6*), a T-DNA insertion line (Supplementary Fig. 2h). **Increased accumulation of acetyl-CoA and histone H3K27 acetylation in** *acc1* **mutants.** Given the role of ACC1 in metabolizing acetyl-CoA, we first examined whether the level of acetyl-CoA is altered in *acc1* mutants using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). As shown in Fig. [2a,](#page-2-0) the levels of acetyl-CoA were significantly higher in both *acc1-5* and *acc1-4* than in wild-type (WT) plants. To assess whether the elevated concentration of acetyl-CoA affects histone acetylation, we analysed the levels of histone acetylation at eight lysine residues of histone tails in *acc1-5* and WT by immunoblotting. Significantly increased acetylation was detected at H3K27, but not at the other seven lysine residues in *acc1-5* compared with WT (Fig. [2b](#page-2-0) and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Similar change was also detected in *acc1-4* (Fig. [2b](#page-2-0) and Supplementary Fig. 4a).

Genome-wide profiling of H3K27ac and other histone acetylation marks in acc1-5 mutant and WT plants. For each ChIP-seq experiment, we conducted two biological replicates (correlation plots between two replicates are shown in Supplementary Fig. 6a). We also compared H3K27ac target genes identified in WT (Supplementary Dataset 1) with those identified in a previous ChIP-chip study<sup>28</sup> and found that 87.6% of the previously identified genes were re-identified in our work (Supplementary Fig. 6b). This result is consistent with the immunoblotting estimations of H3K27ac levels in acc1-5 shown in Fig. 2b. ChIP-seq experiments were then performed in WT and *acc1-5* to examine whether the mutation of *ACC1* also affects genomewide histone acetylation patterns at the other seven lysine residues (H3K9, H3K14, H3K18, H4K5, H4K8, H4K12, and H4K16). All the ChIP-seq data show high reproducibility between the two biological replicates (Supplementary Fig. 8). The genome-wide enrichments of each of the seven histone acetylation marks were analysed and no significant differences between WT and *acc1-5* were observed (Supplementary Fig. 9). These results are consistent with immunoblotting estimations of acetylation levels at these seven lysine residues of histone H3 and H4 in *acc1-5* as shown in Fig. [2b.](#page-2-0) However, at the specific genomic locus level, we did identify some genomic regions and genes that show elevated enrichment of acetylation in *acc1-5* compared with that in WT (Supplementary Fig. 10), However, the numbers of genes (from 14 to 46) are much less than that affected by H3K27ac (Supplementary Dataset 3). Collectively, our findings suggest that elevated cytosolic acetyl-CoA levels in *acc1* causes increased histone acetylation over hundreds of genomic loci but predominantly at H3K27.

**Genome-wide profile of H3K27ac in the** *acc1-5 acla-1i* **conditional double mutant.** The immunoblotting results (Fig. [2e\)](#page-2-0) show that increased H3K27ac in *acc1-5* are dependent on the availability of cytosolic acetyl-CoA. To investigate whether genome-wide increase in H3K27ac in *acc1-5* is indeed a result of the elevated accumulation of acetyl-CoA, we performed H3K27ac ChIP-seq analyses with the *acc1-5 acla-1i-1* conditional double mutant (correlation plots between two replicates are shown in Supplementary Fig. 11). As controls, mock-treated *acc1-5 acla-1i-1* double mutant and WT Col plants were also examined in parallel. After a 24hour induction, we observed that the H3K27ac level in *acc1-5 acla-1i-1* dropped back to a level closer to that in WT either on the genomewide scale (Fig. [4a](#page-5-0)) or on the 802 hyperacetylated genes (Fig. [4b](#page-5-0)). ChIP-seq data for the selected genes are shown in Fig. [4c](#page-5-0), and validated by ChIP-qPCR (Fig. [4d](#page-5-0) and Supplementary Fig. 12). This result is consistent with our earlier observation that there were decreased levels of acetyl-CoA in the induced conditional double mutant relative to the *acc1-5* single mutant (Fig. [2d\)](#page-2-0). These findings further support a direct link between the accumulation of acetyl-CoA and the increase of H3K27ac in *acc1-5*.

**GCN5/HAG1 is required for the H3K27 hyperacetylation in**  *acc1-5***.** Next we investigated the mechanism whereby the histone hyperacetylation is achieved in response to the elevated levels of acetyl-CoA in *acc1-5*. The status of histone acetylation in a cell is determined by the balance between the activities of HATs and HDACs. We first examined whether the genes encoding HATs and HDACs are up- or downregulated in *acc1*. Transcript levels of the 12 HAT genes and the 18 HDAC gene[s3](#page-9-2)[,29](#page-9-26) were measured and no significant difference was detected between WT and *acc1-5* (Supplementary Fig. 13). We then took a pharmacological approach to test whether the activities of existing HATs are required for the hyperacetylation of H3K27 in *acc1-5*. After treated with the inhibitor (MB-3)[30](#page-9-27)[,31,](#page-9-28) the growth of *acc1-5* seedling was arrested (Fig. [5a](#page-6-0)) and the levels of H3K27ac on all the selected genes were decreased (Fig. [5b](#page-6-0) and Supplementary Fig. 14a). These results suggest that HAT activities are required for the increased H3K27ac in *acc1-5*.

The 12 HATs are grouped into four subfamilies (GNAT, MYST, TAFII250 and p300/CBP[\)3](#page-9-2) . The two MYST subfamily HATs were shown to specifically acetylate histone 4[32](#page-9-29)[,33,](#page-9-30) thus are unlikely to be involved in the hyperacetylation of H3K27 in *acc1-5*. Among the five members in the p300/CBP subfamily, two (HAC2 and HAC4) were shown to lack activity[32](#page-9-29)[,34.](#page-9-31) Therefore, we searched mutant resources for the remaining eight HATs and were able to find previously identified mutants (T-DNA insertion lines and chemically induced) for six of the eight HAT genes, *HAC1*, *HAC5* and *HAC12* (*p300/CBP* subfamily), and GCN5/HAG1, HAC7/HAG2 and ELP3/ HAG3 (GNAT subfamily). A H3K27ac western blotting analysis was performed on all the six mutants and only *hag1-6* (SALK\_150784, a T-DNA insertion line of *GCN5/HAG1*) [35](#page-9-32) showed notable reduction in H3K27ac, suggesting the potential involvement of GCN5/ HAG1 in hyperacetylation of H3K27 (Supplementary Fig. 15a,b). To examine whether GCN5 participates in acetyl-CoA-promoted hyperacetylation of H3K27 in *acc1*, we crossed *hag1-6* with *acc1-5* to generate double mutants and found less leaf fusion in *acc1-5 hag1-6* than in *acc1-5* (Fig. [5c](#page-6-0)). It suggests that the leaf fusion phenotype of *acc1-5* is dependent on *GCN5*. The acetyl-CoA-induced increases in H3K27ac at the six tested genes in *acc1-5* were significantly reduced or abolished in *acc1-5 hag1-6* (Fig. [5d](#page-6-0) and Supplementary Fig. 14b). In addition, we also crossed the other five HAT mutants (except that of ELP3/HAG3, for which pollination was unsuccessful because of severe floral defects in that mutant[36](#page-9-33)) individually with *acc1-5*. The H3K27ac levels in each of the double mutants were examined at selected genes and found to be unchanged compared with that in *acc1-5* (Supplementary Fig. 15c–f). These results suggest that GCN5 is a key HAT involved in sensing the acetyl-CoA levels and the hyperacetylation of H3K27 in *acc1-5*.

**Transcriptome and metabolome analyses in** *acc1-5***.** To assess the effects of increased H3K27ac on global gene transcription, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis in WT and *acc1-5*. More upregulated than downregulated genes were found in *acc1- 5* (1298 versus 561, twofold cutoff with *P<*0.001) (Supplementary Dataset 4). Gene ontology analysis indicates that upregulated genes in *acc1-5* are enriched in reactive oxygen species metabolism, response to jasmonic acid, amino acid metabolism and flavonoid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Dataset 4). Furthermore, the 802 genes that were hyperacetylated at H3K27 in *acc1-5* showed significantly higher transcript levels in *acc1-5* than in WT (Fig. [6a](#page-7-0)). We validated the RNA-seq data by qRT-PCR for the selected genes (Fig. [6b\)](#page-7-0). Quantitative RT–PCR was also performed in the *acc1-5 acla-1i-1* conditional double mutants. As shown in Fig. [6b,](#page-7-0) repressing *ACLA-1* in the *acc1-5* background by induced microRNA expression (*acc1-5 acla-1i-1*) could restore the expression of most of the selected genes to WT levels (Fig. [6b\)](#page-7-0).

In the gene ontology results presented in Fig. [3e,](#page-3-0) H3K27 hyperacetylated genes are enriched in primary metabolic processes including amino acid biosynthesis. Consistently, we found in our transcriptomic data that 22 amino acid biosynthesis genes showed at least twofold (*P*<0.001) increase in *acc1-5* (Fig. [6c\)](#page-7-0). These data lead us to examine changes in accumulation of compounds within primary metabolism and related pathways. #### Discussion

Recent studies in animal and yeast cells have demonstrated a dynamic interplay between metabolic processes and gene expression through the modification of chromatin<sup>8,9,11,38</sup>. Acetyl-CoA not only serves as a central metabolic intermediate, but also provides the acetyl group necessary for post-translational protein acetylation, thereby regulating enzymatic activities and chromatin structures<sup>39,40</sup>. Knockdown of cytosolic ACCase in yeast and mouse hepatocytes caused the hyperacetylation of histones and extramitochondrial proteins, respectively<sup>10,41</sup>. Conversely, knocking down *ACL* led to hypoacetylation of histone H2B, H3 and H4 in mammalian cells<sup>8</sup>. However, the relationship between cytosolic acetyl-CoA and his-

tone acetylation has not been reported in plants. The identification of *acc1-5*, a moderate mutant allele, allowed us to study the changes in acetyl-CoA levels and their influence on histone acetylation in plants. Our genome-wide histone acetylation profiling and transcriptomic data demonstrate that increased cytosolic acetyl-CoA in *acc1* mutants contributes to gene activation by promoting histone hyperacetylation mainly at H3K27. Thus, H3K27 appears to be more sensitive to the increased concentration of acetyl-CoA than other lysine residues of histone tails in plants. This differs from yeast and mammalian cells, where multiple lysine residues of histone tails are hyperacetylated when there is an increase in the levels of cytosolic acetyl-CoA or the ratio of acetyl-CoA/CoA<sup>8-10</sup>. Our work has filled a major gap in understanding how cytosolic acetyl-CoA level could affect histone acetylation in plants (Fig. 6f). It is of note that our promoter reporter results (Fig. 1r–w) show that *ACC1* expression is

These observations suggest that the abundance of acetyl-CoA may serve as a metabolic signal in coordinating metabolic state with gene transcription during plant growth and development. On another note, a recent work showed that treatment with acetate can improve drought tolerance<sup>42</sup>, which is likely to result from acetyl-CoA-promoted histone acetylation on genes in the jasmonic acid signalling pathway<sup>42</sup>. In our RNA-seq data, the genes involved in jasmonic acid metabolism and response are also upregulated in *acc1-5* (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Dataset 4). These observations suggest that acetyl-CoA regulated histone acetylation play a crucial role in plant response to environmental stresses and warrant further investigation.

Histone acetyltransferases play a key role in adding acetyl groups to lysine residues of histones. In mammalian cells, histone acetylation can be regulated by the acetyl-CoA level<sup>8</sup>. However, it is still unclear which acetyltransferases are involved in this process. In this work, we found that knocking out histone acetyltransferase GCN5 partially suppresses *acc1-5* phenotypes (Fig. 5c) and GCN5 activity is required to promote the hyperacetylation of H3K27 on a number of genomic loci in *acc1-5* (Fig. 5d). This involvement of GCN5 (and the related protein complex) in acetyl-CoA-promoted global histone acetylation is similar to what was reported in yeast cells<sup>9</sup>. In *Arabidopsis*, it was reported that GCN5 not only promotes H3K27ac on light-responsive genes<sup>43</sup>, but also catalyses histone acetylation at other lysine residues such as K9, K14 and K36 of H3<sup>44,45</sup>. The specific change in the H3K27ac level in *acc1-5* may be due to post-translational modifications on components of the GCN5 protein complex and the amount of acetyl-CoA. It was reported previously that the concentration of acetyl-CoA can alter the specificity of acetyltransferases<sup>46,47</sup>. In addition, we also found that the H3K27ac levels at two genomic loci in *acc1-5 hag1-6* are still significantly higher than that in WT (Fig. 5d), suggesting that other histone acetyltransferases might be also involved in promoting H3K27ac in *acc1* mutant. Thus, further investigations are needed to identify other acetyltransferases involved.

Our metabolome-profiling experiments revealed the over accumulation of amino acids in *acc1-5* (Fig. 6e). This change is consistent with the increased H3K27ac over, and the transcript levels of, the amino acid biosynthesis-related genes (Fig. 6c). In addition, overaccumulation of isocitrate and  $\alpha$ -ketoglutarate in *acc1-5* (Supplementary Fig. 12) suggests that disrupting the carboxylation of cytosolic acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA might channel the carbon flow to amino acids biosynthesis. This metabolic reprograming may provide an alternative way for carbon storage in *acc1-5*.

**Plant material and growth conditions.** The *acc1-4* and *elo3* mutants were described previously<sup>20,36</sup>. Seeds of SALK\_055428 (*acc1-6*), SALK\_150784 (*hag1-6*)<sup>35</sup>, SALK\_051832 C (*hac7*), SALK\_082116 (*hac1*), SALK\_074472 (*hac5*) and SALK\_052490 (*hac12*) were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC) at the Ohio State University. All *Arabidopsis* seeds used are in the Columbia (Col-0) background, except *acc1-4*, which is in the Landsberg erecta (Ler-0) background. To induce artificial microRNA, 11-day-old seedlings were transferred into medium with or without 17 $\beta$ -estradiol for 24 h. For inhibitor treatment, seedlings were geminated in ½ MS medium for 3 days and then transferred into media in the absence or presence of 100  $\mu$ M MB-3 ( $\alpha$ -methylene- $\gamma$ -butyrolactone, Sigma)<sup>30</sup>. **Transgene constructs.** To express tagged versions of ACC1, the 2 kb promoter and the genomic region of *ACC1* was PCR amplified from genomic DNA and cloned into the Gateway entry Vector pDONR221 (Invitrogen) by a BP reaction. LR reactions were then performed with the destination vectors[48](#page-10-1) pMDC107 and pMDC163 to generate binary vectors carrying the fusion constructs with *GFP* (*ProACC1:ACC1–GFP*) and *GUS* (*ProACC1:ACC1–GUS*). The synthesized fragment was cloned into pDONR221 by a BP reaction and a LR reaction was conducted with the destination vecto[r48](#page-10-1) pMDC7. Primers used for plasmid constructions are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

**GUS staining and microscopy.** GUS staining was performed following a previously described metho[d50.](#page-10-3) After staining, seedlings were transferred to glass slides and photographed using stereo microscopy (Zeiss). To detect green fluorescence signal, root tips were cut from 7-day-old seedlings and transferred onto glass slides with 50μl propidium iodide (PI) solution (1μgml–1). The green fluorescence was detected by confocal microscopy (Leica) with excitation at 488nm and emission at 505nm to 525nm.

**Acetyl-CoA measurement.** Acetyl-CoA levels were measured using a previously described method with minor modification[s51.](#page-10-4) Briefly, 100mg of frozen *Arabidopsis* seedlings at 12 days after germination (DAG) were homogenized and 300μl of ice cold 5% perchloric acid (PCA) was immediately added. After brief vortexing and centrifugation at 18,000*g* for 5min at 4 °C, 150μl of supernatant was collected. All the rest of the steps were performed as previously described, including the HPLC gradient and conditions[51](#page-10-4). The peak area was quantified using Agilent ChemStation software (Agilent Technologies). To measure acetyl-CoA levels during a day, 12 DAG seedlings were collected at 2h, 6h, and 10h after sunrise and 2h, 6h, and 10h after sunset (corresponding to ZT (Zeitgeber) time 2, 6, 10, 14, 18, and 22, respectively).

**Analysis of gene transcript levels.** Total RNAs were extracted from *Arabidopsis* seedlings grown on ½ MS medium for 12 DAG using the RNeasy Plant Total RNA Isolation kit (Qiagen). Genomic DNA was removed by treating with DNase TURBO (Ambion). For qRT–PCR, the first-strand cDNA was synthesized from 200ng of total RNA with random primer in a 20μl reaction volume using cDNA synthesis kit (ABI). Quantitative PCR was performed with a CFX96 Real-time PCR Instrument (Bio-Rad) using the SsoFast EvaGreen Supermix kit in a volume of 10μl. *ACT7* was used as the internal reference. Primers for qPCR can be found in Supplementary Table 2. To measure the transcript levels of *ACC1* during a day, plant materials were collected with the same way as those for acetyl-CoA measurement. RNA-seq was performed as previously described[52.](#page-10-5) Briefly, libraries were prepared using the TruSeq RNA sample preparation kit (Illumina) following the manufacture's protocol. All the libraries were sequenced on Illumina Hiseq 2500 platform using a paired-end scheme (2×50bp) with TruSeq v3 chemistry. The sequencing reads were mapped to TAIR10 version of the *Arabidopsis thaliana* genome using Topha[t53](#page-10-6) version 1.4.0 with default setting, except that the minimum and maximum intron length of 20bp and 4000bp were required, respectively. Then, mapped reads were assembled according to TAIR10 version of genome annotation using cufflink[s54](#page-10-7) version 2.1.1 with default settings. To analyse differential expression, the assembled transcripts from three independent biological replicates in Col and *acc1-5* were included and compared by using cuffdiff[54](#page-10-7) version 2.1.1 with default setting. Genes with at least twofold change in expression (*P<*0.001) were considered to be differentially expressed.

**Western blotting.** The 12 DAG seedlings grown on ½ MS medium were ground to fine powder in liquid nitrogen and dissolved in 3× SDS loading buffer. The mixture was sonicated for 15 seconds with power 30 (Fisher scientific model-50) and then boiled for 10min. Insoluble materials were removed by centrifugation for 10min at 15,000*g*. The supernatants were then used for western blot using the following antibodies: anti-H3, ab1791 (Abcam); anti-H3K9ac, 07-352 (Millipore); anti-H3K14ac, 07-353 (Millipore); anti-H3K18ac, 07-354 (Millipore); anti-H3K27ac, 07-360 (Millipore); anti-H4K5ac, 07-327 (Millipore); anti-H4K8ac, 07-328 (Millipore); anti-H4K12ac, 07-595 (Millipore); anti-H4K16ac, 07-329 (Millipore). Three independent extracts were performed for all the samples and the intensities of blotting signals were quantified by using ImageJ software. The average intensities from three independent extracts are shown in Fig. 2d,e and Supplementary Figs 15a and 18c.

**Chromatin immunoprecipitation.** ChIP was performed as previously describe[d55,](#page-10-8)[56](#page-10-9) with minor changes. Briefly, 2 g of 12 DAG *Arabidopsis* seedlings grown on ½ MS medium were collected and cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 15min under vacuum and then ground into fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Chromatin was isolated and sheared into 200–800bp fragments by sonication. The sonicated chromatin was incubated with 5μl antibody or rabbit IgG (ab171870,

Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. All the antibodies used for ChIP were the same as those for western blotting. The ChIP DNA was used for Illumina single-end (1×50bp) sequencing or qPCR. ChIP-qPCR was performed with three biological replicates, and the results were calculated as a percentage of input DNA or normalized with histone H3 signals at the same genomic loci. The primers used for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table 2. One region (*PLT7-2*) without H3K27ac modification was used as a negative control. To collect enough plant material from the *acc1-5 hag1- 6* double mutants for the H3K27ac ChIP assay, for each biological replicate, we identified around 50 *acc1-5 hag1-6* double mutant seedlings from ~800 progenies of the *acc1-5*<sup>+</sup>/– *hag1-6*<sup>+</sup>/– double heterozygous parental plants by PCR and sequencingbased genotyping. Since both *acc1-5* and *hag1-6* mutant plants fail to set seeds, we had to make crosses between the *acc1-5***<sup>+</sup>/–** and *hag1-6***<sup>+</sup>/–** parental plants and select double heterozygous plants (*acc1-5***<sup>+</sup>/–** *hag1-6***<sup>+</sup>/–**) to propagate seeds.

**ChIP-seq and data analysis.** Ten nanograms of ChIP DNA were used for library preparation using the Illumina Genomic DNA Sample Prep kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. Two biological replicates were prepared and sequenced for each ChIP-seq experiment. An Illumina HiSeq 2500 instrument was used for highthroughput sequencing of the ChIP-seq libraries. The raw sequence reads were aligned to the TAIR10 assembly using the Bowtie program (Illumina[\)57](#page-10-10) with the default setting. Only perfectly and uniquely mapped reads were retained for further analysis. A summary of the number of reads for each of the replicates is given in Supplementary Table 3. To determine the correlation between biological replicates, Pearson correlation was computed using deepTools[258](#page-10-11) on normalized signal intensity. Then the MACS progra[m59](#page-10-12) was used to convert mapped and mixed reads (mix reads from both replicates and all the reads were extended 100bp) to wiggle (WIG) format, which could be visualized with the Integrated Genome Browser (IGB[\)60](#page-10-13). The SICER program[61](#page-10-14) (window size=200, gap size=200, false discovery rate (FDR)≤0.01) was used to call acetylation enriched domain and quantitatively compare acetylation levels in WT and *acc1-5*. The acetylation enriched regions were converted to gene list using the PeakAnalyzer progra[m62](#page-10-15). For individual histone modification, two separate gene lists were generated from each replicate and only the common genes from both replicates were picked for the final gene list. The final lists (Supplementary Information) were manually checked to avoid false positives caused by the 200bp window size used in the SICER program and incorrect conversion in the PeakAnalyzer program. Gene ontology analysis was performed by using the web-based GOEAST tool[63](#page-10-16) with the default settings. All the genome-wide occupancies were plotted using deepTools[258](#page-10-11) software with the same number (WT and *acc1-5*) of mixed reads (mixed reads from both replicates) from each experiment. The reads are as follows: 14 million reads for H3K9ac, H3K14ac, H3K18ac, H4K5ac, H4K8ac, H4K12ac and H4K16ac; 5.5 million reads for H3K27ac (including samples of WT, *acc1-5*, *acc1-5 acla-1i-1* mock, and *acc1-5 acla-1i-1* induced).

**Metabolites extraction and metabolome profiling.** Fifty milligrams of 12 DAG WT and *acc1-5* seedlings were collected and ground in liquid N2 using a ball-mill. The fine powders were suspended in 1ml of ice cooled methanol:water (4:1) by vortexing. The mixtures were sonicated in water bath sonicator for 15min followed by centrifugation at 11,000*g* for 10min at 4 °C. A 700 µl bolus of the supernatant was transferred into fresh tubes and evaporated to dryness using a vacufuge at ambient temperature. The residue was re-dissolved in 400 µl 1:1 mixture of methanol:water and vortexed vigorously. All samples were filtered using 0.2μm PTFE syringe filter (Whatman). Five microlitres of 1 µgml–1 13C6 phenylalanine internal standard (Cambridge Isotopes) were added to all samples. Seven replicates for both WT and *acc1-5* were prepared and subjected to the following LC-MS metabolomic analysis. In detail, samples were transferred into 150 µl vial inserts and placed into amber glass HPLC vials and 5 µl were injected into an Agilent 1290 HPLC coupled to a Q-Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Compounds were resolved using a SeQuant ZIC-HILIC column: 3.5 µm, 100Å, 100×2.1mm (EMD Millipore) with mobile phase 5mM ammonium acetate, pH 4.00 (A); B=90% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) and the following gradient: 87% B for 5min, decreased to 55% over 8min and held for 4min before returning to 87% over 3min. Mass ranges of 93–1400*m/z* and 65–975*m/z* were analysed by full MS at 140,000 resolution in the positive and negative ionization modes respectively. The automatic gain control (AGC) and maximum injection time (IT) were 3×106 and 524 ms respectively. The heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source was operated with sheath gas, 30 arbitrary units; auxiliary gas, 8 arbitrary units; probe temperature, 450 °C; capillary temperature 250 °C; and S-lens RF level, 60. Spray voltage was 3.9 kV and 3.5 kV in the positive and the negative ionization modes respectively. The top 12 data-dependent acquisition experiments were also conducted for one sample of each group to generate MS/MS datasets for compound identification. This involved a full MS scan at 35,000 resolution, AGC target 3×106 , maximum IT 128 ms and MS/MS scans at 17,500 resolution, AGC target 1×106 , maximum injection time 64 ms, 1.0m/z isolation window, 30 normalized collision energy (NCE), intensity threshold 1.6×105 and 10 s dynamic exclusion.

**Metabolome data analysis.** Following data acquisition, Thermo.RAW files were converted to.mzml format using ProteoWizard[64](#page-10-17) with peak peaking filter applied. Files were imported into R using the XCMS package[37.](#page-9-34) Features were detected

with the centWave[65](#page-10-18) method and a p.p.m. tolerance of 1.0. The pre-filter was set to six scans with a minimum 5,000 intensity, the signal to noise threshold was 5 and noise was set to 3×106 and 1×106 for the positive and the negative modes respectively. Retention time correction was conducted using the obiwarp[66](#page-10-19) method; the grouping included features present in at least 25% of all samples, the allowable retention time deviation was 10 s, and the *m/z* width was set to 0.015. The 'fillPeaks' function with default settings and remaining zeros imputed with twothirds the minimum value on a per mass basis. Salt clusters and other ionization artefacts were removed from the feature list using the McMillan correction without retention time filter applie[d44](#page-9-42). Compounds were identified by accurate mass, comparison of retention times to authentic standards or by accurate mass and comparison of fragmentation patterns to MS/MS databases.
