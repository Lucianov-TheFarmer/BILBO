Plant immunity must be strictly controlled to balance the trade-off between defense and growth. #### SUMMARY

Programmed cell death (PCD) and immunity in plants are tightly controlled to promote antimicrobial defense while preventing autoimmunity. However, the mechanisms contributing to this immune homeostasis are poorly understood. Here, we isolated a rice mutant ebr1 (enhanced blight and blast resistance 1) that shows enhanced broad-spectrum bacterial and fungal disease resistance, but displays spontaneous PCD, autoimmunity, and stunted growth. EBR1 encodes an E3 ubiquitin ligase that interacts with OsBAG4, which belongs to the BAG (Bcl-2 associated athanogene) family that functions in cell death, growth arrest, and immune responses in mammals. EBR1 directly targets OsBAG4 for ubiquitination-mediated degradation. Elevated levels of OsBAG4 in rice are necessary and sufficient to trigger PCD and enhanced disease resistance to pathogenic infection, most likely by activating pathogen-associated molecular patterns-triggered immunity (PTI). Together, our study suggests that an E3-BAG module orchestrates innate immune homeostasis and coordinates the trade-off between defense and growth in plants.

### INTRODUCTION

During coevolution with pathogens, plants have developed a set of defense strategies to cope with biotic stress, with an innate immune machinery consisting of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)-triggered immunity (PTI) conferred by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), and pathogen-effectorstriggered immunity (ETI) conferred by race-specific resistance (R) proteins [\(Spoel and Dong, 2012](#page-12-0)). Both PTI and ETI can induce cell death, the so-called hypersensitive response (HR), a type of programmed cell death (PCD) that prevents the spread of the pathogen inside the plant. In particular, ETI is often accompanied by rapid PCD [\(Coll et al., 2011\)](#page-11-0). Different PCD mechanisms play an integral role in plant defense [\(Zebell and Dong, 2015\)](#page-12-1), and misregulation of host defense usually triggers autoimmunity and PCD (Alca´ [zar and Parker, 2011; Cheng et al., 2011; Stutt](#page-11-1)[mann et al., 2016](#page-11-1)). Therefore, autoimmunity and the associated PCD must be strictly controlled to mitigate a trade-off between defense and growth. Considerable efforts have been made to understand the plant PCD pathways by using lesion mimic (*lm*) mutants in which PCD is misregulated. Many *lm* mutants exhibit autoimmunity phenotypes in the absence of pathogens, and more than a dozen *LM* genes have been identified ([Lorrain](#page-11-2) [et al., 2003; Bruggeman et al., 2015\)](#page-11-2), which encode diverse proteins [\(B](#page-11-3)u[schges et al., 1997; Dietrich et al., 1997; Gray et al.,](#page-11-3) € [1997; Yamanouchi et al., 2002; Zeng et al., 2004; Tong et al.,](#page-11-3) [2012\)](#page-11-3). However, the regulatory mechanisms of plant PCD and autoimmunity remain largely unknown, other than extensive studies on R protein regulation [\(Kawasaki et al., 2005; Alca´](#page-11-4) zar [and Parker, 2011; Cheng et al., 2011; Cheng and Li, 2012; Gou](#page-11-4) [et al., 2012; Huang et al., 2014, 2016; Stuttmann et al., 2016](#page-11-4)).

Bcl-2-associated athanogene (BAG) proteins function in diverse processes in mammals, including apoptosis, autoimmunity, and tumorigenesis [\(Takayama et al., 1995; Takayama and](#page-12-2) [Reed, 2001; Kabbage and Dickman, 2008](#page-12-2)). Plant genomes also encode BAG proteins [\(Doukhanina et al., 2006; Kang](#page-11-5) [et al., 2006\)](#page-11-5); however, only a few plant BAG proteins have been studied, which appear to function in cytoprotection under stress conditions and can inhibit PCD ([Kabbage and Dickman,](#page-11-6)

<span id="page-2-0"></span>[2008\)](#page-11-6). The *Arabidopsis atbag6* mutants show enhanced susceptibility to the necrotrophic fungus *Botrytis cinerea* ([Doukha](#page-11-5)[nina et al., 2006](#page-11-5)). Transgenic banana plants overexpressing *MusaBAG1* enhanced resistance to *Fusarium* wilt [\(Ghag et al.,](#page-11-7) [2014\)](#page-11-7). Interestingly, AtBAG6 cleavage by an aspartyl protease is necessary for autophagy and fungal resistance [\(Li et al.,](#page-11-8) [2016b\)](#page-11-8). Therefore, plant BAGs likely function as positive regulators of cell death and immunity.

Protein ubiquitination has emerged as a key mechanism in immunity both in plants and in animals, with E3 ligases as the key factors determining substrate specificity ([Bhoj and Chen, 2009;](#page-11-9) [Cheng and Li, 2012; Marino et al., 2012; Duplan and Rivas,](#page-11-9) [2014; Li et al., 2014](#page-11-9)). E3 ligases can be categorized depending on their structures: RING or U-box, HECT, and cullin-RING ligases (CRLs) ([Vierstra, 2009\)](#page-12-3). Several RING-type E3 ligases play important roles in plant immunity. For instance, the *Arabidopsis* E3 RIN2 and RIN3 interact with the R receptor RPM1, and the *rin2rin3* double mutant reduced RPM1 and RPS2-dependent HR ([Kawasaki et al., 2005\)](#page-11-4). The *Arabidopsis* EDR1 kinase is a negative regulator of PCD and interacts with the E3 ATL1, a positive regulator of PCD and disease resistance [\(Serrano](#page-12-4) [et al., 2014](#page-12-4)). Another RING-type E3 MIEL1 mediates degradation of MYB30 and attenuates autoimmunity [\(Marino et al., 2013\)](#page-12-5). E3 ligases also play important roles in immunity in the model crop rice. The rice RING-type E3 OsBBI1 positively regulates the HR and broad-spectrum resistance against *Magnaporthe oryzae* (*M. oryzae*) [\(Li et al., 2011](#page-11-10)). Another RING-type E3 XB3 is required for the accumulation of the rice PRR XA21 [\(Wang et al., 2006](#page-12-6)). The rice U-box E3 SPL11 negatively regulates cell death, defense, and flowering time ([Zeng et al., 2004; Li et al., 2012\)](#page-12-7). Another U-box E3 OsPUB15 is the substrate of the R protein PID2; transgenic rice plants overexpressing *OsPUB15* displayed PCD and defense activation [\(Wang et al., 2015\)](#page-12-8).

Although many E3 ligases are involved in plant PCD and immunity, in most cases, their targets and molecular mechanisms remain unknown [\(Cheng and Li, 2012; Marino et al., 2012\)](#page-11-11). Functional recognition of these E3 targets will undoubtedly provide insights into the molecular mechanisms of how the plant finetunes PCD to avoid autoimmunity and fitness cost under nonpathogenic conditions, which is also particularly important in molecular breeding for broad-spectrum disease resistance with high-yield potential in crops such as rice. Here, we report the isolation and mechanistic characterization of a rice mutant *ebr1* (*enhanced blight and blast resistance 1*), which displayed spontaneous PCD and non-race-specific resistance to bacterial blight caused by *Xanthomonas oryzae* pv. *oryzae* (*Xoo*) and fungal blast caused by *Magnaporthe oryzae* (*M. oryzae*). *EBR1* encodes a previously unknown RING-type E3, which interacts with a BAG family protein OsBAG4, leading to its ubiquitination and degradation. OsBAG4 accumulation in *ebr1* and OsBAG4 overexpression trigger autoimmunity and broad-spectrum disease resistance most likely through activating PTI. We demonstrate that the EBR1-OsBAG4 interaction module fine-tunes PCD, immunity, and defense cost in rice.

#### Map-Based and Microarray-Assisted Cloning of EBR1

For cloning of the *EBR1* gene, a large F2 mapping population was developed. We initially mapped the *EBR1* locus to the centromeric region of chromosome 5 and further narrowed the locus to a 2,800-kb region near the centromere, but could not finely locate the locus because of recombination block at the centromere ([Figure 2](#page-3-0)A). We then adopted a microarray strategy to screen for the *EBR1* candidate using the Affymetrix Rice GeneChip (Table S1). One candidate gene (LOC\_Os05 g19970) was located in the mapping interval and decreased expression in *ebr1*. Genomic sequencing revealed that the candidate gene had a single base substitution (T to A) in the 5<sup>0</sup> splice site of intron 3 in *ebr1* ([Figure 2B](#page-3-0)). We suspected that this mutation might disturb the correct splicing of the intron. Indeed, two longer cDNA products were amplified by RT-PCR in *ebr1*. Sequencing of the cDNAs revealed three types of transcripts in *ebr1*, of which two main transcripts were intron retained, one retained intron 3, and another retained 10 nt of the 5<sup>0</sup> portion of intron 3, resulting in premature termination of the coding region. The wild-type transcript was almost completely depleted in the *ebr1* mutant [\(Figure 2](#page-3-0)C). Western blotting using the anti-EBR1 antibody confirmed that EBR1 was hardly detected in *ebr1* ([Figure 2](#page-3-0)D), indicating that *ebr1* is a knockdown mutant. To verify that LOC\_Os05 g19970 is the corresponding *EBR1* gene, we carried out genetic complementation. All resulting transgenic plants (*pEBR1::EBR1*/*ebr1*) rescued the autoimmunity and growth phenotypes of *ebr1* ([Figures 2E](#page-3-0)–2I, S1C–S1E, and S2A–S2F). Therefore, we concluded that LOC\_Os05 g19970 is the functional *EBR1* gene.

#### EBR1 Encodes a RING-Type E3 Ligase

*EBR1* showed a distinct spatiotemporal expression pattern, with high expression in seedling leaves, roots, nodes, and anthers as detected by qRT-PCR (Figure S3F). The expression pattern was further confirmed with a b-glucuronidase (GUS) reporter fused to the *EBR1* promoter (-3 kb). Transgenic lines expressing the *EBR1::GUS* reporter showed a similar expression pattern (Figure S3G). Importantly, *EBR1* was induced by pathogen infection (Figure S3H), further supporting its role in rice immunity. *EBR1* encodes a protein of 853 amino acids with a C3HC4-type RING finger motif at the N terminus (amino acids 5–45). A BLAST search [\(http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi\)](http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) revealed EBR1 homologs in all sequenced plant genomes including *Arabidopsis*, wheat, maize, cotton, soybean, and poplar (Figures S4A and S4B). However, none of the plant homologs has been functionally characterized. EBR1 also contains four ZnF\_C2H2-like domains in the N terminus (Figure S5A). It is well-known that many C2H2 zinc finger proteins function as transcription factors. To determine whether EBR1 functions as a transcription factor, we performed a transcriptional activation assay using the rice zinc finger protein DST as a positive control [\(Huang et al.,](#page-11-14) [2009\)](#page-11-14). The result showed that EBR1, unlike DST, could not activate reporter gene expression (Figure S5B), suggesting that EBR1 is not a transcription factor.

The EBR1 homolog HEL2/Ydr266c in yeast functions as an E3 ligase [\(Singh et al., 2012; Duttler et al., 2013](#page-12-10)), suggesting that EBR1 might also have E3 activity. To test this, we expressed EBR1 fused to a glutathione S-transferase (GST) tag in *Escherichia coli*, with the *Arabidopsis* RING-type E3 SDIR1 as a positive control ([Zhang et al., 2007\)](#page-12-11). We found that GST-EBR1, like GST-AtSDIRI, could execute self-ubiquitination in an in vitro ubiquitination assay ([Figure 4A](#page-5-0)): clear protein ubiquitination was observed at 30 min after incubation (Figure S5C). These results demonstrate that EBR1 has E3 ligase activity. The RING finger motif is essential for E3 activity ([Zhang et al., 2007](#page-12-11)). Indeed, the N terminus of EBR1 with an intact RING domain was sufficient for E3 activity (Figure S5D), whereas a single amino acid substitution in the RING domain (H22Y) abolished its E3 activity [\(Figures 4](#page-5-0)A and S5E). Thus, EBR1 is a RING-type E3 ligase.

#### EBR1 Targets OsBAG4 for Ubiquitination Degradation

We next examined the degradation of endogenous OsBAG4 in rice cell extracts using a cell-free system as reported previously [\(Xu et al., 2012](#page-12-12)). As shown in [Figure 4](#page-5-0)F, MG132 (a proteasome inhibitor) treatment delayed OsBAG4 degradation, indicating that OsBAG4 was degraded by the 26S proteasome. Further, we found that OsBAG4 showed delayed degradation and accumulated to higher levels in *ebr1* mutant plants compared with wild-type plants ([Figures 4](#page-5-0)G and 4H), suggesting that EBR1 targets OsBAG4 for proteasomal degradation in planta. To further confirm that OsBAG4 is a substrate of EBR1 for ubiquitinationdependent degradation, we carried out an in vitro ubiquitination assay. We purified MBP-EBR1, which possesses E3 ligase activity, and MBP-EBR1H22Y, which has lost E3 ligase activity [\(Figures](#page-5-0) [4A](#page-5-0), S5E, and S5F), and developed transgenic rice constitutively expressing an OsBAG4-Flag fusion protein (OsBAG4-OX). We incubated lysates containing the OsBAG4-Flag fusion protein with wheat E1 and E2, His-ubiquitin, and either MBP-EBR1 or MBP-EBR1H22Y. Immunoprecipitation of OsBAG4-Flag using anti-Flag beads uncovered high molecular weight bands only in the reaction with MBP-EBR1, but not with MBP-EBR1H22Y [\(Figure 4](#page-5-0)I). This result strongly suggests that OsBAG4 was indeed polyubiquitinated by EBR1. Together, these findings clearly demonstrated that EBR1 and OsBAG4 form a protein module by which EBR1 ubiquitinates and controls the steadystate level of OsBAG4.

#### OsBAG4 Triggers Cell Death and Autoimmunity

The accumulation of OsBAG4 in *ebr1* led us to hypothesize that the increased level of OsBAG4 triggers PCD and autoimmunity. To test the hypothesis, we first engineered plants overexpressing *OsBAG4-Flag* (OsBAG4-OX) in the wild-type ZH11 [\(Figures](#page-7-0) [5A](#page-7-0) and S5G). We observed that all of the OsBAG4 overaccumulation lines displayed a PCD phenotype, associated with H2O2 accumulation, similar to that of *ebr1* ([Figures 5B](#page-7-0)–5D). More importantly, the OsBAG4-OX lines also enhanced resistance to both *Xoo* and *M. oryzae* [\(Figures 5](#page-7-0)E–5H and S1E). The overexpression of *OsBAG4* resulted in shorter plants, reduced tillers, smaller panicle, and decreased grain yield compared with the wild-type (Figures S6C–S6H), consistent with a trade-off between the OsBAG4-triggered defense and growth. The results indicate that OsBAG4 functions as a positive regulator of disease resistance, and its ectopic expression triggers PCD and autoimmunity.

#### EBR1 and OsBAG4 Form a Molecular Module Controlling Immune Homeostasis

The above results suggested that the accumulation of OsBAG4 is a major cause of the autoimmunity and disease resistance in *ebr1*. Therefore, decreasing *OsBAG4* expression might suppress the *ebr1* mutant phenotypes. We generated *OsBAG4- RNAi* plants in the *ebr1* background and selected two stable lines (OsBAG4-RNAi-1/*ebr1* and OsBAG4-RNAi-2/*ebr1*) for further experiments, in which OsBAG4 abundance was markedly reduced to the wild-type level [\(Figure 6](#page-8-0)A). The expression of other *OsBAG* genes was not changed (Figures S5H–S5L), indicating no off-target of *OsBAG4* RNAi. The cell death was largely suppressed in the OsBAG4-RNAi/*ebr1* plants [\(Figure 6B](#page-8-0)). Consequently, the increased disease resistance in *ebr1* was also largely attenuated in OsBAG4-RNAi/*ebr1*, near to the degree of the wild-type [\(Figures 6](#page-8-0)C–6F and S1E). Similarly, the growth phenotypes were also restored in OsBAG4-RNAi/*ebr1* (Figures S6I–S6K). Importantly, we knocked down *OsBAG4* in wild-type ZH11 protoplasts by RNAi and found that the transcript levels of many PR genes including *OsJAR1*, *OsAOS2*, *CEBiP*, *Cht1*, *OsPAL1*, *OsPAL2*, *PR3*, *PR4*, *PR5*, and *PBZ1* involved in rice PTI and the salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonate (JA) pathways [\(Tanaka et al., 2003; Kaku et al., 2006; Mei et al., 2006; Balbi and](#page-12-13) [Devoto, 2008; Liu et al., 2012\)](#page-12-13) were decreased in OsBAG4-RNAi protoplasts [\(Figures 6G](#page-8-0) and 6H). These results clearly demonstrate that the accumulation of OsBAG4 contributes to the *ebr1* mutant phenotypes and plays a bona fide role in rice immunity.

We then wanted to determine whether OsBAG4 could also induce autoimmunity in other plants. Using the tobacco transient expression system, we found that ectopic expression of *OsBAG4* in tobacco leaves also caused cell death and H2O2 accumulation [\(Figures 7A](#page-9-0)–7C). However, co-expression of EBR1, but not EBR1H22Y, with OsBAG4 promoted OsBAG4 degradation and abolished the OsBAG4-triggered cell death [\(Figures 7](#page-9-0)A and 7C). Therefore, the EBR1-OsBAG4 module also functions in other plants. The requirement for the E3 ligase activity of EBR1 also clearly confirmed EBR1-mediated OsBAG4 degradation in planta. Together, we conclude that EBR1 and OsBAG4 form a unique module to fine-tune immune homeostasis.

#### OsBAG4 Appears to Activate PTI in Rice

To obtain further insight into the mechanism underlying the PCD and autoimmunity in *ebr1* and OsBAG4-OX, we first performed transcriptome profiling by RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq). The RNA-seq analysis showed that *ebr1* and OsBAG4-OX shared similar expression profiles (Figure S7A), with activation of many defense-related genes compared with the wild-type ZH11 (Table S3), including those involved in the SA and JA pathways (Figure S7B). Consistent with this, we detected a synchronous increase of SA and JA levels ([Figures 7](#page-9-0)D and 7E), providing a possible explanation for the enhanced resistance to both virulent *Xoo* and *M. oryzae* strains in *ebr1* and OsBAG4-OX plants. Therefore, we conclude that the protein module orchestrates immune homeostasis and basal disease resistance in rice. To further dissect whether OsBAG4 functions in rice PTI or ETI, we conducted additional inoculation experiments with avirulent strains of *Xoo* and *M. oryzae* because the wild-type ZH11 contains race-specific R genes against *Xoo* and *M. oryzae*. We found that the EBR1-OX lines in which OsBAG4 accumulated less (Figure S3A) did not decrease race-specific resistance to the avirulent strains of the pathogens ([Figures 7](#page-9-0)F, 7G, S7C, and S7D). The result suggests that the EBR1-OsBAG4 module likely functions in PTI, but not ETI.

Importantly, as shown in [Figure 6H](#page-8-0), a similar decreased expression of PR genes involved in rice PTI was also observed in the EBR1-OX plants that accumulated less OsBAG4 protein and showed increased disease susceptibility (Figure S3I). Consequently, chitin-induced H2O2 generation was decreased in EBR1-OX (Figures S7E and S7F). The results together clearly

### DISCUSSION

PCD plays a central role in innate immunity in both plants and animals, and the HR, a form of PCD associated with resistance to pathogens, is a major hallmark of plant immunity ([Jones and Dangl, 2006;](#page-11-15) [Coll et al., 2011; Zebell and Dong,](#page-11-15) [2015\)](#page-11-15). During the past two decades, the identification of many plant mutants displaying spontaneous PCD has deepened our understanding of PCD signaling in plants. Several rice genes that control cell death have also been isolated, and these are involved in diverse processes including immunity, revealing the compli-

cated network in PCD and immunity in plants ([Lorrain et al., 2003;](#page-11-2) [Bruggeman et al., 2015](#page-11-2)). In this study, we report the functional identification of the E3 ligase EBR1 and the BAG protein OsBAG4, and show that EBR1 controls OsBAG4 homeostasis.

The ubiquitin/26S proteasome system (UPS) has been implicated in almost all aspects of growth and development, as well as in responses to biotic and abiotic stress in plants [\(Vierstra,](#page-12-3) [2009\)](#page-12-3). A large number of UPS components, in particular E3 ligases, have been shown to be involved in plant immune responses ([Yang et al., 2006; Lu et al., 2011; Cheng and Li,](#page-12-15) [2012; Marino et al., 2012; Li et al., 2014; Serrano et al., 2014\)](#page-12-15). In rice, the U-box E3 OsPUB44 acts as a positive regulator of peptidoglycan and chitin-triggered PTI [\(Ishikawa et al., 2014\)](#page-11-16). The rice RING-type E3 APIP6, a positive regulator of PTI, is the direct target of the *M. oryzae* effector AvrPiz-t ([Park et al.,](#page-12-16) [2012\)](#page-12-16). Therefore, many of the plant E3 ligases might be virulence targets during plant-microbe interactions, which function as either negative or positive regulators in innate immunity, depending on their substrate specificity. In this study, we showed that EBR1 is a previously unknown RING-type E3 ligase that functions in plant immunity. SPL11 ubiquitinates and thus degrades SPIN6, therefore linking with the OsRac1-associated defense ([Liu et al., 2015\)](#page-11-17). EBR1 represents a paradigm that an RING-type E3 ligase is negatively involved in both PCD and immunity in rice. Therefore, E3 ligases are involved in the plant immune system through targeting diverse defense pathways. Our study thus adds to our knowledge of ubiquitination and E3 ligases in PCD and immune activation in plants [\(Cheng and Li,](#page-11-11) [2012](#page-11-11)).

In this study, we identified the EBR1 E3 substrate OsBAG4, a member of the BAG family members that form complexes with signaling molecules and molecular chaperones in diverse cell proliferation and cell death processes, particularly in human diseases ([Takayama and Reed, 2001; Kabbage and Dickman,](#page-12-17) [2008](#page-12-17)). The accumulation of OsBAG4 accounts for autoimmunity and disease resistance observed in the *ebr1* mutant. Therefore, we identified a BAG protein that positively regulates PCD and imnot change disease resistance ([Doukhanina et al., 2006; Hoang](#page-11-5) [et al., 2015\)](#page-11-5). BAG proteins might have evolutionarily diverged in roles for PCD and immunity among monocots and dicots.

Overaccumulation of OsBAG4 triggers autoimmunity and enhances disease resistance in *ebr1* mutant or OsBAG4-OX plants. More importantly, the EBR1-OsBAG4 module most likely functions in rice PTI, and SA and JA signals were synchronously magnified in *ebr1* and OsBAG4-OX. Similar synchronous enhancement of the SA and JA pathways was also observed in our previous study [\(Tong et al., 2012](#page-12-9)). This is of great interest, given that the SA and JA signals usually antagonize each other [\(Robert-Seilaniantz et al., 2011; Yang et al., 2013\)](#page-12-18). It is also notable that strong activation of PTI by OsBAG4, associated with PCD and reactive oxygen species [ROS] accumulation, could confer broad-spectrum resistance to diverse pathogens that have very different lifestyles in rice. The EBR1-OsBAG4 module therefore fine-tunes plant innate immunity through a previously unrecognized mechanism: tight control of ubiquitinationmediated degradation of a cell death and immunity regulator. This finding is interesting, given that most E3 ligases quench autoimmunity by ubiquitin-mediated degradation of PRR or R proteins (Alca´ [zar and Parker, 2011; Cheng and Li, 2012\)](#page-11-1). Further dissection of OsBAG4 downstream signaling will certainly provide the molecular mechanisms of the BAG-mediated immune responses, as well as broad-spectrum disease resistance.

Autoimmunity, and thereby broad-spectrum disease resistance, usually compromises plant fitness [\(Cheng et al., 2011;](#page-11-13) [Gou et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2012; Li et al., 2016a](#page-11-13)). We observed strong growth retardation of *ebr1* and OsBAG4-OX plants, whereas EBR1 overexpression promoted plant growth (Figures S2 and S6). We thus propose that OsBAG4 should also affect certain growth pathways that are mediated by hormones, because many genes involved in hormone pathways are regulated in *ebr1* and OsBAG4-OX (Figure S7B). Therefore, autoimmunity triggered by OsBAG4 must be fine-tuned to avoid growth retardation in wild-type rice. The surveillance and quenching of autoimmunity are fulfilled by the ubiquitination-dependent degradation of OsBAG4 by EBR1. It will also be worthy to investigate the molecular mechanisms involved in growth regulation by OsBAG4, which may provide molecular approaches for breeding for broadspectrum disease-resistant crops with reduced yield penalty.

#### EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

#### Mapping of EBR1 and Gene Expression

The *EBR1* locus was initially mapped at the centromeric region of chromosome 5. A candidate gene was selected by microarray analysis and was confirmed by genetic complementation and RNAi. See Supplemental Experimental Procedures for details.

#### Pathogen Inoculation and Histological Analysis

Plants were inoculated with different *Xoo* strains. Lesion lengths were measured at 14 days postinoculation (dpi). For evaluation of blast disease, seedlings were spray-inoculated with spore suspensions (1 3 10<sup>5</sup> spores/ mL) in a dew groth chamber as previously described ([Deng et al., 2006](#page-11-18)). Disease symptom and fungal growth were evaluated at 7 dpi as described previously [\(Qi and Yang, 2002\)](#page-12-19). Cell death was detected by trypan blue staining, and H2O2 accumulation was assayed with 3,3<sup>0</sup> -diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining as described previously ([Tian et al., 2014\)](#page-12-20). See Supplemental Experimental Procedures for details.

#### Yeast Two-Hybrid Experiments

All Y2H assays were carried out according to the Clontech Yeast Protocols Handbook. See Supplemental Experimental Procedures for details.

#### Protein Immunoblotting and Coimmunoprecipitation

Proteins were extracted from leaves. Western blotting was performed using the immunoreaction kit (Toyobo). Coimmunoprecipitation (coIP) was performed with anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (Sigma-Aldrich), and western blotting was performed using the anti-Myc, anti-Flag, or anti-EBR1 antibody. See Supplemental Experimental Procedures for details.

#### Ubiquitination Assays

E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of EBR1 and its mutant proteins was assayed as described previously ([Zhang et al., 2007\)](#page-12-11). EBR1-mediated OsBAG4 ubiquitination was assayed as described previously [\(Zhang et al., 2015\)](#page-12-21). The OsBAG4-Flag fusion protein was prepared and immuno-purified from seedlings of *OsBAG4-Flag* plants (OsBAG4-OX). See Supplemental Experimental Procedures for details.

#### Cell-Free Protein Degradation Assay

Cell-free protein degradation was assayed as described previously ([Xu et al.,](#page-12-12) [2012\)](#page-12-12). In brief, total proteins were extracted from seedlings with the extraction buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 4 mM PMSF, 5 mM dithiothreitol). Equal amounts of extracts were incubated with or without 50 mM MG132. Samples were taken at indicated intervals for the western blotting using the OsBAG4 antibody.

#### In Vitro Pull-Down Assay

The GST-OsBAG4-HA and His-EBR1 fusion proteins were produced in *E. coli*. Soluble proteins were incubated with 100 mL glutathione agarose beads (GE Healthcare) for 2 hr at 4C. The beads were washed five times and then incubated with an equal amount of bacterial lysates containing His-EBR1 for another 2 hr at 4C. The beads were washed five times, and His-EBR1 was detected by immunoblotting using an anti-His antibody.

#### Luciferase Biomolecular Complementation Assay

The coding regions of *EBR1* and *OsBAG4* were cloned into the vectors JW771 and JW772, respectively. Each was fused to the C-terminal half (cLUC-EBR1/ OsBAG4) and the N-terminal half (EBR1/OsBAG4-nLUC) of luciferase (LUC), respectively; cLUC and nLUC were used alone as controls. The resulting plasmids were transformed into *Agrobacterium* (GV3101) and then co-infiltrated into *N. benthamiana* leaves with *Agrobacterium* carrying *p19* (35S::P19-HA) for transient expression. After 3 days of growth, the leaves were sprayed with 1 mM luciferin and the LUC activity was monitored.

#### RNAi in Rice Protoplasts

Protoplast isolation and PEG-mediated transformation were carried out as described previously [\(Yoo et al., 2007](#page-12-22)). In brief, protoplasts were prepared from seedling sheaths at a concentration of 2 3 10<sup>6</sup> cells/mL. For transformation, 10 mg plasmid DNA (pGreenII-OsBAG4-RNAi) was mixed with 100 mL protoplasts, with empty vector as a control; then 110 mL 40% PEG was added for 15 min in the dark. Transformations were stopped by adding 103 W5, and the protoplasts were collected by centrifugation at 200 rpm for 3 min. The protoplasts were cultured for another 12 hr under dark at room temperature for RNA preparation.

#### Transient Expression and Cell Death Induction in N. benthamiana

The OsBAG4-triggered cell death in *N. benthamiana* was investigated as described previously ([Bai et al., 2012\)](#page-11-19). *Agrobacterium* (containing the EBR1 and OsBAG4 expression plasmids, respectively) suspensions (optical density at 600 [OD600] = 0.5) were infiltrated into 4-week-old *N. benthamiana* leaves. GFP (pK7WGF2) was expressed as an internal control. Cell death and H2O2 accumulation were detected at 2–3 dpi. Total proteins were extracted and detected by western blotting using the anti-GFP, anti-HA, or anti-OsBAG4 antibody.

#### SA and JA Assay

The preparation and quantification of SA and JA were performed as previously described ([Tong et al., 2012](#page-12-9)). JA and SA levels were analyzed by GC-MS using labeled internal standards; three biological replicates were used for each genotype.
