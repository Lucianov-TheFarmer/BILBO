#### **Abstract (200 words)**

The rice (*Oryza sativa* L.) ethylene-responsive transcription factor gene *SUB1A-1* confers tolerance to prolonged, complete submergence by limiting underwater elongation growth. Upon desubmergence, *SUB1A-1* genotypes rapidly recover photosynthetic function and re-commence development towards flowering. The underpinnings of the transition from stress amelioration to the return to homeostasis are not well known. Here, transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were conducted to identify mechanisms by which *SUB1A* improves physiological function over the 24 hours following a sub-lethal submergence event. Evaluation of near-isogenic genotypes after submergence and over a day of re-aeration demonstrated that *SUB1A* transiently constrains the remodeling of cellular activities associated with growth. *SUB1A* influenced the abundance of *ca.* 1400 transcripts and had a continued impact on metabolite content, particularly free amino acids, glucose, and sucrose, throughout the recovery period. *SUB1A* promoted recovery of metabolic homeostasis but had limited influence on mRNAs associated with growth processes and photosynthesis. The involvement of low energy sensing during submergence and recovery was supported by dynamics in trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P) and mRNAs encoding key enzymes and signaling proteins, which were modulated by *SUB1A*. This study provides new evidence of convergent signaling pathways critical to the rapidly reversible management of carbon and nitrogen metabolism in submergence resilient rice.

#### **Summary**

The submergence tolerance gene *SUB1A* of rice continues to benefit plant physiology following desubmergence. This study examined *SUB1A*'s impact on the transcriptome and metabolome over a 24 h diurnal period following desubmergence. The rapid return to metabolic homeostasis was affected by *SUB1A* through a limited number transcriptal changes.

#### **Introduction**

Flooding is a frequent and increasing threat to crops, particularly in Southeast Asia, where rice is a nutritional and economic staple. Over 30% of rice acreage is vulnerable to flash floods (Bailey-Serres *et al.* 2010), and catastrophic flooding is likely to become more frequent in coming decades (Hirabayashi *et al.* 2013; Kirtman *et al.* 2013). Although rice has several anatomical adaptations, including root aerenchyma, a suberized exodermal layer, and adventitious roots, that enable it to thrive in waterlogged soil, complete submergence of aerial organs can be fatal in the absence of key regulatory genes that provide an effective flooding survival strategy (Voesenek & Bailey-Serres 2015).

When the shoot of a plant is submerged, photosynthesis and aerobic respiration are limited by low light penetration through turbid waters and a 10,000-fold reduction in CO<sup>2</sup> and O<sup>2</sup> diffusion. Ethylene diffusion away from the plant is also restricted underwater, which activates transcription factors that stimulate shoot elongation responses in many rice genotypes (Fukao *et al.* 2006; Hattori *et al.* 2009). Rice varieties cultivated in deep paddies encode one or more group VII ethylene-responsive transcription factor (ERF-VII), *SNORKEL1* and *SNORKEL2*, that enables rapid elongation of stem internodes in slowly established floods. This survival strategy is associated with enhanced cell division and production of and response to gibberellin (GA) in the stem internodes (Hattori *et al.* 2009; Ayano *et al.* 2014). However, cultivars typically grown in shallow lowland paddies lack *SNORKEL*s and possess the *semidwarf1* allele of the GA biosynthetic enzyme GA20 oxidase, the key Green Revolution mutation that limits internode elongation. In the deep flash-floods that lowland rice often endures due to Monsoon rains, enhanced underwater leaf growth occurs despite reduced photosynthesis and the inefficient conversion of carbohydrate reserves into ATP via anaerobic metabolism in O2-depleted (hypoxic) cells (Ram *et al.* 2002). This

situation leads to cellular energy starvation that can be lethal if aerial tissues do not regain oxygenation by escape from the floodwaters.

Submergence tolerance is a multi-phasic trait, requiring both survival while underwater and recovery after flood waters recede (Fukao *et al.* 2011; Luo *et al.* 2011; Striker 2012; Shingaki-Wells *et al.* 2014). In rice, a successful submergence survival adaptation is growth quiescence. Halting growth while shoots are submerged effectively rations carbohydrate reserves of source tissues, extending the survivable submergence period from a few days to two weeks or more (Fukao *et al.* 2006). This trait is conferred by a *SUBMERGENCE1A* (*SUB1A-1*), a strongly submergence-induced allele of an ERF-VII gene (Xu *et al.* 2006). The *SUB1A*-mediated growth-restricting mechanism during submergence involves the repression of ethylene and GA-induced elongation of aerial organs, which by contrast are promoted in internodes of deepwater rice and leaves of lowland rice varieties (Fukao *et al.* 2006; Fukao & Bailey-Serres 2008). Two global analyses evaluated the shoot transcriptomes of the near-isogenic genotypes M202 and M202(Sub1) plants of similar developmental age and grown under near-identical conditions in the same greenhouse. One observed 117 genes that were distinct in the *SUB1A-1* genotype under control conditions (signal log<sup>2</sup> ratio > |1|, false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p-value ≤ 0.01: Mustroph et al., 2010). This number increased to 693 putative *SUB1A*-regulated genes after a day of submergence. In plants grown and treated in the same greenhouse for 1 to 6 d of submergence, 1231 *SUB1A*-regulted genes were reported using a different microarray platform (Jung *et al.* 2010). Both studies identified a broad impact of *SUB1A* on genes controlling processes including anaerobic metabolism, elongation growth, reactive O<sup>2</sup> species (ROS) regulation and leaf senescence, supporting targeted gene studies and extending the network of regulation mediated by *SUB1A* during full submergence (Xu *et al.* 2006; Fukao & Bailey-Serres 2008).

The transition from submergence to air is marked by acute stress. Re-exposure to light and tropospheric O<sup>2</sup> causes a burst in ROS production (Blokhina *et al.* 2000; Luo *et al.* 2012). Leaf dehydration severe enough to induce leaf rolling typically occurs within an hour of desubmergence in rice, despite ample water availability at the root (Setter *et al.* 2010; Fukao *et al.* 2011). This post-submergence leaf dehydration has been linked to reduced hydraulic conductance at the base of young leaves, although the specific mechanism behind this reduction is not known (Setter *et al.* 2010). *SUB1A* improves reoxygenation tolerance and physiological recovery compared to submergence-intolerant plants, and it partially ameliorates the dehydration response (Fukao *et al.* 2011). A recent study linked the more rapid photosynthetic recovery in *SUB1A*-containing plants with higher photosystem II (PSII) electron transport efficiency (Alpuerto *et al.* 2016).

Trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P), an intermediate in trehalose synthesis from glucose-6P and UDP-glucose, has emerged as a key component of plant energy sensing (Fernandez *et al.* 2010; Tsai & Gazzarrini 2014). Although present at very low levels in plant tissues, T6P content correlates with sucrose availability, and T6P promotes growth by indirectly inhibiting SNF1-related protein kinase (SnRK1) activity (Paul 2007; Figueroa & Lunn 2016). The Nipponbare cultivar encodes 11 putative trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS) genes and nine putative trehalose-6-phosphate phosphatase (TPP) genes, varying in transcript regulation and enzyme activity (Shima *et al.* 2007; Zang *et al.* 2011). TPS activity depends on OsTPS1 but may be influenced by interactions with other TPS proteins (Zang *et al.* 2011). TPPs contribute to responses to environmental cues in rice. The levels of *OsTPP1* and *OsTPP2* mRNAs change in response to salt, cold, and osmotic stress and ABA treatment. It was found that *OsTPP1* overexpression enhanced tolerance to cold and salt stress (Pramanik & Imai 2005; Shima *et al.* 2007), and *OsTPP7* was identified as a genetic determinant of efficient germination in rice seedlings, especially under hypoxia (Kretzschmar *et al.* 2015).

*OsTPP7* expression likely increases T6P turnover—thus signaling low sucrose availability to promote endosperm starch breakdown—during seed and seedling submergence (Kretzschmar *et al.* 2015). Differential regulation of carbohydrate metabolism is similarly a key element of *SUB1A*-mediated survival of submergence (Fukao *et al.* 2006) as well as prolonged darkness (Fukao *et al.* 2012). The latter hints that *SUB1A* may heighten sensing or response to changes in energy status to better manage carbon catabolism during submergence or recovery. A challenge to studying the role of T6P is its accurate quantitation, which can be improved by use of reverse-phase ion-pair (RPIP)-UPLC-MS.

The aims of this study were twofold: one, to further resolve the critical role of *SUB1A-1* in submergence tolerance; and two, to characterize the gene transcript and metabolite regulation associated with the rapid physiological recovery in the shoot after submergence. Consideration of the contribution of *SUB1A* to this process could reveal mechanisms relevant to flood tolerance across species. To achieve these goals, targeted metabolite profiling and RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) were conducted for submergenceintolerant rice and a near-isogenic, submergence-tolerant 'Sub1' variety throughout a 24-h (diurnal) submergence recovery period. The findings revealed a broad and persistent impact of *SUB1A* on metabolism through desubmergence recovery that involved differential expression of around 1400 genes during a 24 h period, considerably fewer than the genotype lacking *SUB1A*. The collective results of this and other studies indicate that *SUB1A* does not directly regulate rebuilding of the photosynthetic apparatus upon desubmergence but more likely aids preservation of plastid integrity during the stress. Newly recognized signatures of *SUB1A* include a transient dampening of genes associated with cell division, chromatin structure, and signaling associated with starch catabolism.

#### **Materials and Methods**

#### *Plant Material and Growth Conditions*

Rice seeds (*Oryza sativa* L. ssp. japonica) of cultivar M202 and its near-isogenic, Sub1 introgression line, M202(Sub1), were sterilized for 30 min in a solution of 5% (v/v) bleach and 0.01% (v/v) Tween-20, rinsed and soaked overnight in DI water. After germinating in covered glass dishes on wet paper towels for 5 d, seedlings were transplanted into 10 cm pots in the greenhouse at a planting density of 20 plants per pot. Plants were grown in the greenhouse for 12 d before beginning the submergence treatment.

Plants were submerged in 121 L bins, in which water was allowed to equilibrate to ambient temperature for 24 h before submergence. Submergence began at ZT 6:00 and continued for 72 h, at which time plants were placed back on the greenhouse bench for reoxygenation. Whole shoots were harvested and flash-frozen in liquid N<sup>2</sup> immediately upon desubmergence (3d\_sub) at midday (ZT9:00), at dusk (ZT15:30), midnight (ZT19:45), dawn (ZT0), and again at midday 24 h following desubmergence (ZT9:00).

#### *Metabolite extraction*

For NMR and GC-MS analysis, frozen leaf tissue was ground in liquid N<sup>2</sup> and lyophilized overnight. When dry, 1.5 mL of 80:20 methanol:H2O was added to 50 mg of lyophilized tissue, shaken for 5 min, and centrifuged for 4 min at 12,000 g. Aliquots of supernatant were transferred to clean tubes, vacuum centrifuged until dry, and stored at -20 °C until analyzed. Samples were analyzed by <sup>1</sup>H NMR with an Avance 14.1 T NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA), and GC-MS was performed with a 7890A gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a Rxi-5Sil MS column (Restek, Bellefonte, PA, USA). Further details of NMR and GC-MS sample preparation and analysis are available in Supplemental Methods (SM).

Extraction for RPIP-UPLC-MS analysis was adapted from Delatte et al. (2009). One mL of 3:8 chloroform:acetonitrile was added to 20 mg of dried plant tissue and shaken for 5 min. Liquid/liquid extraction was performed twice; each time, 0.8 mL H2O was added to the sample, then samples were shaken for 5 min and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 4 min before transferring the aqueous layer to a fresh microcentrifuge tube. Samples were subjected to weak-anion exchange solid phase extraction (SPE) before UPLC-MS analysis. SPE cartridges were conditioned with 1 mL methanol followed by 1 mL of 5% (w/v) NH4OH in H2O. The dried extracts were reconstituted in 2 mL H2O, loaded into the cartridge and washed with 1 mL water followed by 1 mL methanol. After drying, cartridges were eluted into a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube with 2 mL of 80:20 water:methanol containing 5% formic acid, and the eluate was dried by vacuum centrifugation. Further details of RPIP-UPLC-MS sample preparation and analysis are available in Supplemental Materials (SM).

## *Metabolite statistical analysis*

Either peak areas (NMR, GC-MS, RPIP-UPLC-MS subset 3-phosphoglycerate (3PG), 6 phosphogluconate (6PG), adenosine monophosphate) or peak heights (RPIP-UPLC-MS subset glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), sucrose-6-phosphate (S6P), T6P) for metabolites in each replicate were used in statistical analysis to determine genotype, time, and treatment-based differences in metabolite content. The metabolite data were normalized by dividing the area of the individual components by the summed area for all the identified metabolites (Lee & Fiehn 2008). Normalized data were analyzed by repeated measures ANOVA using the MIXED procedure in SAS (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and these normalized areas are depicted in the metabolite figures and datasets.

# *RNA-seq*

Total RNA was isolated from frozen shoot tissue with the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). cDNA libraries for each sample were prepared from purified mRNA

according to Wang *et al.* (2011) for paired-end (≥50 nt) sequencing on the Illumina platform (Illumina Hi-Seq2500/Illumina NextSeq500) at the University of California, Riverside Institute for Integrative Genome Biology Genomics Core Facility. Sequencing was performed for control plants at the first time point (ZT6:00) and submerged plants at all time points. Three biological replicates were sequenced for each genotype × time × treatment combination.

Reads were trimmed with Trim Galore! (Babraham Bioinformatics) to remove adapter sequences and aligned to the *O. sativa* ssp. japonica cv. Nipponbare genome (IRGSP release 7) (Kawahara *et al.* 2013) with TopHat2 (Kim *et al.* 2013). All genes are reported using the Rice Annotation Project (RAP) identification system unless otherwise noted. Data from separate sequencing runs were pooled for each sample at the read-counting stage, and reads aligned per gene were counted with HTSeq (Anders *et al.* 2015). Genes less than 3 counts per million (cpm) at all time points were filtered before subsequent analysis. Several normalization strategies were tested, of which log(TC) was selected as the most effective. Log(TC) was calculated as

$$\log_{e}[read\ count \times \left(\frac{mean\ mapped\ reads\ across\ all\ samples}{mapped\ reads\ in\ current\ sample}\right)].$$

Normalized read counts were analyzed by repeated measures ANOVA using the MIXED procedure in SAS. Genes with non-normally distributed residuals were analyzed by repeated measures ANOVA using simulated bootstrapped "*F*"-distributions. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were considered to be those for which the FDR-corrected p-value < 0.05 and the log<sup>2</sup> fold change (log2FC) between genotypes ≥ 1 or ≤ -1, which corresponds to doubling or halving of transcript abundance, respectively. *K*-means clustering of DEG patterns was done by the FASTCLUS procedure in SAS. Identification of overrepresented GO slim terms in gene sets was performed with a hypergeometric test using the GOstats package in R (Falcon & Gentleman 2007).

#### **Results and Discussion**

Twelve d old M202 and M202(Sub1) plants were submerged for 3 d, after which whole-shoot tissue was harvested at five time points: immediately upon desubmergence at midday (hereafter referred to as 3d\_sub), dusk, midnight, dawn, and again at midday 24 hours after desubmergence (referred to as midday). As a control, tissue was sampled from plants of both genotypes that had been maintained under control conditions at the same time as 3d\_sub (ZT6, control). Whole-shoot tissue from these time points, along with tissue from nonsubmerged control plants harvested at the same time points, was used for transcriptome and metabolite analyses. This duration of submergence is non-lethal for both genotypes and is sufficient to induce both significant depletion of carbohydrates and chlorophyll relative to control plants, as well as induce physiological differences between tolerant and intolerant genotypes (Fukao *et al.* 2006; Fukao and Bailey-Serres, 2008; Fukao et al., 2011; Barding *et al.* 2013). Total poly(A) <sup>+</sup> mRNA was sequenced from three biological replicate samples per time point, yielding data for 20,502 genes with ≥ 3 cpm reads that were included in the statistical analysis. Principal components analysis (PCA) showed stronger impacts of treatment and time on the transcriptome than genotype (Fig. 1a). However, the perturbation of the transcriptome by submergence was more dramatic for M202 than M202(Sub1). By midday, 24 h after desubmergence, the transcriptome of desubmerged plants of both genotypes was largely similar to that of the 3d\_sub control plants. At this resolution, recovery occurred for both genotypes following sub-lethal stress.

# *SUB1A alters transcription of biosynthesis-related genes during submergence*

Although the growth and maturation of the near-isogenic lines are indistinguishable under normal growth conditions (Fukao *et al.* 2006; Fukao & Bailey-Serres 2008), we detected 300 mRNAs differentially regulated by *SUB1A* under control conditions (FDR-

corrected p < 0.05, |log2FC| ≥ 1) (Supplemental Table S1). In response to submergence, the transcriptomes of the two genotypes became more distinct, as already visible by PCA (Fig. 1a). This was further evaluated by monitoring changes in mRNA abundance in each genotype in response to the stress and by comparing the transcriptomes under control and submergence conditions between genotypes. The expression levels of 7994 gene transcripts were significantly different (FDR-corrected p < 0.05) between M202-3d\_sub and M202 control, and expression levels of 7085 gene transcripts were significantly different between M202(Sub1)-3d\_sub and M202(Sub1)-control. These genes were further filtered for an absolute log<sup>2</sup> change in transcript abundance ratio of at least 1. Based on these criteria, in M202, 4115 genes were up-regulated and 1703 genes were down-regulated by submergence, and in M202(Sub1), 3263 genes were up-regulated and 1570 genes were down-regulated by submergence (Supplemental Table S1). Most genes up-regulated by submergence overlapped between the two genotypes, but among genes down-regulated by submergence, a much smaller percentage were shared (Fig. 1b), Supplemental Table S1). On the whole, the presence of *SUB1A-1* in the genome resulted in fewer differentially regulated mRNAs.

Gene ontology (GO) analysis identified the functional categories of genes that were significantly overrepresented among the genes differentially regulated by submergence (Fig. 1c), Supplemental Table S1). Genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism, growth and development were up-regulated by submergence in both genotypes. There were more shared biological process GO terms for down-regulated genes in the two genotypes, including photosynthesis, response to abiotic stimulus, secondary metabolic processes, and transport among others (Fig. 1c). Key photosynthesis genes down-regulated in both genotypes included a cytochrome *b*6*f* complex subunit (OS07G0556200), Psb28 (OS01G0938100), chlorophyll *a*/*b* binding proteins (OS02G0197600, OS02G0764500), and Rubisco small subunit proteins (OS12G0274700, OS12G0292400).

Genes associated with the peroxisome were only overrepresented among those upregulated in M202(Sub1), suggesting more active scavenging of ROS in the submergencetolerant genotype, which is in keeping with previous findings of reduced superoxide and hydrogen peroxide levels in shoot tissue of the submergence-tolerant genotype after flooding (Fukao *et al.* 2011). In contrast, several biosynthesis-related GO terms, including cellular biosynthetic process, cellular macromolecule metabolic process, macromolecule biosynthetic process, gene expression, protein metabolic process, ribosome biosynthesis and translation were strongly overrepresented among up-regulated genes in M202 but not in M202(Sub1). The absence of up-regulation of these mRNAs encoding the translational apparatus in M202(Sub1) is consistent with restriction of growth by *SUB1A* during submergence. *Arabidopsis thaliana* (ecotype Columbia), which is able to endure over seven days of complete submergence, also does not generally up-regulate ribosomal protein gene and translation factor mRNAs in submerged rosettes (Lee *et al.* 2011). Thus, not only did M202 shoots up-regulate more transcripts, those that were up-regulated are involved in highly energy consuming processes associated with growth.

# *SUB1A modifies gene expression in distinct patterns through recovery*

To systematically explore the RNA-seq data, a two-way ANOVA was performed to compare the factors genotype and treatment (submerged and control). At the first time point of the study (3d\_sub), 632 genes were differentially expressed (DE) based on genotype (i.e., *SUB1A*-regulated) during submergence (Supplemental Table S1). The statistical comparison of M202(Sub1) at 3d\_sub and M202 at 3d\_sub was also made in a repeated-measures ANOVA that considered the *SUB1A* response in the context of the 24 h recovery period for the two genotypes (recovery analysis) (Supplemental Table S2). Using the same significance filters as above (FDR-corrected p < 0.05, |log2FC| ≥ 1), this analysis found 669 genes were DE at 3d\_sub, and 1383 genes were found to be DE by *SUB1A* for at least one time point

during recovery. For genes differentially regulated by *SUB1A* at 3d\_sub, overrepresented GO terms for up-regulated genes included stress response, stimulus response, abiotic stimulus response, endogenous stimulus response, DNA binding and nucleic acid binding transcription factor activity; fewer GO terms were overrepresented among down-regulated genes (Supplemental Table S2). Just over 220 genes were DE at dusk, 247 at midnight, 248 at dawn, and 198 at midday (24 hours after desubmergence). Few GO terms were overrepresented among DE genes during recovery (Supplemental Table S2).

To determine the global effect of *SUB1A* on gene transcript patterns during submergence recovery, *k*-means clustering was performed on the 1383 *SUB1A*-influenced genes. Six *SUBA*-dependent expression patterns were identified and overrepresented GO terms identified (Fig. 2); Supplemental Table S3). Two clusters are characterized by downregulation at different magnitudes by *SUB1A* throughout post-submergence recovery (Cluster 1 and 6). Overrepresented GO terms in the most strongly repressed group of genes, cluster 1 (60 genes), included protein metabolic process and cellular protein modification process. This cluster also included numerous hypothetical proteins, a number of which had nearly undetectable levels of mRNA in M202(Sub1) at any time point. We cannot rule out that some genes in cluster 1 may be DE due to genomic regions differences between the two genotypes that resulted from limited recombination during the breeding of M202(Sub1). Cluster 6 genes (424 genes) are down-regulated by *SUB1A* at multiple time points, but to a lesser magnitude than those genes in cluster 1. Cluster 6 genes were enriched for extracellular functions and metabolic processes and included several abundant mRNAs encoding major enzymes involved in the glyoxyate cycle*.* Also included were *SUB1C* (OS09G0286600), known to be negatively regulated by *SUB1A-1* (Fukao *et al.* 2006), and *CIPK15* (OS11G0113700), which is required for starch catabolism during anaerobic cultivation of seeds to seedlings (Lee *et al.* 2009).

Cluster 2 mRNAs (166 genes) were also highly regulated by *SUB1A* but in a manner controlled by diurnal time point. These were repressed by *SUB1A* at 3d\_sub, and again at midnight and dawn, but *SUB1A* generally enhanced their abundance at dusk and midday following desubmergence. Many GO terms were significantly overrepresented in this cluster, including DNA metabolic process, epigenetic regulation of gene expression, cellular protein modification process, and anatomical structure morphogenesis. Notable was the enrichment for cell cycle (cyclin, kinesin motor, microtubule assembly, cytokinesis, DNA replication) and development, indicating that *SUB1A* imposes a transient restriction in abundance of gene transcripts associated with cell division and developmental progression. However, this cluster did not include genes associated with ribosome biogenesis or translation, as these did not meet the strict criteria of DE required for inclusion in the *k*-means cluster analysis.

Cluster 3 genes (331 genes) were up-regulated by *SUB1A* during submergence, but showed small and varied differences over the recovery period. This cluster includes key genes associated with anaerobic metabolism and its regulation in multiple species, such as *ALCOHOL DEHYDROGENASE* (*ADH1/2*), *PYRUVATE DECARBOXYLASE* (*PDC1/PDC4*) and orthologs of *Arabidopsis HYPOXIA REPSONSE ATTENUATOR1* (OS01G0318400), *PLANT CYSTEINE OXIDASE* (OS01G0185500) and *LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARY41* (OS01G0511000). Genes in cluster 4 (258 genes) were significantly down-regulated by *SUB1A* at 3d\_sub than in M202 but show little to no transcriptional difference for the rest of the recovery period. These represented diverse GO categories. Cluster 5 genes (144 genes) were up-regulated by *SUB1A* throughout stress and recovery and included many genes associated with metabolic processes.

*SUB1A* transiently dampens the abundance of transcripts relevant to cell division and development, as illustrated by cluster 2. This cluster included genes in the GO category related to epigenetic regulation, revealing widespread limitation of histone-encoding gene

transcripts by *SUB1A*, although for many genes the effect was not statistically significant (Fig. S1). These mRNAs were generally up-regulated by submergence in both genotypes, but to a greater magnitude in M202. A smaller impact of *SUB1A* in one of the three replicates restricted the number of significant pairwise differences between the genotypes at the individual time points. Nonetheless, the consistent SUB1A-dependent trends in histone gene transcript abundance could indicate a biologically meaningful rule for specific histones in chromatin remodeling in submergence tolerance and recovery. Alternatively, the more limited up-regulation of histone mRNAs by *SUB1A* could reflect energy conservation and growth restriction during submergence and at midnight, as suggested for the translational apparatus.

#### *SUB1A effects are more persistent in the metabolome than in the transcriptome*

Untargeted and targeted metabolite analysis using NMR (Fig. S2), RPIP-UPLC-MS (Fig. S3), and GC-MS (Fig. S4) on the same whole-shoot tissue samples illuminated the timing of metabolic recovery from submergence and *SUB1A*'s impact. This analysis included an evaluation of the metabolome of the submerged and recovered plants, as well as plants harvested over the diurnal time course that were not perturbed by the stress (control). Based on PCA the two genotypes have similar metabolite fluctuations over the 24 h diurnal cycle under control conditions (Fig. 3a). The metabolomes were most similar at the dusk, midnight and dawn time points (variation limited to PC2). The samples showed the greatest variability on the PCs plotted at the midday time point, which was sampled at the beginning and end of the experiment.

Overall, the metabolomes of the two genotypes were similarly modified by submergence and during the recovery period (Fig. 3b). The PCA demonstrated several trends: first, that submergence had a larger influence on the metabolome than the distinction in genotype; second, that metabolome recovery lagged behind the transcriptome (Fig. 1a and 3a,b); and third that the effects of *SUB1A* lessened in the metabolome and transcriptome over the recovery period (Fig. 1a, 3b). Of the 41 metabolites measured in submerged or recovering plants, all but three (lactate, phenylalanine, 6-phosphogluconate (6PG)) were significantly impacted by the submergence/recovery treatment for at least one genotype and/or time point; 15 were significantly impacted by *SUB1A* at 3d\_sub, 22 were affected at dusk, 24 at midnight, 30 at dawn, and 16 at midday (Supplemental Table S4). Thus, the presence of *SUB1A-1* imparted broad and sustained effects on the shoot metabolome during submergence and recovery.

Despite the orthogonal and complementary nature of GC-MS and NMR, differences exist between the two data sets. For example, the overall change in alanine, valine, threonine, tyrosine, and asparagine were consistently greater in M202 but the magnitude of the change differed between analytical methods. These variations can be due to several factors. One cause can be data treatment (such as normalization), which is influenced by the number of metabolites detected and the overall contribution to intensity. Another cause can be differences in sensitivity and sample preparation, as previously described for glutamine and glutamate (Barding *et al.* 2013). A third cause can be that NMR is susceptible to signal overlap (convolution). For example, resonance overlap with sucrose may account for the difference in serine content measured by the two methods. Nonetheless, the results of both analytical methods provide strong support of *SUB1A* regulation of metabolites including intermediates of hormone biosynthesis, as well as carbon and nitrogen metabolism.

# *SUB1A impacts sterol metabolism in control conditions*

*SUB1A* effects were discerned for metabolites associated with brassinosteriod (BR) biosynthesis under control conditions. Levels of β-sitosterol, and stigmasterol, and campesterol were regulated by *SUB1A* and submergence (Fig. S4). In non-submerged control plants, all three measured sterols were significantly lowered by *SUB1A* at the first time point, indicating a role for *SUB1A* in their diurnal regulation even in the absence of stress. They were also consistently and significantly reduced by submergence, and remaining significantly lower than controls throughout recovery in both genotypes (Fig. S4). The biosynthesis of these compounds requires multiple oxidation steps, and therefore, their decline during submergence could reflect changes in availability of cellular O<sup>2</sup> content, although this could not be measured in our experimental system. Campesterol is a biosynthetic precursor to BRs, which are involved in the regulation of growth and development with GA (Chung & Choe 2013) and condition sensitivity to ABA (Northey *et al.* 2016). Campersterol is modified through multiple oxidation reactions to form castasterone, which is directly oxidized to brassinolide. A previous study found increased levels of castasterone at 1 and 6 d after submergence in M202(Sub1) shoot tissue, alongside up-regulation of BR biosynthesis gene transcripts (Schmitz *et al.* 2013). Here, the decline in campesterol after 3 d of submergence and its gradual rise during recovery was not significantly affected by *SUB1A*. The BR biosynthesis gene *DWARF4* (OS03G0227700) was significantly up-regulated by *SUB1A* at 3d\_sub (log2FC = 2.83; cluster 3), whereas a second BR biosynthesis gene, *DWARF1* (OS03G0602300), was significantly down-regulated by *SUB1A* at the same time point (log2FC = -1.02), consistent with findings of 24 h submerged plants treated in our greenhouse (Mustroph *et al.* 2010) but in contrast to the results of Schmitz *et al.* (2013). The lack of agreement between tthese independent studies that used the same genotype and microarray platform could reflect differences such as light availability during submergence, as levels of photosynthesis would have ramifications on cellular O<sup>2</sup> content that could impact the remodeling of the transcriptome and O2-requiring steps in steroid synthesis. Nonetheless, the collective metabolite and transcriptome data indicate that *SUB1A* may influence BR biosynthesis prior to or during a submergence event.

# *SUB1A restricts changes in metabolites of central pathways in association with regulation of key genes*

To glean additional perspective on the impact of *SUB1A* on both submergence and desubmergence recovery, we focused on transcriptomic and metabolomic adjustments associated with several central metabolic pathways. Glycolysis and the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) catabolize sugars mobilized from long- and short-term storage pools to the energy-generating tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and anaerobic respiratory pathways (Fig. 4; Fig. 6). The study monitored intermediates of starch catabolism, glycolysis, the PPP, and end-products of fermentation: sucrose, glucose, glucose 6-phosphate (G6P), 6PG, 3-phosphoglycerate (3PG), lactate, and malate, which is also an intermediate of the TCA cycle (Fig. 4b). The study also yielded data for two other TCA intermediates, succinate and citrate, as well as γ-aminobutanoic acid (GABA), and 17 proteinogenic amino acids.

Many metabolites measured were modulated during the diurnal time course in untreated (control) plants and in response to submergence and desubmergence recovery. The results can be summarized as follows: First, consistent with our prior analyses (Barding *et al.* 2012), sucrose and glucose contents were similar for both genotypes under control conditions. Second, levels of 23 of the 41 metabolites were diurnally regulated under control conditions (Supplemental Table S4). Third, as observed previously (Barding *et al.* 2012, 2013), both genotypes catabolized sucrose and accumulated amino acids (alanine, asparagine, aspartic acid, glutamate, glutamine, histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, serine, threonine, tryptophan, tyrosine, valine) in response to submergence. Finally, there was clear evidence of recovery of amino acid homeostasis by 24 h post desubmergence, particularly in M202(Sub1) (methionine, alanine, asparagine, aspartic acid, β-alanine, citrate, fumarate, malate threonine, tryptophan, tyrosine).

Systematic evaluation of the metabolome and RNA-seq data for enzymes associated with central carbon metabolism allowed recognition of additional *SUB1A* influence, notably during recovery (Fig. 4). Submergence had the greatest impact on sucrose, glucose, and malate content (Fig. 4b). Although submergence depleted sucrose by more than 50% in both genotypes, significantly higher levels were maintained in M202(Sub1), consistent with previous soluble carbohydrate measurements (Fukao *et al.* 2006) and *SUB1A's* growth quiescence strategy (Fukao *et al.* 2008). Following desubmergence, sucrose recovered to within 22% of controls by dusk, but neither genotype fully returned to control levels within 24 h. These distinctions correspond well with *SUB1A's* restriction of the elevation of transcripts associated with sucrose catabolism, including amylases and sucrose synthases, with differences evident at multiple recovery time points (i.e., lower in M202(Sub1) after 3d\_sub) (Fig. 4a).

Although sucrose was reduced in both genotypes by submergence and throughout recovery, glucose was only increased significantly by submergence in M202(Sub1) and remained significantly higher in recovering M202(Sub1) than in control M202(Sub1) for the duration of the experiment (Fig. 4b). The impact of *SUB1A* on glucose content was not significant at 3d\_sub, but M202(Sub1) substantially increased glucose by dusk and during the night following desubmergence. *SUB1A* did not impact PPP throughput based on 6PG, which was lower but not significantly so in *SUB1A*-containing plants at dusk, midnight, and dawn. These plants also began to increase malate levels at dusk, but *SUB1A*'s impact was not statistically distinguishable until dawn. Transcripts encoding cytoplasmic malate dehydrogenase (CMDH) and malic enzyme (NADH ME1/ME2) were slightly but not significantly down-regulated by *SUB1A* during submergence.

Previous comparison of selected enzymes and metabolites (starch, soluble sugars, ethanol) associated with anaerobic metabolism in these genotypes over a time course of

submergence demonstrated that *SUB1A* enhances the potential for anaerobic metabolism, but this was not manifested at the level of ethanol production because of restricted starch catabolism (Fukao *et al.* 2006). Consistently, the RNA-seq data also indicated a higher capacity for *SUB1A* to synthesize enzymes of glycolysis and anaerobic respiration during submergence, as well as hasten the shift back to aerobic respiration and photosynthetic metabolism after desubmergence. Transcripts of gene family members encoding sucrose synthase, hexokinase, aldolase, phosphofructokinase, and pyruvate kinase (PK) were upregulated at 3d\_sub in both genotypes, although levels were curbed in many instances by *SUB1A*. By contrast, as previously reported, *PDC* and *ADH1*/*2* transcripts were more highly up-regulated at 3d\_sub in M202(Sub1). The only product of anaerobic metabolism successfully measured in the metabolome analyses was lactate, which was variable, likely due to its efflux from plant cells (Xia & Saglio 1992). Although pyruvate could not be resolved by either GC-MS or NMR and ethanol production cannot be reliably measured in the large volume submergence system used, we previously demonstrated that *SUB1A* limits ethanol synthesis (Fukao et al. 2006). Beyond supporting earlier evidence that the metabolic flow from glycolysis into anaerobic respiration is enhanced by both genotypes in response to submergence, the data demonstrated that the potential for anaerobic metabolism waned during recovery.

The metabolite profiling demonstrated commonalities and distinctions between genotypes, with several indications of more rapid recovery of photosynthesis in the tolerant genotype. 3PG is an intermediate in glycolysis and a product of the Calvin-Benson cycle. It peaked at midday in non-submerged control plants, plummeted during submergence and did not recover by midday, 24 h after desubmergence (Fig. 4b). 3PG levels were not different in the two genotypes at any time point, suggesting it was largely diurnally regulated. S6P is the biosynthetic precursor to sucrose, and its accumulation likely indicates active photosynthetic

carbon assimilation. S6P was greatly depleted by submergence and recovered rapidly in M202(Sub1), whereas in submerged/recovering M202 this metabolite remained statistically lower than control levels for the duration of the experiment (Fig. S3). Thus, *SUB1A* drove significant differences in S6P between the submerged plants at dusk, dawn, and midday. These improvements in S6P in M202(Sub1) could reflect more rapid recovery of sucrose synthesis in M202(Sub1) resulting from starch degradation or photosynthesis. As the S6P increase in M202(Sub1) was evident at time points when light was available (e.g., from desubmergence to dusk and after the following dawn) but not at midnight, more rapid recovery of photosynthesis was responsible. Distinctions in the rapid recovery of transcripts encoding several enzymes also support enhanced photosynthetic recovery by M202(Sub1). This included the rapid rebound in several β-amylase (BAM) and one pyruvate orthophosphate dikinase (OS03G0432100) mRNAs by dusk. *SUB1A* constitutively promoted a phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPCK) mRNA (OS09G0315700; *OsPPC2b*) and regulated the decline during recovery of another (OS01G0208700; *OsPPC4*). These data support the conclusion that photosynthetic recovery is more rapid in M202(Sub1) following submergence, as demonstrated through direct monitoring by Alpuerto *et al.* (2016). Despite this recovery, M202(Sub1) appears to continue restricting starch catabolism during the early period of recovery.

*SUB1A and submergence influence key metabolites and gene transcripts associated with lowenergy sensing and carbohydrate catabolism*

T6P functions in the control of sucrose consumption under low energy conditions and is generally well correlated with sucrose availability and content (O'Hara *et al.* 2013; Figueroa & Lunn 2016), Because T6P is present at very low concentrations in plant tissue, its detection and quantification are challenging. Reported methods to quantify T6P include

anion-exchange chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (AEC-MS) (Delatte *et al.* 2009) and hydrophilic interaction chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (HILIC-MS) (Toraño *et al.* 2012), both of which have drawbacks. In AEC-MS, the detection threshold is relatively high, and special instrumentation is needed to remove non-volatile salts prior to MS. For HILIC-MS, extensive column equilibration with the target sample matrix is necessary to reproducibly separate the target compound, and matrix effects can confound separations when the nature of the matrix varies with sample type, which is a frequent problem in metabolomics. Here we applied an alternative reverse-phase ion-pair (RPIP)- UPLC-MS as described in the Supplementary Methods, which uses an ion-pairing reagent (IPR) that has an ionic functional group that can ion-pair with the target compound and a hydrophobic tail that interacts with the column stationary phase.

T6P levels were consistently lower in M202(Sub1) throughout the diurnal time course in control plants and decreased significantly in both genotypes at 3d\_sub (FDRcorrected p < 0.001, both genotypes) (Fig. 5). Although *SUB1A* did not significantly impact T6P levels in submerged or recovering plants at any time point, levels trended higher at dawn and dusk in the tolerant genotype. Based on the RNA-seq data, the major *SUB1A*-dependent difference in the T6P pathway genes was a pronounced down-regulation of *TREHALASE1* (*TRE1*) by *SUB1A* at 3d\_sub, which was maintained to a lesser magnitude through dawn and then up-regulated more in M202(Sub1) than M202 at midday (Fig. 5). *TREHALOSE-P PHOSPHATASE* (*TPP*) genes, including *OsTPP7* that enhances coleoptile elongation of rice seed germinated underwater (Kretzschmar *et al.* 2015), displayed only minor differences between genotypes. Several *TREHALOSE-P SYNTHETASE* (*TPS*) transcripts, on the other hand, were similarly up-regulated by submergence in both genotypes. In summary, T6P levels declined dramatically in shoots during submergence, but at the resolution of these

experiments this is not influenced by *SUB1A* despite marked differences in regulation of transcripts encoding enzymes associated with T6P metabolism.

Cellular sensing of carbon starvation by the sucrose non-fermenting kinase 1 (SnRK1) is negatively regulated by T6P content and positively regulated by other factors. In rice established from seed to seedling underwater, sugar starvation promoted by O<sup>2</sup> deficiency activates a signaling cascade that includes calcineurin B-like interacting protein kinase 15 (CIPK15) (Lee et al., 2009). CIPK15 is required for activation of SnRK1, although demonstration of direct phosphorylation is lacking, promoting phosphorylation of the transcription factor MYBS1 leading to transcription of -amylase genes including *AMY3D*. This pathway enhances the breakdown of starch in the endosperm and its mobilization to the germinating seedling to fuel the anaerobic metabolism necessary for the coleoptile to elongate until it reaches the floodwater surface to establish aeration. *CIPK15* (OS11G0113700) was highly up-regulated at 3d\_sub in both genotypes, but to a significantly higher level in M202 (M202 log2FC = 8.7; M202(Sub1) log2FC = 4.0) (Fig. 5). Notably, the dampening of *CIPK15* mRNA accumulation by *SUB1A* continued throughout the recovery. The closely related *CIPK14* (OS12G0113500) displayed a nearly identical pattern of regulation. A submergence-induced increase in production of CIPK15 and possibly CIPK14 could enable increased signaling of a sucrose limitation.

We propose that the decline in T6P and increase in *CIPK15* mRNA in response to submergence fosters SnRK1 activation leading to -amylase accumulation and leaf starch catabolism. The significantly less pronounced up-regulation of *CIPK15* mRNA in M202(Sub1) (Fig. 5) is consistent with the more modest increases in *AMY2A/Ramy2A* and *AMY3D/Ramy3DAmy3* (Fig 3a). MYBS1 interacts with MYBGA to transactivate *AMY3D* through distinct *cis*-elements during germination (reviewed by Lee *et al.* 2014). The dampening of carbohydrate consumption during submergence by *SUB1A* could be enforced twofold: first by elevation of SLENDER LEAF RICE1/ SLENDER LEAF RICE1 LIKE1 to limit GA promoted MYBGA activity (Fukao et al., 2008) and second by dampening of CIPK15 activation of SnRK1 to limit MYBS1 activity. Thus, the restriction of starch catabolism by *SUB1A* in shoot tissue likely involves convergence of GA-regulated and sucrose/low energy-sensing modules. The decline in T6P observed at 3d\_sub in both genotypes would promote SnRK1 activation, but this does not appear to be influenced by *SUB1A* at the resolution of these experiments. It will be of interest to explore through mutational analyses the interplay between CIPK14/15 activation and T6P repression of SnRK1-mediated low energy sensing during submergence.

#### *SUB1A reduces stress-induced amino acid accumulation and may modulate TCA cycle flux*

Free amino acid levels for alanine, asparagine, β-alanine, glutamate, glutamine, histidine, isoleucine, leucine, methionine, serine, S-methylmethionine, threonine, tryptophan, tyrosine, and valine were all significantly increased by submergence, in some cases by an order of magnitude (Fig 7b; Fig. S3). Levels of many of these were also altered during recovery. In the case of isoleucine and leucine, the difference between submerged and control was reduced to the point of non-significance by midday. However, treatment differences for alanine, asparagine, β-alanine, glutamate, glutamine, histidine, lysine, methionine, Smethylmethionine, serine, threonine, tryptophan, tyrosine, and valine remained statistically distinguishable for at least one genotype at midday (Fig. S3). Overall, amino acid metabolism in submerged/recovering plants did not return to control conditions within the time frame of recovery evaluated.

The accumulation of alanine and GABA are a signature of hypoxic response in multiple species including rice (Muench *et al.* 1993; Mustroph *et al.* 2014). Pyruvate catabolism to alanine is likely to be energetically favorable over ethanol production under a severe hypoxia or anoxia as it neither requires NADH nor results in carbon loss through evolution of CO2. The production of alanine under hypoxia promotes substrate level ATP production by 2-oxyglutarate dehydrogenase (2OGDH), due to bifurcation of the TCA cycle under hypoxic conditions (António *et al.* 2016). GABA production may also be beneficial in terms of maintaining cytosolic pH and recycling through the GABA shunt into succinate to be consumed by the mitochondrial electron transport chain once it is reactivated upon reaeration (Mustroph *et al.* 2014; António *et al.* 2016).

Alanine and GABA were drastically more abundant in submerged/recovering plants relative to the controls, but alanine accumulation was significantly attenuated by *SUB1A* for the duration of the experiment (Fig. 7). *SUB1A* also lessened accumulation of valine, threonine, isoleucine, glutamate, and asparagine as reported (Barding *et al.* 2012, 2013), which continued for at least 24 h after desubmergence Both genotypes saw broad upregulation of members of gene families involved in amino acid biosynthesis during submergence (Fig. 6a). But there were limited transcript changes that might explain *SUB1A*'s impact on free amino acid accumulation through the recovery period, although there was a trend of slight down-regulation among enzymes of these pathways, especially at 3d\_sub. One exception pertains to asparagine, which was also significantly lower in M202(Sub1) than in M202 throughout recovery. Asparagine functions as a link between carbon and nitrogen metabolism; it is known to negatively regulate nitrate reduction, and asparagine synthetase (ASN) activity can be inhibited by sugars (Joy 1988; Lam *et al.* 1996; Stitt *et al.* 2002). The *SUB1A*-reduced accumulation of asparagine during submergence could indicate better management of nitrogen metabolism, linked to maintenance of higher levels of sugars (Fig. 6b; pathway 8). Differences in ASN may also be at play, as *SUB1A* limited the pronounced up-regulation of *OsASN1* (OS03G0291500; M202 log2FC = 10.0; M202(Sub1) log2FC = 7.6).

Likely the dynamics in free amino acids reflects the level of carbohydrate catabolism coupled with redirection of metabolic flux due to O<sup>2</sup> limitation. Based on coupled metabolome and transcriptome data, the reorganization of the TCA cycle could result in changes in flux that do not require *de novo* transcription and enzyme synthesis. In addition to differences in carbon catabolism, the higher accumulation of amino acids in M202 could also include contributions from reduced rates of protein synthesis (Schippers *et al.* 2015), increased autophagy, or other directed proteolysis of cell components that enhances mobilization of nitrogen resources.

While genes encoding enzymes of the TCA cycle tended to be up-regulated by submergence (Fig. 6a), metabolites of the TCA cycle were generally reduced by submergence (Fig. 6b). Malate fell by 49-53%, and was still 26-31% lower than in control plants after 24 h of recovery (Fig. 6b). Malate is also produced via the peroxisomal glyoxylate cycle. This involves conversion of isocitrate to glyoxylate and succinate by isocitrate lyase (ICL; OS07G0529000) followed by the malate synthase (MS) conversion of glyoxylate and acetyl-CoA to malate. Transcripts encoding ICL and MS were up-regulated by submergence in both genotypes, but with significantly less up-regulation of *ICL* mRNAs in the tolerant genotype (M202 log2FC 12.5; M202(Sub1) log2FC 8.6). Succinate levels were also over 50% depleted in M202 and M202(Sub1) at 3d\_sub, and although they recovered to within 10% of control levels by midnight (Fig. 6b). Succinate levels were significantly reduced again at the dawn and midday recovery time points in both genotypes, as would be expected if electron transport was active. Citrate levels were largely unchanged by submergence but were significantly higher from dusk through dawn in M202(Sub1) (Fig. 6b), while transcripts were not substantially impacted by *SUB1A* for either citrate synthase or aconitase genes (Fig. 6a; pathways 18 and 19). Similarly, malate was higher in M202(Sub1) at dawn and midday, on trajectory for a more rapid recovery than submergence-intolerant M202, although malate in

submerged plants was still well below that for control plants in both genotypes (Fig. 6b). These data demonstrate that *SUB1A's* impact on recovery included TCA metabolites and DE of family members of key enzymes.

# *RNA-seq data are consistent with previous studies*

Transcripts of several genes involved in the *SUB1A* growth quiescence response previously were evaluated during submergence by semiquantitative and qRT-PCR in the same genotypes under nearly identical conditions (Fukao *et al.* 2006, 2011, 2012; Fukao & Bailey-Serres 2008). Although the magnitude of submergence- and *SUB1A*-induced differences sometimes varied, expression levels at 3d\_sub measured by RNA-seq were largely consistent with prior reports (Fig. S5). Another study recorded significant *SUB1A*regulation of levels of mRNAs encoding three families of ERF transcription factors using microarrays with tissue produced in the same greenhouse (Jung *et al.* 2010). The 3d\_sub response of the current study is largely consistent with that report, and the recovery data further demonstrate notable regulation during recovery of ERF-VIIs by *SUB1A* including *SUB1B* and *SUB1C* (Fig. S6).

# *Photosynthetic machinery mRNAs show little regulation by SUB1A*

Although photosynthesis is resource-limited by reduced light penetration and gas diffusion through water during submergence (Winkel *et al.* 2014), damage to or degradation of the photosynthetic machinery imposes within-plant limitations that can persist after desubmergence. Chlorophyll loss, reduced maximum PSII quantum efficiency, decreased Rubisco activity, and limited hydraulic conductance have all been identified as potential limitations to photosynthetic recovery in rice (Panda *et al.* 2008; Sarkar & Panda 2009; Setter *et al.* 2010). Key genes, especially those for photosystem subunits, chlorophyll binding

proteins, and rate-limiting enzymes of the Calvin cycle, showed transcriptional downregulation during submergence in both genotypes in this study. This could reflect acclimation to an environment of restricted gas exchange or reduced investment of energy into the photosynthetic apparatus.

Submergence-tolerant rice cultivars that possess *SUB1A-1*, including M202(Sub1) and the *Sub1* locus donor parent FR13A, are able to recover photosynthesis more rapidly, and the improvement has been linked to better maintenance of chlorophyll and nonphotochemical quenching capacity during submergence (Panda *et al.* 2008; Sarkar & Panda 2009; Alpuerto *et al.* 2016). However, we found no obvious, direct transcriptional basis for improved photosynthetic performance in M202(Sub1). Transcript data were examined for genes encoding thylakoid proteins, including photosystem subunits, light harvesting antenna proteins, electron transport proteins (Supplemental Fig. S7), and the Calvin-Benson cycle (Supplemental Fig. S8). The largest fold-changes occurred for a plastoquinone oxidoreductase (OS04G0473025) mRNA at 3d\_sub and at dawn, when light availability was limiting regardless of gene regulation. These data indicate that *SUB1A*'s mechanism of maintaining electron transport and nonphotochemical quenching is not likely to involve direct transcriptional regulation of nuclear-encoded subunits of the photosynthetic apparatus. Protection from ROS species which threaten the thylakoid membrane and its components is likely a mechanism (Delong & Steffen 1997; Blokhina *et al.* 2003), as oxidative stress is reduced upon desubmergence in M202(Sub1) compared to the submergence-intolerant genotype (Fukao *et al.* 2011).

*SUB1A negatively regulates transcripts associated with chloroplast and chlorophyll turnover* Maintenance of chloroplast ultrastructure has been associated with greater submergence tolerance when compared among three grass species: *Vetiveria zizanioides*,

*Paspalum notatum*, and *Axonopus compressus* (Xia *et al.* 2009). Previously, *SUB1A* was shown to prolong chlorophyll maintenance under submergence and in complete darkness (Fukao *et al.* 2006, 2012). The *Arabidopsis* gene *CV* (*CHLOROPLAST VESICULATION*) induces chloroplast degradation during abiotic stress, with silencing of *CV* enhancing salt, drought, and oxidative stress tolerance (Wang & Blumwald 2014). The rice *CV* ortholog OS05G0575000 was significantly down-regulated in M202(Sub1) (log2FC -1.2) but not M202 (log2FC 0.08) at 3d\_sub; a significant difference between genotypes (Fig. S9). This suggests *SUB1A* could maintain the photosynthetic machinery during submergence for faster recovery of photosynthesis. *Arabidopsis* plants that express a dexamethasone (DEX) inducible *CV* elevate glutamine synthase 1 (GS1) protein and activity upon DEX treatment. Consistent with its higher *CV* mRNA levels, only M202 significantly up-regulated *GLUTAMINE SYNTHETASE (GS) 1;2* (OS03G0223400) mRNA at 3d\_sub (M202 log2FC 1.49; M202(Sub1) log2FC 0.35) (Fig. 6 pathway 6). The submergence-sensitive genotype also had higher levels of transcripts encoding enzymes involved in chlorophyll catabolism (*RCC1*, *NYC1*, *NYC2*, *SGR*), and significantly lower levels of transcript encoding the negative regulator of senescence *ONSET OF SENESCENCE* (*OsDOS*; OS01G0192000) (Fig. S9). These results are consistent with the prior transcript studies that examined the prolonged tolerance of darkness conferred by *SUB1A* (Fukao *et al.* 2012). These findings support the hypothesis that *SUB1A* limits chloroplast degradation mediated by CV, a process that involves formation of vesicles from plastids that are trafficked to the vacuole for recycling. The restriction of this pathway could further reinforce energy conservation as a mechanism to limit growth during a transient period of submergence.

Continued exploration of the mechanisms maintaining photosynthetic capacity and regrowth ability during and after submergence will be an important target of crop improvement. This is emphasized by the finding that the donor of the *Sub1* locus (FR13A) tolerates and recovers from submergence better than *Sub1* introgression lines (Singh *et al.* 2014). Although *SUB1A* is a crucial component of "climate-smart" rice, there are undoubtedly more genes and mechanisms existing in rice germplasm that, when identified, may be exploited to further improve crop yield stability.

#### **Conclusions**

This study demonstrates that rice plants that endure 3 days of complete submergence are able to significantly recover both their shoot transcriptome and metabolome to the prestress (control) state within a 24 h recovery period. The rebound of the transcriptome preceded that of metabolism. Under the conditions tested, the submergence tolerance regulator *SUB1A-1* influenced both the transcriptome and metabolome primarily during submergence. Consistent with prior reports, *SUB1A* swayed the regulation of mRNAs and metabolites to impart more conservative consumption of carbohydrate reserves. Evaluation of levels of T6P, a metabolite that reports sucrose status to the low energy-activated SnRK1, identified a rapidly reversible nadir in T6P levels. Although *SUB1A* did not clearly influence T6P content, it dampened the up-regulation of *CIPK15* mRNA, which is required for SnRK1 activation and downstream starch catabolism in coleoptiles of anaerobically germinating rice seedlings (Lee *et al.* 2009). These results enable the integration of earlier knowledge of *SUB1A*'s dampening of GA-responsive starch catabolism. The results also clearly show that *SUB1A* positively impacts the transition from stress amelioration to the return to homeostasis, as plants with *SUB1A* showed evidence of resumed photosynthesis through improved glucose and S6P content within hours of desubmergence. Additionally, the limited up-regulation of a regulator of chloroplast degradation during submergence and the more rapid recovery of metabolites associated with photosynthesis in the *SUB1A* genotype demonstrates an advantage in the return to homeostasis, which involves greater repair and rebuilding of damaged or degraded components in the intolerant genotype. This study underscores the

value of combined analysis of transcript accumulation and metabolite content, to gain an understanding of effective stress and recovery strategies. It also points to several new targets for manipulation of submergence tolerance.
