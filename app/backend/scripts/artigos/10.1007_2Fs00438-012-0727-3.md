## Digital gene expression analysis of early root infection resistance to *Sporisorium reilianum* f. sp. *zeae* in maize

Shaopeng Zhang · Yannong Xiao · Jiuran Zhao · Fengge Wang · Yonglian Zheng

Received: 30 June 2012/Accepted: 11 November 2012/Published online: 30 November 2012 © Springer-Verlag Berlin Heidelberg 2012

Abstract The maize smut fungus, Sporisorium reilianum f. sp. zeae, which is an important biotrophic pathogen responsible for extensive crop losses, can infect maize by invading the root during the early seedling stage. In order to investigate disease-resistance mechanisms at this early seedling stage, digital gene expression analysis, which applies a dual-enzyme approach, was used to identify the transcriptional changes in the roots of Huangzao4 (susceptible) and Mo17 (resistant) after root inoculation with S. reilianum. During the infection in the roots, the expression pattern of pathogenesis-related genes in Huangzao4 and Mo17 were significantly differentially regulated at different infection stages. The glutathione S-transferase enzyme activity and reactive oxygen species levels also showed changes before and after inoculation. ## Introduction

Maize head smut, which occurs throughout the world wherever corn (maize) is grown, is caused by *Sporisorium reilianum* f. sp. *zeae* (Ali and Baggett 1990). The maize yield is severely reduced by the replacement of grain kernels and tassel spikelets with large black smut sori. In China, particularly in the main region of cereal production, yield loss can be 3,00,000 tons due to this disease each year (Bai et al. 1994; He et al. 2005; Wang et al. 2002).

Head smut in maize is not evident until tassels and ears appear (Matyac and Kommedahl 1985). When compatible, the yeast-like sporidia of different mating types recognize each other and fuse to produce dikaryotic hyphae (Martinez et al. 2002; Ruiz-Herrera and Martínez-Espinoza 2010; Schirawski et al. 2005). Then, this soil-borne pathogen can infect the host plant at the seedling stage by penetrating the roots (Frederiksen 1977; Martinez et al. 2000). The hyphae of *S. reilianum* invade into the roots and grow systemically in the meristem and growing point of the plant. The hyphae act like a biotrophic endophyte and maintain a biotrophic relationship with the host until sporulation at a specific time point (Martinez et al. 1999; Mendgen and Hahn 2002). During the entire vegetative stage of maize, there are no disease symptoms. Only at the flowering stage, the disease symptoms become apparent in the male and female inflorescences of maize. In order to fully understand this maize––S. reilianum interaction, the study of early infection events of S. reilianum is the necessary first step.

The long co-evolution of host and pathogen has caused complex mechanisms of interaction involving the innate immune system of plants and the virulence factors of pathogens (De Pinto et al. [2006;](#page-14-0) Stahl and Bishop [2000](#page-15-0)). The first layer of plant defense, called basal immunity, is based on recognition of conserved microbial molecules leading to pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) triggered immunity (Jones and Dangl [2006\)](#page-15-0). Chitin, a major constituent of the cell walls in most fungi, is a potent PAMP-triggered immunity in several plant species (Zipfel [2009\)](#page-16-0). Chitin responsive genes that lose functions by T-DNA insertion show increased susceptibility of Arabidopsis to the powdery mildew pathogen Erysiphe cichoracearum (Ramonell et al. [2005\)](#page-15-0). The second layer of defense, resistance gene-based immunity, relies on the recognition of effectors, such as nucleotide binding leucinerich repeats (NB-LRRs), and mediated disease resistance can be effective against biotrophic and hemi-biotrophic pathogens (Jones and Dangl [2006\)](#page-15-0).

In turn, pathogens can adopt diverse strategies to overcome plant defenses and enhance their fitness in the body of the host. Some pathogens overcome the first cell layer by applying mechanical force or by secreting cuticle and cell wall-degrading enzymes (Mendgen et al. [1996](#page-15-0); van Kan [2006](#page-16-0)). Ustilago maydis, as an example, possesses 33 putative plant cell wall-degrading enzymes including glucanases, pectin lyases and pectin methyl esterases (Ka¨mper et al. [2006](#page-15-0)). Some prokaryotic plant pathogens, such as Pseudomonas syringae pv.tomato, make use of ubiquitination to evade and manipulate these defense reactions (Rosebrock et al. [2007](#page-15-0)). Some eukaryotic plant pathogens, such as F. oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici, secrete an effector that can both trigger and suppress resistance gene-based immunity depending on the special situation (Houterman et al. [2008\)](#page-15-0). In a word, these studies exemplify that host– pathogen interactions vary widely. In order to explore the early root resistance mechanism to S. reilianum and uncover part of the mysteries behind this interaction mechanism, a global approach for understanding the pathosystem is, therefore, merited.

Genome-wide expression analysis will be quite helpful for investigating complex mechanisms of host–pathogen interplay. Over the past decade, there have been two main approaches for genome-wide expression analysis: the hybridization-based approach and the sequence-based approach. Recently, the sequence-based methods have become increasingly popular due to developments in sequencing technologies (Shendure and Ji [2008](#page-15-0)). In contrast to cross-hybridization methods, sequence-based methods require no prior knowledge of sequence and transcript composition, which is limited to fully sequenced genomes, and provide quantitative measures of transcript abundance based on read count over a wide dynamic range (Blencowe et al. [2009;](#page-14-0) Simon et al. [2009;](#page-15-0) 't Hoen et al. [2008](#page-15-0)).

RNA-seq is quickly becoming a valuable tool for transcriptomics, such as digital gene expression technology (Wang et al. [2009](#page-16-0)). Digital gene expression (DGE) technology uses a 3<sup>0</sup> -targeted sequencing strategy to generate a single 20–21 base tag from the 3<sup>0</sup> -end of a given transcript. The length of the tag depends on the kind of restriction enzyme used in library construction. The clean tags are collected and mapped to the genome. The number of tags is proportional to the abundance of cognate transcripts in the specific tissue (Audic and Claverie [1997](#page-14-0)). In this study, we used DGE technology for gene expression analysis utilizing the newly assembled B73 maize reference genome. A dual-enzyme approach that enhanced coverage in cases where a restriction site was either absent or within 20 bases of the poly (A) tail was used (Eveland et al. [2010](#page-15-0)).

In this study, we used activated teliospores of S. reilianum to inoculate the radicle root tip at the early seedling stage, and analyzed the expression profile of roots in response to infection of S. reilianum based on the latest dual-enzyme DGE technology.

## Materials and methods

Sample and inoculum preparation

Two distinct inbred lines of maize, Huangzao4 and Mo17, were used in our studies, which are the representative lines of Chinese Tangsipingtou and American Lancaster heterotic groups, respectively. Huangzao4 is highly susceptible to head smut, the disease incidence of which is 50–100 %. Mo17 is highly resistant to head smut, the disease incidence of which is 0–1 %. These materials were provided by the research fellow Shihuang Zhang from the Institute of Crop Sciences at the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Wang et al. [2002;](#page-16-0) Zhang et al. [2002\)](#page-16-0). Seeds of Huangzao4 and Mo17 were initially sterilized by soaking in 75 % alcohol for 5 min, soaked in water overnight, and were then placed in sterilized sand for germination at 25 C in the dark.

The teliospores of S. reilianum, which were used for smearing inoculation, were provided by the research fellow Shihuang Zhang (the Institute of Crop Sciences at the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences) and Zhenhua Wang (Northeast Agricultural University). After drying the teliospores, every 10 mg was weighted and collected into 1.5 ml centrifuge tubes. They were incubated in 2 % (wt/vol) chloramine T (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd, China) for 15 min of sterilization. After rinsing three times by water, they were incubated in 0.02 M HCl for 6 h at 25 C in the dark and 2 % (wt/vol) sucrose solution for 48 h at 25 C in the dark to stimulate the germination of teliospores (Fig. 1a, b; Kang et al. [1994;](#page-15-0) Li et al. [1995](#page-15-0)). After 2 days' shaking culture (200 r/min) of the germinated teliospores in the 2 % sucrose solution at 28 C in the dark, the filamentous hyphae could be formed in the teliospore suspension (Fig. 1c).

2 days after seed germination, the average length of radicle root was 7–8 cm. The water suspension of germinated teliospores was smeared and incubated on the surface of root ends (2–3 cm) using 200 ll pipette tips (Fig. 1d). Then, these seeds were transferred to the cultivation box, in which the inoculation parts of the roots were covered with sterile sands and the upside sampling parts of roots were out of the sands and exposed to the air (Fig. 1e). Fungal hyphae in the root tissue were examined by staining with lacto phenol cotton blue (LPCB) for 5 min (Fig. [2\)](#page-3-0). After that, the seedlings were incubated in a culture chamber for further infection of S. reilianum at 23 C:20 C (light:dark, 14:10 h) and 20 % relative humidity (Fig. 1f; Osorio and Frederiksen [1998](#page-15-0)).

### DNA, RNA isolation and PCR assay

The PCR-based assay was used to detect the fungal DNA of S. reilianum that had proliferated into the un-inoculated part of the roots in maize, from which gene-specific primers SR1 and SR3 were derived from the genome of S. reilianum (Ni et al. [2006](#page-15-0); Xu et al. [1999](#page-16-0)). This assay can detect fungal DNA of less than 8 pg, irrespective of the presence of maize DNA (Xu et al. [1999\)](#page-16-0). We extracted the total DNA of the sampling part in the root for each seedling to detect the hyphal existence by PCR assay so as to investigate the early infecting differences between these two inbred lines. The PCR-positive rates were calculated for every thirty seedlings. For each PCR reaction, total S. reilianum DNA was used as the positive control and the sterile water was used as the negative control so as to prevent the false result of PCR. Three independent biological replicates were performed for each test.

## Mapping and extraction of gene information

The collapsed reads were mapped to the maize reference genome using Vmatch (<http://www.vmatch.de>) sequence analysis software (Eveland et al. [2010\)](#page-15-0). Persistent indices were constructed using the Vmktree function for each of the 10 unmasked, assembled maize chromosomes and for an additional chromosome 0, including non-assembled sequences (B73 RefGen\_v1). For each of the 11 indices, the unique DpnII and NlaIII tags that have complete matches to all possible 20- and 21-mer sequences, respectively, were checked using the Vmatch algorithm. This included both sense and antisense matches and strand information for each tag. In the second step of mapping, an editing distance of 1 was used to allow for single mismatches, insertions, or deletions. Mapping data from all indices were then parsed together. The final results from DpnII and NlaIII libraries were analyzed independently. A working gene set was used (gene build 4a.53; maizesequence.org), and tags that mapped uniquely up to three places in the maize genome were selected to extract gene information using the Ensembl Application Perl Interface [\(http://uswest.](http://uswest.ensembl.org/info/docs/api) [ensembl.org/info/docs/api\)](http://uswest.ensembl.org/info/docs/api). In order to maximize the capture of complete UTRs, each gene model and the gene space was then computationally extended by 300 nucleotides at both 50 - and 3<sup>0</sup> -ends.

## Statistical analysis

At present, there are three major algorithms to determine whether read counts for a gene are significantly different across experimental conditions: edgeR, DEseq and DEB (Anders [2010](#page-14-0); Robinson et al. [2010](#page-15-0); Yao and Yu [2011](#page-16-0)). In this study, genes that were differentially expressed between CK and P1, CK and P2, P1 and P2 stage were identified using a free open-source ''DEseq'' package based on R software, which allowed analysis of experiments with no biological replicates ([http://www-huber.embl.de/users/](http://www-huber.embl.de/users/anders/DESeq/) [anders/DESeq/](http://www-huber.embl.de/users/anders/DESeq/); Anders [2010\)](#page-14-0). DEseq used a special model based on the negative binomial distribution, which had enough power to detect differential expression for discrete and skewed count data. Differentially expressed genes were firstly ranked by p values and then were filtered for further analysis according to the filtering criteria (log2Foldchange [2, p\0.01; Fig. [3](#page-4-0), Supplementary Fig. 1).

## Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

The gene ontology (GO) enrichment networks of these differentially expressed genes, which can map over-represented functional genes in a given gene set, were constructed using the BiNGO2.3 package of Cytoscape software based on the ZmB73\_4a.53\_filter gene set (Maere et al. [2005](#page-15-0); Tatebe et al. [2010](#page-16-0); Zhang et al. [2009](#page-16-0)). A hypergeometric test was selected for statistical calculation. The Benjamini and Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to control false negatives (Benjamini and Hochberg [1995\)](#page-14-0).

#### Measurement of enzyme activities

For enzyme activity assays, 30 radicle roots for each phenotype were harvested and pooled as one sample after 6 days of inoculation. The Bradford assay that relied on the binding of the dye coomassie blue G250 to protein was used to measure the concentration of protein in these samples (Bradford [1976\)](#page-14-0). Glutathione S-transferase (EC 2.5.1.18) activity was determined using the colorimetric assay kit (Jiancheng Biotechnology Co., Nanjing, China) for quantitative determination. Two biological and three technical replicates were performed for each sample.

## Histochemical detection of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Super anion radicals were detected using nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) staining (Jabs et al. [1996](#page-15-0)). Hydrogen peroxide accumulation was detected using 3,3<sup>0</sup> -diaminobenzidine 4 HCl (DAB) staining (Schraudner et al. [1998\)](#page-15-0). Radicle roots of Huangzao4 and Mo17 were, respectively, collected before inoculation and 6 days after inoculation. The front parts of the roots were cut and immersed in 1 mg/ml NBT in 0.01 M PBS buffer (pH 7.8) for 60 min or in 1 mg/ml DAB (pH 3.8) in 0.01 M PBS buffer (pH 7.8) for 10 min at room temperature under laboratory lighting. Stained roots were cleaned in clean water for 10 min prior to photography.

## Lignin content determination

For lignin content measurement, 1.5 g pooled samples with dried radicle roots for P1 and P2 phenotypes were harvested and then were deactivated immediately at 105 C for 30 min. Samples were deeply dried at 65 C for 2 days and then ground into a fine powder. After vacuum drying, lignin content was quantified according to the modified method described by Kirk and Obst ([1988\)](#page-15-0). The acidinsoluble lignin was determined gravimetrically, and the acid-soluble lignin was determined spectrophotometrically. The total lignin was equal to the sum of the acid-soluble lignin and the acid-insoluble lignin. Three independent biological replicates were performed for each sample.

## Histochemical test of lignin accumulation

Radicle roots of Huangzao4 and Mo17 for P1 and P2 stages, respectively, were collected after 6 days of inoculation. Cross-sections were made from the roots using a vibrating blade microtome (VT1200S, Leica Microsystems, Deerfield, IL, USA). Lignin accumulation was tested using Wiesner reagent (Pomar et al. [2004](#page-15-0)). 100 lm sections were incubated for 20 min in a phloroglucinol solution (5 % in 95 % ethanol) and then were treated with 12 M HCl for 2 min.

#### Results

Early infection difference between the susceptible and resistant lines

The hyphal growth of S. reilianum in the radicle root was observed after 6 days of inoculation to investigate the infection pattern. In the inoculation part of root, a fungal sheath was formed surrounding the root and the long filamentous hyphae of teliospores were appressed on the root surface (Fig. [2a](#page-3-0), b). After cutting through the length of root in the inoculation part, the penetrated hyphae could be observed (Fig. [2](#page-3-0)c). In the un-inoculated sampling part of root, no visible hyphae were observed on the root surface (Fig. [2](#page-3-0)d, e). But inside the root of sampling part, a small amount of proliferated hyphae could still be found (Fig. [2](#page-3-0)f, g). It is clear that the filamentous hyphae of S. reilianum could penetrate through the surface of the root into the host tissues and gradually proliferate into the un-inoculated part through the inside of root.

Six time points (2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 14 dpi) were set to determine the general rule of early infection differences between Huangzao4 and Mo17. According to the results, proliferating hyphae in the un-inoculated part of the inoculated root could not be detected by PCR until the 4th day after inoculation (Fig. [2](#page-3-0)h). The S. reilianum detection rate dramatically increased till the 10th day after inoculation with the time process in both Huangzao4 and Mo17. However, at the same time point of each test, the PCRpositive detection rates of S. reilianum in Huangzao4 were significantly higher than that in Mo17 (Fig. [2](#page-3-0)h). During this early root infection process, hyphal-infecting efficiency in the roots was obviously different between susceptible and resistant maize lines.

In order to deeply clarify the underlying molecular mechanisms of these responding differences between Huangzao4 and Mo17, the mock-inoculated group (CK) and the pathogen-inoculated group (P1 and P2) were then set for further expression analysis.

## Sequencing and mapping

To generate digital expression signatures for early radicle roots of maize with different phenotypes after 6 days of inoculation, we used the Illumina Genome Analyzer technology for massive parallel sequencing. We constructed the libraries for each sample using the DpnII and NlaIII restriction enzymes. This dual-enzyme method could improve coverage in a case where a restriction site was absent or within 20 bases of the poly (A) tail (Eveland et al. [2010\)](#page-15-0). To enable multiplexing of DpnII and NlaIII libraries, we used a custom sequencing primer that incorporated the restriction site at the 5<sup>0</sup> -end of each read. The specificity of the restriction site thus allowed for library recognition and sorting of reads sequenced concurrently in a single lane. Six DGE libraries were produced for each restriction enzyme. We used Vmatch (<http://www.vmatch.de>) to map these unique consensus sequence tags to the maize reference genome (B73 RefGen\_v1; Eveland et al. [2010;](#page-15-0) Selitrennikoff [2001\)](#page-15-0). We allowed one given tag to map consistently to three places in the genome. Tags mapping to four or more places were considered the repeats and were removed from subsequent analyses. Ensembl Perl Application Programming Interface ([http://uswest.ensembl.org/info/docs/api\)](http://uswest.ensembl.org/info/docs/api) was used to associate mapped tags with the working gene set of 1,08,745 gene models including evidence-based (86 %) and abinitio predictions (14 %; gene build 4a.53 from maizesequence.org). In total, we indentified 38,268 working genes in the DpnII library and 42,684 working genes in the NlaIII library. Of these, 11 % were DpnII specific, while 20 % were NlaIII specific, and 34,015 genes overlapped between the DpnII and NlaIII library (Fig. [3a](#page-4-0)).

## Differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

In our study, we used the DEseq package of R software to normalize each dataset and we filtered for the DEGs based on a specific significance value: p\0.01, log2Foldchanges [2 (Anders and Huber [2010](#page-14-0); Anders [2010](#page-14-0)). We compared gene expression profiles between CK and P1, CK and P2, P1 and P2 in both Huangzao4 and Mo17, which resulted in plots of the log2Foldchanges against the base means, showing in red those genes that were significant at p\0.01 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Differences in transcriptional changes between Huangzao4 and Mo17 after inoculation were clearly shown, in which most of the DEGs from P1 to P2 stages were down-regulated in Huangzao4 but up-regulated in Mo17.

Because of the different restriction sites for DpnII (GATC) and NlaIII (CATG), the library from each restriction enzyme was actually different. Data from the DpnII and NlaIII libraries were first analyzed separately, and DEGs were filtered, respectively, and then combined together for subsequent analysis. We then found there were different transcriptional responses between Huangzao4 and Mo17 during this early infection process.

Comparing the transcriptome of CK with the P1 stage from Huangzao4 DpnII and NlaIII datasets, we identified 576 (468 up-regulated, 108 down-regulated) DEGs at the P1 stage (log2FoldChange[2, p\0.01). For Mo17, we identified 329 (166 up-regulated, 163 down-regulated) DEGs at the P1 stage. Comparing the CK with the P2 stage for Huangzao4, we identified 613 (391 up-regulated, 222 down-regulated) DEGs at the P2 stage (log2Fold-Change [2, p\0.01). For Mo17, we identified 425 (186 up-regulated, 239 down-regulated) DEGs at the P2 stage. Interestingly, overlapping genes between CK/P1 and CK/P2 in Huangzao4 were much more than that in Mo17, which implied that there might be different defense scheduling at the transcriptional level during the transition of infection from P1 to P2 stages in Huangzao4 roots compared to Mo17 (Fig. [3](#page-4-0)b, c). Total raw data files of these genes have been deposited in the GEO database (GSM368915).

Comparing the transcriptome of the P1 stage with the P2 stage from Huangzao4 DpnII and NlaIII datasets, 409 (69 up-regulated, 340 down-regulated) DEGs were found at the P2 stage (log2FoldChange [2, p\0.01). Comparing the same two stages in the Mo17 datasets showed 155 (124 upregulated, 31 down-regulated) DEGs at the P2 stage. Just as discussed above, there were dramatically different gene expression trends between Huangzao4 and Mo17 when comparing the P1 stage with the P2 stage. At the P2 stage, 74 % of DEGs in Huangzao4 were down-regulated compared to P1; however, 80 % of the DEGs in Mo17 were upregulated compared to P1. This further indicated there was a sizable difference in modulating the defense responses during the different infection processes (i.e., P1 and P2 stages) in Huangzao4 and Mo17 roots.

Expression profiles of pathogenesis-related (PR)-like genes

Pathogenesis-related proteins (PR proteins) are induced in plants in response to a pathogen attack (Kitajima and Sato [1999\)](#page-15-0). They have been classified into 17 groups (PR1 through PR-17) based on their amino acid sequences and biochemical functions (Sels et al. [2008](#page-15-0)). According to the biological functions of these PR proteins, we extracted and clustered corresponding PR-like genes from all DEGs. Specifically, there were 5 antifungal genes, 11 chitinase genes, 2 thaumatin-like genes, 3 proteinase-inhibitor genes, 7 proteinase genes, 18 peroxidase genes, 8 ribonuclease-like genes, 4 lipid-transfer genes, 5 germin-like genes and 5 other PR-like genes (Fig. [4](#page-8-0)). All these genes are listed in Supplementary Table 1. As shown in Fig. [4](#page-8-0), it was very clear that all of these PR-like genes were differentially regulated at different stages of infection in Huangzao4 and Mo17. In general, most of these genes in Huangzao4 were relatively suppressed after inoculation compared to Mo17.

#### Gene ontology enrichment analysis of DEGs

To understand the functional enrichment of these DEGs in Huangzao4 and Mo17 at different infection stages, we performed gene enrichment in molecular functions, biological processes and cellular component categories based on GO (Ashburner et al. [2000](#page-14-0)).

There were two relations that needed to be understood in order to investigate what really happened during this early infection process: (1) the general relation between before and after inoculation, and (2) the special relation between the P1 and P2 stages (Figs. [5](#page-9-0), [8\)](#page-12-0).

Transcriptional changes before and after inoculation

For Huangzao4, there were 298 DEGs (268 up-regulated, 30 down-regulated) that were co-regulated in the same way in both P1 and P2 stages compared to CK. For Mo17, there were 87 co-regulated genes (44 up-regulated, 43 downregulated) in both P1 and P2 stages compared to CK. GO analysis showed that there were similar molecular responses before and after inoculation in both Huangzao4 and Mo17, such as the defense response to fungus, lyase activity, manganese ion binding activity and apoplastic responses (Fig. [5](#page-9-0)a, b; Supplementary Table 2). Comparatively, there were also some differences between Huangzao4 and Mo17, such that many genes with glutathione transferase activity were significantly regulated only in Huangzao4 after inoculation (Fig. [5b](#page-9-0)). In order to investigate the similarities and differences in the defense response before and after inoculation between Huangzao4 and Mo17, we focused on two defense-related GO categories: the category of ''defense response to fungus activity'' and the category of ''glutathione transferase activity''.

Two genes with defense responses to fungus activity were enriched in both Huangzao4 and Mo17, which were barwin-related endoglucanase genes that may play an important role in degrading chitin of the fungal cell wall. These two genes (GRMZM2G454556 and GRMZM2G 419675) shared the same coding sequence. The qPCR validation for one of them was performed, and the result was basically congruent with DGE reads, which showed that defense responses were actually induced to different extents in Huangzao4 and Mo17 at the P1 stage, and then were reinforced significantly in Mo17 but not in Huangzao4 at the P2 stage (Fig. [6](#page-10-0)a, e). Compared to Mo17, these defense responses in Huangzao4 were relatively inhibited by S. reilianum in this early infection process, the mechanism of which is still unknown, but gave us some new ideas about how this pathogen interplayed with the host.

In addition, another large GO category (i.e., the catalytic activity category) was only enriched in Huangzao4 but not in Mo17. We then focused on those genes with glutathione S-transferase activity (GST) and did qPCR validation for them (GRMZM2G161891, GRMZM2G052571 and GRMZM2G044383), which were basically congruent with DGE reads (Fig. [6b](#page-10-0)–d, f–h). The result showed remarkable up-regulation of these GST genes in the roots of Huangzao4 after inoculation.

Changes of lignin accumulation between the P1 and P2 stages

To validate the lignin variations in the roots of Huangzao4 and Mo17, the lignin contents of pooling roots of Huangzao4 and Mo17 were quantitatively measured, which showed that the invasion of hyphae resulted in a 2 % decrease in the total root lignin content at day 6 after inoculation in Huangzao4 but showed few changes in Mo17 (Fig. [10a](#page-14-0)). These radicle roots were extracted from young and tender parts of only 8-day-old seedlings and, therefore, the total lignin contents were naturally in small amounts and this 2 % alteration of lignin content was actually a significant physiological change between P1 and P2 stages (p\0.05).

The Wiesner reaction was used to further detect the lignin deposition on root cross-sections after 6 days of inoculation. In Mo17, few differences were seen in the stele and epidermis of roots between the P1 and P2 stages (Fig. [10b](#page-14-0)). However, the cross-section of Huangzao4 roots at the P2 stage showed decreased lignin deposition, especially in the epidermal intercellular spaces of roots compared with the P1 stage (Fig. [10b](#page-14-0)).

We concluded that this reduction of lignin deposition may still contribute to the susceptibility of Huangzao4 by allowing the gradual degradation of the plant cell wall so as to benefit the penetration and colonization of hyphae in the roots. Conversely, the increase of lignin deposition may delay or inhibit the process of penetration of hyphae into the roots.

#### Discussion

The way of early host–pathogen interaction is a crucial determinant of whether the host will finally develop disease symptoms as the consequence of infection. As an initial step toward developing a comprehensive understanding of disease-resistance mechanisms from the view of host– pathogen interactions, we performed a transcriptome analysis of the root infection of S. reilianum f. sp. zeae in Huangzao4 and Mo17, which showed very clear different resistant levels. From our understanding, the reason for this difference between Huangzao4 and Mo17 lies not only in the intensity of the responses of plants by activating primary defenses, but also in the virulence of the pathogen by suppressing basal defense in different growing contexts.

After inoculation, many PR-like genes were differentially regulated, which is an important role in plant defense against pathogens and stressful environments (Fig. [4\)](#page-8-0). An important feature of most PRs is their antifungal effect. Antifungal proteins (1, 3-beta-glucanase), chitinases and proteinases can be an important antifungal tool in weakening fungal cell walls directly (Van Loon and Van Strien, [1999](#page-16-0)). Most of these genes were down-regulated in Huangzao4, but up-regulated in Mo17. Enzyme inhibitory activity of thaumatin-like proteins and proteinase-inhibitors are also exerted against fungi by inactivating the proteins secreted by these pathogens (Hejgaard et al. [1991](#page-15-0)). These genes were significantly up-regulated in Mo17, but relatively weakly regulated in Huangzao4. The peroxidase and germin-like protein activity also contributes to fungal resistance by strengthening and modifying the plant cell wall (Passardi et al. [2004](#page-15-0)). Similarly, these genes were also differently regulated between Huangzao4 and Mo17. The results uncovered a clear difference in the regulation of

At this early infecting event of head smut, there is a clear general issue about ''to surrender or to resist'' for both host and pathogen, with both needing to adapt to severe environmental conditions or risk death. The difference between susceptible and resistant reactions may be not only due to the intensity and sustainability of the host responses, but also due to the inhibition effect and self-regulation of the pathogen in different environmental contexts. We still do not know the special ''language'' of this crosstalk between S. reilianum and maize, but there are clearly some mutual influences and restrictions between them, which establish a basis for further studies involving infection resistance especially for biotrophic fungal pathogens.
