Running head: OsbZIP76 regulates rice endosperm cellularization

### Abstract

Following double fertilization, plant endosperm nuclei undergo syncytial divisions, followed by synchronous cellularization. Cellularization is a key event during endosperm development, but our understanding of its regulation is limited to Arabidopsis. In this study we show that OsbZIP76 regulates cellularization in rice (Oryza sativa). Activation of OsbZIP76 coincided with the initiation of cellularization, and its knockdown or knockout mutants exhibited precocious cellularization. Genes involved in endosperm development or starch biosynthesis were prematurely activated in the osbzip76 caryopsis. As a putative transcription factor, OsbZIP76 alone lacked transcriptional activation activity; however, it interacted with the nuclear factor Y (NF-Y) family transcription factors OsNF-YB9 and OsNF-YB1 in yeast and in planta. OsbZIP76 and OsNF-YB9 were predominantly expressed in the endosperm and the proteins colocalized. Seeds of osnf-yb1 and osbzip76 mutants showed reduced size and reduced apparent amylose content. The parent-of-origin-dependent expression of OsbZIP76 is variable in different rice accessions. In summary, OsbZIP76 is an endosperm-expressed imprinted gene that regulates endosperm development in rice.

**Key words**: Cellularization, endosperm development, NF-YB transcription factors, OsbZIP76, rice

#### **INTRODUCTION**

The endosperm is an essential component of cereal seeds (Olsen 2004) and provides most of the calories consumed by humans and livestock. Therefore, a complete understanding of the molecular mechanism of endosperm development, particularly in grain crops, is of considerable practical significance. The endosperm is a product of double fertilization. One of the two sperm cells in a pollen grain fertilizes the diploid central cell of an embryo sac. The primary endosperm cell is then formed, and starts to divide within a few hours after fertilization in monocots such as maize (*Zea mays*; Olsen 2001). Due to the uncoupling of cell cycles from cytokinesis at this stage, these divisions produce a syncytial endosperm cell with multiple nuclei. Cellularization, the process of separation of nuclei by a cell membrane, is then synchronized, generating the first layer of endosperm cells. Subsequent periclinal and anticlinal cell divisions fill the central vacuole of the seed with cellularized endosperm cells (Wu et al. 2016a). The syncytial development and cellularization of the endosperm are highly conserved in higher plants (Olsen 2001, 2004).

The endosperm of rice (*Oryza sativa*) usually begins cellularizing 3 days after fertilization (DAF) and finishes the process at 5 DAF, with minor variations caused by genetic background and environmental factors such as temperature (Chen et al. 2016; Wu et al. 2016a). Cellularization affects the cell number of endosperm (Ishimaru et al. 2013), whereas the storage compounds that accumulate in the endosperm cells determine the final yield and quality of the rice grains.

Previously discovered regulators of endosperm development have largely been transcription factors (Agarwal et al. 2011). For example, OsMADS29 is a key factor in the degeneration of maternal tissues (Yang et al. 2012; Yin and Xue 2012).

RNA-interference- (RNAi-) mediated gene suppression of *OsMADS29* inhibits starch biosynthesis and grain filling of the endosperm, owing to defective programmed cell death (Yin and Xue 2012). *OsMADS6* is highly expressed in the florets and endosperm, and its mutation induces severe defects in the endosperm and delays starch filling (Zhang et al. 2010). Several basic leucine zipper (bZIP) proteins of rice, including OsbZIP20, OsbZIP33, and OsbZIP58/RISBZ1, directly interact with ACGT elements located in the promoter region of *Waxy* (*Wx*), to activate the expression of granule-bound starch synthase (Wang et al. 2013). OsbZIP58/RISBZ1 may coordinate with the rice prolamin box-binding factor (RPBF), a Dof (DNA-binding with one finger) family transcription factor, to promote storage protein biosynthesis in seeds (Kawakatsu et al. 2009).

Another class of transcription factors belonging to the Nuclear factor Y (NF-Y) family are predominantly expressed in the endosperm of Poaceae, and are hence assumed to function in seed development (Yang et al. 2016; E et al. 2018). NF-Y family members form heterotrimers comprising NF-YA, NF-YB, and NF-YC subunits (Laloum et al. 2013). OsNF-YB1 directly binds to the CCAAT *cis*-element of sugar transporter genes to modulate grain filling in rice (Bai et al. 2015). In addition, OsNF-YB1 dimerizes with OsNF-YC11 or OsNF-YC12 in the cytoplasm; these dimers are imported to the nucleus, where they interact with, and form heterotrimers with ethylene-responsive factors (ERFs), such as OsERF115 (Xu et al. 2016; E et al. 2018). These trimers recognize the GCC-box, and activate downstream genes for endosperm development (Xu et al. 2016). The OsNF-YB1/OsNF-YC12 heterodimer can recruit OsbHLH144 in the nucleus to modulate the expression of *Wx* (Bello et al. 2018). In addition, OsNF-YC10 and OsNF-YB1 can form a heterodimer in yeast (E et

al. 2018). Loss of function of *OsNF-YC10* alters the size and shape of rice grains (Jia et al. 2019).

Genomic imprinting is a non-Mendelian phenomenon common to the endosperm of higher plants (Gehring 2013). When a gene is imprinted, only one of the parental alleles is expressed depending on the parent-of-origin, owing to epigenetic differences between the alleles (Köhler et al. 2012). Some imprinted genes, such as *MEDEA* (*MEA*) and *FERTILIZATION-INDEPENDENT SEED2* (*FIS2*), play important roles in *Arabidopsis* endosperm and seed development (Kinoshita 1999; Luo et al. 2000). Moreover, endosperm collapse in interspecific or interploid hybrids is associated with expression disorder of imprinted genes (Kirkbride et al. 2015; Chen et al. 2016b; Florez-Rueda et al. 2016).

In rice, our understanding of the significance of imprinted genes in endosperm development is still limited. The rice homolog of *Arabidopsis AGL62*, *OsMADS87*, is a maternally expressed gene (Ishikawa et al. 2011). Its misregulation results in endosperm breakdown in heat-stressed seeds and in interspecific hybrid seeds (Ishikawa et al. 2011; Folsom et al. 2014; Chen et al. 2016a). However, the underlying molecular mechanism remains unknown.

*OsbZIP76* is predominantly expressed in the endosperm and is an imprinted gene in rice (Luo et al. 2011; Yuan et al. 2017). It also showed misregulated expression in the heat-stressed endosperm (Chen et al. 2016a). To discover its function in rice endosperm development, we generated CRISPR/Cas9 mutant and RNAi lines, and found that *OsbZIP76* is a negative regulator of cellularization, and is critical to endosperm development by interacting with OsNF-YB9 and OsNF-YB1 in rice.

#### **RESULTS**

#### **Expression profiles of** *OsbZIP76*

To investigate the expression of *OsbZIP76* throughout all tissues, we performed quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analyses. The results showed that *OsbZIP76* was exclusively expressed in the fertilized seed among the tissues we analyzed, including shoot, root, leaf blade, leaf sheath, flag leaf, stem, young panicle, anther, unfertilized caryopsis, and fertilized caryopsis (**Figure 1A**). Next, we investigated the expression dynamics of *OsbZIP76* in caryopses of different ages and found that *OsbZIP76* was activated starting at 3 DAF. The abundance of *OsbZIP76* transcripts peaked at 5 DAF and fell thereafter (**Figure 1B**). The *in situ* hybridization assay revealed that *OsbZIP76* transcripts accumulated predominantly in the syncytium at 3 DAF and in the starchy endosperm at 6 DAF (**Figure 1C–F**), suggesting that expression of *OsbZIP76* is predominantly endosperm specific.

#### **Subcellular localization and transcriptional activation activity of OsbZIP76**

To determine if OsbZIP76 functioned as a transcription factor, we investigated its subcellular localization *in planta*. We fused OsbZIP76 with a Venus tag and transiently expressed the recombinant protein in the epidermal cells of *Nicotiana benthamiana* leaves. In contrast to the ubiquitous expression pattern of a positive control (a Venus tag driven by a CaMV 35S promoter), fluorescent signals were mainly observed in the nucleus, but they were detectable in the cytoplasm as well (**Figure 2A**).

OsbZIP76 is a putative transcription factor. To test its function, we fused OsbZIP76 with the DNA-binding domain (BD) of the GAL4 transcription factor of yeast. However, the yeast transformants that expressed the recombinant protein could not survive on the selective medium (**Figure 2B**). Thus, OsbZIP76 cannot activate transcription by itself.

### **Mutation of** *OsbZIP76* **resulted in reduced seed size**

We generated knockout lines of *OsbZIP76* by CRISPR/Cas9 editing. We obtained two independent homozygous lines in the T0 generation: *osbzip76-1* had a 34-bp deletion and *osbzip76-2* had a 1-bp insertion (**Figure 3A**). Both frameshift mutations resulted in truncated proteins due to premature stop codons (**Figure S1**). We also generated knockdown lines using an RNAi approach. *OsbZIP76* was significantly downregulated in the endosperm of these lines (**Figure S2A**). Therefore, the two independent RNAi lines (76RNAi-1 and 76RNAi-2) and the two independent knockouts (*osbzip76-1* and *osbzip76-2*) were used in subsequent experiments.

The vegetative growth of these lines was not significantly different from that of the wild type (WT) (**Figure S2B**). At maturity, most exhibited similar heights and tiller numbers as the WT (**Figures 3B–D, S2C, S2D**). However, the 1,000-grain weight of the knockout mutants was significantly reduced in comparison with that of the WT, which may be due to reduced seed thickness (**Figures 3E–H, S2F**). In agreement, the RNAi lines also produced smaller seeds than the WT (**Figure S2E, F**).

knockdown lines cellularized faster than the WT (**Figures 4A, S3A**). At 3 DAF, the mutants had begun cellularizing, but the WT was still syncytial. Cellularization was completed by 4 DAF in the knockouts and in RNAi76-1, and by 5 DAF in the WT (**Figures 4A, S3A**).

The endosperm cells accumulate starch and other storage components after the onset of cellularization. The I2-KI staining assay indicated that the *osbzip76* mutants accumulated starch earlier than the WT (**Figures 4B, S3B**). Thus, knockout or knockdown of *OsbZIP76* caused precocious cellularization in the rice caryopsis.

#### **Transcriptomic analysis of the caryopses of** *osbzip76*

We next performed whole-transcriptome shotgun sequencing (RNA-Seq) to analyze the global transcriptomic changes in the caryopses of the *osbzip76* mutants. Due to the higher ambient temperatures in the summer of 2018, rice caryopsis development was accelerated, reflected by precocious cellularization at 2 DAF in WT and mutants (**Figure S4A**). In line with this observation, *OsbZIP76* expression was activated in WT caryopses at 2 DAF (**Figure S4B**). Therefore, we collected WT and *osbzip76* caryopses at 2 DAF for RNA-Seq. Because the transcriptomic profile of one *osbzip76-2* replicate (*osbzip76-2\_*rep1) did not well group with the other mutant samples (**Figure S4C**), we excluded it from subsequent analyses. This variation could be due to micro-environmental differences between individuals, given that the plants were grown in an open field. We analyzed three WT biological replicates and three mutant samples (*osbzip76-1*\_rep1, *osbzip76-1*\_rep2 and *osbzip76-2*\_rep2). Using cut-off thresholds of fold change (FC) > 2 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, we identified 60 downregulated and 372 upregulated genes in the caryopses of the mutants at 2 DAF (**File S1**).

A gene ontology (GO) analysis indicated that the terms "metabolic process" (FDR = 2e−62), "biological regulation" (FDR = 1.5e−18), and "response to stress "(FDR = 7.2e−14) were significantly overrepresented among the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Notably, a group of exclusively endosperm-expressed genes (Nie et al., 2013) were enriched, most of which (30 out of 31) were upregulated in the mutants (**Figure 5**; **File S1**). By surveying public databases, we found that these DEGs were usually activated after 3–4 DAF in *O. sativa* spp. *japonica* cv. Nipponbare (**Figure 5**). In line with our expectations based on the I2-KI staining (**Figure 4B**), key genes involved in starch biosynthesis, such as *RISBZ1*, *RPBF*, *AGPL2*, *OsAGPS2*, *GBSSI*, and *OsSBEIIb*, were upregulated in the *osbzip76* knockout lines (**Figure S5**).

As revealed by GO analysis, the genes involved in expression regulation were highly enriched among the DEGs (FDR = 7.2e−14). There were 25 transcription factors among the DEGs (**File S2**). Eight of them belonged to the NAC (NAM ATAF1/2 CUC2) family (*ONAC20*, *ONAC24*, *ONAC25*, *ONAC26*, *ONAC30*, *ONAC59*, *ONAC60* and *ONAC127*) and four belonged to the NF-Y family (*NF-YA8*, *NF-YB1*, *NF-YC8,* and *NF-YC12*). Many of the differentially expressed transcription factor family genes (*ONAC20*, *ONAC24*, *ONAC25*, *ONAC26*, *ONAC127*, *OsbZIP58*, *Dof3*, *OsbHLH144*, *OsNF-YB1*, *OsNF-YC8*, *OsNF-YC12*, and *OsSLRL2*) were predominantly expressed in the caryopsis (**Figure S6**). Indeed, some of these genes, namely *OsbZIP58*, *Dof3*, *OsbHLH144*, and *OsNF-YB1*, are key regulators of the biosynthesis of starch and storage proteins (Yamamoto et al. 2006; Kawakatsu et al. 2009; Wang et al. 2013; Bello et al. 2019).

#### **OsbZIP76 physically interacts with OsNF-YB1 and OsNF-YB9**

As described earlier, OsbZIP76 alone did not activate transcription of downstream genes (**Figure 2B**). Therefore, we performed a yeast two-hybrid assay to identify proteins that interacted with OsbZIP76 to enable its functionality. We found that OsNF-YB9, an NF-Y family member, interacted with OsbZIP76 in yeast (**Figure 6A**). OsNF-YB9 is preferentially expressed in the endosperm (E et al. 2018) and shows an expression profile similar to that of *OsbZIP76* in the developing caryopsis, with its expression activated at 3 DAF and peaking at 5 DAF (**Figure 6B**). We transiently coexpressed OsbZIP76:GFP and OsNF-YB9:mCherry recombinant proteins in the epidermal cells of *N. benthamiana* and found that they colocalized in the nucleus and in the cytoplasm (**Figure S7**).

To confirm the physical interaction between OsbZIP76 and OsNF-YB9, we performed a split luciferase (LUC) complementation assay by fusing OsbZIP76 and OsNF-YB9 with the N-terminal of LUC (nLUC) and the C-terminal of LUC (cLUC), respectively. The luciferase activity was detectable only when OsbZIP76:nLUC and OsNF-YB9:cLUC were coexpressed in *N. benthamiana* leaves (**Figure 6D**), suggesting that they physically interact *in planta*. Moreover, a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay confirmed their interaction in the cytoplasm and the nucleus (**Figure 6D**).

In a previous study, we found that OsNF-YB1, another NF-YB family member, was also predominantly expressed in the endosperm of rice (E et al. 2018). The split luciferase complementation and BiFC assays suggested physical interaction between OsbZIP76 and OsNF-YB1 (**Figure S8A, B**). The expression of *OsNF-YB1* lagged behind that of *OsbZIP76,* as indicated by the expression profiles in the caryopsis

(**Figure S8C**). *OsbZIP76* expression was not significantly different in the 5-DAF caryopses of the *osnf-yb1* mutant (**Figure S8D**), indicating that *OsbZIP76* is not directly regulated by OsNF-YB1. Mutation of *OsNF-YB1* causes endosperm chalkiness, reduced seed size, and decreased amylose content (Xu et al. 2016). The *osbzip76* mutants exhibited reduced seed size (**Figure 3B–D**) and decreased apparent amylose content (**Figure S9**) as well. Therefore, we hypothesized that OsbZIP76 interacted with OsNF-YB family transcription factors, such as OsNF-YB1 and OsNF-YB9, to regulate endosperm development in rice.

#### **Parent-of-origin expression of** *OsbZIP76* **is variable**

*OsbZIP76* is a potentially imprinted gene in rice, with predominant expression of the maternal allele in the endosperm (Luo et al. 2011; Yuan et al. 2017). To confirm the imprinted status of *OsbZIP76*, we performed intra- and inter-specific reciprocal crosses. We used a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) to distinguish the parental origin of the transcripts in hybrids derived from a cross between "Wufeng" (*O. sativa* ssp. *indica*) and "Yu6" (*O. sativa* ssp. *japonica*), and between "Yu6" and common wild rice (*O*. *rufipogon*; **Figure 7A**). We cloned the RT-PCR products from the hybrid endosperm and sequenced the cDNA. Hybrids derived from a "Wufeng" (♀) x "Yu6" (♂) cross carried the "Yu6" allele in 13 out of 14 colonies; all 10 randomly picked colonies from a "Yu6" (♀) x "Wufeng" (♂) cross carried the "Yu6" transcript as well (**Figure 7B**). The control, *OsFIE2*, a biallelically expressed gene in rice (Chen et al. 2018), showed the expected 2m:1p ratio in either direction of the cross (**Figure S10**). These findings suggested that *OsbZIP76* is an allele-specific imprinted gene in "Wufeng" x "Yu6" hybrids. However, consistent with previous findings (Luo et al. 2011; Yuan et al. 2017), in the reciprocal crosses between "Yu6" and the common

wild rice (*Oryza rufipogon*), the maternal allele was predominantly expressed in a parent-of-origin manner (**Figure 7A, C**), suggesting that *OsbZIP76* is a maternally expressed gene in that specific case. Together, these results imply that the parent-of-origin expression pattern of *OsbZIP76* depended on the genetic background of the parents and that the imprinting status of the gene may vary among accessions.

### **DISCUSSION**

Genomic imprinting is common in the endosperm of plants (Gehring 2013). Some imprinted genes are essential for endosperm development in *Arabidopsis* (Tiwari et al. 2008; Liu et al. 2014; He et al. 2017). In genome-wide surveys, hundreds of rice genes have been identified as imprinted (Luo et al. 2011; Yuan et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2018). As with other species, the parent-of-origin expression pattern of some imprinted genes in rice depends on the genetic background of the parents (Chen et al. 2018). In the current study, we found that *OsbZIP76* belongs to this class of imprinted genes (**Figure 7**). Inactivation of *OsbZIP76* did not affect vegetative development, but reduced the seed weight (**Figures 3, S2**) and accelerated endosperm cellularization (**Figures 4A, S3A**).

Cellularization is a key event in endosperm development (Olsen 2001, 2004). Impaired cellularization leads to seed abortion and may contribute to reproductive isolation between species (Ishikawa et al. 2011; Sekine et al. 2013). Our understanding of the molecular mechanism of early endosperm development is limited to *Arabidopsis*. Polycomb group proteins and type I MADS-box transcription factors are important for the regulation of early endosperm development in *Arabidopsis* (Pires 2014). Members of the FIS-PRC2 complex function as positive

regulators of cellularization; *fie*, *fis2*, *mea*, and *msi1* mutants show seed failure triggered by delayed or arrested cellularization (Grossniklaus et al. 1998; Luo et al. 2000; Vinkenoog et al. 2000; Hennig 2003). A group of type I MADS-box genes are highly upregulated in the syncytium, and downregulated in the cellularized endosperm cells (Bemer et al. 2010; Chen et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2018). Many type I MADS-box genes are activated in *fis*-*prc2* seeds (Zhang et al. 2018). Loss of function of the MADS box genes *AGL62* and *AGL80* causes premature cellularization in *Arabidopsis* (Portereiko et al. 2006; Kang et al. 2008). Mutation of *PHE1/AGL37* suppresses the seed abortion of *mea* (Köhler et al. 2003). Therefore, type I MADS-box genes probably antagonize FIS-PRC2 in the process of cellularization. We found that *OsbZIP76* may also regulate cellularization. The activation of *OsbZIP76* was coincident with cellularization in rice and *osbzip76* mutants cellularized precociously (**Figures 4A, S3A**), suggesting that OsbZIP76 is a negative regulator of the transition from syncytium to cellularization in rice.

The DEGs in *osbzip76* were enriched with genes that are preferentially expressed in the endosperm (**Figure 5**). Given that *OsbZIP76* is also predominantly expressed in the endosperm, the direct regulation of these genes by OsbZIP76 is worth investigating. Many transcription factor family genes were precociously activated along with the premature cellularization of *osbzip76* (**File S2**). *OsbZIP58/OsRISBZ1* and *Dof3/OsRPBF*, two master regulators for the biosynthesis of starch and storage proteins (Yamamoto et al. 2006; Kawakatsu et al. 2009), were upregulated in the caryopses of *osbzip76* (**Figure S5**). Notably, *NAC* and *NF-Y* family genes were enriched among the differentially expressed transcription factors, the expression of many of which is endosperm-specific or endosperm-preferential (**Figure S6**). These genes likely play important roles in seed development and storage compound

accumulation; a recent study revealed that ZmNAC128 and ZmNAC130 can target genes for starch and protein biosynthesis and activate their expression (Zhang et al. 2019).

Endosperm development in rice is sensitive to ambient temperature (Chen et al. 2016a; Paul et al. 2020). In line with our expectations, the samples used for RNA-seq showed early cellularization due to the relatively high temperatures during reproductive growth. The moderate heat stress advanced cellularization by one day (**Figure S4**). Therefore, we used 2-DAF-old caryopses for RNA-seq. The higher temperature may complicate the interpretation of the RNA-seq data. However, because the seed set-rates of the WT and *osbzip76* mutants were almost normal (data not shown), we believe the heat stress was moderate. Therefore, we can assume that most of the DEGs reflect the precocious cellularization of *osbzip76*, while some of them might reflect the differential responses to the high temperature in the WT and mutant caryopses.

We found that two NF-YB family members, OsNF-YB1 and OsNF-YB9, could bind to OsbZIP76 (**Figures 6, S7**). Similar to *OsbZIP76*, *OsNF-YB9* is exclusively expressed in the endosperm (**Figure 6B**). A subgroup of NF-Y members, including *OsNF-YB1* and *OsNF-YB9*, were expressed mainly in the endosperm (Yulong et al. 2016; E et al. 2018). NF-YB and NF-YC can form heterodimers in the cytoplasm; this heterodimer is then transported into the nucleus to interact with an NF-YA family member (Laloum et al. 2013). In a trimeric NF-Y complex, NF-YA binds to the CCAAT-box, and NF-YB and NF-YC activate gene expression. In addition, NF-YB/NF-YC heterodimers can bind different transcription factor family members. OsNF-YB1 regulates starch biosynthesis and seed development (Sun et al. 2014; Bai

et al. 2015; Xu et al. 2016; E et al. 2018). It may coordinate with other transcription factors, such as bHLH114, ERF114, ERF115, and NF-Y family members to regulate downstream target genes, such as *Wx* and sugar transporter genes (Bai et al. 2015; Xu et al. 2016; Bello et al. 2018). The *osbzip76* mutants exhibited a phenotype similar to that of *osnf-yb1*, including reduced seed size and decreased apparent amylose content (**Figures 3**, **S9**). However, the role of *OsNF-YB1* and *OsNF-YB9* in cellularization requires further investigation.

#### **MATERIALS AND METHODS**

#### **Plant materials**

The rice plants were grown in paddy fields during the summer season or in a greenhouse (14 h of light and 10 h of darkness, 32°C/28°C day/night temperature, and 50% humidity) during the winter season. Various tissues were collected for RNA isolation. The seeds were labeled at the day of fertilization. Caryopses of different ages were collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA isolation.

#### **Vector construction and rice transformation**

For RNAi vector construction, the targets were cloned into pENTR/D-TOPO (Invitrogen) and cloned into the pANDA vector with a Gateway cloning kit (Invitrogen). Targets for the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene targeting were designed using CRISPR Primer Designer (Yan et al. 2015). The targets were then cloned into the vector BGK032 using the CRISPR/Cas9 vector construction kit (Biogle). All the resultant constructs were sequenced to ensure that no mutations had been introduced. The constructs were transformed into rice calli through an *Agrobacterium*-mediated

strategy, as described previously (Chen et al. 2016). The primers used in the study can be found in **File S3**.

#### **RNA extraction and RT-PCR**

Total RNA was isolated using the Plant RNA Kit (OMEGA) following the manufacturer's protocol. The extracted RNA was treated with an RNA-free DNase set (OMEGA) to remove contaminating DNA. cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TAKARA) and oligo-dT primers. The standard procedure provided by the manufacturer was used for the reactions. A 2-μL sample of the diluted cDNA was used for real-time PCR (qPCR) in a 20 μL reaction volume using the AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme). The qPCR reactions were performed on a CFX Connect Real-Time System (Bio-Rad). Three independent replicates were set up for each sample. Quantification of the relative expression was calculated using normalized Ct. The proteasome gene (LOC\_Os03g63430) of rice was used as the endogenous control.

#### **RNA** *in situ* **hybridization**

The caryopses of Kittake (*O. sativa* ssp. *japonica*) were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C. After dehydration through an ethanol series, tissues were embedded in Paraplast Plus (Sigma) and sectioned at 10 µm thickness using an RM2235 rotary microtome (Leica). The sequences intended to be used as probes were amplified and cloned into pEASY-T3 (Transgen). The probes were then transcribed *in vitro* under the control of an SP6 and T7 promoter with RNA polymerase, using a DIG RNA Labeling Kit (Roche) for DIG-labeled RNA antisense or

sense probes. RNA hybridization and immunological detection of the hybridized probes were carried out according to manufacturer's instructions.

#### **Subcellular localization**

The coding sequence of *OsbZIP76* was amplified and cloned into a p1300LV vector using the ClonExpress® II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). The coding sequences of *OsbZIP76* and *OsNF-YB9* were cloned into pHBG and pHB-mCherry, respectively, for the colocalization assay. Primer information can be found in **File S3**. The constructs were transformed into *Agrobacterium* strain GV3101 and transiently expressed in the epidermal cells of *N. benthamiana*. Fluorescence signals were observed using a laser confocal microscope (LSM 710; Zeiss).

#### **Transactivation assay and yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay**

The coding sequences of *OsbZIP76* were cloned into pGBK-T7 using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). Meanwhile, we amplified the GAL4 activation domain from a pGAD-T7 empty vector and fused it with the DNA-BD of GAL4 as a positive control. The constructs were then transformed into yeast strain Y2HGold to investigate transcriptional activation on selection media. *OsbZIP76* and *Os-NF-YB9* were cloned into pGBK-T7 and pGAD-T7, respectively, using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). Primers used for the experiment can be found in **File S3**. A Y2H cDNA library of early developed caryopses (2–5 DAF) was constructed by OE Biotech (Shanghai). *OsbZIP76* was cloned into pGBK-T7 as a bait for library screening. Yeast transformation and screening procedures were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (Clontech).

#### **Luciferase activity assay**

*OsbZIP76* and *OsNF-YB1*/*OsNF-YB9* were cloned into the binary vectors JW771 and JW772, respectively, and fused with the N- and C-terminals of luciferase (nLUC and cLUC, respectively). *nLUC-OsbZIP76*, *cLUC-OsNF-YB9*, and *cLUC-OsNF-YB1*, in different combinations of *cLUC* and *nLUC*, were transiently expressed in *N. benthamiana* leaves for about 48 h. A Split Luciferase Complementation Assay System (Promega) was used to detect interactions. The chemiluminescence signal was detected using a Tanon Imaging System (5200 Multi; Tanon). Primers used for the experiment can be found in **File S3**.

#### **Sectioning and microscopy**

The caryopses of WT and mutant rice were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde fixative solution overnight at 4°C. After dehydration through an ethanol series, the samples were embedded in resin, sectioned at 2 μm thickness, and stained with 1% Toluidine Blue O and iodine solution. The sections were then observed and photographed using a bright-field microscope (Olympus).

### **Transcriptome analysis**

RNA isolated from caryopses of the WT, *osbzip76-1*, and *osbzip76-2* at 2 DAF was used for RNA-Seq. We set up three biological replicates for WT and two replicates for *osbzip76-1* and *osbzip76-2*. The samples were submitted to Novogene Co. Ltd (Tianjin) for library preparation and sequencing using the Illumina Nova 6000 platform. The clean reads were imported into CLC Genomics Workbench 12.0 and mapped to the reference genome (IRGSP-1.0) using global alignment mode with the default mapping parameters. The reads per kilobase per million mapped read values

were calculated. The statistical differential expression test for the two groups (mutants vs. WT) was then performed. Because one replicate of *osbzip76-2* was not well-grouped with the other mutant samples, it was excluded from the analysis. The RNA-Seq dataset has been deposited at GEO (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under the accession number GSE139445.
