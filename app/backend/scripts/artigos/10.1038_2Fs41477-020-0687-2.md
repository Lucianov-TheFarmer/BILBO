# Movement and differential consumption of short interfering RNA duplexes underlie mobile RNA interference

Emanuel A. Devers <sup>1,5</sup>, Christopher A. Brosnan<sup>1,2,5</sup>, Alexis Sarazin<sup>1</sup>, Daniele Albertini<sup>1</sup>, Andrea C. Amsler<sup>1</sup>, Florian Brioudes <sup>1</sup>, Pauline E. Jullien <sup>1,3</sup>, Peiqi Lim<sup>1,4</sup>, Gregory Schott<sup>1</sup> and Olivier Voinnet <sup>1</sup>

In RNA interference (RNAi), the RNase III Dicer processes long double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) into short interfering RNA (siRNA), which, when loaded into ARGONAUTE (AGO) family proteins, execute gene silencing<sup>1</sup>. Remarkably, RNAi can act non-cell autonomously<sup>2,3</sup>: it is graft transmissible<sup>4-7</sup>, and plasmodesmata-associated proteins modulate its cell-to-cell spread<sup>8,9</sup>. Nonetheless, the molecular mechanisms involved remain ill defined, probably reflecting a disparity of experimental settings. Among other caveats, these almost invariably cause artificially enhanced movement via transitivity, whereby primary RNAi-target transcripts are converted into further dsRNA sources of secondary siRNA<sup>5,10,11</sup>. Whether siRNA mobility naturally requires transitivity and whether it entails the same or distinct signals for cell-to-cell versus long-distance movement remains unclear, as does the identity of the mobile signalling molecules themselves. Movement of long single-stranded RNA, dsRNA, free/AGO-bound secondary siRNA or primary siRNA have all been advocated<sup>12-15</sup>; however, an entity necessary and sufficient for all known manifestations of plant mobile RNAi remains to be ascertained. Here, we show that the same primary RNAi signal endows both vasculature-to-epidermis and long-distance silencing movement from three distinct RNAi sources. The mobile entities are AGO-free primary siRNA duplexes spreading length and sequence independently. However, their movement is accompanied by selective siRNA depletion reflecting the AGO repertoires of traversed cell types. Coupling movement with this AGO-mediated consumption process creates qualitatively distinct silencing territories, potentially enabling unlimited spatial gene regulation patterns well beyond those granted by mere gradients.

In the *SUC:SUL Arabidopsis* line<sup>11</sup> (*SS*), an inverted-repeat transgene expressed under the phloem companion cell-specific promoter *SUC2* spawns 24- and 21-nucleotide-long short interfering RNA (siRNA) in a Dicer-like-3 (DCL3)- and DCL4-dependent manner, respectively. The latter siRNA species suffice to mediate post-transcriptional gene silencing of the *MAGNESIUM CHELATASE* (*SUL*) messenger RNA (mRNA), resulting in mobile RNA interference (RNAi) manifested as vein-proximal chlorosis<sup>11</sup> (Fig. 1a). To explore RNAi long-distance movement, we employed *Arabidopsis* grafting involving *SS* plants as RNAi-incipient tissues

and wild-type plants as RNAi-recipient tissues (Fig. 1b). In assaying shoot-to-root movement, the SS siRNA accumulating in RNAi-recipient wild-type roots were near-identical in length, sequence and distribution, but one order of magnitude less abundant than in RNAi-incipient SS shoots (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Figs. 1a and 2a). Confirming previous work<sup>11</sup>, siRNA from the SS-non-overlapping part of SUL were at background levels (<10 reads per million; Supplementary Fig. 1b), supporting the unbiased spread of a long-distance, non-amplified (that is, primary RNAi) signal. Strong activity of the SUC2 promoter within the root phloem companion cells<sup>16-18</sup> allowed for exploration of the siRNA signature of primary RNAi cell-to-cell movement in the cylindrical cell layers of SS root tips (Fig. 1d) expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP)- or FLAG-HA(FHA) epitope-tagged alleles of the viral silencing suppressor P19. In contrast with longer double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) or DNA, P19 homodimers specifically bind 21- or 22-nucleotide small RNA (sRNA) duplexes<sup>19</sup> that concomitantly undergo 1- to 2-nucleotide 3' trimming in vivo20. To isolate SS siRNA from RNAi-incipient versus RNAi-recipient cell layers via P19 immunoprecipitation, we used: (1) the innermost vascular cylinder (stele)-specific SHORTROOT (SHR) promoter, which overlaps with the SUC2 expression domain ~150 µm from the root tip upwards<sup>18</sup>; and (2) the outermost epidermis-specific WEREWOLF (WER) promoter, which drives expression parallel to the SUC2 expression domain, albeit several cell layers away. Layer-restricted GFP signals yielded by pSHR::GFP:P19 and pWER::GFP:P19, and immunofluorescence conducted in  $SS \times pSHR$ ::FHA:P19 and  $SS \times pWER$ ::FHA:P19 roots used to collect P19-bound sRNA, confirmed the cell autonomy of P19 (Fig. 1e, f and Supplementary Fig. 3a). Mixing separate lysates from the pWER::FHA:P19 and SS lines before immunoprecipitation ruled out artefactual P19-sRNA complex formation (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Selective enrichments of miR160 (stele) and miR822 (epidermis) indeed validated the cell specificities of the immunoprecipitates<sup>21,22</sup> (Supplementary Fig. 3c). As expected, of the 21- and 24-nucleotide input SS siRNA sequences, only the 21-nucleotide sequences were enriched in P19 immunoprecipitates (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 3c), yet at markedly lower levels in the epidermis versus stele, supporting the unbiased radial spread of a non-amplified signal. We exclude the movement of long, single-stranded RNA because siRNA biogenesis in recipient tissues would have required dsRNA amplification as part of transitivity, leaving as plausible candidates: (1) long dsRNA; (2) non-loaded siRNA; or (3) AGO-bound siRNA. We can exclude AGO-bound siRNA because cell layer-specific GFP-translational fusions to AGO1, which are mandatory for *SS* and TuYV silencing[28](#page-9-16)[,29,](#page-9-17) are cell autonomou[s21](#page-9-9) (Supplementary Fig. 7). The 24-nucleotide *SS* siRNA sequences are mostly three-nucleotide extensions of 21-nucleotide species (Supplementary Fig. 8a), indicating their processing by DCL3 and DCL4, respectively, from the same dsRNA sequences whose potential movement was investigated in *dcl234* triple-mutant roots grafted onto *SS* shoots (Fig. [2e](#page-2-0) and Supplementary Fig. 8b). To further ascertain the identity and nature of the *SS-*, TuYVand *IR71*-derived primary RNAi mobile signal, we re-analysed the abundance and sequence signatures of P19-bound (*SS* and TuYV) and Meselect-isolated (*IR71*) siRNA. Of all *SS* siRNA sequences, 64.7% were neither enriched nor depleted in epidermal- versus stele-specific FHA:P19 immunoprecipitates (Supplementary Fig. 9a). However, the remaining 35.3% of *SS* siRNA sequences displayed a 53/48% depletion in 5′-U/A siRNA and concurrent 118/208% enrichment in 5′-C/G siRNA in the epidermis versus stele, respectively (Fig. [4a\)](#page-4-0). Spatially different P19 expression per se does not cause these 5′-nucleotide biases because the total siRNA pools in *pSHR::FHA:P19* versus *pWER::FHA:P19* were highly similar. Since P19 acts upstream of AGO loading[31,](#page-9-19) differential availabilities of *SS* siRNA duplexes in the signal-recipient epidermis versus signal-incipient stele are probably involved. 5′-U and 5′-A sRNA sequences of 21 or 22 nucleotides are respective signature cargoes of AGO1 and AGO2 (ref. [32\)](#page-9-20), and indeed both bind the appropriately sized *SS* siRNA sequences in roots (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Thus, the depleted 5′-U/A siRNA content in *pWER::FHA:P19* probably entails their loading and consumption by AGO1 and AGO2 as RNAi spreads over contiguous layers. Accordingly, 5′-C-biased AGO5 is not detected in roots or leaves (Supplementary Fig. 10b,c), and 5′-G-biased AGOs are undescribed in plants, rationalizing the parallel, selective stabilization, by P19, of 5′-C/G over 5′-U/A 21-nucleotide siRNA sequences in the epidermal recipient tissue.

In TuYV-infected *pSHR::FHA:P19* and *pWER::FHA:P19* roots, a subpopulation of vsiRNA reads also displayed a 96/41% depletion in 5′-U/A siRNA and a concurrent 476/251% enrichment in 5′-C/G siRNA in the epidermis versus stele, respectively (Fig. [4b](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Fig. 9b), agreeing with major antiviral roles for 5′-U-biased AGO1 and 5′-A-biased AGO2 in *Arabidopsis*[32](#page-9-20)[–34.](#page-9-21) AGO-mediated siRNA consumption is not a P19-related artefact because Meselect coupled to total sRNA sequencing (which, unlike P19 immunoprecipitation, probes all potential AGO cargoes) also revealed a 85/30% decrease in 5′-U (22 nucleotides; AGO1)/5′-A (24 nucleotides; AGO4), and a coincident 440/550% increase in 5′-C/G, affecting most *IR71*-derived siRNA sequences in the epidermis versus vasculature, respectively (Fig. [4c](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Figs. 9c and 11b,c). No equally consistent, selective bias was observed for the 3′-terminal nucleotides of *SS*, TuYV or *IR71* siRNA, further supporting an AGO loading-specified process (Supplementary Figs. 11d–f and 12). These observations further rule out long dsRNA precursor cell-to-cell movement because its reiterative de novo processing should have spawned siRNA sequences of similar 5′-nucleotide identities across layers.

We conclude that mobile silencing triggered by *SS*, *IR71* or TuYV is a manifestation of the same process, exemplifying that primary (that is, non-amplified) siRNA are necessary and sufficient for intercellular and long-distance RNAi spread. This is regardless of siRNA origin (transgenic*/*endogenic inverted repeats or natural infection), dsRNA precursor-intrinsic sequence contexts, siRNA loading-dependent strand prevalence or size (21, 22 or 24 nucleotides). siRNA duplexes, not single strands, are mobile because P19 only binds double-stranded Dicer products[19.](#page-9-7) We further conclude that AGO-mediated consumption of mobile siRNA generates qualitatively distinct silencing domains (Fig. [5a–c](#page-6-0)). In leaves, depletion of *IR71-*derived 5′-A 24-nucleotide siRNA (AGO4 cargoes) additionally suggests a vasculature-to-epidermis transition from transcriptional gene silencing-proficient to -deficient domains. However, assuming that AGO concentrations remain, for the most, non-limiting across cells, movement coupled to consumption creates a conundrum. Indeed, siRNA duplexes would have to avoid loading altogether in order to move, and hence should display one or several distinctive features from their consumed counterparts. Moreover, for mobile siRNA sequences to then become active in recipient cells, this feature should be reversible. We are currently investigating the possible molecular nature and biological output(s) of such a feature.

Regardless, the hitherto undescribed AGO-mediated sRNA consumption process implies that—far from a linear gradient, as is commonly inferred—primary RNAi movement may adopt a virtually infinite spectrum of patterns depending on: (1) the intrinsic AGO repertoires/concentrations of traversed cell types; and (2) the amounts and nature/diversity of 5′-nucleotide identities/sizes of their sRNA cargoes (Fig. [5a,b\)](#page-6-0). An expected, albeit as yet untested, outcome is considerable flexibility conferred to spatial target gene regulation. sRNA consumption further implies that the end point of siRNA activity does not necessarily parallel that of its physical spread, which, therefore, has been so far inadequately estimated by activity sensors or phenotypic readouts. That multiple AGOs, with distinct levels in distinct cell types, cooperate in post-transcriptional gene silencing/transcriptional gene silencing with cargoes of variable 5′-nucleotide identities predicts extended activity patterns with diffuse visible edges for mobile siRNA populations such as those spawned by *SS* (Fig. [5a,c\)](#page-6-0). Non-cell-autonomous miRNA-mediated silencing also involves non-loaded mobile duplexe[s21.](#page-9-9) Thus, according to the consumption model, activity patterns from mobile miRNA sequences would probably be narrower with sharper edges because miRNA sequences are homogenously 5′-U single species mostly effected by AGO1 only (Fig. [5d](#page-6-0)). Side-by-side phenotypic analyses of mobile siRNA versus miRNA patterns indeed support these predictions, with consumption also rationalizing observations previously made by others on non-autonomous miRNA action (Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Discussion).

Of all siRNA sequences from endo-*IR71*, 85% showed variation by consumption (Supplementary Fig. 9c), whereas it only affected 35% of *SS*-derived and 12% TuYV-derived siRNA (Supplementary Fig. 9a,b), suggesting that siRNA from more strongly expressed loci such as transgenes and viruses (Supplementary Table 1) override the process. Overriding might be beneficial in virus infections during which mobile en masse immunization, as opposed to fine-tuned spatial gene regulation, is desirable. Transitivity acting on cellular transcripts would also probably override consumption (Fig. [5\)](#page-6-0) by causing relay amplification of virtually infinite 5′-nucleotide contexts. Incidentally, *Arabidopsis* RNA quality control robustly shields mRNA against secondary siRNA production by RNA-dependent RNA polymerases (RDRs)[40](#page-9-27)[,41](#page-9-28), with the notable exception (under unusual molecular premise[s39,](#page-9-26)[42](#page-9-29)–[45](#page-9-30)) of *trans*-acting siRNA and phased siRNA precursors. While emphasizing the importance of single cell type-resolution analyses[21](#page-9-9), our work reveals that an unanticipated degree of versatility in spatial gene regulation, well beyond that expected from mere gradients, is potentially granted by coupling siRNA movement with their selective consumption.

#### **Methods**

**Plant materials.** Unless specifcally stated, all wild-type and mutant plant material was based on the Columbia (Col-0) ecotype. Te *dcl1-11* (ref. [46](#page-9-31)), *dcl2-1dcl3-1 dcl4-2* (ref. [47\)](#page-9-32), *rdr1-1* (ref. [48](#page-9-33)), *rdr2-1* (ref. [48](#page-9-33)), *rdr6-15* (ref. [39](#page-9-26)), *rdr126* (*rdr1-1 rdr2-1*

*rdr6-15*[\)49,](#page-9-34) *ago1-18* (ref. [35](#page-9-22)), *ago1-42* (ref. [36\)](#page-9-23) and *SS*[11](#page-9-2) mutant and transgenic lines have been described previously. Te *pIR71::GUS* line was a gif from C. Himber. Briefy, it contains the PCR-amplifed 1.5-kilobase upstream region of At3g06435 (*IR71*) cloned into pENTR1A *Eco*RI and *Xho*I sites, which was then recombined into pMDC162 (ref. [50](#page-9-35)) using LRII clonase (Invitrogen).

**Growth conditions.** Unless otherwise stated, plants were germinated and grown under long daylight conditions at 22°C on a 0.5× Murashige and Skoog salt mixture. For experiments based on root isolation, plants were grown vertically on 0.5× Murashige and Skoog media covered with a mesh (Sefear Nitrex 03–65125) or using a hydroponics system[51,](#page-9-36)[52,](#page-9-37) for seedlings or mature plants, respectively. For mature plants, surface-sterilized seeds were distributed onto seed holders made of 0.5-ml reaction tubes filled with 0.65% plant agar. Tube tips were cut, put into a 1ml tip box containing 500ml 1× Cramer's solution (1.5mM Ca(NO3)2, 1.25mM KNO3, 1mM (NH4)2SO4, 750µM Mg(SO4), 500µM KH2PO4, 100µM Na2O3Si, 72µM Na[Fe(EDTA)•(H2O)], 50µM KCl, 50µM H3BO3, 10µM MnSO4, 2µM ZnSO4, 1.5µM CuSO4 and 0.075µM (NH4)6Mo7O24) and covered with saran wrap. Once the seedlings germinated and the primary root was reaching the nutrient solution (~2weeks), plants were distributed to multiple tip boxes containing a maximum of four plants per box. The nutrient solution was exchanged once per week.

**Micrografting.** Micrograftings were done as previously described[53](#page-9-38). In short, seedlings were germinated on vertical plates containing half-strength Murashige and Skoog medium for 5d. The grafting by transverse cut within a millimetre of the shoot apex and subsequent butt alignment was executed under a stereomicroscope on a 0.45-µm nitrocellulose filter (Millipore) placed on top of two layers of moist Whatman No. 1 filter paper in a 90-mm Petri dish. If necessary, one cotyledon was removed to help orientation of the scion on the rootstock. After successful alignment, the plates were sealed with parafilm and incubated vertically for 7d under continuous light conditions.

**Cloning procedures.** Cloning of cell-specific FHA:P19 lines was performed using multisite gateway technology (Invitrogen). The epidermal specific promoter (pWER) was PCR amplified either from genomic DNA of wild-type *Arabidopsis* plants or from plasmid templates provided by E. van der Graaff and T. Laux (pSHR). Promoter sequences were amplified using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 2 and recombined into the Gateway vector pDONR4- 1r. P19 was amplified from the plasmid pBINP19 using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 2 and recombined in the entry vector pDONR P2R-P3. FHA tag (2×FLAG-2×HA) was PCR amplified (see Supplementary Table 2) and recombined in pDONR221. Recombination into the destination vector pK7m34GW resulted into an amino-terminally tagged P19 construct driven by either pWER or pSHR. Unaffected duplex affinity of the tagged P19 was confirmed by retention of the miRNA star strand in input samples and immunoprecipitation of lines expressing FHA:P19 compared with non-transformed wild-type plants (see miR390-3p in Fig. [3c](#page-3-0)), similar to a previously described retained duplex affinity of an identically tagged P19 construc[t21.](#page-9-9) Tagged P19 retains high binding specificity to siRNA and its signature sRNA size selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 15). GFP:P19 clones were made by recombining a plasmid containing enhanced GFP (in pDONR221)[54](#page-9-39) with the same P19 sequence in pDONR P2R-P3 in combination with the promoter clones. Cell-specific GFP:AGO1 was made by recombining enhanced GFP–pDONR221 with AGO1 genomic DNA cloned into pDONR P2R-P3 (ref. [55](#page-9-40)) in combination with cell-specific promoter clones. Clones were sequenced for authenticity and the final vectors were created by recombined cell-specific promoters with FHA and P19 in the destination vector pK7m34G[W56](#page-9-41). All GFP reporters were created in the same way, only into the destination vector pB7m34G[W56.](#page-9-41) All transgenic constructs were introduced into *Arabidopsis* using the floral dip metho[d57.](#page-9-42) The amiRSUL construct is based on the miR319 backbone and was cloned as previously describe[d58.](#page-9-43)

**Immunoprecipitations.** All of the following steps were carried out on ice or in a 4 °C cold chamber. For FHA:P19 immunoprecipitations, fresh roots from the appropriate lines were harvested, weighed and ground in immunoprecipitation buffer (50mM Tris-HCL (pH7.5), 150mM NaCl, 10% glycerol and 0.1% NP-40) using a minimum of 3ml per gram of fresh weight and containing one tablet per cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) per 10ml. To remove cell debris, the lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 12,000*g* for 30min. The supernatant was transferred into a fresh 1.5-ml tube and input aliquots of 200 and 20µl were stored for subsequent RNA extraction and western blot analysis, respectively. Equal volumes of extract were used for all simultaneously processed samples and, per ml of lysate, 20 µl of anti-HA magnetic beads (Pierce) were added and incubated for 1h on a spinning wheel. The beads were washed three times for 20min on a spinning wheel with fresh immunoprecipitation buffer. During the last washing step, 10% of the volume was removed and the beads were separated and stored in 1× Laemmli buffer until western blotting. The residual 90% of the beads were removed and the P19-bound sRNA was extracted with 1ml TRI Reagent, as described below.

Argonaute immunoprecipitation was done as described above, with the following changes. Pre-cleared lysate was incubated for 1–2h with either polyclonal anti-AGO1 (1:1,000; Agrisera), anti-AGO2 (1:500) anti-AGO4 antibody (1:500[\)59](#page-9-44) or anti-AGO5 (1:250; Agrisera) on a rotating wheel. Subsequently, 20µl of protein A magnetic (Supplementary Fig. 10) or agarose beads (Fig. [4](#page-4-0)) were added and incubated for another 1h under constant rotation. After washing (as described above), both RNA and protein were extracted from the same sample using the TRI Reagent-based method according to the manufacturer's instruction. Proteins were precipitated from the phenol phase by the addition of three volumes of acetone, overnight incubation at −20 °C, followed by centrifugation at 12,000*g* for 10min and washing with 80% acetone.

**Protein extraction and gel blot analysis.** Total proteins were extracted via lysis during immunoprecipitation experiments or by grinding in liquid nitrogen and resuspending in two volumes of RIPA buffer (50mM Tris, 150mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate and 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulphate (pH7.5)) containing cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Extracts were cleared by centrifugation at 13,000*g* for 10min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were normalized using a modified Lowry procedure with the DC Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad), resolved on sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels, and electro-transferred to Immobilon-P PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking for 30min in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)+0.1% Tween 20 supplemented with 5% skim milk powder, subsequent antibody incubations were carried out overnight at 4 °C in the same solution. Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated FLAG (Sigma–Aldrich; A8592-1MG) or HA (Sigma– Aldrich; H6533-1VL) antibodies were diluted to 1:5,000. Membranes were washed four times in 1× PBS+0.1% Tween 20, and then if necessary incubated for 1h at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (Abcam; ab6721) or goat anti-rat (Cell Signaling; 7077S). After washing again four times in 1× PBS+0.1% Tween 20, detection was performed using the ECL Western Blotting Detection Kit (GE Healthcare) and revealed either by exposure to film or using the ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (Bio-Rad). Membranes were stained with Coomassie blue to confirm equal loading. For Supplementary Fig. 10, primary antibodies were used with the following dilutions: anti-AGO1 (1:8,000; Agrisera), anti-AGO2 (1:4,000), anti-AGO4 (1:4.000; Eurogentec; see ref. [59\)](#page-9-44) and anti-AGO5 (1:4,000; Agrisera).

**RNA isolation, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis and quantitative PCR with reverse transcription (qRT–PCR).** Total RNA, P19 and Argonaute-bound sRNA isolations were done using TRI Reagent or equivalents and CHCl3, according the manufacturer's instructions. 0.5 µl Glycogen (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an aliquot (1:10 of the total water phase volume) of 3M sodium acetate (pH5.6) were added and RNA was precipitated on ice by adding one volume of isopropanol for 1h. The pelleted RNA was washed twice with ice-cold 80% (vol/vol) ethanol, resuspended in ultrapure H2O or 50% (vol/vol) formamide containing low-molecular-weight loading buffer for total RNA or immunoprecipitations, respectively, and then stored at −20 °C.

RNA was treated with DNase I and reverse transcribed using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according the manufacturer's instructions. The qRT–PCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 II (Roche) using the SYBR FAST quantitative PCR kit (KAPA Biosystems) as described previousl[y54.](#page-9-39) Crossing values (*C*p values) were determined by selecting 'second derivative max' on the LightCycler 480 II, and an average of *C*p values from technical replicates (same set of primers and cDNA) was used for downstream calculations. Data are presented as the averages of three biological replicates calculated from ∆*C*p values obtained by comparing the gene of interest to *ACTIN2* as reference gene.

**RNA gel blot analysis.** Low-molecular-weight northern blots were performed as described previousl[y54.](#page-9-39) In short, for total RNA, a defined amount of RNA (2–10 µg) was dried using a SpeedVac set for aqueous samples at 30 °C (Eppendorf), and resuspended in 50% formamide containing low-molecular-weight loading buffer before loading onto a pre-run 17.5% denaturing polyacrylamide gel containing 8M urea. The gel was resolved in 0.5x TBE buffer and the RNA was electro-transferred to Hybond-NX Nylon (GE Healthcare). Subsequently, the RNA was immobilized to the membrane by *N*-Ethyl-*N'*-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC)-based cross-linking, as described previousl[y60](#page-9-45). Probes against sRNA were either generated by a 5′-phosphate exchange reaction mediated by T4 PNK (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the presence of [γ-32P]dATP (Hartmann Analytic) using oligonucleotides complementary to probed miRNA, U6 or tRNAmet sequences or, in case of siRNAs, by a Klenow-based labelling using a gel-purified PCR template with the Prime-a-Gene (Promega) labelling kit in the presence of [α-32P]dCTP. Hybridization of the probes to the membrane was done overnight at 42 °C using PerfectHyb hybridization buffer (Sigma–Aldrich) and subsequently washed three times for 15min with 2× saline-sodium citrate/2% sodium dodecyl sulfate at 50 °C. The hot membrane was then exposed overnight to a phosphor screen (Fuji) and scanned using a Typhoon FLA 9500 scanner (GE Healthcare). Multiple sequences were probed on individual membranes by stripping twice with boiling 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate and re-probing.

For high-molecular-weight northern blots, the RNA was blotted to a HyBond-NX Nylon membrane (GE Healthcare) by capillary transfer after

separating on a 1% agarose gel containing 2.2M formaldehyde. The RNA was crosslinked to the membrane by irradiating twice with ultraviolet light (1,200 J each). Radiolabelled probe generation and hybridization was carried out as described above, with the difference in the hybridization and wash temperature being 65 °C.

**sRNA sequencing.** If not stated otherwise, RNA was processed into sequencing libraries using adapted Illumina protocols by (and sequenced at) Fasteris [\(http://www.fasteris.com\)](http://www.fasteris.com) using the Illumina HiSeq sequencer. For libraries of leaf peelings from wild-type plants shown in Fig. [2d](#page-2-0) and Supplementary Fig. 5, 2 µg of total RNA was processed into sequencing libraries and sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencer at the Functional Genomics Center Zurich [\(http://www.fgcz.ch/](http://www.fgcz.ch/)). For libraries of leaf peelings from wild-type and *ago1-18* and *ago1-42* mutant plants, 1 µg of total RNA was processed into sequencing libraries using the Small RNA-Seq Library Prep Kit (Lexogen) after gel-based size selection of total RNA using the ZR small-RNA PAGE Recovery Kit (Zymo Research). Successful libraries were gel purified and sequenced at the Functional Genomics Center Zurich.

**sRNA sequencing data processing.** For libraries sequenced at Fasteris, de-multiplexing and adapter removal was done by Fasteris. For IR71 libraries, reads were clipped using fastx\_clipper from fastx-toolkit [\(http://hannonlab.cshl.](http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/) [edu/fastx\\_toolkit/](http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/)), looking for the first 15 nucleotides of the adapter sequence (TGGAATTCTCGGGTG). 17- to 30-nucleotide reads with identical sequences were grouped using the processReads function from the ncPRO-seq pipelin[e61](#page-9-46) and aligned against the *Arabidopsis thaliana* genome (TAIR10) using Bowtie 2 (ref. [62\)](#page-9-47) with the options -k 100 --score-min L,0,0. For sRNA libraries from plants containing the *SS* transgene or infected by TuYV, the transgene or the virus sequences were included in the Bowtie 2 index as extra-chromosome.

**sRNA profile representation.** For Figs. [1c,g,h,](#page-1-0) [2d](#page-2-0) and [3d](#page-3-0) and Supplementary Figs. 1b, 5 and 13, simple genomic position comparison was applied to retrieve sRNA read counts and positions corresponding to the selected loci (IR71, *SS* or TuYV). These were then used to calculate the normalized read counts (reads per million mapped reads) for each nucleotide and to produce a graphical representation using R. Reads lengths were colour coded as indicated in the respective figure captions. For Supplementary Fig. 2, the same method was applied but normalization was done by calculating, for each nucleotide, the proportion based on the global coverage of the locus. The obtained values for each library were overlaid and visualized by adding transparency to the blue channel.

**sRNA sequence abundance comparison.** For the MA plots comparing sRNA abundance in Supplementary Fig. 9, only 19- to 24-nucleotide-long sequences were considered. Read counts for each sequence were normalized by the total number of 19- to 24-nucleotide-long reads mapped to the respective locus and used to calculate the loge[fold change] as well as the average number of reads between conditions. When more than one replicate was available, the mean normalized read counts between replicates were used. The abundance of sequences in each quadrant of the plot was represented by histograms (values are indicated in red). Calculation and graphical representation were done using R. Sequences with a loge[fold change] higher than 0.5 or lower than −0.5 were considered as enriched in one or the other condition. 5′- and 3′-nucleotide content (Fig. [4](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12) was then determined using the awk substr command.

**Leaf epidermis peeling (Meselect).** The leaf epidermis peeling was performed according to the Meselect protocol[23](#page-9-11). The lower epidermis of leaves was separated from the vasculature and upper side of the leaf by peeling after placing the leaf between two sticky tapes. To remove residual mesophyll cells, the tapes were incubated in protoplasting solution (1% cellulase Onozuka R10 (Yakult), 0.25% Maceroenzyme R10 (Yakult), 0.4M mannitol, 10mM CaCl2, 20mM KCl, 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 20mM 2-(*N*-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (MES (pH5.7)) under gentle shaking on a rocking platform at 50 r.p.m. The epidermal side was incubated for 15min and the vasculature was incubated for 20–30min. Subsequently, the tissues were washed twice with washing buffer (154mM NaCl, 125mM CaCl2, 5mM KCl, 5mM glucose and 2mM MES (pH5.7)). The epidermal tapes were directly frozen in liquid nitrogen and, from the residual tapes, the vasculature was removed using forceps and frozen in liquid nitrogen as well. The epidermal tapes and vasculature were stored at −80 °C until protein and RNA extraction.

**Immunolocalization.** Five-day-old seedlings were fixed by infiltrating twice with fresh paraformaldehyde fixation buffer (4% paraformaldehyde and 5% DMSO in 1× MTSB (50mM PIPES, 5mM EGTA, 5mM MgSO4 and KOH (pH6.8))) under vacuum for 5–10min. Root samples were incubated for 1h at room temperature. Samples were rinsed twice in PBS and twice in H2O and were then placed on a glass slide with few drops of water. Once completely dry, root samples were rehydrated. The water was then poured off and root samples were rinsed in 1× PBS+0.1% Triton X-100 (PBST). Cell wall digestion was done with 3% Driselase (Sigma–Aldrich) in PBST for 1h at 37 °C in a humid chamber. Subsequently, root samples were rinsed twice with PBST and the membrane was permeabilized with

permeabilization buffer (3% NP-40and 20% DMSO in PBST) for 1h at room temperature in a humid chamber. Root samples were then blocked by rinsing four times in PBST for 5min, then rinsing twice with 1× PBS and twice with 3% BSA in 1× PBS. For FHA:P19 detection, primary antibody raised against Flag from rat (1:250; Roche), diluted in 3% BSA in 1× PBS, was added. Root samples were incubated overnight at room temperature in a humid chamber and were rinsed five times with 1× PBS. Secondary antibody goat anti-rat IgG coupled to Alexa 568 (1:400; Invitrogen), diluted with 3% BSA in 1× PBS, was added and incubated for 45min at room temperature in a humid chamber. Subsequently, the root samples were rinsed once with 1× PBS, three times with PBST and twice with 1× PBS before imaging.

**GUS staining.** Tissues were fixed in 90% ice-cold acetone for 15min under a vacuum, embedded in paraffin and sectioned using a microtome to 10µm before imaging. Samples were stained in staining solution (1mM X-Gluc, 4mM potassium ferro-/ferricyanide, 0.1% Silwet L-77 and 100mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH7.0)) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Stained plants were imaged under a microscope.

**Virus infection.** Virus infections with TuYV or TuYV–GFP were carried out by *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*-based leaf infiltration, as described previousl[y26](#page-9-14). In short, cultures of *A. tumefaciens* carrying the respective virus clone on a binary vector (pBIN series) were grown to OD600=0.5, pelleted, resuspended with infiltration buffer (10mM MgCl2, 10mM MES (pH5.6) and 100 µM Acetosyringone) and incubated for 2h at room temperature. Three individual leaves of 24-day-old plants were infiltrated with ~100 µl bacterial suspension. Positive infections were monitored by PCR with reverse transcription from leaf samples at 10d post-infection using the 3′-Klenow primers listed in Supplementary Table 2, or in case of TuYV–GFP by monitoring GFP fluorescence in the root vasculature using a fluorescence binocular (Leica).

**Live-cell and immunofluorescence imaging.** Fluorescent transcriptional or translational fusion-expressing roots or TuYV–GFP-infected roots were imaged under a Zeiss 780 confocal laser scanning microscope using 20× or 40× water immersion objectives immediately after excision from growing plants, as described previously[54.](#page-9-39) Images shown in all figures are representative of consistent results observed in multiple experiments.

# Life sciences study design

All studies must disclose on these points even when the disclosure is negative.

Sample size No statistical predetermination of sample size was used. For stable mutants used for qRT-PCR or blot analysis a biological sample size was chosen to be at minimum of three (3). Sample size was deemed sufficient as the read-out was of qualitative rather than quantitative nature and could be seen as kind of a quality control, e.g. is the sample infected with virus or not, are particular small RNAs present or not, is the mechanical separation in case of Meselect clean or is tissue cross contamination present or is the IR71 promoter active in one tissue or the other. In such cases statistical significance was not calculated because it didn't add a particular scientific value. In case of IR71 promoter analysis and virus infections the qRT-PCR data was also backed up by an independent experimental approach using microscopy to validate the finding. For sRNA deep sequencings of SS and TuYV the technical sample size converted to a sequencing library was one (1). The sample consisted of a pool of independent biological individuals of a sample size >3 and as described in detail in the manuscript. The sample size was deemed sufficient for our needs as the initial experiment was of qualitative in nature, e.g. do we detect small RNAs in tissue B while their origin is in tissue A. Scientific conclusions were only drawn after continuous observation of the same phenotype over three (3) independent RNAi triggers carried out in independent experiments including independent sequencing runs and using two different technical approaches (biochemical versus mechanical separation). Here, the nature of the read-out was qualitative rather than quantitative so statistical determination of the significance of differences in the individual phenotypes was not aimed for. Due to the expected technical variation in mechanical separation we used two (2) independent pools of samples for the deep sequencings used for IR71 small RNA analysis via Meselect and three (3) independent pools for the independent repetition of WT samples and inclusion of ago1-18 and ago1-42 mutants. A high degree of qualitative agreement of the small RNA profile between sample pools and independent experiments indicated that the sample size was sufficient and the technique of mechanical separation was robust. Data exclusions No experimental data was excluded Replication All experiments in the manuscript were successfully repeated at least three (3) times, apart from the following: sRNA deep sequencings of SS and TuYV samples were single sequencing runs as explained above in the section "sample size". However, prior to sequencing the sample the experiment to isolate incipient and recipient pools of the respective sRNAs, and which was documented by Northern- and Westernblot, was at least successfully repeated three times. Small RNA deep sequencing of leaf peeling samples was done in two independent experiments once in biological duplicates (2) on WT samples and another time as triplicates (3) on WT, ago1-18 and ago1-42 samples, as indicated in the section "sample size" above and in the manuscript. The only failed attempts in replication arose in the preliminary data stage during initiation of the study and included only the virus infected samples as the virus infection was not 100% efficient. The infection itself relies on Arabidopsis leaf infiltration with Agrobacterium tumefaciens carrying a binary vector with the full length virus as described in the manuscript. To not include the transgene in our sampling, but authenthic replicating virus, we restricted sampling to the roots. However, not all of the root system was infected. We then used to pre-screen plants for infections either via dot blot (for the WT virus) or visual inspection (GFP tagged virus) to confirm successful infections. Once we introduced this pre-screening step all replications of the experiments using the virus were successful and are what is shown in the paper. Randomization Randomization was not relevant to the study as no statistical comparison between the samples was calculated due to the qualitative nature of the data read-out and interpretation. Blinding Blinding was not relevant to the study as no statistical calculations were carried out on the qualitative data read-out. However, a blinding was indirectly applied on deep sequencing analysis as the researcher handling the samples and the researcher doing the bioinformatic analysis were not the same and the sample ID for sequencing were converted into a sequencing library ID which did not give away information of the nature of the sample to the bioinformatician. After processing of the bioinformatic data sample information relating to the corresponding libraries was revealed for data analysis. For the quality control steps including the qRT-PCR of the meselect samples knowledge of the source of the samples was not possible to blind due to the physically different appearance of the different tissue samples.
