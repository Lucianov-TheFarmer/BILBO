#### **ORIGINAL ARTICLE**

# **Investigating the NAD-ME biochemical pathway within C4 grasses using transcript and amino acid variation in C4 photosynthetic genes**

**Alexander Watson‑Lazowski1,3  [·](http://orcid.org/0000-0002-2323-8287) Alexie Papanicolaou1,3 · Robert Sharwood2,3 · Oula Ghannoum1,3**

Received: 25 February 2018 / Accepted: 28 July 2018 © Springer Nature B.V. 2018

#### **Abstract**

Expanding knowledge of the C4 photosynthetic pathway can provide key information to aid biological improvements to crop photosynthesis and yield. While the C4 NADP-ME pathway is well characterised, there is increasing agricultural and bioengineering interest in the comparably understudied NAD-ME and PEPCK pathways. Within this study, a systematic identification of key differences across species has allowed us to investigate the evolution of C4-recruited genes in one C3 and eleven C4 grasses (Poaceae) spanning two independent origins of C4 photosynthesis. We present evidence for C4-specific paralogs of NAD-malic enzyme 2, MPC1 and MPC2 (mitochondrial pyruvate carriers) via increased transcript abundance and associated rates of evolution, implicating them as genes recruited to perform C4 photosynthesis within NAD-ME and PEPCK subtypes. We then investigate the localisation of AspAT across subtypes, using novel and published evidence to place AspAT3 in both the cytosol and peroxisome. Finally, these findings are integrated with transcript abundance of previously identified C4 genes to provide an updated model for C4 grass NAD-ME and PEPCK photosynthesis. This updated model allows us to develop on the current understanding of NAD-ME and PEPCK photosynthesis in grasses, bolstering our efforts to understand the evolutionary 'path to C4' and improve C4 photosynthesis.

**Keywords** C4 photosynthesis · Gene recruitment · Convergent evolution · Poaceae · NAD-ME · PEPCK

Photosynthesis is the primary biological process for the production of carbohydrates, responsible for maintenance of plant growth and productive yield. Enhancing photosynthetic capacity has long been a target to improve crop yields (Long et al. [2006;](#page-14-0) Sharwood [2017\)](#page-15-0). knowledge to improve crop production in the future. Following long periods of low carbon dioxide (CO2) concentrations over geological time (Petit et al. [1999](#page-14-1)), a large number of plant lineages have evolved one of several carbon concentrating mechanisms (CCMs) to improve photosynthetic rates (Price et al. [2008\)](#page-14-2). One subset of plant species that operate a CCM is classified as C4 plants, due to their initial fixation of a 4-carbon acid by the CCM. This CCM mechanism required anatomical and biochemical modifications to enable collaboration between the bundle sheath and mesophyll cells, resulting in elevated CO2 concentrations around the key photosynthetic enzyme ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/ oxygenase (Rubisco), improving photosynthetic rates under most environmental conditions (Ehleringer et al. [1997\)](#page-13-0). The evolution of C4 photosynthesis represents a pivotal event in plant evolution to the extent that, a large proportion (~ 25%) of today's total plant productivity is generated from this relatively small subset of plant species (~3%) (Still et al. [2003](#page-15-1)).

Three subtypes of C4 photosynthesis are thought to exist (Hatch et al. [1975](#page-14-3)), each known by the primary decarboxylase enzyme they use for releasing CO2 inside the bundle sheath from the transiently fixed 4-carbon acid. These are: NADP-malic enzyme (NADP-ME), NAD-malic enzyme (NAD-ME) and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK). Each decarboxylase functions within a different cellular compartment, NADP-ME within the chloroplast, NAD-ME within the mitochondria and PEPCK within the cytosol. Decarboxylation within these subtypes is not unique to one decarboxylase enzyme, with some species utilising a second decarboxylase (Muhaidat et al. [2007;](#page-14-4) Wingler et al. [1999](#page-15-2)). This additional decarboxylase is referred to as a secondary decarboxylase, as they are usually less abundant than the primary decarboxylase, but still provide a significant contribution to the C4 cycle. Some PEPCK species have been shown to exhibit substantial levels of photosynthetic NAD-ME activity (Carnal et al. [1993\)](#page-13-1), while some NADP-ME and NAD-ME species exhibit photosynthetic PEPCK activity (Hatch and Kagawa [1976](#page-13-2)). Evidence of a species utilising both NADP-ME and NAD-ME photosynthetically is limited, but recent work has noted elevated transcript abundance of both decarboxylases within a single species (Washburn et al. [2017\)](#page-15-3). Modelling work has also suggested the possibility that no pure PEPCK species can exist, and all PEPCK species will require some NAD-ME activity (Wang et al. [2014\)](#page-15-4). The significance of utilising a secondary decarboxylase is not yet fully understood, but has been shown to provide significant flexibility in metabolism (Furbank [2011](#page-13-3); Bellasio and Griffiths [2013](#page-13-4); Sharwood et al. [2014](#page-15-5)). Most well-known C4 species, such as maize, sorghum and sugarcane, use NADP-ME as their primary decarboxylase. This has led to research around NADP-ME species being more prevalent, leaving limited genetic resources for the majority of C4 plants.

Previous taxonomic work has shown that C4 photosynthesis has evolved independently over 60 times (Sage et al. [2011](#page-14-5)). This C4 mechanism exists within monocots and eudicots, but is yet to be found in canopy forming tree species. Within grass species there are at least 18 C4 lineages (Sage et al. [2011\)](#page-14-5), with the majority of species within these lineages falling into either the Chloridoideae or Panicoideae subfamilies (Edwards et al. [2010](#page-13-5)). All three subtypes are represented within the Panicoideae subfamily with dominance of NADP-ME species, whereas virtually all species within the Chloridoideae are either NAD-ME or PEPCK. These subfamilies represent an interesting subset for investigating gene recruitment into C4 photosynthesis, covering all three subtypes and multiple independent origins of C4 photosynthesis.

Even though the lineages of C4 photosynthesis are well documented, the individual paths C4 species took to evolve the necessary physiology and gene regulation to become C4 is yet to be fully defined. Orthologues of genes involved in C4 photosynthesis exist in non-C4 species, where they are involved in an array of other plant processes (Aubry et al. [2011;](#page-13-6) Maier et al. [2011\)](#page-14-6). Recent work has shown a large driver of C4 gene recruitment to be its C3 ancestral state (Moreno-Villena et al. [2017](#page-14-7)), with genes exhibiting ancestral elevated transcript abundance being repeatedly co-opted into the C4 pathway. A suite of 'C4 specific' paralogs has also been identified, originating from events such as gene duplications (Christin et al. [2009a,](#page-13-7) [b\)](#page-13-8). Both co-opted and duplicated genes utilised by C4 photosynthesis regularly show significantly higher expression than their non-photosynthetic orthologues and paralogs (Christin et al. [2013](#page-13-9)), setting a good benchmark to identify potential C4 genes. There is strong evidence for convergent and parallel evolution of key C4 genes (Christin et al. [2013](#page-13-9); Emms et al. [2016](#page-13-10)), highlighting the precise nature of core processes within C4 photosynthesis. Parallel to this, divergent characteristics of C4 physiology and morphology have been discovered, such as differences in bundle sheath/mesophyll signalling (Rao et al. [2016\)](#page-14-8), indicating the potential for unique optimisation across species.

Here, our overall objective was to elucidate genes involved in the poorly understood NAD-ME biochemical pathway. C4-specific genes are still to be confirmed or elucidated for key enzymes of the NAD-ME pathway, including decarboxylases and mitochondrial organic acid transporters, as well as the cellular location of aspartate amino transferase. These proteins are essential, as they underpin the efficiency of the CO2 concentrating mechanism in the NAD-ME/PEPCK C4 subtypes. Consequently, we undertook a genomic and molecular evolution study across one C3 and eleven C4 species. We focus on the two main C4-rich subfamilies of the Poaceae (Chloridoideae and Panicoideae), which represent relatively understudied and taxonomically complex independent C4 lineages. In addition, we integrated published genome data in order to build a phylogenetic framework. As a result we are able to predict the functionality of novel C4-specific genes within the NAD-ME pathway.

# **Materials and methods**

### **Plant material**

Twelve grass species (*n* = 1) were grown from seeds in 5 L pots containing potting mix (Scotts, Australia) and received natural illumination as described previously in Sharwood et al. ([2016a\)](#page-15-6). Plants were watered regularly with the addition of a commercial liquid fertiliser (General Purpose, Thrive Professional). Species from the Chloridoideae [*Leptochloa dubia* (NAD-ME), *Leptochloa fusca* (NAD-ME), *Astrebla pectinata* (NAD-ME) and *Chloris gayana* (PEPCK)] and Panicoideae [*Panicum bisulcatum* (C3), *Panicum coloratum* (NAD-ME), *Panicum virgatum* (NAD-ME), *Panicum antidotale* (NADP-ME), *Panicum monticola* (PEPCK), *Axonopus fissifolius* (NADP-ME), *Sorghum bicolor* (NADP-ME) and *Echinochloa frumentaceae* (NADP-ME)] subfamilies were analysed during this study. All plants (except *P. monticola* and *P. antidotale*) were grown in the greenhouse facility at the Hawkesbury Campus of Western Sydney University, Richmond, NSW, Australia. The two other species were grown at the Australian National University, Canberra, Australia. At both sites, growth temperature ranged between 32 °C (day) and 18 °C (night) and mid-day light intensity averaged 800 µmol m−2 s −1 during the growth period. Additionally, four grass species were grown (*n*=3) in a controlled environment chamber (BioChambers walk-in growth cabinet) at Western Sydney University to confirm gene expression trends (Online Supplementary 1). These species were *Panicum bisulcatum* (C3), *Setaria italica* (NADP-ME), *Leptochloa fusca* (NAD-ME) and *Chloris gayana* (PEPCK). Three of these species overlap with the twelve sampled, and *S. italica* is a close relative of *P. antidotale*. These plants were potted in 4 L pots containing seed raising and cutting mix (Scotts, Australia) topped with commercial slow release plant fertiliser (Flowers, Fruit and Vegies, Hortico), and watered regularly with the addition of a commercial liquid fertiliser (General Purpose, Thrive Professional). Chamber temperature was set to 28 °C (day) and 22 °C (night) with 14 h days and a daytime light intensity of 1000 µmol m−2 s −1.

### **Transcript analysis**

Leaf discs (0.5–1.5 cm2 ) from the youngest expanded leaf of each plant were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until ready for extraction. Plants were aged between 28 and 32 days post germination. Discs were cut from the central region of each leaf to avoid tip to base transcriptome variation, avoiding the leaf midrib. Leaf discs were ground into a fine powder using a microfuge pestle (Bio-Rad) in a 1.4 mL tube with the aid of liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using PureZol (BioRad) following the method described in Sharwood et al. ([2011\)](#page-15-7). Aliquots of RNA were treated with DNase I (AMBION) following the manufacturer's protocols. Prior to library preparation, ribosomal RNA was removed using the RiboZero Plant rRNA kit (Epicentre).

Strand specific RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) was carried out on the extracted RNA using an Illumina Hi-Seq 2500 machine with 125 bp paired-end sequencing at the Western Sydney University NGS Facility. Between 30 and 40 million reads were returned per sample. Raw data can be found on GenBank under BioProject ascension PRJNA392207. Raw reads were processed using Trimmomatic (Bolger et al. [2014\)](#page-13-11) and Blue (Greenfield et al. [2014\)](#page-13-12) to both trim the adapters and reduce any sequencing errors. Reads were assembled for each species using *de novo* assembly using Trinity (Haas et al. [2013\)](#page-13-13). If there was a published genome, a genome guided assembly was also performed using Trinity, then both the genome guided and *de novo* assembly were combined using PASA (Haas et al. [2003\)](#page-13-14) to remove any redundancy. This gave a set of non-redundant contiguous sequences (contigs) for each species. Bowtie2 (Langmead and Salzberg [2012\)](#page-14-9) coupled with eXpress (Roberts and Pachter [2013\)](#page-14-10) was used to calculate the number of effective raw reads which mapped to each contig, and to then normalise via trimmed mean normalisation and library size within each species, to give normalised transcript abundance [transcripts per million (TPM)]. Additional chamber grown plants were analysed using the same pipeline as above, other than the use of Kallisto (Bray et al. [2016\)](#page-13-15) instead of bowtie2 and eXpress to map the raw reads. When using transcript abundance to identify potential C4 candidates each species was considered separately as evidence suggests not all species within a subtype perform C4 photosynthesis identically (Furumoto et al. [2011](#page-13-16)). Contigs were subjected to open reading frame prediction and translation using TransDecoder (Haas et al. [2013\)](#page-13-13), before annotation into orthologous groups via *Zea mays, Pancium virgatum* and *Arabidopsis thaliana* genomes using Orthofinder (Emms and Kelly [2015](#page-13-17)). Extensive manual curation was then performed, and each orthologous group of interest was mined for contigs and aligned in Geneious version 8.1.8 (Kearse et al. [2012\)](#page-14-11) to identify the orthologues with the highest homology to each gene of interest.

### **Alignments and phylogeny**

Amino acid sequences from each species were aligned using MAFFT (Katoh et al. [2002](#page-14-12)), a 1.53 gap penalty, a 0.123 offset value, the G-INS-i algorithm and the BLOSUM62 scoring matrix. Nucleotide sequences from each species were aligned for each gene via MAFFT translational align (Katoh et al. [2002\)](#page-14-12), using a the same parameters as for the amino acid sequences (Online Supplementary 2). *Oryza sativa* orthologue sequences were included in the alignments to use as a root for the eventual phylogenetic trees when required. Aligned sequences were trimmed to a core mRNA codon sequence that was available for all species to prevent missing data from confounding our analyses. This gave a comparable nucleotide and amino acid sequence for each gene. Phylogenetic trees were built using RaxML (Stamatakis [2014\)](#page-15-8), using a GTR CAT model for nucleotide sequences, a CAT BLOSUM62 model for protein sequences and 1000 bootstrap replicates performed on the best scoring maximum likelihood tree.

### **Evolutionary traits**

TraitRateProp provides a statistical framework to test the hypothesis of association between a trait and rate of sequence evolution, as well as testing for residues which may be functionally associated with the trait (Levy Karin et al. [2017a\)](#page-14-13). TraitRateProp requires three inputs, an ultrametric phylogenetic tree of all species being analysed, an alignment file of the gene sequences and a list specifying whether a single trait was present in each species ('trait file'). The ultrametic was built using MrBays (Huelsenbeck and Ronquist [2001\)](#page-14-14) from *MatK* (GRMZM5G836994) and *rbcL* (GRMZM2G385635) sequences retrieved from the RNA-Seq (Online Supplementary 2) using the custom command block within Geneious (Online Supplementary 3). Protein alignments were carried out as described in the previous section. Start and stop codons (which are under purifying selection) were removed, sequences combined and referenced as partitions within the command block. The trait file identifies each species with a 1 or 0 based on whether a trait is or is not present, within this study the trait being photosynthetic subtype. A Bayes factor cut-off of more than 5 has previously been shown to limit the number of false positives inferred when using the Bayes factor (Levy Karin et al. [2017b](#page-14-15)), and so this cut-off was used to distinguish residues with a likely association with the trait. All files were uploaded to the TraitRateProp website [\(http://www.traitrate.tau.ac.il/prop/\)](http://www.traitrate.tau.ac.il/prop/) and ran using their online server (July 2017).

To test the hypothesis that genes of interest did or did not undergo positive selection at the origin of each lineage we used PAML and PAML-X (Yang [2007](#page-15-9); Xu and Yang [2013](#page-15-10)). We used nucleotide alignments and phylogenetic trees built previously using RaxML, and implemented the branch-site model within CodeML (Zhang et al. [2005\)](#page-15-11) to test if any residues were under selection. This approach tests the ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions (dN/dS ratio; omega) under two scenarios, one a null model where all codons evolve under either purifying selection (omega<1) or relaxed selection (omega = 1). The second scenario allows sites to evolve under purifying or neutral selection in the whole tree, except for foreground branches, where they evolve under positive selection (omega>1). These two scenarios are then compared to estimate the posterior probability of each base evolving under positive selection using a Bayes empirical model (Yang et al. [2005\)](#page-15-12). Foreground branches were selected to represent a rapid switch to C4 photosynthesis to test the hypothesis above, and complement the continuous species optimisation investigated via TraitRateProp. Therefore each branch of origin for NAD-ME photosynthesis was labelled as a foreground branch, and NAD-ME species-specific branches were labelled as background.

### **Published genomes**

Previously published genomes and expression data were mined to bolster conclusions and provide species to root phylogenetic trees when required. Genomes required were identified within Phytozome [\[http://www.phytozome.jgi.](http://www.phytozome.jgi.doe.gov) [doe.gov](http://www.phytozome.jgi.doe.gov): (Goodstein et al. [2012\)](#page-13-18)] and published transcript data extracted from raw RNA-Seq files downloaded from the Sequence Read Archive (*Zea mays*; SRR6904772, *Panicum hallii*; SRR3112194, *Setaria viridis*; SRR5020972, *Oryza sativa*; SRR072076) via the same pipeline described above. The additional species used were *Arabidopsis thaliana* (TAIR10; Lamesch et al. ([2011\)](#page-14-16)), *Oryza sativa* [genome v7\_JGI; Ouyang et al. ([2006\)](#page-14-17)], *Panicum hallii* (genome v2.0), *Setaria italica* [genome v2.2; Bennetzen et al. ([2012](#page-13-19))], *Setaria viridis* (genome v1.1) and *Zea mays* [genome Ensembl-18; Schnable et al. ([2009\)](#page-15-13)]. A list of the published ID's of genes referenced in the manuscript can be found in the supplementary data (Online Supplementary 4).

For NADP-ME and PEPCK the C4-specific copies have previously been identified, and so the *Z. mays* copies of these genes were used to extract sequences: NADP-ME4 (Christin et al. [2009b\)](#page-13-8) and PEPCK1 (Christin et al. [2009a\)](#page-13-7) (GRMZM2G085019 and GRMZM2G001696 respectively). Contigs with the highest sequence similarity to each gene were extracted and the TPM of each estimated. The aligned protein sequences of NADP-ME and PEPCK were compared to previously identified C4-specific bases (Christin et al. [2009a](#page-13-7), [b\)](#page-13-8) and matched to transcript abundance (Fig. [1](#page-4-0)). Here, as expected, only NADP-ME species contained the residues associated with the C4 copy of NADP-ME4, as well as showing consistently elevated abundance. The two species which use PEPCK as a primary decarboxylase, alongside two NADP-ME species (*P. antidotale* and *E. frumentaceae*), consistently matched the residues associated with the C4-specific PEPCK1 and showed elevated transcript abundance.

### **Aspartate amino transferase**

Multiple AspAT isoenzymes have been recruited into the C4 pathway (Hatch and Mau [1973](#page-14-19)). Understanding the recruitment strategy of these genes aids in the elucidation of the metabolite path that each subtype follows. [1995](#page-15-16); Hatch and Mau [1973\)](#page-14-19). Here we took the PTS2 peroxisome associated motif (R-L(I)-XXXXX-H(Q)-L) previously investigated in the *A. thaliana* AspAT3 (Eubel et al. [2008](#page-13-20)), and found it to be conserved within the AspAT3 transcripts of the grass species when the full sequence was recovered (Fig. [4](#page-7-0)c). This suggests that a percentage of the total enzyme pool may be transported to the peroxisome. Both aspartate and OAA have been hypothesised to enter the peroxisome through specific channels/porin-like proteins (Rottensteiner and Theodoulou [2006](#page-14-21)), and a balanced conversion of the two can occur, as long as the exchange of co-factors between the peroxisome and mitochondria is maintained (Rottensteiner and Theodoulou [2006\)](#page-14-21). Increased transcript abundance of these genes would be expected if any substantial C4 flux through the peroxisome was occurring due to the required exchange of substrates and co-factors, and so it is likely that transport of AspAT to the peroxisome would only create a diversion from the documented pathway. NADP-ME species showed consistently elevated transcript abundance for AspAT5 (chloroplastic), but not for either the peroxisome or mitochondrial AspAT. Further, NAD-ME and PEPCK species showed consistently elevated transcript abundance for AspAT3 (peroxisome), but only NAD-ME showed consistently elevated transcript abundance for AspAT1 (mitochondrial). Utilising TraitRateProp, both AspAT3 and AspAT1 showed significant rates of evolution (*P*=≤0.05) associated with the trait of elevated C4 subtype transcript abundance. Finally, AspAT5 no evidence of an evolutionary association, but this is likely to be due to the relatively small phylogenetic distances of the NADP-ME species used here.

# **C4 transporters**

Rapid transport of organic acids and sugar phosphates between mesophyll and bundle sheath cells, as well as between cellular organelles, is crucial for the co-ordination between the C3 and C4 cycle and for the efficient functioning of the CCM (Leegood and Walker [1999\)](#page-14-23). Without necessary C4 transporters, plants are hindered and substantial reductions in growth have been noted (Weissmann et al. [2016;](#page-15-18) Furumoto et al. [2011\)](#page-13-16). The majority of chloroplast transporters have been characterised with either mutants or substantial transcript evidence, but most mitochondrial transporters have only been hypothesised, or remain completely elusive. Pyruvate transport through the chloroplast has been well characterised as a combination of BASS2/ NHD1 (Furumoto et al. [2011;](#page-13-16) Bräutigam et al. [2011\)](#page-13-21), and increased transcript abundance was apparent in all NAD-ME and PEPCK species (Online Supplementary 6). Our results are supported by previous literature on NADP-ME species, with some species expressing BASS2/NHD1, while others (mainly Andropogoneae species) exhibiting no transcript expression. Pyruvate transport out of the mitochondria has been hypothesised to be a mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC) (Weber and Bräutigam [2013](#page-15-19)), but gene candidates have yet to be characterised. Here we analysed both MPC1 (AT5G20090) and MPC2 (AT4G14695), the two known distinct mitochondrial pyruvate transporters (Schell et al. [2014\)](#page-14-24). Two copies of each gene were present in all species analysed, with one of each having consistently elevated transcript abundance in NAD-ME and PEPCK species, when compared to NADP-ME and C3 species (Fig. [4d](#page-7-0)). TraitRate-Prop returned no evidence of positive selection associated with the trait of either NAD-ME or PEPCK subtype for either MPC1 or MPC2, but MPC proteins are known to be highly conserved (Timón-Gómez et al. [2013](#page-15-20)), so this may be expected.

The main family of mitochondrial transporters is known as the mitochondrial carrier family proteins (MCFs; also known as the SLC25a family in mammals), in which aspartate, malate and OAA transporters have previously been shown to fall into (Haferkamp and Schmitz-Esser [2012](#page-13-22); Palmieri [2013](#page-14-25)). Here we extracted orthologues of all available MCFs to look for instances of elevated expression (Online Supplementary 6). Five MCFs showed evidence of elevated transcript abundance in NAD-ME and PEPCK species when compared to NADP-ME and C3. One of these was DTC (Pavir.3KG088200.1; also known as a SLC25a11), which is a dicarboxylate/tricarboxylate transporter known to exchange both malate and OAA for tricarboxylates and at a lower rate, sulphate (Picault et al. [2002](#page-14-26)). This MCF has be previously been classified as a potential C4 transporter (Rao and Dixon [2016](#page-14-27)). Two other MCFs which showed evidence of elevated transcript abundance were DiC and PiC (Online Supplementary 6). DiC (Pavir.6NG276900.1; also known as SLC25a10) is a known malate and OAA transporter (via exchanges with phosphate), while PiC (Pavir.1NG473900.1; also known as SLC25a3) is a known phosphate transporter (Palmieri et al. [2008](#page-14-28)). Both MCFs have been previously classified as potential C4 transporters, likely working in tandem due to the antagonistic exchange of phosphate (Bräutigam et al. [2011;](#page-13-21) Rao and Dixon [2016\)](#page-14-27). Here, DTC, DiC and PiC did not show consistently elevated transcript abundance within a subtype, but numerous individual NAD-ME and PEPCK species did show evidence of elevated transcript abundance (Online Supplementary 6). The final two MCFs which showed evidence of elevated transcript abundance were CRC1 (Pavir.9NG454100.1; also known as SLC25a20/29), an annotated carnitine/acylcarnitine transporter, and MFRN (Pavir.9KG473700.1; also known as SLC25a28/37), an annotated iron transporter (Palmieri [2013\)](#page-14-25). No candidates known to transport aspartate into or CO2 out of the mitochondria were identified, leaving these transporters still to be elucidated if they are required. Transcript expression patterns were supported by additional replications of select species (Online Supplementary 1).

## **An updated model for C4 NAD‑ME and PEPCK photosynthesis**

Using novel evidence presented here, alongside confirmatory transcript abundance of previously suggested/confirmed C4 photosynthetic genes (Online Supplementary 1 and 6), we present an updated model for NAD-ME and PEPCK photosynthesis (Fig. [5\)](#page-9-0). We add several genes to the pathway (NAD-ME2b, MPC1a and MPC2a), update the localisation of AspAT and postulate the functional role of several C4 mitochondrial transporters. This model provides a complete overview of the known and hypothesised C4 genes utilised within the pathways, and also highlights where genes remain to be elucidated. Plasticity between the pathways has previously been shown, suggesting both subtypes contain both NAD-ME and PEPCK activity (Wang et al. [2014](#page-15-4)), but the extent of this plasticity has yet to be completely elucidated.

## **Discussion**

This study explored transcript abundance and amino acid variation of genes recruited into the C4 pathway in species originating from the Chloridoideae (*Leptochloa dubia, Leptochloa fusca, Astrebla pectinata* and *Chloris gayana*) and Panicoideae (*Panicum coloratum*, *Panicum antidotale*, *Panicum bisulcatum*, *Panicum monticola*, *Panicum virgatum*, *Axonopus fissifolius*, *Sorghum bicolor* and *Echinochloa frumentaceae*) subfamilies, two independent origins of C4 plants within the Poaceae. The species used contain four NADP-ME, five NAD-ME and two PEPCK C4 subtypes, as well as a C3 species as a control. Using this information we propose an updated model of NAD-ME and PEPCK photosynthesis in grasses.

### **Decarboxylases**

### **Confirmation of photosynthetic NADP-ME**

From an agricultural perspective NADP-ME is the most crucial decarboxylase to understand as it makes up the highest <span id="page-9-0"></span>**Fig. 5** Schematic models of NAD-ME and PEPCK C 4 photosynthesis. percentage of C4 grasses which are utilised as food crops. The photosynthetic copy of NADP-ME has previously been identified as NADP-ME4 (Ding et al. [2015](#page-13-23); Christin et al. [2009b](#page-13-8)) and is believed to have evolved via duplication from a C3 chloroplastic ancestor (Maier et al. [2011\)](#page-14-6). Here we present consistently elevated transcript abundance in species which utilise NADP-ME photosynthetically compared to those which do not. Increased expression of the photosynthetic NADP-ME is expected, as enzyme assays regularly show higher C4-specific enzyme activity when compared to species utilising the non-photosynthetic enzyme (Drincovich et al. [2001\)](#page-13-24). Previous studies suggest the C4-specific NADP-ME gene duplicated at the base of the Panicoideae subfamily, and additional duplications have since occurred within the subfamily, such as in the Andropogoneae tribe (Christin et al. [2009b](#page-13-8)). Due to these duplications NADP-ME4 became a multi-gene family within the Andropogoneae tribe, but only one of these copies is believed to be photosynthetic (NADP-ME4c). Analysis of C4 residues previously identified (Christin et al. [2009b](#page-13-8)) in this set of species confirms evidence towards convergent evolution. It also highlights species that have yet to evolve potentially beneficial residues and could, therefore, be targeted for future crop improvement.

### **Is photosynthetic PEPCK only present in PEPCK and NADP-ME grass species?**

Although PEPCK species are not highly utilised as crops, they include important forage species (e.g. green panic). In addition, a secondary PEPCK shuttle exists in numerous C4 NADP-ME crops which has the potential to be responsible for a substantial proportion of total decarboxylase activity (Sharwood et al. [2016b\)](#page-15-21). Transcript abundance was elevated in species that use PEPCK as a primary photosynthetic decarboxylase, although there was some overlap between those species that use it as a primary and a secondary decarboxylase. Previous enzyme assays have shown that there can be higher activity in species which use it as a secondary decarboxylase when compared to species which utilise it as a primary (Pinto et al. [2014](#page-14-29)), making some overlap between primary and secondary to be expected. Therefore, characterising PEPCK as a secondary/primary decarboxylase is not trivial through either biochemistry (enzyme assays) or genomics (gene expression). Previous work strongly indicates multiple gene duplication events within each subfamily of C4 plants, which have then undergone convergent evolution. The previous set of residues identified (Christin et al. [2009a](#page-13-7)) was analysed and we found the residues matched those identified within PEPCK species. However, those species which use PEPCK as a secondary decarboxylase were much more variable. Two NADP-ME species (*P. antidotale* and *E. frumentaceae*) showed evidence of the C4-specific PEPCK, interestingly the two species with elevated PEPCK expression (TPM > 100). NAD-ME species have previously been thought to have photosynthetic PEPCK activity (Wang et al. [2014\)](#page-15-4), but displayed low transcript abundance and contained no C4-specific residues (Fig. [1a](#page-4-0)). However, these abundance results are in line with previous PEPCK transcript abundance studies from NAD-ME grasses (Rao et al. [2016\)](#page-14-8). We postulate that if NAD-ME species do have PEPCK activity it is either minimal, or may be due to the secondary PEPCK shuttle not being consistently expressed. It may also be the case that secondary PEPCK is only utilised under certain environmental conditions or developmental stages, a perspective that is recently gaining more support (Pick et al. [2011](#page-14-30); Ludwig [2016\)](#page-14-31). This highlights the need for a comprehensive analysis of both enzymes and transcripts to classify the use of a secondary decarboxylase as large variation in expression of PEPCK by NADP-ME and NAD-ME species is apparent. Understanding this variation could open up avenues of crop improvement via the plasticity and optimums of the secondary shuttle.

### **Only NAD-ME2 is co-opted into C4 photosynthesis within grasses**

Species utilising the NAD-ME photosynthetic decarboxylase are often overlooked, as these species have limited economic benefit (apart from some millets consumed in Africa, India, China and parts of America) and are comprised of some of the world's most noxious weeds. However, due to the rise of useful NAD-ME biofuel crops and a move towards exploring the feasibility of using NAD-ME in the transformation of C3 into C4 species, they are recently gaining more attention. Here, one of our main objectives was to shed light on this understudied C4 pathway. NAD-ME has been shown to function as a heterodimer enzyme of both NAD-ME1 (α subunit) and NAD-ME2 (β subunit) in the C4 dicotyledonous *Amaranthus hypochondriacum* (Long et al. [1994;](#page-14-32) Maier et al. [2011](#page-14-6)), but octamers of only one subunit of NAD-ME have been shown to form in several C4 grasses (Ohsugi and Murata [1980;](#page-14-33) Murata et al. [1989\)](#page-14-34). Previous gene expression studies have produced conflicting results: Rao et al. ([2016](#page-14-8)) reported a role for only NAD-ME2 within *P. virgatum*, while Bräutigam et al. [\(2011\)](#page-13-21) reported increased abundance of both NAD-ME1 and NAD-ME2 in the C4 dicot *Cleome gyandra* when compared to closely related C3 species. The large evolutionary distance of these species could be responsible for these differences, but what this variability means to the NAD-ME pathway is yet to be elucidated. In our study, NAD-ME1 showed no evidence of elevated transcript abundance between species, whereas NAD-ME2 exhibited consistently elevated transcript abundance in species which utilise NAD-ME as a primary photosynthetic decarboxylase. TraitRateProp provided statistical evidence that the trait of NAD-ME photosynthetic subtype was significantly associated with the rate of evolution in NAD-ME2 (*P*≤0.0001), but not in NAD-ME1. This indicates a functional relationship between the NAD-ME subtype and the NAD-ME2 protein. Indeed, TraitRateProp returned several residues with a high probability of being associated with C4 functionality within NAD-ME2.

When all identified paralogs and orthologues of NAD-ME2 were investigated phylogenetically, a clear distinction was apparent between species known to use NAD-ME photosynthetically, aligned with differences based on transcript abundance. This lead to the creation of four subgroups of NAD-ME2 (Fig. [3a](#page-6-0)): NAD-ME2a(i) (Panicoideae respiratory), NAD-ME2a(ii) (Chloridoideae respiratory), NAD-ME2b(i) (Panicoideae photosynthetic) and NAD-ME2b(ii) (Chloridoideae photosynthetic). In the NAD-ME species *P. virgatum*, two copies of NAD-ME2 exist, NAD-ME2a and NAD-ME2b. Interestingly though, in the genome of the other NAD-ME species *P. hallii*, only one copy of NAD-ME2 could be identified, NAD-ME2b. Only NAD-ME2a was expressed in NADP-ME species, whereas both copies (NAD-ME2a and NAD-ME2b) were expressed in NAD-ME and PEPCK species. This aligns with the theory that most or, possibly all, PEPCK species require photosynthetic NAD-ME activity (Hatch and Kagawa [1976](#page-13-2); Wang et al. [2014\)](#page-15-4). Those with two copies indicate a gene duplication of NAD-ME2, as has been previously shown with multiple other C4-specific genes (Christin et al. [2013;](#page-13-9) Emms et al. [2016\)](#page-13-10), but the lack of a second copy in the genome of *P. hallii* suggests that if this was the case the respiratory copy can be lost. To investigate the origin of the duplication, a branchsite model within PAML was utilised to identify residues under positive selection at the earliest shared branch containing only the photosynthetic copy of NAD-ME within each lineage (Fig. [3](#page-6-0)b). A suite of residues was under significant positive selection (*P*≤0.05), suggesting that a duplication may have occurred at each point of origin of photosynthetic NAD-ME, and also showed evidence of convergent evolution within the Panicoideae and Chloridoideae lineages at this evolutionary time. This early signal of convergent evolution, along with the signal of continued convergent evolution from Fig. [2](#page-5-0)b, would explain why the NAD-ME2b proteins from both the Panicoideae and Chloridoideae NAD-ME species form a single clade, even though they are from distinct lineages.

Taken together, these results suggest that only NAD-ME2 has been recruited into a C4-specific role within the Poaceae grasses, and NAD-ME1 has not. Within *A. thaliana*, evidence suggests the NAD-ME2 can function as a homodimer (Tronconi et al. [2008](#page-15-15)), but evidence from NAD-ME grasses suggests that octamers of one NAD-ME subunit can be formed (Murata et al. [1989;](#page-14-34) Ohsugi and Murata [1980](#page-14-33)), leading us to predict that one of these is the most likely functional form of photosynthetic NAD-ME2 in grasses.

### **Variable cellular locations of photosynthetic aspartate amino transferase**

The reversible reaction of aspartate to OAA is a key step in both NAD-ME and PEPCK photosynthesis, as aspartate is the main C4 acid used to concentrate CO2 inside the bundle sheath within these subtypes (Hatch et al. [1975](#page-14-3)). Three distinct copies of AspAT have been previously classified (Hatch and Mau [1973](#page-14-19)), and all three were identified within this study (Fig. [4a](#page-7-0)). Transcripts of chloroplastic AspAT (AspAT5) were consistently elevated in NADP-ME species, suggesting movement of aspartate through the chloroplast, agreeing with recent models of NADP-ME C4 photosynthesis (Rao and Dixon [2016](#page-14-27)). Mitochondrial AspAT (AspAT1) transcripts were consistently elevated and showed significant associated rates of evolution in NAD-ME species (*P*≤0.05), concurrent with decarboxylation occurring with the mitochondria of NAD-ME species (Sommer et al. [2012](#page-15-22)). Transcript abundance of mitochondrial AspAT was not elevated in PEPCK species. Although the presence of photosynthetic NAD-ME2 was shown in PEPCK species, expression was low. Another possibility is the movement of OAA into the mitochondria rather than aspartate, as high expression of cytosolic AspAT is present in PEPCK species, potentially reducing/removing the requirement for mitochondrial AspAT. Transcripts of the third AspAT (AspAT3) were consistently elevated and showed significant associated rates of evolution in both NAD-ME and PEPCK species (*P*=≤0.05), but its location is yet to be fully elucidated, with suggestions of the peroxisome (Miesak and Coruzzi [2002](#page-14-20)), cytosol (Hatch and Mau [1973](#page-14-19); Taniguchi et al. [1995](#page-15-16)) and chloroplast (Rao and Dixon [2016\)](#page-14-27). Here we suggest that it is located within the cytosol and peroxisome, due to the presence of a conserved sequence motif associated with peroxisome signalling (PTS2). No evidence of increased transcript abundance for PEX7 or any peroxisomal transporter was identified. *In vitro* measurements have shown the rate limiting step for PEX7 transport can be the export of PEX7 out of the peroxisome via PEX5 (Rodrigues et al. [2014](#page-14-35)), but again there was no evidence of increased PEX5 transcript abundance. For any substantial reaction to occur the transport of substrates and the exchange of co-factors needs to be considered. Although both OAA and aspartate can cross the peroxisome membrane and create a balanced reaction (Rottensteiner and Theodoulou [2006\)](#page-14-21), increased activity would also require the transport of 2-oxoglutarate and glutamate between the mitochondria, peroxisomes and cytosol. As no peroxisomal transporters showed evidence of up-regulation we predict the peroxisomal contribution towards the C4 cycle is likely fractional when compared to the total AspAT3 activity. Protein evidence from previous studies using the NAD-ME species *P. milleaceum* labelled the locations of C4 AspAT as mitochondrial and cytosolic (Hatch and Mau [1973;](#page-14-19) Taniguchi et al. [1995](#page-15-16)), and not within the chloroplast. Further to this, results from Hatch and Mau [\(1973](#page-14-19)) showed a small proportion of cytosolic AspAT activity to correspond specifically with a peroxisomal marker enzyme. As both the data presented above and published evidence suggests, a small proportion of AspAT3 is transported to the peroxisome, but it is likely an even smaller fraction contributes towards the C4 pathway. Therefore, we predict that cytosolic AspAT3 activity is still predominant within the C4 cycle. However, it remains that an increased amount of AspAT3 is transported to C4 peroxisomes within grasses (Hatch and Mau [1973\)](#page-14-19) which could create a diversion of substrates. This could reduce the efficiency of the cytosolic reaction and therefore is important to note when identifying avenues to optimise C4 photosynthesis. We expect AspAT3 to be preferentially expressed within mesophyll cells of NAD-ME species, which has been previously shown in *P.virgatum* (Rao et al. [2016\)](#page-14-8), to convert OAA into aspartate before movement into the bundle sheath. No cell specific transcript expression is currently available for PEPCK species, but we expect AspAT3 to be equally expressed in both mesophyll and bundle sheath cells to convert OAA to aspartate within the mesophyll and aspartate back to OAA within the bundle sheath.

# **The role of additional C4 transporters**

Elucidating the transporters of the C4 pathway is essential to understanding the movement of metabolites through both the bundle sheath and mesophyll cells. Most C4 chloroplast transporters have been well characterised with both gene knock-outs and substantial transcript evidence (Weissmann et al. [2016](#page-15-18); Christin et al. [2009b](#page-13-8); Furumoto et al. [2011](#page-13-16); Huang et al. [2016\)](#page-14-36), while several C4 mitochondrial transporters have yet to be discovered. In this study, two mitochondrial pyruvate transporters were identified as likely C4 transporters via consistently elevated transcript abundance within NAD-ME and PEPCK species, MPC1a and MPC2a. Characterisation of mitochondrial pyruvate transport in humans and yeast shows that optimum transport is facilitated by an MPC1 and MPC2 heterodimer complex. Knock-outs of one of the two proteins in yeast and *Drosophila* reduced rates of pyruvate transport (Bricker et al. [2012](#page-13-25)). We propose that this complex is likely formed in plants, and the increased expression of both MPC1a and MPC2a is required in NAD-ME and PEPCK species to fulfil the necessary transport requirements of C4 photosynthesis. Pyruvate transport back into the chloroplast was confirmed to be via the BASS2/NHD1 complex (Furumoto et al. [2011](#page-13-16)) in NAD-ME and PEPCK species. Orthologues of the MCFs were extracted and tested for evidence of elevated transcript abundance in the hope of elucidating other mitochondrial transporters. DTC transcripts showed evidence of elevated transcript abundance in NAD-ME and PEPCK species, a mitochondrial dicarboxylate/tricarboxylate transporter known to have the ability to transport malate and OAA between the mitochondria and cytosol in exchange for dicarboxylates, tricarboxylates and at a lower rate, sulphate (Picault et al. [2002](#page-14-26)). Mitochondrial malate/OAA transport can also be carried out by DiC, a dicarboxylate transporter (Palmieri et al. [2008](#page-14-28)). DiC can import malate/OAA in exchange for a phosphate, and likely works in combination with PiC (a phosphate transporter), which can return the exchanged phosphate allowing for the continued import of malate/OAA. Although DTC and DiC fulfil similar roles in terms of malate/OAA transport, their unique optimisation in terms of substrate exchange means they may be utilised within C4 plants under varying conditions/substrate availability. Together, DiC and PiC are the most likely candidates for malate/OAA import into the mitochondria as they could self-sustain the net import required to maintain the C4 cycle. Additionally DiC transcripts have previously been shown to be upregulated in the NAD-ME dicot *C. gynandra* (Bräutigam et al. [2011](#page-13-21)) and NAD-ME grass *P. virgatum* (Rao et al. [2016](#page-14-8)). Net import of malate/ OAA through DTC would be less consistent as it would be reliant on available tricarboxylates or sulphate within the mitochondria to facilitate an exchange, but a short term net import could be possible if these substrates are available. Here DTC and DiC transcripts weren't consistently elevated, but showed evidence of elevated abundance in varied NAD-ME and PEPCK species, suggesting a possible flexi C4 role for the two transporters. Interestingly, the Chloridoideae species had much higher expression of both DTC and DiC/ PiC when compared to Panicoideae species, suggesting a potential optimisation within their lineage to transport greater amounts of OAA into the mitochondria rather than aspartate. The two other transporters which showed evidence of elevated transcript abundance in NAD-ME and PEPCK species were a carnitine/acylcarnitine transporter and an iron transporter. The increased movement of carnitine/acylcarnitine is notable, as both could have potential functions related to C4 activity. Acylcarnitines (acetylcarnitine) have been shown to negatively affect the catalytic activity of AspAT (Kerner et al. [2015](#page-14-37)), and so could be used to regulate the enzyme. Another possibility is the use of carnitine to release CO2 from citrates (Bressler and Brendel [1966](#page-13-26)), a substrate that is exchanged by DTC for malate/OAA. This could be used to further increase CO2 concentration within the bundle sheath if excess citrate is available. These possibilities require further research and provide additional avenues of investigation. Within this study no aspartate transporter was identified as a candidate for C4 photosynthesis. With a role for cytosolic AspAT and mitochondrial malate/OAA transporters, it could also be considered that there is no requirement for increased aspartate import into the mitochondria. This is important to consider as it would affect the ratio of metabolite flux into the mitochondria, and is therefore important when considering genetic engineering of the NAD-ME cycle.

# **Conclusion**

Here we use transcript abundance and amino acid variation to advance the current model of C4 NAD-ME and PEPCK photosynthesis within grass species (Fig. [5](#page-9-0)). As well as the addition of several C4 genes, we update the functional location of the key C4 enzyme AspAT, and the functional roles of key C4 mitochondrial transporters. This information will contribute to efforts to improve both C3 and C4 photosynthesis by providing novel gene targets, as well as advancing the current understanding of the 'path to C4'.
