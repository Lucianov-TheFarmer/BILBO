#### Introduction:

Plants have evolved numerous chemicals to defend themselves against attack from herbivores and pathogens. One example is the glucosinolates (GLS) which are produced by members of the *Brassicaceae* family (Wittstock and Burow, 2010). Upon tissue damage resulting from attack of chewing insect, necrotrophic pathogen infection, or other mechanical damage, GLS present in plant cells and are hydrolyzed by thioglucosidase enzymes termed myrosinase (MYR), resulting in various indolic molecules (Andréasson et al., 2001). The effects elicited by indole GLS hydrolysis products go beyond toxicity and range from mechanistic protection, such as increased callose deposition, strengthening of the cell wall and inhibition of cell penetration (Clay et al., 2009), to strategies such as modulation of the hypersensitive response (Zhao et al., 2015).

Indole-3-carbinol (I3C) is one such GLS-breakdown product formed from the breakdown of indole-3-methylglucosinolate (I3M) (Wittstock and Burow, 2010). I3M is the predominant indole glucosinolate, and one of the most prominent glucosinolates detected in roots. The cleavage of I3M to I3C is mostly catalyzed by myrosinase (Andréasson et al., 2001; Koroleva et al., 2010), however, I3C can also form in a MYR-independent manner (Kim et al., 2008). I3C has a deleterious effect on herbivorous insects (Kim et al., 2008; Li et al., 2002) as well as considerable antimicrobial activity (Sung and Lee, 2007).

We previously demonstrated that I3C serves as a signaling molecule in plants that modulates the auxin response by antagonizing auxin binding to the TIR1 receptor (Katz et al., 2015b). I3C also effects the PIN1 and PIN2 auxin transporters (Katz et al., 2015a), and induces specific autophagy (Katz and Chamovitz, 2017). Hence exogenous I3C at high concentrations inhibits Arabidopsis growth and development (Katz et al., 2015b).

The eukaryotic ribosome is a complex structure composed of four rRNAs and about 80 ribosomal proteins (r-proteins) (Barakat et al., 2001), organized into two unequal subunits, the small subunit (40S) and large subunit (60S). Both of these contain dozens of ribosomal proteins arranged on a scaffold composed of ribosomal RNA (rRNA).

Here we report the isolation and analysis of an I3C-tolerant Arabidopsis mutant which overexpresses a ribosomal S30 gene. S30 is a small component of the 40S subunit of the ribosome. The 40S subunit monitors the complementarity between tRNA anticodons and mRNA (Ben-Shem et al., 2011; Yusupova and Yusupov, 2017). In *Arabidopsis thaliana*, 249 r-protein genes were identified encoding 32 putative small subunit proteins (Barakat et al., 2001). Individual ribosomal proteins may have extraribosomal functions in addition to those in the translation machinery (Lu et al., 2015; Nadano et al., 2000; Warner and McIntosh, 2009; Wool, 1996). We show here that overexpression of S30 results in a transcriptional priming which helps in overcoming exposure to I3C.

#### Results:

#### Isolation of an Arabidopsis line tolerant to I3C

Indole-3-carbinol (I3C) influences plant growth and development by manipulating auxin signaling. Phenotypically this is easily seen by inhibition of root elongation following exogenous I3C treatment (Katz et al., 2015b). To explore how exposure to I3C effects plant growth and development, we screened for Arabidopsis mutants that are tolerant to high concentrations of exongenous I3C. For this purpose we screened the Full-length cDNA Over-eXpression (FOX) library (Ichikawa et al., 2006) on 400µM I3C, which inhibits 95% of root elongation (Katz et al., 2015), for mutants with relatively longer roots.

After three screening stages (Materials and methods), we remained with one Indole-3-Carbinol Tolerant mutant, which we termed ICT1. ICT1 showed increased root elongation relative to WT on MS media supplemented with 400µM I3C, while no difference in root length was observed when grown on MS (Figure 1a, b).

In the absence of I3C, ICT1 is essentially phenotypically indistinguishable from WT (Figure 1c). However, upon incubation with 400  $\mu$ M I3C in first two weeks of development, WT was twice as inhibited in the root-elongation assay than ICT1. Upon subsequent transfer to I3C-free media, ICT1 recovers faster than the WT: WT recovered normal developmental rates two weeks following transfer, while ICT1 resumed normal growth within one week (Figure 1c).

#### **Overexpression of S30 gene causes tolerance to I3C**

To identify the transgene carried by ICT1, DNA was isolated and FOX-directed PCR was performed. ICT1 carries the AT2G19750 gene which encodes a S30 ribosomal protein, a small (~7kDa) basic protein which is co-expressed with other ribosomal subunit proteins, located in the "platform" region (Yusupova and Yusupov, 2017). In Arabidopsis S30 is encoded by three paralogues genes located on different chromosomes; the three genes have ~76% identity between their cDNA sequences and encode identical proteins with differing expression frequency. Of the three paralogs, AT2G19750 appears to have the highest expression (Barakat et al., 2001). Overall, these genes are constitutively expressed at low levels in all vegetative tissues except pollen (Becker et al., 2003; Chen et al., 2003).

To verify that tolerance to I3C is caused by the overexpression of AT2G19750 (S30) and not by the knockout of the underlying gene, we analyzed additional lines predicted to overexpress AT2G19750. These new lines included two additional FOX transgenic lines, annotated as overexpressing S30 (obtained from RIKEN Japan), and independent transgenic lines generated in our lab (Materials and methods). The nomenclature is summarized in Figure 2.

We screened the various ICT1 lines for changes in AT2G19750 transcript levels. As seen in Figure 2a, S30 transcript levels increase 1.7-6 fold in the two FOX lines ICT1-8098 and ICT1-478 and four transgenic over-expression mutants, ICT1 – 2/28/33. All mutants analyzed showed significantly increased root growth on 400 $\mu$ M I3C (Figure 2b, c), indicating that the tolerance is not a random result of fortuitous transgene integration, but rather is a result of the overexpression of the S30 gene. For subsequent phenotypic analysis, we continued with over-expression mutants ICT1-2 and ICT1-8098.

#### ICT1 is tolerant to exogenous I3C up to 500 $\mu M$

To calculate the degree of tolerance to I3C, growth rates of WT and the ICT1 overexpression mutants were determined for increasing concentrations of I3C. Root lengths were measured at several time points over increasing I3C concentrations (Figure 3). The growth rate of the WT and ICT1 mutants is similar on MS, however it is 3 and 2.3 times faster on 400µM I3C in ICT1-2 and ICT1-8908 respectively. In Figure 3 we see that all plants show maximum inhibition at 750 µM I3C, however WT is 50% inhibited at ~ 50µM while ICT1-2 and I3CR1-8098 are 50% inhibited at 300 µM and 200 µM respectively. 90% inhibition is observed at 400µM I3C in WT but only in 750 µM and 500 µM in ICT1-2 and I3CR1-8098 respectively (Figure 3).

## ICT1 is tolerant specifically to I3C

To test if overexpression of S30 causes tolerance specifically to I3C, or rather causes general tolerance to external chemical stress, we tested root growth on three groups of molecules: two subcategories of indol derivates: 1. Breakdown products of indol-3-acetaldoxime such as I3C, indol-3-acetic acid (IAA), and 3–Indoleacetonitril (IAN) ; and 2. breakdown products of I3C such as 3, 3Diindolylmethane (DIM), indole-3-carboxaldehyde (I3xA), and methyl Indole 3-carboxylate (MI3Cx). The third group contains benzoxazinoids: 2-Benzoxazolinon (BOA) and 6-Methoxy-2-benzoxazolinone (mBOA). Benzoxanoids are chemically unrelated to I3C, but with similar function of plant defense in *grammae* (Figure 4a). WT and the ICT1 mutants were assayed for root growth on 50% and 90% inhibition concentrations (IC) of the above molecules (Supplementary Table 1).

As seen in Figure 4b, all chemicals assayed inhibited root growth in the WT. ICT1 did not exhibit any pronounced tolerance to IAN, I3CxA or MI3Cx, or the benzoxanoids. These results suggest that the tolerance in ICT1 was limited to three chemicals: I3C, IAA and DIM, and is not a result of a general chemical-induced stress tolerance.

#### I3C acts as an auxin antagonist both in Col-0 and in ICT1.

ICT1 exhibited slight tolerance to IAA (Figure 4b, c). It was previously shown that I3C acts as an auxin antagonist on TIR1 (Katz et al., 2015b). We assayed the mutants for response to exogenous auxin. Growth of WT on exogenous IAA cause concentration-dependent inhibition of root elongation (Katz et al., 2015b; Rahman et al., 2007). Addition of 50 and 200 µM I3C to IAA rescues the IAA phenotype, showing longer roots (Figure. 5a, Supplementary Figure 1). Higher concentration of 500 µM I3C causes 95% inhibition by itself, therefor we do not see the rescue effect. ICT1 show similar phenomena, implying I3C acts as an auxin antagonist both in Col-0 and in ICT1 mutant, such that the tolerance identified likely arises from a TIR1-independent mechanism.

# ICT1 is transcriptionally primed for stress response, glucosinolate synthesis and transport.

To garner understanding as to how treatment with I3C effects the plant, and how the overexpression of S30 influences the response to I3C, we adopted a transcript profiling approach to elucidate the effect of I3C on gene expression in *A. thaliana*. RNA was isolated in triplicates from 10-days-old Col-0, and the two independent lines, ICT1-2 and ICT1-8098, each grown on MS or MS supplemented with 200 or 400 µM I3C, and analyzed by RNA seq using an Illumina HiSeq 2500 (total of 24 samples, see Supplementary Methods). Following initial data analyses, several thousand genes were identified as I3C-responsive as reflected by the log2 fold change values (Supplementary Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 2). To validate the I3C-induced changes in gene expression identified in Col-0, 10 transcripts were selected and subjected to RT-qPCR (Supplementary Figure 3).

The transcriptome results were further analyzed in two steps. Functional enrichment analysis was conducted on different groups as follows. We first asked which are the I3Cresponsive genes in the wt. The majority of genes influenced are common to both I3C concentrations 200  $\mu$ M and 400  $\mu$ M, with no significant change in expression intensities (Supplementary Figure 2). The gene ontology groupings identified in the common downregulated genes include auxin-pathway genes, similar to that reported for an earlier study employing microarray technology (Katz et al., 2015a), thus validating of our system.

Additional ontology groupings influenced by both concentrations of I3C include root development (Supplementary Figure 4), glucosinolate metabolic process (Supplementary Figure 5 and Supplementary Table 3), cytokinin, gibberellin, cell wall and transport-related genes. Up-regulated gene groups include genes known to be involved in the ethylene pathway and in responses to bacteria or fungi, proteasome activity, stress responses, cell death and apoptosis. ABA pathway and membranerelated genes were also misregulated following I3C treatment (Supplementary Figures 6 and 7).

At 400  $\mu$ M, there are 3 times more misregulated genes that at 200  $\mu$ M. These additional genes did not define additional ontology families, rather additional genes in the ontologies identified at 200  $\mu$ M. Thus, the additional stress of the higher concentration appears to have an additive effect on the number of genes misregulated, rather than on influencing additional pathways (Supplementary Figure 2).

We next checked if the resistance to I3C in ICT1 is manifested at the transcriptome level. In the absence of I3C, we noticed that the two ICT1 lines exhibited a misregulation of a number of genes that are induced (123) or repressed (169) by I3C in the WT (Figure. 6a). We refer to these genes as "primed". This primed group includes up-regulated genes involved in both abiotic (oxidative) and biotic (pathogen) stress responses. Glucosinolate biosynthesis and some transport functions are down-regulated in the I3Ctolerant mutants (Figure 6b). Most of the genes identified as I3C-responsive in WT are also misregulated in ICT1 following treatment with exogenous I3C. Moreover, a small number of genes are up (122) or down (45) regulated specifically in ICT1 but not in WT. Most of the up regulated genes are indicated in signaling defense and stress responses.

#### ICT1 is partially resistant to abiotic and biotic stress

This transcriptome analysis led us to hypothesize that the tolerance to I3C in ICT1 is not a result of an inhibition of the I3C-induced transcriptional response, but rather due to a general transcriptional priming of stress responses. To test this hypothesis, we monitored the growth-response of both ICT1 over-expression mutants to oxidative and pathogen stress. As seen in Figure 6c, the root-length assay revealed that ICT1 is tolerant to  $H_2O_2$ , though showed no enhanced tolerance to salt or osmotic stress. The response to two pathogens, *Botrytis cinerea* and *Pseudomonas PST DC3000* was monitored. As seen in Figure 6d, in wt *Botrytis* infiltration leads to rapid and clearly visible necrosis of over 50% of the leaf area within 7 days. In two independent ICT1 lines, a similar infiltration leads to partial necrosis, and in a relatively healthy leaf. To assay the response to *Pseudomonas*, we counted colony forming units over time from infected leaf discs. As seen in Figure 6e, ICT1 had 2.5 fold less colony forming units relative to wt. Thus, the priming of stress response genes identified above, manifests also in tolerance to both oxidative and biotic challenges.

#### Discussion:

Our previous studies identified indole-3-carbinol as a signaling molecule which modulates auxin signaling, and potentially interfaces between plant responses to herbivory and plant growth (Katz and Chamovitz, 2017; Katz et al., 2015a; Katz et al., 2015b). To further understand how I3C interfaces with plant metabolism and growth, we employed a genetic approach to isolate mutants tolerant to I3C treatment. Following the screening of 20,000 seeds from the full-length cDNA Over-eXpression (FOX) library (Ichikawa et al., 2006), we isolated one over-expression mutant with stable tolerance to I3C, which we termed ICT1. ICT1 carries a transgene over-expressing AT2G19750, which encodes the S30 subunit of the 40S ribosome.

Several lines of evidence indicate that the tolerance to I3C in ICT1 is due to overexpression of the S30-encoding gene. First, additional FOX lines which over-express AT2G19750, showed similar tolerance to exogenous I3C. While clearly we would rather the tolerance be 100% consistent, the exact level of tolerance in different experiments over the course of this study varied. However, it is important to emphasize that even though the experiments were performed with the inherent variation above, the significant tolerance to I3C at elevated concentrations in the transgenic plants remained in all cases. Second, we generated independent lines which over-express AT2G19750, and these lines also exhibited tolerance to I3C (Figure 2b, c). Third, all of the tolerant lines studied had increased expression of the S30-encoding AT2G19750 (Figure 2a). Thus, the tolerance to I3C is not due to fortuitous integration of the transgene, but likely arises directly from the over-expression of S30.

We term the phenotype as "tolerant" and not "resistant", as while ICT1 grew better in the presence of I3C than did WT, the growth rate was still inhibited. While the growth rate of Col-0 roots was inhibited at 50% by 100 $\mu$ M I3C, ICT1 was inhibited at 50% by ~250  $\mu$ M. However, both over-expression mutants were nearly fully inhibited at 750  $\mu$ M. Thus, ICT1 maintains growth in 400 $\mu$ M I3C, and recovers normal growth rates upon removal of I3C, while the WT is stunted by the I3C treatment, and remains with slower growth rates even following removal of the chemical.

Common mechanisms of tolerance to chemical exposure include detoxification and reduced uptake, mechanisms which can yield non-specific chemical tolerance (Maurel et al., 2015; Nguyen et al., 2018). Uptake experiments of external I3C revealed no differences between ICT1 and WT (not shown). Interestingly, with two exceptions discussed below, we found that the tolerance in ICT1 is specific to I3C treatment, and not a general tolerance response to all indoles/glucosinolates or other toxic molecules.

The two exceptions were that ICT1 did show increased tolerance to exogenous IAA and DIM. IAA and I3C share high structural similarity and both bind to the same binding site on TIR1 (Katz et al., 2015b). DIM, a stable I3C breakdown product, is a dimer of I3C, thus shares high structural similarity with I3C, similar to IAA. Indeed, numerous studies highlight that I3C and DIM share several biological activities (Licznerska and Baer-Dubowska, 2016; Wang et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2012). As ICT1 is not tolerant to other indole molecules, we conclude that ICT1 cells are permeable to indoles. However, while ICT1 is slightly resistant to auxin, it maintains the previously reported I3C antagonism of IAA action (Katz et al., 2015b). Thus, I3C antagonizs auxin in both WT and ICT1, and the tolerance to I3C must result from an IAA-independent mechanism in I3C action.

The ICT1 lines had no obvious phenotype, and our preliminary analysis of loss-offunction mutants of S30 shows that the particular gene studied here is not essential, perhaps due to genetic redundancy.

In lieu of a functional description for S30, we employed transcript profiling in an attempt to understand the influence of S30 overexpression on the plant, as well as to describe the transcriptional effects of I3C exposure on the WT. The transcript levels of several hundred genes change following a two-hour exposure to I3C. At a global level, exposure to I3C influences the expression of a number of genes involved in various phytohormone pathways, down-regulating, perhaps unsurprisingly, genes related to mostly aliphatic, but also indolic, glucosinolates, auxin and root development, while inducing genes involved in various defense responses. An increase in the concentration of I3C treatment did not lead to increases in the magnitude of changes in expression, nor in the expression of novel gene groups. Rather, the increased concentration of I3C led to the misregulation of additional genes in the pathways affected by the lower concentration. Thus, exposure to high doses of I3C apparently leads to increased growth arrest due to the misregulation of numerous genes in the same pathways regulated by low I3C concentrations.

While we had predicted that the tolerance to I3C may be reflected in differences in the genes misregulated following a brief exposure to I3C, in practice, many of these same genes are also misregulated in ICT1 following a two-hour exposure to I3C. However, we noticed that many of these same genes are already misregulated in ICT1, relative to the WT, under normal growth conditions. That is, genes that are misregulted in WT upon exposure to I3C, are misregulate in ICT1 under normal conditions. Thus, overexpression of S30 apparently leads to a change in gene expression, such that the plant is primed to respond to I3C. These primed genes include those involved in plant responses to oxidative and biotic stress. Indeed, we then found that ICT1 is partially resistant to oxidative and biotic challenge.

While our motivation was to identify components of the I3C-signaling pathways, we identified a subunit of the ribosome whose over-expression leads not only to tolerance to I3C, but also to several environmental challenges. How this subunit mediates these responses is unclear. Indeed, the function of many individual ribosomal subunits is unclear, and in this case, we do not know if the over-expressed protein incorporates into the ribosome, or has some mode-of-action in a form independent of the ribosome. Unfortunately, numerous attempts at raising antibodies to S30 were inconclusive.

What might be the mode of action by which overexpression of S30 leads to I3C tolerance? Little information exists about the roles of individual component of the 40S ribosomal subunit (Wool, 1996). Studies have indicated that individual ribosomal proteins have extra-ribosomal functions in addition to those in the translation machinery (Lu et al., 2015; Nadano et al., 2000; Warner and McIntosh, 2009; Wool, 1996). Interestingly, higher expression of S30 protein is correlated with breast cancer susceptibility (Zhang et al., 2018). Moreover, S30 shows differential expression in different cancer lines. However, this doesn't affect ribosomal structure and function. rather, this difference appears to be related to the extra-ribosomal function of \$30 (Nadano et al., 2000). A possible extra-ribosomal function of S30 could be as an antimicrobial peptide (AMP), which are found in all multicellular organisms. AMPs disrupt membrane structure of the bacteria. Ubiquicidin, an AMP isolated from murine macrophages (Hiemstra et al., 1999), is identical to mouse and human S30 (Wiesner and Vilcinskas, 2010). Thus, in our case, overexpression of S30 could also affect its extraribosomal functions rather than the ribosome itself. The proposed role of S30 as an AMP in Arabidopsis fits well with the bacterial and fungi resistance of ICT1 overexpression mutants.

Whatever the mechanism, over-expression of S30 apparently has potential agronomic value as a target for manipulating plant protection.

#### Methods:

*Plant material and growth assays.* The Arabidopsis strains used in this work were all in the Columbia-0 (Col-0) background. The gain of function lines psx00478 and psx08098 contain pBIG2113SF binary vector, expressing a single cDNA under 35S promoter, were ordered from FOX library (Ichikawa et al., 2006). Transgenic lines were generated in the lab using pBINPLUS (van Engelen et al., 1995), in which AT2G19750 gene was cloned downstream of the 35S promoter, and transformed to WT Col-0 via *Agrobacterium* cv3101 mediated transformation. The presence of the transgene was confirmed by PCR with specific primers for S30. The loss-of-function line Sail\_178\_c10.v1 contains tDNA insertion inside the S30 gene, was ordered from Sail library (Sessions et al., 2002). Seeds were cultivated in Petri plates using medium containing 0.8% agar, half-strength Murashige and Skoog salts (MS), and 1% sucrose, pH 5.7. The Petri plates were placed in chambers at 22°C under light/dark conditions of 16 h white light at 75 lmol m<sup>2</sup> sec<sup>-1</sup> and 8 h darkness, at 55% relative humidity. For root phenotype experiments, plates were placed vertically in the chambers.

**Preparation of indole and benzoxazinoids metabolites.** Indole-3-carbinol (I3C), 3indoleacetic acid (IAA/auxin), indole-3-acetonitrile(IAN), 3,3-diindolylmethane (DIM), indole-3-carboxaldehyde (I3xA), methyl-indole-3-carboxalate (Mi3Cx), 2-Benzoxazolinon (BOA) and 6-Methoxy-2-benzoxazolinone (mBOA) (Sigma,

https://www.sigmaaldrich.com/) were dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide to produce 1 M solutions, and stored in the dark at -20°C.

**Screening.** The FOX library was obtained from RIKEN Japan. Seeds were sown on 500  $\mu$ M I3C. 20,000 different FOX lines were screened in pools of 50. Seedlings with longer roots than wild type (WT) were isolated and assayed two more times for root length on 500  $\mu$ M I3C. To exclude the possibility of long root mutants, putative I3C-tolerant lines were sown on both 500  $\mu$ M I3C and MS plates. Only lines that presented longer roots in presence of I3C but not on MS were selected for further analysis.

**Cloning.** S30 cDNA was cloned in the sense orientation into the binary vector pBINPLUS (van Engelen et al., 1995) between the  $35S-\Omega$  promoter containing the translation enhancer signal and the Nos terminator, generating Pro35S:S30 construct. The original cloning vector pBINPLUS served as a control where applicable. Primers S30 for and

rev1/2 were used to clone S30. The constructs were electroporated into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. Col-0 was used for transformation with Agrobacterium.

(http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/19289445?dopt=Abstract), with up to 3 mismatches allowed per read, the minimum and maximum intron sizes were set to 11 and 11,000, respectively, and an annotation file was provided to the mapper. Only uniquely mapped reads were counted to exons, using 'HTSeq-count' package version 0.6.1 with 'union' mode

(http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/25260700?dopt=Abstract). Normalization and differential expression analyses were conducted using DESeq2 R package version 1.18.1 (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/25516281?dopt=Abstract).

**qRT-PCR.** The Quantabio Kit (95047-100) was used to synthesize the cDNA from the total RNA. Each RT-qPCR reaction was set up in a 15- $\mu$ l volume containing 0.6  $\mu$ l of cDNA, 0.4  $\mu$ l of genespecifc primers, 7.5  $\mu$ l of SYBR (Quantabio 65072-012) and 6.1  $\mu$ l of sterile distilled water. Actin and PPR (AT1G49240 & AT1G62930) were used as the reference genes. Each reaction included three biological replicates, which were analyzed via StepOne software, using the 2^ $\Delta\Delta$ Ct method for relative abundance calculation. The experiment was repeated 3 times.

Infection with Botrytis cinerea. B. cinerea spores were diluted to 5 x105 spores mL-1 in 0.53 potato dextrose broth. Droplets (7  $\mu$ L) of 0.53 potato dextrose broth with 105 B. cinerea spores were deposited on leaf surfaces of 4-week-old Arabidopsis plants (three leaves per plant). After incubation of the inoculated plants at high humidity for 7 d, the size of the disease lesion was measured. At least 15 lesion diameters were evaluated for each independent treatment (five plants). The lesion area was then measured with ImageJ.

*Infection with Pseudomonas.* Five-week-old Arabidopsis plants (Col-O, ICT1-2 and ICT1-8098) were inoculated with Pseudomonas PST DC3000 WT (Pst) by infiltration with a suspension (1 x105 CFU mL-1) of Pst in a 10 mM MgCl2 solution using a needleless syringe. Three 1-cm-diameter leaf discs were sampled at 0,3 and 6 days post-inoculation (dpi) from five inoculated plants and ground in 1 mL of 10 mM MgCl2. Samples were then 10-fold serially diluted and plated on LB plates supplemented with 25 mM rifampicin. The colony counts were recorded 2 d after incubation at 28°C.
