#### 35 **Abstract**

36 Genomic imprinting is an epigenetic phenomenon by which certain genes display 37 differential expression in a parent-of-origin-dependent manner. Hundreds of imprinted 38 genes have been identified from several plant species. Here we identified, with a high 39 level of confidence, 208 imprinted gene candidates from rice (*Oryza sativa*). Imprinted 40 genes of rice showed limited association to the transposable elements, which contrasts 41 with findings from *Arabidopsis thaliana*. Generally, imprinting in rice is conserved within 42 a species, but intraspecific variation was also detected. The imprinted rice genes do not 43 show signatures of selection, which suggests that domestication has had a limited 44 evolutionary consequence on genomic imprinting. Though conservation of imprinting in 45 plants is limited, we show that some loci are imprinted in several different species. 46 Moreover, our results suggest that different types of epigenetic regulation can be 47 established either before or after fertilization. Imprinted 24-nt small RNAs and their 48 neighboring genes tend to express alleles from different parents. This association was 49 not observed between 21-nt small RNAs and their neighboring genes. Together, our 50 findings suggest that regulation of imprinting can be diverse, and genomic imprinting 51 has evolutionary and biological significance.

#### 53 **Introduction**

54 Imprinted genes are expressed in only one of the parental alleles in a 55 parent-of-origin-dependent manner (Köhler et al., 2012; Gehring, 2013). Genes that 56 exclusively or preferentially express the maternal or paternal alleles are termed 57 maternally expressed genes (MEGs) or paternally expressed genes (PEGs), respectively. 58 Genomic imprinting has been observed in many species, from mammals to flowering 59 plants (Pires and Grossniklaus, 2014), and hundreds of putative imprinted loci, including 60 protein-coding genes and non-coding RNAs, have been discovered in plants (Gehring et 61 al., 2011; Hsieh et al., 2011; Luo et al., 2011; Waters et al., 2011; Wolff et al., 2011; 62 Zhang et al., 2011; Waters et al., 2013; Xin et al., 2013; Pignatta et al., 2014; Xu et al., 63 2014; Florez-Rueda et al., 2016; Hatorangan et al., 2016; Klosinska et al., 2016; Zhang et 64 al., 2016). However, there is little conservation of imprinting among plant species 65 (Waters et al., 2013; Hatorangan et al., 2016), which suggests that the evolution of 66 genomic imprinting in plants is very rapid.

67 Genomic imprinting is regulated epigenetically by either DNA methylation or histone 68 modification, and in some circumstances, both mechanisms are involved (Huh et al., 69 2008; Köhler and Weinhofer-Molisch, 2010). In plants, imprinting is predominantly 70 expressed in the endosperm (Luo et al., 2011; Raissig et al., 2013; Klosinska et al., 2016), 71 which is hypomethylated relative to the embryo and vegetative tissues (Gehring et al., 72 2009; Zemach et al., 2010; Rodrigues et al., 2013; Xing et al., 2015; Klosinska et al., 2016). 73 The DNA glycosylase *DEMETER* (*DME*), which is responsible for DNA demethylation, is 74 expressed predominantly in the central cell prior to fertilization (Choi et al., 2004). 75 Imprinting disorders can be found in *dme* mutants (Hsieh et al., 2011; Wolff et al., 2011; 76 Vu et al., 2013), indicating that proper DNA methylation status is required for the 77 establishment of genetic imprinting in the endosperm. Several studies have revealed 78 that imprinted genes usually neighbor transposable elements (TEs) (Wolff et al., 2011; 79 Pignatta et al., 2014; Hatorangan et al., 2016). High expression of *DME* in the central cell

80 promotes demethylation of TEs and their adjacent imprinted genes (Gehring et al., 2009). 81 Therefore, maternal alleles of imprinted genes are activated in the central cell, but the 82 paternal alleles are hypermethylated and remain silenced in the sperm cell, which 83 eventually leads to expression of only the maternal allele after fertilization. Polycomb 84 Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2)-mediated Histone H3 Lysine 27 Trimethylation (H3K27me3) 85 is required for proper genomic imprinting (Huh et al., 2008; Köhler and 86 Weinhofer-Molisch, 2010). Mutation of *fertilization-independent endosperm* (*FIE*), a 87 member of the Fertilization Independent Seed (FIS)-PRC2 family, may result in disruption 88 of imprinting at some loci (Hsieh et al., 2011; Wolff et al., 2011).

89 Genetic evidence has revealed that several MEGs are likely important for seed and/or 90 endosperm development in *Arabidopsis thaliana* (Chaudhury et al., 1997; Luo et al., 91 2000; Tiwari et al., 2008; Gerald et al., 2009; Costa et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2014). However, 92 many mutants of paternally expressed imprinted genes show no altered phenotype 93 (Köhler et al., 2005; Shirzadi et al., 2011; Bratzel et al., 2012; Vu et al., 2013; Wolff et al., 94 2015). In addition, conservation of imprinting between closely related species is limited 95 (Waters et al., 2013; Hatorangan et al., 2016). These observations cast doubt on the 96 importance of genomic imprinting in plants. The kinship theory (Haig and Westoby, 1991) 97 hypothesizes that imprinting arose as a consequence of conflict between male and 98 female gametes. Specifically, this theory suggests that males gain fitness benefits from 99 greater transmission of resources from the mother to the offspring, but females benefit 100 by suppression of growth-related demands from the offspring that are driven by 101 paternally active genes. Numerous observations support the kinship hypothesis (Köhler 102 et al., 2012; Pires and Grossniklaus, 2014; Rodrigues and Zilberman, 2015). However, in 103 plants, this theory has been challenged (Rodrigues and Zilberman, 2015). Recent 104 findings in Arabidopsis revealed that some PEGs may be involved in hybrid 105 incompatibility (Wolff et al., 2015), implying that genomic imprinting may play a role in 106 speciation. However, the evolutionary significance of genomic imprinting requires

107 further investigation in different plant species.

108 Rice (*Oryza sativa*) is a model monocot and a vital agricultural crop. Consistent with 109 results from dicots, studies in maize (*Zea mays*) (another monocot) have revealed that 110 differential DNA methylation and H3K27me3 modification between parental genomes 111 are essential for imprinting of monocots (Waters et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2014a). 112 However, our understanding of the epigenetic regulation of genomic imprinting in rice 113 remains limited. Through a genome-wide survey of imprinted genes from reciprocal 114 intraspecific crosses, Luo et al. (2011) and Yuan et al. (2017) identified more than three 115 hundred imprinted candidates in rice. However, there was little overlap in the identity of 116 these candidates between the studies (Yuan et al., 2017). The ability to identify 117 imprinted genes is dependent largely on the availability of informative single-nucleotide 118 polymorphisms (SNPs). Therefore, discovery of imprinted genes from distinct 119 intraspecific crosses is necessary to obtain greater certainty for identifying imprinted 120 candidates. Moreover, due to its rapid evolution, variation in imprinting has been found 121 among accessions in both Arabidopsis and maize (Pignatta et al., 2014; Waters et al., 122 2013). In this study, we explored imprinted genes in rice by making two independent 123 reciprocal crosses between *indica* and *japonica* cultivars, as well as crosses between 124 cultivated and wild rice. Our results showed that the regulatory mechanisms of genomic 125 imprinting in rice are diverse. The findings will help us to better understand the 126 regulation and evolution of imprinting in plants.

#### 129 **Results**

#### 130 **Identification of imprinted genes in cultivated rice**

131 To explore the imprinted genes in rice, we used three-line hybrid rice strains to construct 132 reciprocal crosses. A cytoplasmic male sterile (CMS) line carries a sterility gene encoded 133 in the cytoplasmic genome, such that the pollen produced is sterile. A "maintainer" line 134 shares the same nuclear genome as its corresponding CMS line but has a distinct 135 cytoplasmic genome. Due to the absence of the cytoplasmic sterility gene, the 136 maintainer line is fertile. Using rice CMS and maintainer lines for crossing greatly 137 increases the efficiency of hybridization and enables us to rigorously control the timing 138 of fertilization and to avoid false hybridization events. Here, we used the *japonica* CMS 139 lines (Liuqianxin-A and Yu6-A) and their maintainer lines (Liuqianxin-B and Yu6-B), and 140 the *indica* CMS lines (Rongfeng-A and Wufeng-A) with their corresponding maintainer 141 lines (Rongfeng-B and Wufeng-B) to make two distinct reciprocal-cross sets, which we 142 refer to as Liuqianxin-A/Rongfeng-B (LR), Rongfeng-A/Liuqianxin-B (RL), Yu6-A/Wufeng-B 143 (YW), and Wufeng-A/Yu6-B (WY). The LR and RL reciprocal crosses, and YW and WY 144 reciprocal crosses are indicated as LR-RL and YW-WY hereafter for convenience. 145 Meanwhile, we made crosses of Wufeng-A/Wufeng-B (WW), Rongfeng-A/Rongfeng-B 146 (RR), Liuqianxin-A/Liuqianxin-B (LL), and Yu6-A/Yu6-B (YY), which resembled selfing of 147 inbred lines as controls to validate imprinting.

148 Morphological observations suggested that seeds produced by LR, RL, YW, and WY 149 developed normally **(Figure 1A and B)**. The endosperm from these reciprocal crosses 150 was collected at five days after fertilization (DAF) for RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq). By 151 comparing LR-RL transcriptome data to whole genomic resequencing data from 152 Liuqianxin-A and Rongfeng-B, we identified 62,102 SNPs from 12,299 endosperm 153 expressed genes in the LR-RL cross that could be used to distinguish parental alleles. 154 Similarly, by comparing the RNA-seq data from YW-WY to genomic resequencing of 155 Yu6-A and Wufeng-B, we identified 59,107 SNPs from 11,044 genes. In total, parental

156 expression of 4,946 genes in LR-RL and 6,308 genes in YW-WY deviated from the expected 2:1 maternal-to-paternal ratio [*χ* <sup>2</sup> 157 test; *p* < 0.05, false discovery rate (FDR) < 158 0.05].

159 Next, we surveyed parent-of-origin effects of these genes by examining expression levels 160 that were greater than 2-fold (>4:1 for moderate MEGs and <2:2 for moderate PEGs) or 161 5-fold (>10:1 for strong MEGs and <1:5 for strong PEGs) relative to the expected 162 expression levels in both directions of the reciprocal crosses **(Figure 1C and D)**. 163 Therefore, for MEGs, the proportion of maternal alleles should be greater than 0.8 164 [4/(1+4)] or 0.91 [10/(1+10)], while for PEGs, it should be less than 0.5 [2/(2+2)] or 0.29 165 [2/(2+5)]. According to this criterion, there were 177 and 118 MEGs identified from 166 LR-RL using the 0.8 and 0.91 thresholds, respectively. There were 347 and 148 MEGs in 167 the YW-WY hybrids **(Supplemental Table 1)**. Moreover, there were 504 moderate PEGs 168 and 237 strong PEGs identified from LR-LR and 362 moderate PEGs and 129 strong PEGs 169 identified from YW-WY **(Supplemental Table 1)**.

170 In general, we identified more MEGs but fewer PEGs in the YW-WY cross. By analyzing 171 the ratio of (maternal transcripts): (maternal transcripts + paternal transcripts) at each 172 locus, we found that the peak of the YW distribution (0.75–0.8) was shifted from the 173 expected value of 0.67 **(Figure 1E)**, suggesting that, on average, the mother Yu6-A 174 contributed more mRNA transcripts than the expected 2:1 ratio. A similar trend also was 175 observed in the LR cross, albeit much less substantial than that in YW **(Figure 1E)**. In 176 contrast, most genes did not deviate from the expected ratio of 2:1 in WY and RL **(Figure**  177 **1E)**. Given that the Yu6-A CMS line is completely sterile and cannot self-fertilize, this 178 deviation cannot be caused by false hybridization events. Rice endosperm is easily 179 separated from maternal tissues at 5 DAF, and the two independent YW RNA-seq replicates were highly correlated (*r* 2 180 = 0.9873), indicating that contamination of maternal 181 tissues was unlikely. We used the genomic sequence of Nipponbare, a *japonica* rice 182 variety, as the reference for read mapping. Therefore, it is possible that mapping bias 183 could cause this deviation. However, given that RR and WW are closely related 184 (http://ricedata.cn), but LR did not show a deviation as extreme as YW, it is unlikely that 185 mapping bias caused the deviation.

186 In light of the observations that *indica*/*indica* crosses (WW and RR) exhibited faster early 187 endosperm development than the *japonica/japonica* crosses (LL and YY), and the hybrid 188 seeds were more similar to their maternal parent **(Figure 1A and B)**, we propose that the 189 maternal contribution at 5 DAF is caused by slower endosperm development and a 190 slower maternal-to-zygotic transition when *japonica* rice is used as the mother. To 191 confirm this hypothesis, we reanalyzed the RNA-seq data of endosperm at 7 DAF from 192 the Longtepu (*indica*) and 02428 (*japonica*) reciprocal crosses (L0-0L) (Yuan et al., 2017). 193 Consistent with our hypothesis, when the 02428 line was used as the mother, the peak 194 of the distribution of (maternal transcripts): (maternal transcripts + paternal transcripts) 195 shifted to 0.75 **(Figure 1E)**. These findings suggest that when *japonica* is used as the 196 mother, it contributes more to early endosperm development of the *japonica*/*indica* 197 hybrid. To minimize the influence of this bias, we mainly focused on the 208 strongly 198 imprinted genes (>5-fold bias) shared between LR-RL and YW-WY in the following 199 studies **(Figure 1F)**.

200 Using Reverse Transcription PCR and sequencing, we confirmed that all 14 of the tested 201 candidates were imprinted genes **(Supplemental Figure 1A)**. We also performed 202 single-colony sequencing and pyro-sequencing **(Supplemental Figure 1B to D)** to confirm 203 the imprinting status of two quantitative trait loci, *Grain Weight 2* (*GW2*) and *protein*  204 *phosphatase with Kelch-like repeat domain 2* (*OsPPKL2*), for rice seed development 205 (Song et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2012b), suggesting that genome imprinting may function 206 directly for seed development in rice.

207 For MEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed enrichment for the term "DNA binding" 208 (*p* = 0.00031, FDR = 0.017), whereas for PEGs, the terms "transferase activity" (*p* = 209 6.3e-05, FDR = 0.0014), "kinase activity" (*p* = 6.3e-05, FDR = 0.0014) and "protein 210 binding" (*p* = 0.001, FDR = 0.015) were overrepresented. This result suggested that PEGs 211 and MEGs might have different functions. Interestingly, we noticed that several of the 212 imprinted genes were involved in epigenetic regulation **(Supplemental Table 2)**. A 213 previous study reported that *OsFIE1* was the only imprinted PRC2 gene in rice (Luo et al., 214 2009). Here we found that rice *Embryonic Flower 2a* (*OsEMF2a*), a homolog of the 215 Su(z)12 family, also was imprinted, which was validated by different approaches 216 **(Supplemental Figure 2)**.

#### 218 **Comparison to previously identified imprinted genes in rice**

219 Luo et al. (2011) conducted a genome-wide survey of imprinted genes in rice using the 220 *japonica* cultivar Nipponbare and the *indica* cultivar 9311 as the parents (N9-9N). 221 Approximately 74% (53/72) of the PEGs and 53% (49/93) of the MEGs identified 222 overlapped with the ones identified in the present study **(Figure 1F)**. Similarly, about 80% 223 (76/97) of the PEGs and 44% (72/162) of the MEGs discovered from L0-0L (Yuan et al., 224 2017) were included in our list of candidates **(Figure 1F)**. However, there were fewer 225 overlaps between N9-9N and L0-0L (Yuan et al., 2017). In total, 300 MEGs and 289 PEGs 226 have been identified from rice **(Figure 1F and Supplemental Table 1)**, with PEGs being 227 more conserved than MEGs. About 54.3% of the PEGs (157/289) could be found in at 228 least two sets of the four reciprocal crosses (RL-LR, WY-YW, N9-9N and L0-0L), while only 229 39% of the MEGs (117/300) coincided among the different combinations.

230 Most of the imprinted genes identified in one cross also tended to be imprinted in 231 other reciprocal crosses **(Figure 2A to C and Supplemental Figure 3)**. The ones found in 232 only one set of reciprocals usually lacked informative SNPs or sufficient reads to identify 233 if they were imprinted in other crosses **(Supplemental Figure 3)**. For example, among 234 the 156 imprinted genes identified in the other two studies but not in the present study 235 **(Figure 1F)**, 62 (40%) lacked polymorphisms or expression in WY-YW and LR-RL.

236 Allele-specific imprinting has been found in maize and Arabidopsis (Waters et al., 237 2013; Pignatta et al., 2014). To test for allele-specific imprinting in rice, we used a cut-off 238 criterion. If a strong imprinted gene identified in one reciprocal-cross set failed to show

- 239 moderate imprinting (<2-fold deviation from the 2:1 expected ratio in other reciprocal
- 240 crosses), we considered it as an allele-specific imprinted gene. Seventy allelic specific
- 241 imprinted genes were found in rice **(Supplemental Table 3)**. Given that the mother plant
- 242 likely contributes more to the 5 DAF endosperm of the *japonica* (♀) /*indica* (♂) hybrid

243 than expected **(Figure 1E)**, the number of allele-specific imprinted genes could be 244 overestimated in the present study. However, PEGs should not be affected by the 245 maternal bias. We did find some PEGs showing allele-specific imprinting **(Supplemental**  246 **Table 3)**, which confirmed the existence of allele-specific imprinting in rice. For example, 247 LOC\_Os02g36360 was identified as a PEG in RL-LR and L0-0L but not in WY-YW **(Figure**  248 **2D and Supplemental Table 3)**.

#### 250 **PEGs in rice show weak associations with TEs and tend to be clustered in the genome**

251 TEs are enriched in the proximity of imprinted genes in Arabidopsis and *Capsella rubella*  252 (Wolff et al., 2011; Pignatta et al., 2014; Hatorangan et al., 2016). Yuan et al. (2017) 253 found that miniature inverted-repeat transposable elements were abundant near 254 imprinted genes of rice. However, Luo et al. (2011) did not find TE enrichment in rice. 255 We comprehensively searched for TEs in the rice genome and found 149,007 TEs, which 256 consisted of approximately 36% of the rice genome. We did not find enrichment of TEs 257 within 2 kb flanking regions of imprinted genes in rice. When using the united imprinted 258 genes of RL-LR, WY-YW, N9-9N and L0-0L for analysis, about 66% of MEGs and 57% of 259 PEGs have a TE within the vicinity of the 2-kb flanking region. By contrast, more than 70% 260 of the genes in the rice genome have a TE located within 2 kb **(Figure 3A)**. The chance of 261 finding a PEG close to a TE was lower than that for MEGs **(Figure 3A)**. Consistent results 262 were obtained when different MEGs and PEGs identified from distinct reciprocal crosses 263 were included **(Figure 3A)**. To minimize the influence of variation in TEs across different 264 varieties, we used the 32 MEGs and 22 PEGs **(Supplemental Table 1)** that were most 265 highly conserved among RL-LR, WY-YW, N9-9N and L0-0L for the analysis. The conserved 266 PEGs continued to show a weaker association with TEs **(Figure 3A)**. Moreover, the 267 distance of MEGs to their closest upstream and downstream TE did not differ from a 268 whole-genome control but was smaller than that for PEGs **(Figure 3B and C)**. These data 269 imply that imprinted genes, especially PEGs, showed limited association with TEs in rice.

270 Mammalian imprinted genes are usually clustered in the genome (Edwards and 271 Ferguson-Smith, 2007). However, this distribution pattern was not found in plants, 272 though some micro-clusters were observed across the genomes of different plant 273 species (Gehring et al., 2011; Luo et al., 2011; Wolff et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2011). We 274 used the set of imprinted genes found in MY-YM and RL-LR for clustering analysis. Across 275 a sliding window, we found that the 113 imprinted genes were organized into 51 clusters, 276 using the 13,821 genes with informative SNPs and expression in 5 DAF endosperm as the 277 control. The clusters are distributed unevenly across the genome **(Supplemental Figure**  278 **4)**. Over 34% (86/215) of the rice PEGs were located within clusters, while fewer than 16% 279 (27/215) of the MEGs were in clusters. In general, imprinted genes in the same cluster 280 displayed a consistent maternal or paternal expression pattern **(Supplemental Table 4)**.

### 282 **Expression patterns of imprinted genes**

283 We used Genevestigator to study the gene expression of the imprinted candidates in rice. 284 More than half of the common MEGs of RL-LR and WY-YW were expressed preferentially 285 in the endosperm **(Figure 4A and Supplemental Figure 5)**. The expression preference 286 was more apparent with the set of MEGs shared by RL-LR, WY-YW and N9-9N 287 **(Supplemental Figure 6A)**. Overall, the PEGs did not display preferential expression in 288 the endosperm **(Figure 4A and Supplemental Figure 6B)**. Dynamic expression analysis of 289 the imprinted genes during early seed development indicated that many MEGs and 290 some PEGs were not expressed in the first two to three DAF **(Figure 4B, Supplemental**  291 **Figure 6C and D)**. Their expression became activated at 3-4 DAF in the endosperm, but 292 usually not in the embryo **(Supplemental Figure 6C)**.

293 As demonstrated in Arabidopsis, the MEGs are activated in the central cell before 294 fertilization (Huh et al., 2008). However, we noted that only approximately half of the 295 MEGs were expressed in the ovary, whereas the rest were activated in the endosperm 296 after fertilization **(Figure 4A, Supplemental Figure 7A and B)**. Similarly, approximately 297 only half of the PEGs showed the highest expression in sperm cells **(Figure 4A,**  298 **Supplemental Figure 7A)**. By surveying publicly available transcriptome data, we found 299 that the MEG expression level was generally low in rice sperm cells **(Supplemental**  300 **Figure 7C)**. #### 303 **Epigenetic regulation of gene imprinting in rice**

304 By reanalyzing the publicly available rice DNA methylome from Nipponbare (Zemach et 305 al., 2010), we found that DNA methylation profiles of imprinted genes (RL-LR and 306 YW-WY commons) were quite similar in the embryo, roots and shoot, but there was 307 clear hypomethylation in endosperm **(Supplemental Figure 8)**. In terms of the promoter 308 region, imprinted genes generally exhibited higher CG and CHG methylation levels than 309 their non-imprinted paralogs in the embryo **(Figure 5A to D)**. Moreover, MEGs had 310 higher CG and CHG methylation levels than PEGs **(Figure 5A to D)**. In the endosperm, CG 311 methylation of the promoter regions of imprinted genes was reduced substantially 312 **(Figure 5A and B, Supplemental Figure 9)**, while non-imprinted homologous genes were 313 found at background levels in the embryo **(Figure 5A and B)**. Promoter CHG methylation 314 of imprinted genes was comparable to corresponding non-imprinted homologs in the 315 endosperm **(Figure 5D)**. The CHH methylation level in the endosperm was low, and there 316 were no differences between imprinted genes and their homologs **(Figure 5E and F)**. In 317 the embryo, gene bodies showed higher CG methylation of PEGs compared to MEGs 318 **(Figure 5A)**. However, the higher CG methylation of PEGs in gene bodies was not 319 detected in the endosperm **(Figure 5B)**. The results are in agreement with the findings 320 that PEGs are enriched for differentially methylated regions (DMRs) in gene bodies, 321 while MEGs tend to show DMRs in promoter regions (Rodrigues et al., 2013). In addition, 322 MEGs showed more CHG methylation in the gene body than non-imprinted MEG 323 homologs and PEGs in the embryo **(Figure 5C and D)**. The difference between MEGs and 324 their non-imprinted homologs was not observed in the endosperm, though MEGs were 325 still highly methylated relative to PEGs **(Figure 5D)**.

326 Intraspecific variations of methylation can lead to allele-specific imprinting in plants 327 (Pignatta et al., 2014). To eliminate the impact of the Nipponbare genotype on 328 methylation patterns, we used the highly conserved imprinted genes (22 PEGs and 32 329 MEGs shared across all four crosses) to investigate the effects of methylation on

- 330 genomic imprinting. We found that methylation profiles of the conserved PEGs and their
- 331 non-imprinted homologs were similar in the embryo and endosperm, suggesting that

18

332 methylation has quite limited effects on imprinting of conserved PEGs **(Supplemental**  333 **Figure 10)**.

334 Many MEGs are not expressed in tissues besides the hypomethylated endosperm, while 335 PEGs are expressed broadly, and imprinting shows a limited association with DNA 336 methylation. Therefore, repression of DNA methylation may induce ectopic expression 337 of some MEGs. To test this hypothesis, we applied 5-Aza-2'-Deoxycytidine (AZA), a DNA 338 methylation inhibitor, to the seedlings of Kitaake (*O. sativa* spp. *japonica*) for 10 days. 339 Genome-wide transcriptome analysis showed that the expression of many imprinted 340 genes shared between the RL-LR and WY-YW crosses was disturbed as expected, with 341 the MEGs showing a different response to AZA in comparison to the PEGs **(Figure 5G and**  342 **Supplemental Figure 11A)**. The vast majority of PEGs exhibited few expression changes, 343 while approximately 60% of MEGs showed at least a 2-fold upregulation **(Supplemental**  344 **Figure 11A)**. We also noticed that many of the activated MEGs were not expressed in 345 seedlings under normal conditions **(Supplemental Figure 11B and D)**, implying that 346 decreased methylation caused ectopic expression of some MEGs.

347 Generally, imprinted genes with high expression in seedlings were not sensitive to AZA 348 **(Supplemental Figure 11C)**. We speculated that imprinting regulation of these genes 349 also may rely on PRC2-mideated histone modification or some other mechanisms, such 350 as chromatin structure. Malone et al. (2013) profiled H3K27me3 histone modification in 351 young 6-7 DAF rice endosperm. Using these data, we found that approximately 30% 352 (34/115) of the PEGs common to the RL-LR and WY-YW crosses coincided with 353 H3K27me3 peaks within the gene body or in the 2 kb flanking regions, whereas only 17% 354 (16/93) of MEGs coincided with H3K27me3 peaks **(Supplemental Table 5)**.

### 356 **Association of genomic imprinting with imprinted 24-nt small RNAs**

357 Previous studies suggested that small RNAs (sRNAs) may be involved in the regulation of 358 imprinting (Rodrigues et al., 2013; Pignatta et al., 2014; Yuan et al., 2017). Several

- 359 dozens of imprinted sRNAs have been found in rice (Rodrigues et al., 2013; Yuan et al.,
- 360 2017). Here, we found that more than 36% (28/77) of the imprinted sRNA loci co-localize
- 361 with the imprinted genes in rice **(Supplemental Table 6)**. Interestingly, the bias in the

362 direction of imprinted 24-nt sRNA loci was opposite to its neighboring imprinted genes 363 **(Supplemental Table 6)**, suggesting that paternally expressed sRNAs associate with 364 MEGs, and *vice versa*. To confirm this tendency, we investigated the parental expression 365 of the flanking genes of the imprinted 24-nt sRNAs. Forty-two of the 93 genes had 366 informative SNPs and showed expression in the endosperm. Our results showed that the 367 paternal-sRNA neighbors usually exhibited maternal expression bias (more than 80% of 368 transcripts were maternal), while maternal-sRNA neighbor genes displayed paternal 369 expression bias (fewer than 50% of transcripts were maternal) in comparison with the 370 whole genome **(Figure 6A and B)**. These results suggest that an imprinted 24-nt sRNA is 371 able to oppositely target its genic vicinity for imprinting.

372 We next compared the CHH methylation level of the imprinted genes that either 373 overlapped or did not overlap imprinted 24-nt sRNAs because 24-nt sRNAs are able to 374 promote CHH methylation through the RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) pathway. 375 The MEGs and PEGs around 24nt-sRNAs both showed a high CHH methylation level in 376 the endosperm **(Figure 6C and D, Supplemental Figure 12A)**, which is opposite to the 377 observation that imprinted genes showed a lower overall methylation level in the 378 endosperm **(Supplemental Figure 8)**. We also analyzed the CG and CHG methylation of 379 the imprinted genes neighboring imprinted 24-nt sRNAs. In comparison with the embryo, 380 these MEGs generally showed lower CG and CHG methylation in the endosperm. 381 However, the 24-nt sRNA-associated PEGs exhibited a similar CHG methylation level in 382 the endosperm and embryo **(Supplemental Figure 12B and C)**.

383 Notably, aside from the 24-nt sRNAs, all five of the imprinted 21-nt sRNAs showed 384 the same maternal expression bias as the MEGs with which they overlapped 385 **(Supplemental Table 6 and** Yuan et al., 2017), which likely formed imprinted clusters. 386 Moreover, the CHH methylation pattern of the 21-nt sRNA-associated imprinted genes 387 resembled the control **(Figure 6C)**.

#### 389 **Domestication shows limited influence on genomic imprinting**

390 Conservation of imprinting between cultivated rice and wild rice (*Oryza rufipogon*) 391 remains unknown. We made interspecific crosses between cultivated rice and Dongxiang 392 wild rice (designated as "D" hereafter). Using an RT-PCR sequencing approach, 13 of the 393 14 confirmed imprinted genes of cultivated rice **(Supplemental Figure 1)** also displayed 394 parent-of-origin expression patterns in these crosses **(Figure 7A and B)**. These results 395 suggest that the regulation of genomic imprinting is conserved between *O. sativa* and *O.*  396 *rufipogon*, and domestication likely has little consequences for the evolution of genomic 397 imprinting in rice.

398 We further investigated the demographic history of imprinted loci. Recent evidence 399 suggests that W1 type wild rice is closely related to *indica*, whereas the W2 type is closer 400 to *japonica* rice (Huang et al., 2012; Xu et al., 2012). Here we used πw1/π*in* and πw2/π*ja* to 401 reveal differences in nucleotide diversity (π) between wild rice and *indica* or *japonica*. In 402 *indica*, the πw1/π*in* of PEGs was significantly higher than that of the whole genome (*p* < 403 0.001, **Figure 7C**). However, for *japonica*, the imprinted genes showed no evidence of 404 being selected **(Figure 7D)**. We did not find differences between πw1/π*in* or πw2/π*ja* for 405 MEGs relative to the rest of the *indica* and *japonica* genome **(Figure 7C and D)**. Taken 406 together, we inferred that most of the imprinted genes were not selected during 407 domestication.

#### 433 **Discussion**

434 By generating reciprocal crosses of *indica* and *japonica* rice varieties, we identified 208 435 imprinted genes **(Supplemental Table 1)**. In the present study, we compared the rice 436 imprinted genes to those identified from other plant species. We found that 13–24% of 437 the rice imprinted genes had imprinted homologs in at least two of the species **(Figure**  438 **8A and Supplemental Table 7)**. This finding is consistent with previous estimates that 439 about 20% of imprinted genes are conserved between species (Waters et al., 2013; 440 Hatorangan et al., 2016). We randomly chose imprinted genes identified from at least 441 two species to detect the imprinting status of their most similar homologs in barley. 442 More than 70% of the genes with informative SNPs showed parent-of-origin expression 443 patterns **(Figure 8B)**, which revealed conservation of imprinting at some loci in 444 monocots. Klosinska et al. (2016) found that parentally biased expression patterns was 445 conserved between *Arabidopsis lyrata* and *A. thaliana*. Here we found that 13 out of the 446 14 imprinted candidates identified from cultivated rice also showed parent-of-origin 447 expression in the *O. sativa* and *O. rufipogon* interspecific reciprocal crosses **(Figure 7A**  448 **and B)**, indicating that the regulation of genomic imprinting in the two rice species is 449 conserved. The process of rice domestication likely imposed limited selection on the 450 evolution of imprinting, though PEGs tended to be selected during *indica* rice 451 domestication **(Figure 7C)**.

452 Allele-specific imprinting has been found in plants (Waters et al., 2013; Pignatta et 453 al., 2014). Here we discovered 70 imprinted genes showing parent-of-origin dependent 454 expression in certain genotypes but not in others **(Supplemental Table 3)**. As an example, 455 *OsEMF2a*, a PRC2 family member, was reported as a non-imprinted gene in 456 Niponbare/IR64 crosses (Luo et al., 2009). However, we showed that it is a maternally 457 expressed imprinted gene, either in RL-LR or WY-YW. This provided a case that 458 imprinting may change for some important genes in different crosses. Notably, the 459 *OsEMF2a* corresponding Su(z)12 member, *Fertilization Independent Seed2* (*FIS2*), in

460 Arabidopsis is also imprinted (Jullien et al., 2006).

461 Auxin is important for triggering central cell division and for maintaining endosperm 462 development (Bernardi et al., 2012; Figueiredo et al., 2015). *OsYUCCA11* of rice, 463 *ZmYUCCA1* of maize and *AtYUCCA10* of Arabidopsis are orthologs, and both were 464 identified as PEGs **(Supplemental Table 7)**. T*ryptophan aminotransferase related 1* 465 (*TAR1*), another auxin biosynthetic gene, was identified as a PEG in several plant species 466 **(Supplemental Table 7)**. *OsTAR1* and *OsYUCCA11* contribute to auxin accumulation 467 during rice grain development (Abu-Zaitoon et al., 2012). Likewise, *ZmYUCCA1* is 468 important for endosperm development of maize (Bernardi et al., 2012). These findings 469 indicate that genomic imprinting may have evolutionary significance and function 470 directly in auxin-mediated seed development. Xin et al. (2013) also found that many 471 auxin-related genes showed expression bias in maize. In addition, we noted that several 472 genes identified as PEGs in different plant species were associated with epigenetic 473 regulation, including chromatin-remodeling factors, histone H3K9 methyltransferase 474 genes, and *variant in methylation* genes **(Supplemental Table 7)**. For example, 475 Arabidopsis *SU(VAR)3-9* 

476 *Homolog 7* encodes a SET domain protein and acts as a histone methyltransferase for 477 the epigenetic control of gene expression, which is essential for establishing postzygotic 478 hybridization barriers (Wolff et al., 2015). Imprinting is conserved between orthologs of 479 rice and maize, which led us to hypothesize that these genes may contribute to 480 reproductive isolation in both monocots and dicots.

481 A preferential expression pattern of MEGs in the endosperm was observed in rice 482 **(Figure 4A and Supplemental Figure 6A)**, as well as in Arabidopsis, maize, and sorghum 483 (Wolff et al., 2011; Waters et al., 2013; Klosinska et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2016); 484 however, this preference was not observed for rice PEGs **(Figure 4A and Supplemental**  485 **Figure 6B)**. We inferred that MEGs may be more important for proper endosperm 486 development than PEGs. In agreement with this hypothesis, mutations in PEGs usually 487 were not accompanied by endosperm defects (Wolff et al., 2015), while many MEG 488 mutants exhibited endosperm or seed abnormalities (Chaudhury et al., 1997; Luo et al., 489 2000; Tiwari et al., 2008; Gerald et al., 2009; Costa et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2014). Rice 490 PEGs showed higher GC methylation of gene bodies in tissues other than endosperm 491 **(Supplemental Figure 8A)**, which is consistent with the findings that CG methylation of 492 gene bodies correlates with transcriptional activity (Zilberman et al., 2007).

493 Studies from *Brassicaceae* species revealed that many imprinted genes were often 494 physically located in the vicinity of TEs (Wolff et al., 2011; Pignatta et al., 2014; 495 Hatorangan et al., 2016). TEs were found to be activated in central cells, as well as in the 496 vegetative cells of pollen (Slotkin et al., 2009; Schoft et al., 2011; Calarco et al., 2012; 497 Ibarra et al., 2012). Genomic imprinting was believed to be associated with the dynamic 498 regulation of TEs in the reproductive cells and endosperm (Gehring et al., 2009). 499 Asymmetrical activation of TEs in the central and sperm cell leads to monoallelic 500 expression after fertilization. However, our study suggested that TEs are not more 501 abundant near imprinted rice genes **(Figure 3)**. Interestingly, rice PEGs showed a lower 502 association with TEs than MEGs **(Figure 3)**. This is contrary to the findings in Arabidopsis 503 (Pignatta et al., 2014). Unlike the gene silencing observed in Arabidopsis, many TEs are 504 not silenced in the sperm cells of rice (Anderson et al., 2013). We assumed that the 505 regulation of imprinting in rice may not be the same as that found in Arabidopsis. 506 Notably, in comparison to the PEGs, the promoters of MEGs showed higher methylation 507 in endosperm, which contrasts with findings from Arabidopsis and maize (Pignatta et al., 508 2014; Zhang et al., 2014b). However, this result is consistent with our observation that 509 the rice MEGs have a higher probability of neighboring with TEs in the promoter regions 510 than in the rice PEGs **(Figure 3)**. In agreement with previous findings in Arabidopsis and 511 maize (Zhang et al., 2014b; Moreno-Romero et al., 2016), our methylomic analysis and 512 AZA treatment revealed that activation of the MEGs showed more association with DNA 513 hypomethylation **(Figure 5)**, while H3K27me3 modification is more important for the 514 imprinting of PEGs **(Supplemental Figure 10)**. Further investigation of the differential 515 DNA methylation and histone modification between parental alleles is necessary.

516

517 Many MEGs are activated before fertilization in Arabidopsis. For example, the PRC2 518 components *MEA* and *FIS2* are highly expressed in central cells and gradually repressed 519 after fertilization (Luo et al., 2000; Baroux et al., 2006). The hypomethylated 520 environment of the central cell (Gehring et al., 2006; Jullien et al., 2006; Park et al., 2016) 521 promotes maternal allele expression in the endosperm, whereas the paternal allele 522 remains hypermethylated and silenced in the sperm cell and endosperm. According to 523 this model, MEG expression should persist immediately after fertilization. However, we 524 found many rice MEGs that were activated specifically at 3–4 DAF in the endosperm 525 **(Figure 4B)**. As an example, the PRC2 component gene *OsFIE1* is an exclusively 526 endosperm-expressed gene (Zhang et al., 2012a), but *OsFIE1* was not detectable until 3 527 DAF **(Supplemental Figure 6D)**. Although we do not have evidence showing *OsFIE1* to be 528 absent in central cells, our findings indicated that regulation of imprinting in *OsFIE1* is 529 distinct from *MEA* and *FIS2* in Arabidopsis. Extensive activation of imprinted genes after 530 fertilization was also observed in maize (Xin et al., 2013). Arabidopsis MEG *Formin5* was 531 activated specifically in the endosperm (Gerald et al., 2009). These findings suggest that 532 for some MEGs, differential imprinting between two parental alleles is expressed in the 533 endosperm but not germ cells. Xing et al. (2015) found a dramatic decrease in global 534 DNA methylation in the endosperm of rice from 2 to 3 DAF. The methylation level of 535 endosperm is even lower than that of the central cell (Park et al., 2016). Imprinting of 536 these genes could be caused by asymmetric DNA demethylation in the endosperm, or 537 the regulator of these genes is expressed only after fertilization.

538 We also noticed that several MEGs were expressed in sperm cells **(Supplemental**  539 **Figure 7)**, but their paternal alleles were silenced after fertilization. In addition to the 540 establishment of imprinting in the central cell, this finding suggests that plants can apply 541 selective imprinting markers to the paternal genome to repress gene expression after 542 fertilization. The underlying mechanism needs further investigation. We found that >50% 543 of PEGs were expressed in sperm cells **(Figure 4A)**. Anderson et al. (2013) showed low 544 expression of *Methyltransferase 1* and high expression of *ROS1a*, a DNA glycosylase 545 reversing DNA methylation, in rice sperm cells. These results suggested that similar to 546 what happens in central cells, the hypomethylated environment in the sperm cell 547 facilitates paternal monoallelic expression of PEGs in the endosperm. The RdDM 548 pathway regulates paternal genomic imprinting in Arabidopsis (Vu et al., 2013). However, 549 by surveying the transcriptome, Anderson et al. (2013) revealed no evidence for RNA 550 interference activity in sperm cells of rice. Nonetheless, our result indicates that the 551 24-nt sRNAs may be involved in the regulation of genomic imprinting in rice 552 **(Supplemental Table 6)**. The genic vicinities of imprinted sRNAs generally showed 553 opposite parental expression patterns relative to the imprinted sRNAs **(Figure 7A and B)**.

554 Several imprinted genes found in rice have been functionally studied. Among them, 555 *GW2* and *OsPPKL2* are characterized as two quantitative trait loci that determine seed 556 size in rice, and they also function in grain filling and starch accumulation (Song et al., 557 2007; Zhang et al., 2012b). *Gibberellin-Regulated Calcineurin B-like 2* of rice is involved in 558 the development of the aleurone layer (Hwang et al., 2005). Notably, *Os8N3*, which 559 shows copper reallocation activity, was identified initially as a disease-resistance gene 560 and then found to be essential for pollen and seed development (Yang et al., 2006; Yuan 561 et al., 2010). The *Carbon Starved Anther* gene triggers the expression of sugar 562 partitioning and metabolic genes during pollen and seed development (Zhang et al., 563 2010). These genes appear to be involved in the reallocation of resources and nutrients 564 from maternal tissues to offspring, which provides support for the kinship theory and 565 suggests that at least some imprinted genes are indispensable for proper seed 566 development. Interestingly, activation of many imprinted genes in the endosperm at 3–4 567 DAF **(Figure 4B and Supplemental Figure 6)** coincides with the cellularization event in 568 rice (Ishikawa et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2016). Genetic evidence showed that rice *MADS*  569 *box gene 87*, a transiently activated MEG, can promote endosperm proliferation and 570 repress its cellularization (Ishikawa et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2016). In contrast, the MEG 571 *OsFIE1* promotes cellularization (Folsom et al., 2014). In conclusion, many imprinted 572 genes in rice appear to have biological significance for rice seed development (Yuan et 573 al., 2017).

#### 577 **Plant materials and growth conditions**

578 Wufeng-A and -B, Rongfeng-A and -B, Liuqianxin-A and -B, Yu6-A and -B, and wild rice 579 were grown in Nanchang, Jiangxi Province, China. Wild rice typically is an outcrossing 580 plant. Therefore, to avoid variation within the genome, Dongxiang wild rice was 581 self-fertilized by bagging for six generations and then used for crossing. The RL-LR and 582 WY-YW reciprocal crosses were performed in the summer of 2014. Plants were grown in 583 a paddy field under regular conditions. The ovary donors were emasculated before 584 flowering and moved to a growth chamber on a 14 h/10 h light-dark cycle at 30°C. At 5 585 DAF, the endosperm was carefully collected with a pipette to avoid maternal tissue 586 contamination per the method used by Luo et al. (2011). Two (WY-YW) or three (LR-RL) 587 replicates were used for mining of imprinted genes. Each replicate was pooled with 20– 588 30 endosperm. *Japonica* varieties Nipponbare and Kitaake were grown in a growth 589 chamber (14 h/10 h light-dark cycle, 30°C) and labeled when flowering. The caryopsis at 590 0 to 3 DAF or endosperm at 5 to 7 DAF were collected for RNA isolation. Barley varieties 591 Morex and Bowman were planted in an experimental plot in Chengdu, Sichuan Province, 592 China, with regular nutrient and water management. Reciprocal crosses were performed 593 in the field. The ovary donors were emasculated before flowering. At 10 DAF, the 594 endosperm of the hybrid seeds was collected.

595 For the 5-aza-2'-deoxycytidine treatment assay, Kitaake seeds were sterilized and grown on 1/2 MS medium with 70 mg L−1 596 AZA for 10 days in a growth chamber at 28°C 597 and a 14 h/10 h light-dark cycle. The controls were grown under the same conditions, 598 except the medium was AZA free. Seedlings were collected for RNA-seq. Three biological 599 replicates were used, each one comprising approximately 10 individuals.

#### 601 **DNA and RNA isolation and quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay**

602 DNA was extracted from 100 mg of leaves from Liuqianxin-A, Yu6-A, RR, and WW plants

603 using the EasyPure Plant Genomic DNA Kit (TRANS) following the manufacturer's 604 suggested procedures. RNA was extracted from endosperm or seedlings using a Plant 605 RNA Kit (OMEGA) and treated with a RNA-free DNase set (OMEGA) to remove DNA 606 contamination per the manufacturer's protocol.

607 Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix 608 (TAKARA). Two microliters of the 1:5 diluted cDNA was used for qPCR in a 20 μl reaction 609 using AceQ® qPCR SYBR® Green Master Mix (Vazyme). The reactions were performed using the CFX ConnectTM 610 Real-Time System (BioRad). The proteasome gene 611 (LOC\_Os03g63430) was used as an endogenous control. For each sample, we performed 612 three independent biological replicates with three technical replicates each. Each 613 biological replicate was pooled with 20–30 endosperm. The relative expression level was 614 calculated by the ΔΔCt approach. All primers used in the present study can be found in 615 **Supplemental Table 8**.

#### 617 **DNA resequencing, RNA-seq, and imprinted gene identification**

Libraries were sequenced on the 624 Illumina HiSeq platform.

Filtered reads were aligned to the rice reference 627 genome (MSU v7.0, rice.plantbiology.msu.edu) using bowtie2. Two mismatches were 628 allowed for alignments. Reads that mapped to multiple sites were discarded. GATK was 629 used to process aligned reads for SNPs calling. The obtained SNPs were filtered by

### 647 **Epigenetic analysis of imprinted genes**

648 Rice bisulfite-sequencing data from root, shoot, embryo, and endosperm were obtained 649 from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (Accession: GSE22591). #### 670 **Characterization of imprinted genes**
