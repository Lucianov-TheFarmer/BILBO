# Targeted reprogramming of H3K27me3 resets epigenetic memory in plant paternal chromatin

Michael Borg<sup>1</sup>, Yannick Jacob<sup>2,3</sup>, Daichi Susaki<sup>1</sup>, Chantal LeBlanc<sup>3</sup>, Daniel Buendía<sup>1</sup>, Elin Axelsson<sup>1</sup>, Tomokazu Kawashima<sup>1,5</sup>, Philipp Voigt<sup>6</sup>, Leonor Boavida<sup>7,8</sup>, Jörg Becker<sup>7</sup>, Tetsuya Higashiyama<sup>4</sup>, Robert Martienssen<sup>2</sup> and Frédéric Berger<sup>1</sup>

Epigenetic marks are reprogrammed in the gametes to reset genomic potential in the next generation. In mammals, paternal chromatin is extensively reprogrammed through the global erasure of DNA methylation and the exchange of histones with protamines<sup>1,2</sup>. Precisely how the paternal epigenome is reprogrammed in flowering plants has remained unclear since DNA is not demethylated and histones are retained in sperm<sup>3,4</sup>. Here, we describe a multi-layered mechanism by which H3K27me3 is globally lost from histone-based sperm chromatin in Arabidopsis. This mechanism involves the silencing of H3K27me3 writers, activity of H3K27me3 erasers and deposition of a sperm-specific histone, H3.10 (ref. 5), which we show is immune to lysine 27 methylation. The loss of H3K27me3 facilitates the transcription of genes essential for spermatogenesis and pre-configures sperm with a chromatin state that forecasts gene expression in the next generation. Thus, plants have evolved a specific mechanism to simultaneously differentiate male gametes and reprogram the paternal epigenome.

Stable inheritance of epigenetic marks during cellular proliferation is essential for maintaining cell identity and involves DNA replication-coupled mechanisms<sup>6</sup>. At the DNA replication fork, old histones from the mother cell are recycled and deposited onto nascent strands of daughter cell chromatin<sup>7,8</sup>. This mode of inheritance ensures faithful propagation of the repressive epigenetic mark H3K27me3 across cell divisions in animals<sup>8–10</sup>. In plants, the transmission of H3K27me3-silenced states additionally involves the replication-dependent histone variant H3.1 (ref. <sup>11</sup>). Histone H3.1 differs from H3.3 by four amino acid residues, with variation at residue 31 responsible for conferring selective mono-methylation at lysine 27 (K27) on H3.1 over H3.3 (ref. <sup>12</sup>). This plant-specific feature of H3.1 facilitates Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) function, ensuring efficient maintenance of H3K27me3 domains that maintain the silencing of developmental genes<sup>11</sup>.

In plants, H3K27me3 is also required for developmental phase transitions<sup>13</sup>. For example, flowering in *Arabidopsis thaliana* is initiated by the establishment and maintenance of H3K27me3 at the floral repressor *FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC)*<sup>13</sup>, which is facilitated by H3.1 deposition during DNA replication<sup>11</sup>. H3K27me3-mediated silencing at *FLC* is eventually reset in the next generation to ensure transcriptional re-activation in the early embryo<sup>14,15</sup>. This suggests

that H3K27me3 is reprogrammed before or during early plant embryogenesis, although the mechanism and timing of H3K27me3 resetting is not known. In animals, epigenetic reprogramming initiates in the germline and culminates with histone-to-protamine exchange in the sperm of most species, globally erasing histone marks<sup>4</sup>. Flowering plants never undergo protamine exchange and instead retain histone-based chromatin in sperm (Fig. 1a)<sup>4</sup>. Moreover, in contrast with mammals<sup>2</sup>, DNA methylation is not globally erased from flowering plant sperm<sup>3</sup>, so whether or not the paternal epigenome undergoes reprogramming has remained unclear. There is some evidence that H3K27me3 is reduced in male reproductive tissues<sup>16-18</sup>, but whether H3K27me3 is reprogrammed globally or together with other histone marks is not known, nor is it clear when and how this occurs.

To investigate this, we first examined the status of H3K27me3 in tricellular pollen of *A. thaliana*, where two sperm are encased within a companion vegetative cell (Fig. 1b)<sup>4</sup>. H3K27me3 was barely detectable in sperm chromatin but was enriched in the companion cell nucleus (Fig. 1c). H3K27ac and H3K27me1 marks were appreciable in sperm (Fig. 1d,e), confirming that the absence of H3K27me3 was not a technical artefact. During pollen development, the sperm lineage arises from a progenitor microspore and becomes separated from its sister companion cell by two rounds of replication (Fig. 1b). H3K27me3 was present in the microspore (Fig. 1f) and probably inherited through meiosis<sup>18</sup>. H3K27me3 was also present in daughter nuclei following microspore division but was barely detected in sperm (Fig. 1f). These observations pointed to a mechanism that specifically removes H3K27me3 during sperm differentiation.

In contrast with proliferating somatic cells, sperm chromatin was devoid of the replicative histone variant H3.1 (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig.  $1a-d)^5$ . In addition to the non-replicative histone variant H3.3 (Extended Data Fig.  $1e,f)^5$ , which can carry K27 tri-methylation<sup>19</sup>, sperm chromatin incorporates the sperm-specific histone variant H3.10 (Fig.  $2a,b)^{4,5,20}$ . H3.10 deposition initiated in the nascent sperm precursor and continued as sperm differentiated and matured (Fig. 2b). H3.10 is highly divergent around K27 (Fig. 2c), although this did not impact recognition by antibodies directed against mono- or trimethylated H3.10K27 peptides (Extended Data Fig. 2a-c). We thus hypothesized that H3.10 might be immune to K27 methylation and that its deposition would prevent inheritance of H3K27me3 during sperm development.

We performed in vitro histone lysine methyltransferase (HLM) assays and found that ATXR5/6 and PRC2 were unable to methylate K27 on recombinant H3.10 nucleosomes, in contrast with H3.1 (Fig. [2d](#page-3-0)). Consistently, HA-tagged H3.10 ectopically expressed in planta was not methylated by endogenous H3K27 methyltransferase activity (Fig. [2e\)](#page-3-0). H3.10 was also unable to complement the lethal phenotype of a null H3.3 knockout[22](#page-8-12) (Extended Data Fig. 2d), further highlighting its distinct properties. Hence, H3.10 is unable to carry K27 methylation, suggesting that its incorporation in sperm would cause loss of H3K27me3. Consistently, we observed moderate but significantly increased H3K27me3 levels in *htr10* sperm compared with wild-type sperm (*P*=5.0×10<sup>−</sup>10) (Fig. [1g](#page-1-0)). Orthologous sperm-specific histone H3 variants that harbour mutations at or around K27 are present in *Arabidopsis* relatives and distantly related monocots (Fig. [2c](#page-3-0)), suggesting that flowering plants evolved this unique class of sperm-specific H3 variants to selectively reprogram H3K27me3.

To assess genomic H3.10 distribution in sperm, we developed and validated a low-input chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-Seq) protocol to profile sperm chromatin in *Arabidopsis* (Extended Data Fig. 3a–f). H3.10 deposition was uniform across the genome but relatively depleted from pericentromeric regions enriched with H3K27me1 (Extended Data Fig. 3b,c). This probably reflected delayed replication of heterochromatin since sperm remain suspended in mid-S-phase until the onset of pollinatio[n23.](#page-8-13) The broad H3K27me3 domains that coat developmentally regulated genes in somatic tissues were no longer evident in sperm but were instead relatively enriched with H3.10 (Fig. [3a–d\)](#page-4-0). Regions enriched with H3K4me3 and H3K27ac in somatic tissues were also enriched for H3.10 (Fig. [3e,f\)](#page-4-0), emphasizing how H3.10 deposition occurs genome wide and not only at somatic H3K27me3 domains. A small fraction of H3K27me3 peaks were retained in sperm (Fig. [3g](#page-4-0) and Extended Data Fig. 3g–i), which were much shorter than H3K27me3 domains in somatic tissues (Fig. [3h](#page-4-0)). Notably, H3.10 was depleted over these H3K27me3 peaks in sperm (Fig. [3i,j\)](#page-4-0). Together, these genomic data support the notion that H3.10 deposition and parental histone replacement contribute to global reprogramming of H3K27me3 in sperm.

Because *htr10* mutant plants do not show morphological or fertility defects in sper[m20](#page-8-10), we speculated that genetically redundant mechanisms also operate to remove H3K27me3 during sperm development. Aside from histone replacement, loss of an epigenetic mark can result from a lack of specific writers or enhanced activity of erasers. We thus investigated the expression of methyltransferase subunits of PRC2. In *Arabidopsis*, these writers are encoded by three paralogues—*CURLY LEAF* (*CLF*), *MEDEA* (*MEA*) and *SWINGER* (*SWN*)[13](#page-8-4)—all of which were undetectable during sperm development (Fig. [1h](#page-1-0) and Extended Data Fig. 4a–c). A similar lack of expression was observed for other components of PRC2 (Extended Data Fig. 4d–g) as well as most PRC1 subunits (Extended Data Fig. 4g). The failure of SWN to accumulate after ectopic expression in sperm suggested active PRC2 turnover (Extended Data Fig. 4h). In contrast, expression of the Polycomb machinery was unaltered in egg cells (Fig. [1h](#page-1-0) and Extended Data Fig. 4g). These observations suggest that Polycomb activity is specifically compromised during spermatogenesis.

Next, we asked whether the loss of H3K27me3 might also involve active demethylation, as implicated in the epigenetic resetting of *FLC*[14](#page-8-5). Three closely related Jumonji-C family (JMJ) H3K27 demethylases—*EARLY FLOWERING 6* (*ELF6*), *RELATIVE OF ELF6* (*REF6*) and *JUMONJI 13* (*JMJ13*[\)24—](#page-8-14)were all expressed in sperm, with JMJ13 being particularly enriched (Fig. [1h](#page-1-0)). In somatic tissues, H3K27 demethylases occupy the border of H3K27me3 domains in a pattern mutually exclusive with PRC2 (ref. [25\)](#page-8-15). Given the absence of PRC2 in sperm (Extended Data Fig. 4a–g), H3K27me3 demethylation by JMJ proteins is expected to occur globally. Consistently, *elf6 ref6 jmj13* mutant sperm showed increased levels of H3K27me3 compared with wild type (Fig. [1g,i](#page-1-0)), confirming that active demethylation by JMJ proteins also contributes to paternal H3K27me3 resetting.

JMJ proteins in plants and animals are known to demethylate di- and trimethyl H3K27 but not H3K27me1 (Extended Data Fig. 4i[\)26](#page-8-16)[–28.](#page-8-17) If H3.10 were to replace a large fraction of H3K27me3 in sperm, the H3K27me3 retained upon H3.10 depletion should be converted to H3K27me1 by JMJ demethylase activity. Consistently, *htr10* sperm showed high levels of H3K27me1 as well as de novo accumulation of co-localizing H3K27me1 and H3K27me3 peaks (Fig. [3k–n](#page-4-0)). H3K27me1 accumulation is unlikely to originate from mono-methylation by ATXR5/6 because its only substrate, H3.1 (ref. [12\)](#page-8-3), is not expressed in wild type, nor in *htr10* sperm (Fig. [2f](#page-3-0) and Extended Data Fig. 1a–d). The increased H3K27me1 levels in *htr10* sperm are thus expected to reflect the activity of JMJ demethylases, which remain expressed in *htr10* sperm (Extended Data Fig. 5a–c). Consistently, we observed a reduction in H3K27me1 levels but an increase in H3K27me3 in quadruple *elf6 ref6 jmj13htr10* mutant sperm (Fig. [1g,j\)](#page-1-0). Somatic Polycomb-silenced genes also showed enriched expression in the endosperm (Fig. [4d\)](#page-5-0), which arises from a second fertilization event of the central cell in flowering plant[s31.](#page-8-20) Genomic imprinting is a hallmark of the endosperm, resulting in genes with distinct epigenetic signatures and biased expression depending on their parent of origi[n31](#page-8-20),[32.](#page-8-21) Unlike maternally expressed imprinted genes (MEGs), paternally expressed imprinted genes (PEGs) were preferentially silenced by Polycomb in somatic tissues (Fig. [5a,b](#page-6-0) and Supplementary Table 4). This is consistent with previous reports of H3K27me3 marking PEGs in somatic tissue[s33](#page-8-22), which in sperm is mirrored by specific enrichment of DNA methylation on MEGs (Extended Data Fig. 7c). In sperm, loss of H3K27me3 was accompanied by a gain of H3K4me3 around the transcription start site of PEGs but not MEGs, despite most PEGs remaining untranscribed (Fig. [5c–f](#page-6-0) and Extended Data Fig. 7d). Our data suggest that the paternal allele of PEGs is inherited in a transcriptionally primed state free of H3K27me3 and enriched in H3K4me3, forecasting paternally biased expression in the endosperm. In contrast, no major loss of H3K27me3 is expected on maternal alleles since PRC2 is active in the central cell[13.](#page-8-4) This mechanism mirrors H3K27me3-dependent genomic imprinting in mice embryos where paternal allele-specific expression is caused by paternal loss and maternal persistence of H3K27me3 in the sperm and egg, respectivel[y34](#page-8-23).

Similarly, genes with paternally biased expression in early zygotes[35](#page-8-24) were significantly enriched among the reprogrammed loci in sperm, unlike genes with a maternal bias (Fig. [5g\)](#page-6-0). Among the H3K4me3-primed genes were *BABY BOOM* (*BBM*) (Fig. [3b](#page-4-0)) and *SHORT SUSPENSOR* (*SSP*) (Fig. [4e](#page-5-0)), which encode paternally derived factors that trigger and pattern plant embryogenesis, respectivel[y36](#page-8-25),[37.](#page-8-26) In contrast, unprimed genes that did not accumulate H3K4me3 (Fig. [4a](#page-5-0) (cluster 3) and Supplementary Table 1) were less likely to be transcribed throughout early embryogenesis (Fig. [4b\)](#page-5-0), had lower levels of expression overall (Fig. [5h\)](#page-6-0) and were enriched for tissue-specific regulators of post-embryonic development (Fig. [4a](#page-5-0) (grey labels) and Supplementary Table 5). Priming with H3K4me3 in sperm extended to several other Polycomb targets expressed in the early embryo. These included BBM targets *LEAFY COTYLEDON 1* (*LEC1*), *LEC2* and *FUSCA3* (*FUS3*)[38](#page-8-27) and the LEC1 target *FLOWERING LOCUS C* (*FLC)*[15](#page-8-6) (Figs. [3c,](#page-4-0)[4a](#page-5-0) (red labels) and [5i](#page-6-0) and Extended Data Fig. 7b). Several other key regulators of embryo patterning were also primed, including *WUSCHEL* (*WUS*), *WUSCHEL-RELATED HOMEBOX 2* (*WOX2*), *WOX5*, *WOX8* and *WOX9* (Figs. [4a](#page-5-0) (red labels) and [5i,](#page-6-0) Extended Data Fig. 7b and Supplementary Table 1[\)39.](#page-8-28) Thus, the pattern of H3K27me3 replacement with H3K4me3 in sperm is predictive of gene expression during early plant development.

In conclusion, flowering plants employ a multi-layered mechanism to globally and specifically remove H3K27me3 from histone-based sperm chromatin (Fig. [5j](#page-6-0)). Epigenetic resetting of H3K27me3 facilitates sperm differentiation and has the potential to influence the transcription of genes required in the next generation, including key regulators of embryogenesis, endosperm development and flowering, in addition to as-yet-uncharacterized loci transcribed post-fertilization. Pre-configured chromatin poised with H3K4me3, which is associated with transcription[40](#page-8-29), would assist the rapid zygotic genome activation typical of flowering plants[41](#page-8-30)[,42.](#page-8-31) Whether the bivalent marks detected on residual histones in mouse and human sperm directly impact transcription during early embryogenesis has been debated[43–](#page-8-32)[46.](#page-8-33) This is probably more plausible in species with histone-based sperm chromatin, such as zebrafish[47](#page-8-34) and *Caenorhabditis elegans*[48,](#page-8-35) with the latter requiring paternally derived H3K27me3 for normal offspring development[49](#page-8-36). Our findings suggest that paternal resetting of H3K27me3 and inheritance of H3K4me3-primed genes has the potential to impact transcription after double fertilization in flowering plants, either directly or indirectly. Our findings also imply that germline transmission of H3K27me3 occurs maternally in flowering plants, as shown in animal model species[46](#page-8-33)[,50.](#page-8-37)

The mechanistic insights we describe represent a mode of reprogramming whereby cell type-specific deposition of an atypical histone variant, together with active H3K27 demethylation and lack of PRC2 activity, reprograms a single epigenetic mark (Fig. [5j](#page-6-0)). Restricted divergence around K27 ensures replication-dependent deposition of H3.10 while simultaneously preventing H3K27me3 inheritance without affecting other histone H3 modifications. Global reprogramming of a single epigenetic mark in plant sperm contrasts with the near-complete exchange of histones with protamine in animal sper[m46](#page-8-33) or with H3K27me3 retention in animal species with histone-based sperm[29](#page-8-18). Several as-yet-uncharacterized histone variants exist across eukaryotes that have tissue-specific expression and that are predicted to be poor substrates for chromatin modifiers[4](#page-7-3),[51,](#page-8-38) suggesting that aspects of the mechanism operating during *Arabidopsis* spermatogenesis extends to other species and developmental contexts.

ProDUO1:SWN-Clover was also generated using MultiSite Gateway Technology. The SWN cDNA without a stop codon was recombined upstream of Clover and downstream of the DUO1 promoter into pAlligatorR43 (ref. [57\)](#page-11-5). The SWN cDNA was amplified from flower cDNA using the primers SWN-attB1-F and SWN-attB2-nsR and cloned into pDONR221. Entry clones for the DUO1 promoter[58](#page-11-6) and Clover[57](#page-11-5) were described previously. **CRISPR–Cas9-mediated generation of the** *htr10* **mutant allele.** A CRISPR– Cas9 vector pHEE401[E59](#page-11-7) containing two tandem single-guide RNA (sgRNA) cassettes targeting the *HTR10* locus were used to generate a null *htr10* mutant allele in the Col-0 background. sgRNA sequences targeting *HTR10* were 5′-g GAGCCCTCTTGACTCCACCA-3′ and 5′-g TCGTTGGAAAGGCAATTTG-3′, respectively. T2 plants were screened for lesions at the *HTR10* locus using Sanger sequencing. A 4-base pair (bp) deletion was identified at the predicted cut site for the first sgRNA, which resulted in a frame shift and premature stop codon. Cas9-free T3 seeds were selected by testing batches of seeds for Hygromycin sensitivity. The chosen allele was back-crossed to wild type and the resulting F2 generation was screened for a homozygous *htr10* line using Sanger sequencing.

**Cytological analysis of fluorescent marker lines.** Marker lines were analysed for expression during pollen development, as described previousl[y60](#page-11-8). Floral buds were staged based on their position on the floral axis, with the first open flower (+1) being the mature pollen stage and subsequent younger, closed buds (–1 to –13) containing earlier stages of pollen development. Under our growth conditions, tricellular spores were found in anthers of –1 to –4 buds, bicellular spores were found within –4 to –8 buds and microspores were found in –9 to −13 buds. Spores were released into 0.3M mannitol by slicing open anthers with a hypodermic needle on a microscope slide and imaged immediately. Mannitol allows imaging of fluorescent marker lines under live cellular conditions. 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining is avoided since it creates detergent-induced artefacts, particularly at earlier stages of pollen development. Developmental stages were determined using brightfield microscopy. Microspores are distinguished by a single large nucleus with a prominent nucleolus. Bicellular stages are clearly identifiable due to the membrane of the encased germ (or sperm precursor) cell. At the mid-bicellular stage, the germ cell has a regular spherical shape but elongates to form a distinct tail during late bicellular stages. All marker line analyses were performed using a ZEISS LSM 700 laser scanning confocal microscope.

**Nuclear and whole-mount immunostaining.** Immunostaining of mature pollen nuclei was performed as described previousl[y61.](#page-11-9) Open flowers were collected into a 1.5-ml microfuge tube and pollen was washed out by vortexing in 1ml of ice-cold Galbraith buffer (45mM MgCl2, 30mM trisodium citrate, 20mM MOPS and 1% Triton X-100 (pH7.0)[\)62](#page-11-10) containing ß-mercaptoethanol and 1× complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The pollen suspension was filtered through a 100-µm nylon mesh then vortexed with 500-µm glass beads to disrupt pollen and release nuclei. The resulting suspension was filtered through a 10-µm mesh to remove pollen wall debris. Nuclei were immediately fixed with the addition of formaldehyde to a final concentration of 4% for 30min on ice. The suspension of fixed nuclei was spotted onto glass slides and allowed to dry at room temperature. Antibody incubation and wash steps were automated using an InsituPro VSi staining system (Intavis). Histone modifications were detected using anti-H3K27ac (Abcam; ab4729; 1:100 dilution), anti-H3K27me1 (Millipore; 17-643; 1:100 dilution), anti-H3K27me3 (Millipore; 07-449; 1:100 dilution) and anti-H3 (Abcam; ab195277; 1:500 dilution). DNA was labelled with DAPI supplied in Vectashield mounting medium (Vector Laboratories). Images were taken using a Zeiss LSM 700 laser scanning confocal microscope.

Whole-mount immunostaining of early pollen stages was adapted from a procedure described previously[63.](#page-11-11) Unopened floral buds were collected and ground gently in ice-cold Pollen Isolation Buffer (500mM glucose, 500mM sorbitol, 1mM KNO3, 0.2mM KH2PO4, 1mM MgSO4, 1 µM KI, 0.1 µM CuSO4, 5mM CaCl2 and 5mM MES, adjusted to pH5.8 with Tris-Cl). The resulting crude spore suspension was filtered through a 100-µm nylon mesh and centrifuged at 500*g* for 5min at 4 °C to collect spores. The resulting pellet was resuspended in ice-cold Pollen Isolation Buffer, filtered through a 15-µm nylon mesh and collected by centrifugation again. The spores were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 30min at 4 °C with gentle rotation. After washing twice with 1× PBS for 10min, the spores were embedded in 15% acrylamide, spotted onto polylysine-coated slides and allowed to set under a cover slip. The cover slip was removed with a razor blade and the spore walls were digested by the addition of 200mgml<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> Driselase solution (from *Basidomycetes*; Sigma–Aldrich) and incubated at 37 °C for 3h in a humid chamber. The specimen was washed three times with 1× PBS for 10min and permeabilized with a solution of 1% Triton X-100 and 10% DMSO in 1× PBS at 37 °C for 1h in a humid chamber. H3K27me3 modifications were detected using anti-H3K27me3 (Millipore; 07-449; 1:100 dilution). DNA was labelled by mounting the acrylamide slice in DAPI solution (1µgml<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> DAPI, 0.5× PBS and 5% DMSO). Images were taken using a Zeiss LSM 700 laser scanning confocal microscope.

**Quantification of H3K27me1 and H3K27me3 levels.** For quantification of H3K27me1 and H3K27me3 levels in the mutants, double nuclear immunostaining was performed as described above using anti-H3K27me1 (Millipore; 17-643) or anti-H3K27me3 (Millipore; 07-449) and anti-H3 (Abcam; ab195277), respectively. Alexa 488 anti-rabbit and Alexa 555 anti-mouse were used as secondary antibodies. Images of nuclei were randomly captured for each genotype under standardized conditions on the same day using a Zeiss Axio Imager Z2 fluorescence microscope. Both green fluorescent protein (GFP) and red fluorescent protein (RFP) fluorescence images were captured at the same plane using 63× magnification. Images were processed and quantified using a custom script in ImageJ. After background subtraction, the mean H3K27me1 or H3K27me3 fluorescence was divided by the mean H3 fluorescence, then multiplied by the area of the respective nucleus to determine total normalized fluorescence levels. The data were scaled by dividing all of the values by the mean H3K27me3 fluorescence of wild-type vegetative cell nuclei.

**Western blot.** Acid extracts from 14-d-old *Arabidopsis* leaf nuclei were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a 0.2-μm nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare). To detect histone modifications on histone H3.10 expressed in somatic tissues, 3×HA-tagged HTR10 was expressed in *htr4htr5htr8/+* mutant background under the control of the HTR5 promoter. Because 3×HA-tagged H3.10 migrates slower than endogenous histone H3, the two proteins can be distinguished by their mobility and subsequent size. Histones were probed with anti-H3K4me3 (Abcam; ab8580; 1:1,000 dilution), anti-H3K27me1 (Millipore; 17-643; 1:1,000 dilution), anti-H3K27me3 (Millipore, 07-449, 1:1,000 dilution) and anti-HA (Roche; 11867423001; 1:5,000 dilution) antibodies. Blots were developed with a Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged using a ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

**Histone lysine methyltransferase (HLM) assays.** The protocols for expression and purification of the enzymes (ATXR5, ATXR6, CLF and MEA) used in the HLM assays were described previousl[y12](#page-8-3)[,64.](#page-11-12) A detailed description for preparing the recombinant nucleosomes used in the HLM assays was also described in a previous publicatio[n65](#page-11-13), except for slight modifications regarding the histone H3 proteins present in the nucleosomes used in this study. H3.1 (AT1G09200) and H3.10 (AT1G19890) were PCR amplified from *Arabidopsis* genomic DNA and cDNA, respectively, and cloned into pET3 for expression in *Escherichia coli*. Site-directed mutagenesis (Agilent Technologies) of the pET3-H3.1 construct was performed to create the pET3-H3.10K27A expression construct. Finally, protocols for performing the radioactivity-based HLM assays presented in this study were previously described in multiple publication[s12](#page-8-3)[,66.](#page-11-14)

#### **Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS).** Dry pollen from a

ProHTR10:HTR10-Clover marker line was harvested using a vacuum suction metho[d67](#page-11-15). Sperm nuclei were isolated by FACS using a method described previously[68.](#page-11-16) The pollen was hydrated by resuspension in ice-cold Galbraith buffer (with 0.1% Triton X-100[\)62](#page-11-10) supplemented with 10mM ß-mercaptoethanol and 1× complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). For isolation of intact sperm cells for RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) analysis, Sperm Cell Extraction Buffer (1.3mM H3BO3, 3.6mM CaCl2, 0.74mM KH2PO4, 438mM sucrose, 0.83mM MgSO4 and 7mM MOPS (pH6.0)) was used instead of Galbraith buffer. After gentle rotation at room temperature for ~15min, 500-μm glass beads were added to the pollen suspension and vortexed vigorously to disrupt the tough pollen walls. Pollen wall

debris were removed by filtering the suspension through a 10-μm nylon mesh. The resulting crude suspension was immediately subjected to FACS on a FACSAria III cell sorter. The sorter was run in standard configuration, using a 70-μm ceramic nozzle with 1× PBS running at a constant pressure of 20psi. A 488-nm blue laser was used to excite Clover and detected with a 530/30nm bandpass filter.

**RNA-Seq analysis.** RNA-Seq libraries were generated from total RNA of mature pollen and FACS-isolated sperm cells using Smart-seq2 (ref. [69\)](#page-11-17) with independent biological replicates. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 using 50-bp single-end reads and 125-bp paired-end reads for pollen and sperm, respectively. RNA-Seq libraries of egg cells were isolated from a ProDD45:mtKaede marker lin[e70](#page-11-18). Unfertilized ovules were treated with enzyme solution (1% cellulase (Worthington), 0.3% macerozyme R-10 (Yakult), 0.05% pectolyase (Kyowa Kasei) and 0.45M mannitol (pH7.0)) and released egg cell protoplasts were collected using PicoPipet (NepaGene) with a glass capillary and a micromanipulator (Narishige) under an inverted IX-71 (Olympus) microsop[e71.](#page-11-19) Messenger RNA was extracted from around 10–18 egg cells using a Dynabeads mRNA DIRECT Micro Kit (Invitrogen) and amplified using an Ovation RNA-Seq System V2 (NuGEN). RNA-Seq libraries were prepared using a TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit and a Multiplexing Sample Preparation Oligonucleotide Kit (Illumina). The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina GA2x (Illumina) using 36-bp single-end reads.

Adapter trimming was performed using TrimGalore version 0.4.1 [\(https://](https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) [github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore\)](https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore). Reads were aligned to the *Arabidopsis* genome (TAIR10) using the STAR aligner version 2.5.2[a72.](#page-11-20) Transcripts per million (TPM) values were generated using Kallisto version 0.43.1 (ref. [73\)](#page-11-21) with an index built on TAIR10 cDNA sequences (Arabidopsis\_thaliana.TAIR10.cdna. all.fa.gz). Published RNA-Seq datasets were downloaded and processed in the same manner (Supplementary Table 6[\)35,](#page-8-24)[74–](#page-11-22)[81](#page-11-23). RNA-Seq heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package in R ([https://github.com/raivokolde/pheatmap\)](https://github.com/raivokolde/pheatmap). Differential gene expression analysis of *htr10*, *elf6 ref6 jmj13* and *elf6 ref6 jmj13htr10* was performed using DESeq2 version 1.22.2 (ref. [82](#page-11-24)) for transcripts that had ten counts or more in at least one sample. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were classified as having a *P*value<0.1 after correction for multiple testing (that is, the adjusted *P* value). Overlap of enrichment of DEGs with gene lists of interest was determined with the R package GeneOverlap 1.18.0 function newGOM [\(https://](https://github.com/shenlab-sinai/GeneOverlap) [github.com/shenlab-sinai/GeneOverlap](https://github.com/shenlab-sinai/GeneOverlap)).

**ChIP-Seq analysis.** ChIP-Seq was performed by adapting an ultra-low-input ChIP-Seq protocol (ULI-ChIP-Seq) described previousl[y83.](#page-11-25) Sperm nuclei from ProHTR10:HTR10-Clover plants were isolated by FACS and stored at −80°C in cryopreservation buffer (50mM Tris-Cl (pH8.0), 40% glycerol, 5mM MgCl2, 0.1mM EDTA, 1mM PMSF and 1× Roche protease inhibitor cocktail). Sperm nuclei from *htr10* mutant plants were purified using SYBR Gree[n84.](#page-11-26) Sperm mononucleosomes were generated by digestion with micrococcal nuclease (MNase; New England Biolabs) in MNase digestion buffer (1× NEB MNase reaction buffer, 1.5mM DTT and 5% PEG 6000). EDTA was added to a final concentration of 10mM to stop the MNase digestion. Nuclei were lysed by adding Triton X-100 and sodium deoxycholate to a final concentration of 0.1% each, incubated on ice for 15min and then vortexed. Mononucleosomes were recovered after centrifugation and subjected to immunoprecipitation with anti-H3K4me3 (Abcam; ab8580), anti-H3K27ac (Abcam; ab4729), anti-H3K27me1 (Millipore; 17-643), anti-H3K27me3 (Millipore; 07-449) and anti-GFP (Thermo Fisher Scientific; A-11122) antibodies. Sequencing libraries were prepared using a NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 to generate 50-bp paired-end reads while *htr10* mutant profiles were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 550 to generate 75-bp paired-end reads. All ChIP-Seq profiles were generated with at least two independent biological replicates.

Adapter trimming was performed using TrimGalore version 0.4.1 [\(https://](https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) [github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore\)](https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore). Reads were mapped to the *Arabidopsis* genome (TAIR10) using Bowtie 2 version 2.1.0 (ref. [85](#page-11-27)). Reads were filtered for a MAPQ score>10 using SAMtools version 1.3 (ref. [86\)](#page-11-28) and subsequently filtered for duplicate reads using Picard tools MarkDuplicates version 1.141 [\(https://github.](https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard) [com/broadinstitute/picard\)](https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard). Biological replicates were subsequently merged for downstream analysis after confirming high correlation among replicates (Extended Data Fig. 3a). A cross-correlation matrix based on Pearson's correlation coefficient was generated by comparing processed BAM files using deepTools version 2.5.0.1 utility bamCorrelate[87.](#page-11-29) For data visualization, log2[ratio] bigwig coverage files relative to input were generated using the deepTools version 2.5.0.1 utility bamCoverage with a bin size of 10bp (ref. [87\)](#page-11-29). Bigwig coverage files were visualized along the TAIR10 genome using IGV version 2.4.10 (ref. [88](#page-11-30)). Published ChIP-Seq datasets were downloaded and processed in the same manner (Supplementary Table 6[\)11](#page-8-2)[,89.](#page-11-31) Bigwig coverage files for published leaf ChIP-Seq datasets were generated relative to H3 to be similar to the MNase-digested sperm samples. Peaks were called using the MACS2 version 2.1.0 callpeak function using -f BAMPE --broad --broad-cutoff 0.1 -g 1.2e8 (ref. [90](#page-11-32)). Averaged ChIP-Seq profiles were all generated using the EnrichedHeatmap function normalizeToMatri[x91](#page-11-33) and plotted using a custom script in R.

**Comparison of somatic and sperm H3K27me3 profiles.** Consistent with its erasure from sperm chromatin, library complexity for sperm H3K27me3 ChIP-Seq was poor

compared with other histone marks. Nonetheless, the sperm H3K27me3 replicates were highly correlated and confirmed to have been sequenced to a saturating depth (Extended Data Fig. 3h,i) using Preseq version 2.0 (ref. [92\)](#page-11-34). Read depth discrepancy was accounted for before comparing the number of peaks between sperm and somatic tissues. Leaf and seedling H3K27me3 ChIP-Seq datasets were subsampled to the same read depth as sperm H3K27me3 using SAMtools version 1.3 (ref. [86](#page-11-28)). H3K27me3 peaks were subsequently called on the read depth-normalized BAM files with the MACS2 version 2.1.0 callpeak function using -f BAMPE --broad --broad-cutoff 0.1 -g 1.2e8 (ref. [90\)](#page-11-32). Somatic H3K27me3 domains were defined as the merged overlap between leaf and seedling H3K27me3 peaks (called using default MACS2 narrow peaks from non-subsampled BAM files) with the R package ChIPpeakAnno function findOverlapsOfPeaks[93.](#page-11-35) Similarly, the overlap analysis between sperm H3K27me3 peaks and somatic H3K27me3 domains was also performed with the R package ChIPpeakAnno function findOverlapsOfPeaks[93](#page-11-35).

**Annotation of reprogrammed gene clusters.** H3K27me3-marked genes were defined as TAIR10 genes that overlapped with somatic H3K27me3 domains (see 'Comparison of somatic and sperm H3K27me3 profiles') using the R package ChIPpeakAnno function findOverlapsOfPeaks[93.](#page-11-35) These somatic H3K27me3-marked genes were clustered based on H3K4me3 enrichment in sperm using *k*-means clustering (*n*=3) and visualized as ChIP-Seq heatmaps using the EnrichedHeatmap package in [R91](#page-11-33). Boxplots of RNA-Seq expression were limited to genes with a TPM>0.1. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of gene clusters was performed using the gProfileR package in [R94](#page-11-36) using g:SCS (set counts and sizes) correction, strong hierarchical filtering and limited to biological process Gene Ontology terms.

**Tissue enrichment of reprogrammed gene clusters.** The overlap of the reprogrammed genes (see 'Annotation of reprogrammed gene clusters') with tissue-enriched genes was determined with the R package GeneOverlap function newGOM (<https://github.com/shenlab-sinai/GeneOverlap>). Statistical *P*values for tissue enrichment for reprogrammed gene clusters (combining clusters 1, 2 and 3) was determined through a permutation overlap test (*n*=100 permutations) using the R package ChIPpeakAnno function peakPermTes[t93](#page-11-35). Sperm-enriched genes were defined as having a TPM>5 in sperm and a TPM<1 in seedling, roots, dry seeds and leaves (Supplementary Table 2). Early embryo-enriched genes were defined as the Affymetrix ATH1 array probe sets defined as specific to the pre-globular embryo proper in microarray datasets of laser micro-dissected portions of *Arabidopsis* seed[s95.](#page-11-37) Similarly, early endosperm-enriched genes were defined as a combined list of ATH1 probe sets specific to pre-globular micropylar, peripheral and chalazal endosperm[95](#page-11-37). For early embryo and endosperm-enriched genes, the overlap was limited to H3K27me3-marked genes that were also present on the Affymetrix ATH1 array. Genes with a maternal and paternal bias in early zygotes were defined previously[35.](#page-8-24)

**Analysis of PEGs and MEGs.** PEGs and MEGs were a combined and stringent set from multiple studies that were filtered for potential seed coat contamination[96.](#page-11-38) Polycomb-targeted PEGs and MEGs were defined as those present within the list of somatic H3K27me3-marked genes (see 'Comparison of somatic and sperm H3K27me3 profiles'). Statistical *P*values were determined by chi-squared analysis expressed relative to the proportion of somatic H3K27me3-marked protein-coding genes in the TAIR10 genome. Averaged ChIP-Seq enrichment over PEGs and MEGs was generated using the EnrichedHeatmap function normalizeToMatrix[91](#page-11-33) and plotted using a custom script in R.

**Statistics and reproducibility.** All statistical procedures performed are indicated in the figure captions. Each experiment was repeated independently at least twice with similar results, unless otherwise stated. The numbers of biological replicates for each experiment are also provided in the captions. ImageJ software (version 2.0.0) was used for quantification of H3K27me1 and H3K27me3 levels in pollen nuclei and two-sided Mann–Whitney *U*-tests were used to assess statistical differences between genotypes. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 and limited to transcripts that had ten counts or more in at least one sample. DEGs were classified as having a *P*value<0.1 after correction for multiple testing using Bonferroni correction (that is, the adjusted *P* value). Two-sided Mann–Whitney *U*-tests were performed when comparing TPM expression values of genes belonging to the different reprogrammed clusters. In addition, statistical methods for assessing peak and gene overlaps included one-sided permutation overlap tests or two-sided Fisher's exact tests, respectively. All violin plots (Fig. [1g,j\)](#page-1-0), boxplots (Figs. [4c,f](#page-5-0) and [5h\)](#page-6-0), bar plots (Fig. [5a\)](#page-6-0) and Venn overlaps (Fig. [3n](#page-4-0) and Extended Data Figs. 3g, 5e and 6c) are labelled with precise *P*values. The precise *P*values shown in the heatmaps summarizing statistically enriched overlaps (Figs. [4g](#page-5-0) and [5g](#page-6-0)) are provided as Source Data.
