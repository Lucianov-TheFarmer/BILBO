### 32 **ABSTRACT**

33 Histone modifications regulate gene expression in eukaryotes, but their roles in gene 34 expression changes in interspecific hybrids or allotetraploids are poorly understood. Histone 35 modifications can be mapped by immunostaining of metaphase chromosomes at the single 36 cell level and/or by chromatin immunoprecipitation-sequencing (ChIP-seq) for individual 37 genes. Here we examined H3K4me3 density and transcriptome maps in root-tip cells of 38 allotetraploid cotton (*Gossypium hirsutum* L*.*). The overall H3K4me3 levels were relatively 39 equal between A and D chromosomes, which were consistent with equal numbers of 40 expressed genes between the two subgenomes. However, intensities per chromosomal area 41 were nearly twice as high in the D homoeologs as in the A homoeologs. Consistent with the 42 cytological observation, ChIP-seq analysis showed that more D-homoeologs with biased 43 H3K4me3 levels than A-homoeologs with biased modifications correlated with the greater 44 number of the genes with D-biased expression than that with A-biased expression in most 45 homoeologous chromosome pairs. Two chromosomes displayed different expression levels 46 compared with other chromosomes probably because of translocations, which may affect 47 the local chromatin structure and expression levels for the genes involved. This example of 48 genome-wide histone modifications that determine expression bias of homoeologous genes 49 in allopolyploids provides a molecular basis for the evolution and domestication of polyploid 50 species including important crops.

### 52 **INTRODUCTION**

| 53 | Chromatin structure is a repeating unit of nucleosomes, each consisting of                     |
|----|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 54 | approximately 147 base pairs of DNA wrapping around a histone octamer with 2 copies each       |
| 55 | of H2A, H2B, H3, and H4 core histones (Kornberg, 1974; Luger et al., 1997). The core histone   |
| 56 | tails can be modified to mediate gene expression, growth, and development in plants and        |
| 57 | animals (Jenuwein and Allis, 2001; Berger, 2007; Li et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2010; Ha et al., |
| 58 | 2011). Some modifications, such as histone H3 lysine K9 acetylation (H3K9ac) and lysine 4      |
| 59 | trimethylation (H3K4me3), are euchromatic marks and often associated with active               |
| 60 | transcription, whereas other modifications, such as H3K9me2 and H3K27me3, are known as         |
| 61 | heterochromatic marks and related to gene repression (Jenuwein and Allis, 2001; Li et al.,     |
| 62 | 2007).                                                                                         |
|    |                                                                                                |
| 63 | Changes in chromatin structure and consequently gene expression can occur during               |
| 64 | development and/or in response to internal ("genome shock") and external (environmental)       |
| 65 | signals (McClintock, 1984; Chen, 2007; Chen and Tian, 2007). This genome shock is often        |
| 66 | associated with interspecific hybridization and polyploidization (McClintock, 1984; Comai,     |
| 67 | 2005; Chen, 2007). The additional genetic materials in new polyploid species can facilitate    |
| 68 | adaptation to local environments as well as for crop domestication (Chen, 2007; Soltis et al., |
| 69 | 2014). This may explain why the majority of crop plants are of polyploid origin, and many      |
| 70 | other crops are grown as hybrids. The most widely-cultivated allotetraploid cotton             |
| 71 | (Gossypium hirsutum L.) is an excellent example of polyploidization and domestication from     |

| 73 | doubling is predicted to have occurred at 1-2 million years ago (MYA) through hybridization  |
|----|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 74 | and chromosome doubling between the extant progenitors that are closely related to G.        |
| 75 | arboreum (A2) or G. herbaceum (A1), originating in the Old World, and G. raimondii (D5),     |
| 76 | originating in the New World (Wendel, 1989; Wendel and Cronn, 2003). Interestingly, the      |
| 77 | genome size of G. arboreum (1,746 Mb/1C) is almost twice the size of G. raimondii            |
| 78 | (885Mb/1C), which is largely related to the expansion of transposable elements (TEs),        |
| 79 | including long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons (Hawkins et al., 2006; Wang et al.,    |
| 80 | 2010; Zhang et al., 2015). The TE-rich regions often form heterochromatin and are different  |
| 81 | from genic regions in the euchromatin (Wang et al., 2010; Guan et al., 2014).                |
| 82 | Chromatin modifications can be visualized and quantified using immunostaining of             |
| 83 | metaphase chromosomes that have revealed dramatic regional differences in the                |
| 84 | distribution of specific histone modifications, particularly in centromeric (constitutive)   |
| 85 | heterochromatin in plants and animals (Carchilan et al., 2007; Terrenoire et al., 2010). The |
| 86 | technique has been applied in meiotic (pachytene) chromosomes in maize to display            |
| 87 | regional variation of methylated histone isoforms, with distinctive differences between      |
| 88 | heterochromatic and euchromatic regions (Shi and Dawe, 2006). However, these studies are     |
| 89 | very limited in plants (Houben and Schubert, 2003; Jin et al., 2008). The differences of     |
| 90 | histone modifications between homoeologous chromosomes and their consequences on             |
| 91 | expression of homoeologous genes in a polyploid species are largely unknown.                 |
| 92 | In this study, distribution patterns of histone modifications (H3K4me3 and                   |
| 93 | H3K27me2) in heterochromatic and euchromatic regions of homoeologous chromosomes in          |

| 94  | G. hirsutum were quantified and comparatively analyzed with transcriptome data. The          |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 95  | results have revealed distinctive patterns of heterochromatin and euchromatin between        |
| 96  | homoeologous chromosomes and an association of transcriptionally active chromatin with       |
| 97  | gene-rich regions. Although the overall fluorescent intensities of histone marks are equally |
| 98  | distributed between A and D homoeologous chromosomes, the mean fluorescent intensities       |

| 103 | between A and D homoeologs. Two<br>chromosomes display different expression levels           |
| 104 | relative to other chromosomes, probably because of translocations. These data indicate that  |
| 105 | both histone modifications and genome organization contribute to the expression bias of      |
| 106 | homoeologous genes in allopolyploids.                                                        |

### 283 **DISCUSSION**

### 284 **Euchromatic and heterochromatic marks in plant chromosomes**

| 285 | Histone modifications mediate epigenetic regulation of gene expression, growth, and            |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 286 | development in plants and animals (Berger, 2007; Li et al., 2007; Zhang, 2008). Histone        |
| 287 | marks such as H3K4me3 and H4K12ac are associated with euchromatin and gene activation,         |
| 288 | while marks like H3K27me2 correlate with heterochromatin and gene repression (Jenuwein         |
| 289 | and Allis, 2001; Li et al., 2007). In allotetraploid cotton, the H3K4me3 density difference    |
| 290 | between A and D homoeologous chromosomes could be related to the genome size, which            |
| 291 | is twice as large in the former as in the latter. In addition, retrotransposons including long |
| 292 | terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons are more abundant in the A homoeologs compared          |
| 293 | to D homoeologs (Zhang et al., 2015). Together, more repetitive sequences and larger size in   |
| 294 | the A subgenome than in the D subgenome are associated with more heterochromatin per           |
| 295 | megabase in the A homoeologs than in the D homoeologs<br>and consequently, less                |
| 296 | euchromatin per megabase in the A homoeologs than in the D homoeologs. On each                 |
| 297 | chromosome, the distal portions were enriched with H3K4me3 and H4K12ac so the                  |
| 298 | euchromatin is more abundant near chromosome ends than proximal regions, which is              |
| 299 | consistent with the distribution of genes and genetic recombination along cotton               |
| 300 | chromosomes (Paterson et al., 2012). This gradient distribution of H3K4me3 across              |
| 301 | metaphase chromosomes of G. hirsutum is consistent with the notion that H3K4me3 is             |
| 302 | conserved euchromatic mark in plant species (Fuchs et al., 2006). H4K12ac has very similar     |
| 303 | distribution patterns as H3K4me3, except for clustering of H4K12ac near the nucleolar          |

304 organizing regions (NORs), as previously reported in other plant chromosomes (Belyaev et 305 al., 1997; Jasencakova et al., 2000). This indicates a role for H4K12ac in differential 306 expression of uniparental rRNA genes subjected to nucleolar dominance (Lawrence et al., 307 2004). 308 H3K27 methylation has been linked to several silencing phenomena in animals, 309 including X chromosome inactivation and genomic imprinting (Martin and Zhang, 2005). 310 H3K27me2 is highly concentrated in the centromeric heterochromatin in *A. thaliana*  311 (Lindroth et al., 2004; Mathieu et al., 2005) and in maize (Shi and Dawe, 2006). However, 312 this distribution pattern is not conserved in cotton compared to maize or *Arabidopsis* (Fuchs 313 et al., 2006). H3K27me2 signals are patchy in the metaphase and display some weak bands 314 in interphase cells (Supplemental Fig. 3). Allotetraploid cotton has two major and one minor 315 NORs (Hanson et al., 1995), and H3K27me2 is more prominent in one of them. This suggests 316 that H3K27me2 could cause silencing of one rDNA locus, while H4K12ac could be associated 317 with active rDNA loci. 318 **Both chromatin and genome organization mediate homoeologous gene expression**  319 After polyploidization, genetic changes such as mutations, sequence elimination, and 320 chromosomal rearrangements, as well as epigenetic mechanisms at transcriptional and 321 post-transcriptional levels can contribute to gene expression variation (Chen, 2007; Doyle et 322 al., 2008; Soltis et al., 2014). Polyploidy may induce epigenetic modifications of 323 homoeologous chromosomes to reprogram gene expression and developmental patterns of 324 allopolyploids (Song and Chen, 2015), leading to genome-wide dosage-dependent and

| 325 | independent gene expression novelty (Shi et al., 2015). Histone marks such as H3K4me3 are       |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 326 | known to correlate with gene expression diversity between species and in Arabidopsis            |
| 327 | allopolyploids (Ha et al., 2011; Shi et al., 2015). In this study, we associated H3K4me3 levels |
| 328 | with expression changes of homoeologous genes in allotetraploid cotton. Although the total      |
| 329 | H3K4me3 levels are relatively equal between A and D homoeologous chromosomes, the               |
| 330 | fluorescent intensity per area of H3K4me3 is significantly higher in the D homoeologous         |
| 331 | chromosomes than in the A homoeologs. Consistent with the cytological data, ChIP-seq            |
| 332 | analysis showed more D homoeologs with biased H3K4me3 levels than A homoeologs with             |
| 333 | biased H3K4me3 levels, which could loosen chromatin structure for the D homoeologs              |
| 334 | allowing genes to be expressed at higher levels. Indeed, there are more genes with              |
| 335 | D-homoeolog biased expression than with A-homoeolog biased expression in most                   |
| 336 | homoeologous chromosome pairs. This suggests an overall more effect of H3K4me3 on D             |
| 337 | homoeologs than on A homoeologs.                                                                |
| 338 | Chromosomal translocations can result in hitherto unforeseen and large-scale                    |
| 339 | changes in gene expression that are the consequence of alterations in normal chromosome         |
| 340 | territory positioning in human (Harewood et al., 2010). In allotetraploid cotton,               |
| 341 | homoeologous chromosome pairs with chromosomal translocations could possess different           |
| 342 | chromatin structure and change the number of genes with homoeolog-biased expression             |
| 343 | among the chromosomes involved. Expression levels of the genes between the translocated         |
| 344 | regions were different from that of all chromosomes and were consistent with the difference     |
| 345 | of H3K4me3 distribution levels between translocated regions and all chromosomes. Thus,          |

346 during polyploidization genome reorganization including translocation may alter chromatin 347 structure that induces gene expression changes and potentially phenotypic variation in 348 polyploids.

| 350 | sequenced chromosome unless a set of DNA markers specific to each chromosome is used      |
| 351 | on the same cells where the chromosomes are immunostatined. This would be technically     |
| 352 | challenging and cannot be resolved at the present stage. The current approach using the   |
| 353 | chromosome length, immunolabeling intensity, and gene density maps has shown to work      |
| 354 | well in cotton as in mammalian cells (Terrenoire et al., 2010). The ChIP-seq results have |
| 355 | confirmed the cytological data. Distributions between H3K4me3 intensities and gene        |
| 356 | densities are comparable and consistent between most homoeologous chromosome pairs.       |
| 357 | With improvement of technologies, chromatin and expression analysis of single cells or    |
| 358 | chromosomes would increase the resolution and precision of chromatin regulation. This     |
| 359 | would be extremely valuable to resolve gene expression changes between homoeologous       |
| 360 | chromosomes in polyploid species for the genetic improvement of important polyploid       |
| 361 | crops such as wheat (food), cotton (fiber), and canola (oil).                             |

| 363 | Immunostaining and FISH                                                                   |
| 364 | Root tips were harvested from rapidly growing roots of A-genome extant progenitor         |
| 365 | (Gossypium arboreum L cv. JLZM) and<br>tetraploid cotton (G. hirsutum L acc. TM-1) and    |

| 367 | paraformaldehyde. After washing with PBS, the fixed root tips were digested with 4%            |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 368 | Cellulase Onozuka R-10 (Yakult Honsha, Tokyo, Japan) and 1% Pectinase Y-23 (MP                 |
| 369 | Biomedicals, Santa Ana, California) for 2 h at 37o<br>C. The digested root tips were washed in |
| 370 | PBS and water (10 min each) and squashed in a drop of water. The slides were rapidly           |
| 371 | deep-freezed with liquid nitrogen. After removing coverslips, slides were immediately          |
| 372 | treated with PBS, containing 3% BSA and 1% Triton X-100. Commercial antibodies against         |
| 373 | H4K12Ac (Abcam, Cambridge, Massachusetts) and against H3K4me3, H3K27me2 and                    |
| 374 | H3K9me2 (Upstate Biotechnology, Lake Placid, New York) were diluted into 1:100 in PBS,         |
| 375 | applied to the slides that were subsequently covered with coverslips. The slides were          |
| 376 | incubated in a moist chamber at 37o<br>C for overnight. After washing in PBS, the antibodies   |
| 377 | were detected using the secondary antibodies of Cy3-conjugated anti-rabbit. DNA was            |
| 378 | counterstained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Following histone                    |
| 379 | immunostaining, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed on chromosome          |
| 380 | spreads according a previously published protocol (Han et al., 2009). Digoxigenin-labeled      |
| 381 | probes were detected using fluorescein-conjugated anti-digoxigenin (Roche Diagnostics,         |
| 382 | Indianapolis, Indiana). Chromosomes were counterstained with Vectashield Mounting              |
| 383 | medium containing DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA) under a coverslip. To help        |
| 384 | chromosome identification, we used telomere and genomic in situ hybridization (GISH) for       |
| 385 | chromosome analysis. Telomeric DNA (42 copies of TTTAGGG) was used as                          |

### 389 **Analysis of chromosome images and immunostaining signal intensities**

| 390 | Slides were examined using an Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscope (objective                        |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 391 | UPlanSApo 100× 1.40, oil) at room temperature (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The images of                 |
| 392 | chromosomes, immunostaining and FISH signal channels were captured and merged using                  |
| 393 | Olympus cellSens Dimension software V5.1 with an Olympus DP72 CCD camera. To                         |
| 394 | determine H3K4me3 signal levels of A<br>and D chromosomes, only the cells with                       |
| 395 | non-overlapping chromosomes were analyzed, and the sum of relative signal levels of each             |
| 396 | chromosome was calculated using Olympus cellSens Dimension. Final image adjustments                  |
| 397 | were processed using Photoshop CS 3.0 (Adobe, San Jose, California). For each chromosome,            |
| 398 | the level of H3K4me3 intensities was estimated using the Intensity Profile of the Olympus            |
| 399 | cellSens, which measured the signal intensity per 0.0339 μm per unit. The chromosomes                |
| 400 | were first partitioned into homologous pairs using morphological features (arm length and            |
| 401 | short/long arm ratios) and H3K4me3 patterns and assigned based on arm length of                      |
| 402 | cytological chromosome (from short to long). For quantitative analysis, we divided each              |
| 403 | chromosome into bins (5 Mb per bin) using H3K4me3 fluorescent intensities and gene                   |
| 404 | densities. The correlation between H3K4me3 fluorescent intensities and gene densities in             |
| 405 | each bin on each chromosome pair was estimated using Pearson correlation coefficients                |
| 406 | and tested for statistical significance (Supplemental<br>Table S3; Supplemental<br>Table S4).<br>The |
| 407 | confidence of homoeology assignment was based on Pearson correlation coefficients and                |

- 408 P-values of immunostaining densities (H3K4me3) at distal regions between A and D

## 410 **RNA extractions, RNA-seq libraries and sequencing**

- 411 RNA samples of root tips in three replicates were extracted using a modified mirvana
- 412 mirna isolation kit procedure (Ambion, Austin, USA), and their quality was evaluated on
- 413 Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts) and Agilent Bioanalyzer
- 414 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California). mRNA-seq libraries were constructed
- 415 following the RNA-seq library prep kit protocol and sequenced by Illumina HiSeq 2500
- 416 machine (Illumina, San Diego, California) with100 base pair-end reads at Nanjing Agricultural
- 417 University.
- 418 **RNA-seq data pre-processing and quality control**
- 419 Raw reads were examined and filtered by the following steps: (1) removing the last
- 420 index of original FASTQ format with perl script, (2) performing quality control of the
- 421 sequencing data by NGS QC Toolkit V2.3 (Patel and Jain 2012), and (3) discarding the low
- 422 quality reads to ensure that more than 80% of the bases of each retained read had the
- 423 Illumina quality > 20 (q20 indicating 1% sequencing error rate).
- 424 **Mapping and analysis of gene expression in homoeologous chromosomes**
- 425 Clean reads were mapped onto the *G. hirsutum* genome (Zhang et al., 2015). The
- 426 mapping was processed by TopHat version 2.0.12
- 427 (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/tophat/index.shtml) utilizing Bowtie2 version 2.23
- 428 (http://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/bowtie2/index.shtml). Mapping results generated by

| 429 | TopHat were filtered to retain only uniquely mapped reads before being transferred into      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 430 | Cuffdiff for estimating read counts. For each gene, the value of fragments per kilobase per  |
| 431 | million reads (FPKM) was calculated by in-house scripts based on the count table of Cuffdiff |
| 432 | output. Only the genes with mean FPKM of ≥1 were used for further analysis. The reads        |
| 433 | were used to estimate the number of the genes and expression levels of the each              |
| 434 | homoeolog or chromosome. The same chromosome coordinates were used for visualization         |
| 435 | and distribution of repeats, genes, and FPKM values in the G. hirsutum genome (Zhang et al., |
| 436 | 2015) using the program Circos (Krzywinski et al., 2009). Expression bias of homoeologous    |
| 437 | genes was estimated to identify the genes with differential expression between A and D       |
| 438 | homoeologs using statistical tests (FDR<0.05) with at least 1.5-fold changes in average      |
| 439 | expression levels (FPKM) across three biological replicates. The RNA-seq data were           |
| 440 | deposited at GEO (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) with the accession number                |
