### 31 Abstract

32 Salinity severely reduces plant growth and limits agricultural productivity. Dynamic changes and rearrangement of the plant cell wall is an important response to salt stress, 33 but relatively little is known about the biological importance of specific cell wall 34 components in the response. Here, we demonstrate a specific function of 35  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan in salt hypersensitivity. Salt stress induced the accumulation of 36 37  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan in root cell walls by up-regulating the expression of GALACTAN 38 SYNTHASE 1 (GALS1), which encodes  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan synthase. The accumulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan negatively affected salt tolerance. Exogenous application of 39 D-galactose (D-Gal) causes an increase in  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan levels in wild type and 40 41 GALS1 mutants, especially in GALS1 overexpressors, which correlated with the 42 aggravated salt hypersensitivity. Further analysis revealed that the BARLEY B RECOMBINANT/BASIC PENTACYSTEINE transcription factors BPC1/BPC2 43 positively affected the salt tolerance by repressing GALS1 expression and 44  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan accumulation. Genetic analysis suggested that *GALS1* was genetically 45 46 epistatic to BPC1/BPC2 with respect to the control of salt sensitivity as well as accumulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan. Taken together, our results reveal a new regulatory 47 mechanism where  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan regulated by BPC1/BPC2-GALS1 module 48 aggravates salt sensitivity in Arabidopsis thaliana. 49 50

### 62 Introduction

Salt stress is a major abiotic stress that severely reduces plant growth and limits 63 agricultural productivity (Zhu, 2002; Zhu, 2016). Plants have evolved various 64 strategies to sense and adapt to saline environments (Julkowska and Testerink, 2015; 65 Munns and Tester, 2008; Zhu, 2016). Cell walls provide the cell with both structural 66 support and protection. One of the important plant adaptations to salt stress is 67 differential regulation of growth, accompanying the dynamic changes and 68 rearrangement of the plant cell wall, (Cosgrove, 2015; Tenhaken, 2015; Wang et al., 69 2016). The plant cell wall has a dynamic architecture composed of cellulose 70 microfibrils embedded in an amorphous matrix of pectin and hemicellulose 71 72 polysaccharides as well as structural proteins, and in some cells also lignin (Herburger et al., 2020; Mutwil et al., 2008; Scheller and Ulvskov, 2010). Previous studies have 73 shown that several cellulose synthesis genes, including Cellulose Synthase (CesA1, 74 CesA6 and CesA8), Cellulose Synthase Like D5 (CSLD5), KORRIGAN1 (KOR1) and 75 76 Companion of Cellulose Synthase (CC1 and CC2), are implicated in salt stress (Chen et al., 2005; Endler et al., 2015; Kang et al., 2008; Kesten et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2018; 77 Zhang et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2010). Knocking out CesA6 confers salt stress 78 sensitivity (Zhang et al., 2016). CC1 and CC2 interact with CesAs and microtubules, 79 80 and mutations in CC1 and CC2 disrupt salt tolerance by altering microtubule and cellulose synthase complex (CSCs) properties (Endler et al., 2015; Kesten et al., 81 82 2019). Thus, sustained cellulose biosynthesis is crucial for plants to cope with salt 83 stress.

β-1,4-galactan are generally found as side chains of rhamnogalacturonan I (RGI),
which is a major component of pectin (Atmodjo et al., 2013; Harholt et al., 2010).
β-1,4-galactan is directly synthesized by β-1,4-galactan galactosyltransferase encoded
by *GALACTAN SYNTHASE* genes, of which there are three in *Arabidopsis thaliana*(*Arabidopsis*), *GALS1*, *GALS2* and *GALS3* (Ebert et al., 2018; Liwanag et al., 2012).
Several studies have shown that β-1,4-galactan plays a role in plant growth and
development. For example, overexpressing a fungal galactanase in potato tubers

resulted in decreased  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan and slightly altered mechanical properties 91 92 (Ulvskov et al., 2005). Øbro et al. (2009) found that reduced  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan slightly affected the diameter of stems in *Arabidopsis*. In flax,  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan confers specific 93 mechanical properties to cellulose-enriched plant fibers by modifying  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan 94 metabolism *in muro* (Roach et al., 2011). β-1,4-galactan might also play a role in cell 95 elongation by altering the cell wall properties (McCartney et al., 2003; 96 Moneo-Sánchez et al., 2019). Furthermore,  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan is believed to be a water 97 retaining viscoelastic component with a likely role in modulating the mechanical 98 properties of cell wall (Ha et al., 2005; Harholt et al., 2010; Tang et al., 1999). 99 Nevertheless, even Arabidopsis triple mutants in all three GALS genes showed no 100 observable growth or developmental phenotype under standard laboratory conditions 101 102 (Ebert et al., 2018). However, given the conservation across plants of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan it must serve an important role. Surprisingly, the involvement of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan in plant 103 response to abiotic stresses has not been reported. 104

The BARLEY B RECOMBINANT/BASIC PENTACYSTEINE (BBR/BPC) 105 106 proteins comprise a plant-specific transcription factor family and are present throughout land plants. They have been identified in many species, including 107 Arabidopsis, Oryza sativa, Hordeum vulgare, Glycine max, and Cucumis sativus 108 (Kooiker et al., 2005; Monfared et al., 2011; Mu et al., 2017a; Mu et al., 2017b; 109 Sangwan and O'Brian, 2002; Santi et al., 2003). BPC proteins containing a conserved 110 DNA-binding domain at the C-terminal can directly regulate the expression of target 111 genes by binding GA-rich sequences of promoters (Kooiker et al., 2005; Meister et al., 112 2004; Mu et al., 2017b; Sangwan and O'Brian, 2002; Santi et al., 2003; Simonini et al., 113 114 2012). In Arabidopsis, 7% of promoters contain at least one GA-rich sequence according to a pattern-matching search (Yan et al., 2005). This high frequency of 115 sequence motif in the genome suggest that the BPC proteins might have numerous 116 and distinct functions during plant growth. Indeed, BPC proteins regulate a wide 117 range of developmental process, including seed dormancy, inflorescence meristem, 118 leaf and flower development, lateral root formation and cytokinin response (Monfared 119 et al., 2011; Mu et al., 2017a; Mu et al., 2017b; Shanks et al., 2018; Simonini et al., 120

- 2012; Wu et al., 2020). However, relatively little is known about the potential role ofBPC proteins in abiotic stress responses.
- In this study, we found that  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan significantly aggravated salt sensitivity in *Arabidopsis*. Furthermore, two transcription factors BPC1/BPC2 were found to directly bind to the *GALS1* promoter, which repressed *GALS1* expression, decreased  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan accumulation and increased salt tolerance.

### 129 Salt stress induces the accumulation of $\beta$ -1,4-galactan in root cell wall

Salt stress inhibits primary root elongation (Galvan-Ampudia and Testerink, 2011). To investigate the potential role of the root cell wall in plant response to salt stress, we first determined the root cell wall composition in response to salt stress. Salt stress significantly increased the D-galactose (D-Gal) contents in root cell wall, whereas the amount of other monosaccharide residues remained unchanged (Figure 1A).

Galactose is present in various polymers and glycoproteins in cell walls, including 135 136  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan and xyloglucan. To determine if the increase in D-Gal was related to increased  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan, cell wall material was digested with endo- $\beta$ -1,4-galactanase 137 from Aspergillius niger (Liwanag et al., 2012; Stonebloom et al., 2016), and the D-Gal 138 content was analyzed in the solubilized and residual materials. Treatment with 139 140 endo- $\beta$ -1,4-galactanase released more D-Gal from the cell wall of salt-treated plants than from control plants, whereas the residual material did not show any difference 141 142 (Figure 1B). This indicate that the increase in total D-Gal is specifically due to an 143 increase in pectic  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan content.

The change in cell wall polysaccharides in response to salt stress was further 144 investigated with immunodot assays using LM5 antibody, which specifically 145 recognize pectic  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan (Jones et al., 1997). As shown in Figure 1C, binding 146 was stronger in material from the salt-treated plants than the control plants. 147 148 Meanwhile. LM19, а monoclonal antibody against homogalacturonan (Verhertbruggen et al., 2009), did not show any difference in binding. The detection of 149 the LM5 galactan epitope was strongly increased at the root tips of the salt-treated 150

plants (Figure 1D), and the LM5 fluorescence intensity was enhanced significantly after salt stress (Figure 1E). These results show that salt stress induces the accumulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan in root cell wall.

### 155 GALS1 negatively affects salt stress tolerance

Previous studies have shown that three galactan synthase proteins (GALS1, GALS2 156 and GALS3) are involved in  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan biosynthesis (Ebert et al., 2018; Laursen 157 et al., 2018; Liwanag et al., 2012). To investigate which GALS genes are involved in 158 salt-induced accumulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan in roots, the expression of GALS1, 159 GALS2 and GALS3 under NaCl treatment was examined. The expression of GALS1 160 was strongly induced after 125 mM NaCl treatment, whereas salt stress did not affect 161 162 the expression of GALS2 and GALS3 (Figure 2A-2C). Plants were transformed with a construct where the promoter region of GALS1, GALS2 or GALS3 was fused to a 163 β-glucuronidase (GUS) reporter gene. GUS activity was strongly detected in the root 164 tip of transgenic lines with *Pro-GALS1-GUS* and more weakly observed in the root tip 165 166 of transgenic lines with Pro-GALS2-GUS and Pro-GALS3-GUS (Figure 2D-2F). Salt stress only obviously increased the GUS activities in the root tip of Pro-GALS1-GUS 167 lines (Figure 2D), consistent with the qRT-PCR results. 168

To evaluate the contribution of GALS1 to root growth under NaCl treatment, two 169 170 independent mutants of GALS1 with T-DNA insertion (gals1-1 and gals1-2) were used for salt sensitivity assays (Liwanag et al., 2012). Genotypes and decreased 171  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan in the mutants were confirmed by RT-PCR and immunodetection 172 analysis (Figure S1). Four-day-old seedlings of the wild type and *gals1* mutants were 173 174 transferred to 1/2 MS medium with or without 125 mM NaCl. No differences in primary root length were observed between wild type and gals1 mutants grown on 1/2 175 MS medium without NaCl (Figure 2G). However, in the presence of NaCl, the 176 primary root length of gals1 mutants was longer than those of the wild type (Figure 177 178 2G).

To further investigate the function of GALS1, we expressed *GALS1* driven by the native *GALS1* promoter in the *gals1-1* background and *GALS1* driven by 35S

promoter in Col-0. As shown in Figure S2 and Figure S3, two independent 181 182 complementation lines (COM#1 and COM#2) and GALS1 overexpressors (OE-GALS1#1 and OE-GALS1#5) were confirmed by RT-PCR and immunodetection 183 analysis. Under NaCl-free conditions, no differences in primary root growth were 184 observed among the tested genotypes. Under NaCl treatment, the complemented lines 185 did not differ from wild type, whereas GALS1 overexpressors showed a shorter 186 primary root length (Figure 2H and 2I). Salt stress results in both osmotic and ionic 187 stress in plant (Munns and Tester, 2008). Next, we determined whether salt tolerance 188 of gals1 mutants was a specific response to the sodium ions. As shown in Figure S4, 189 seedlings of gals1-1 and gals1-2 specifically responded to  $Na^+$ , but not  $K^+$ ,  $Cl^-$ , and 190 osmotic stress. These results clearly indicate that GALS1 negatively affects salt 191 192 tolerance.

# 194 β-1,4-galactan synthesized by *GALS1* significantly aggravates salt 195 hypersensitivity in *Arabidopsis*

196 Our results showed that salt stress induced the accumulation of  $\beta$ -1.4-galactan in roots (Figure 1) and GALS1 negatively affected the salt tolerance (Figure 2). The LM5 197 galactan epitope was significantly increased in the root tips of wild type, gals1 198 mutants and GALS1 overexpressor under NaCl treatment. The LM5 fluorescence 199 200 intensity in the GALS1 overexpressor was much stronger than in the wild type, and LM5 signal in *gals1* mutants was much weaker than in the wild type (Figure 3C and 201 202 3D). These findings are consistent with the observation that accumulation of 203  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan aggravates the stress symptoms induced by salt stress.

Since D-Gal can be directly used by plants and exogenous D-Gal increased the D-Gal level but not other cell wall monosaccharides in etiolated hypocotyl cell walls (Laursen et al., 2018), a D-Gal-feeding experiment was conducted to explore the impact of exogenous D-Gal on the salt response (Dörmann and Benning, 1998; Laursen et al., 2018; Seifert et al., 2002). Our results showed that exogenous D-Gal significantly increased the accumulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan in the roots of all tested genotypes (Figure 3C and 3D). To analyze the effect of exogenous D-Gal on salt

sensitivity, four-day-old seedlings of the wild type were transferred to 1/2 MS 211 212 medium supplemented with or without 125 mM NaCl and various concentrations of D-Gal (0, 12.5 mM and 25 mM). In the absence of NaCl, exogenous D-Gal had no 213 obvious effect on primary root growth. When seedling grew on 1/2 MS medium 214 supplemented with 125 mM NaCl, the presence of exogenous D-Gal significantly 215 inhibited the primary root growth in a concentration dependent manner. In contrast, 216 supplementation with exogenous D-glucose (D-Glc), did not affect salt sensitivity 217 218 (Figure S5).

Next, we observed the effect of exogenous D-Gal on suppressing the primary root 219 growth of gals1 mutants and GALS1 overexpressors. We found that exogenous 220 application of D-Gal suppressed the primary root growth of all the genotypes in the 221 222 presence of NaCl. Suppression of primary root length in the wild type was weaker than in GALS1 overexpressors, and stronger than in gals1 mutants (Figure 3A and 3B). 223 Meanwhile, we determined the prevalence of the  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan epitope in the roots of 224 the above tested genotypes. The induction of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan levels correlated with the 225 226 suppression of primary root growth in wild type, GALS1 overexpressors and gals1 mutants (Figure 3C and 3D). These results further confirm the strong correlation 227 between  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan and salt sensitivity. 228

### 230 GALS1 affects crystalline cellulose biosynthesis under salt stress

Ebert et al. (2018) found that loss or accumulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan led to minor 231 changes in cellulose synthesis in Arabidopsis seedlings under normal condition. To 232 explore whether cellulose contents were also affected in gals1 mutant and GALS1 233 234 overexpressor under NaCl treatment, the fluorescence of cellulose stained with 235 Pontamine Fast Scarlet 4B (S4B) in roots from the tested genotypes was determined (Anderson et al., 2010). As shown in Figure 4A and 4B, the cellulose fluorescence 236 intensity in the root of wild type was stronger than that of OE-GALS1#1 237 overexpressor, but lower than that of gals1-1 mutant under NaCl treatment. To 238 239 confirm this, we then analyzed the crystalline cellulose content in root cell wall of these genotypes under NaCl treatment. In the presence of NaCl, the crystalline 240

cellulose content in *gals1-1* root was higher compared to that of the wild type, while
the crystalline cellulose content in *OE-GALS1#1* was lower compared to that of wild
type (Figure 4C). However, there was no significant difference in D-Glc content in
TFA-hydrolyzate between *gals1* mutant and *GALS1* overexpressor under NaCl
treatment, suggesting that GALS1 did not affect the amorphous cellulose content in
response to salt stress (Table S1).

To further investigate whether GALS1 affects other cell wall components in 247 response to salt stress, we measured the contents of pectin and hemicellulose in 248 salt-treated roots of gals1-1, OE-GALS1#1 and Col-0. In the absence or presence of 249 NaCl, the contents of pectin and hemicellulose in gals1 mutants and GALS1 250 overexpressors were similar to those in wild type (Figure 4D and 4E). 251 252 Monosaccharide composition analysis showed that only the D-Gal contents in the root cell wall were affected in gals1 mutant and GALS1 overexpressor under NaCl 253 treatment (Table S1). Together, these results indicate that GALS1 negatively affects 254 crystalline cellulose synthesis only under the salt stress. 255

256 To further confirm the effect on crystalline cellulose, the prcl-1 mutant (a knock-out mutant of CesA6) with a defect in crystalline cellulose synthesis (Fagard et 257 al., 2000) was crossed with the gals1-1 mutant. The prc1-1 mutant was highly 258 sensitive to salt stress compared to the wild type (Figure S6) as previously reported 259 (Zhang et al., 2016). The gals1-1 prc1-1 double mutant was confirmed by PCR and 260 Sanger sequence (Figure S7). As shown in Figure 4F and 4G, the primary root growth 261 262 of gals1-1 prc1-1 double mutant was similar to that of prc1-1 single mutant under both normal and NaCl conditions, even though the gals1-1 single mutant showed a 263 264 salt-resistant phenotype. The crystalline cellulose level in gals1-1 prc1-1 double mutant was also as low as that of the *prc1-1* single mutant, even though the *gals1-1* 265 single mutant showed a high crystalline cellulose level (Figure 4H). The epistasis of 266 prc1-1 over gals1-1 indicate that the sustained crystalline cellulose biosynthesis is 267 268 responsible for the salt-resistant phenotype in the *gals1-1* mutant.

### 270 BPC1/BPC2 directly binds to the promoter of GALS1 in vivo and in vitro

271 To investigate the regulation of the expression of GALS1 under salt stress, we 272 constructed three 5' terminal deletion mutants of the GALS1 promoter fused with the reporter gene Firefly Luciferase (FFluc) to transform into Col-0 (Figure S8A). 273 Seedlings of these Arabidopsis transgenic lines (Pro-GALS1-FFluc) were used to test 274 the GALS1 promoter activity under the NaCl treatment. As shown in Figure S8B, the 275 luminescence signal in NaCl-treated seedlings was significantly stronger than that in 276 control plants. Importantly, there was no significant difference between 277 278 Pro-GALS1-FFluc (1-600) and the other transgenic lines, which clearly demonstrated that the 1-600 bp fragment of the GALS1 promoter retained the GALS1 promoter 279 activity and inducibility in response to salt stress. 280

To identify the putative transcription factors regulating GALS1 expression, we 281 282 constructed an Arabidopsis cDNA library and applied a yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) approach to search for novel transcription factors associated with the GALS1 promoter. 283 By determining the minimal inhibitory concentration of aureobasidin A (AbA) that 284 suppressed background activation, we found that the fragment of GALS1 promoter 285 286 (1-600 bp) could not be directly used for this Y1H system because it was likely being recognized by endogenous yeast transcription factors (Figure S9). Then, a series of 287 deletion constructions of the GALS1 promoter as shown in Figure S9 was used to test 288 the minimal inhibitory concentration of AbA. We found that a slightly shorter 289 290 fragment of GALS1 promoter (90-600 bp) could be used to screen the Arabidopsis cDNA library (Figure S9). Positive interactions are listed in Table S2. Two of the 16 291 292 positive clones encoded a BPC transcription factor, BPC1 (AT2G01930) and three of the 16 positive clones encoded a BPC transcription factor, BPC2 (AT1G14685). 293

To confirm the interactions between the *GALS1* promoter and the BPC1/BPC2 transcription factors, we performed Y1H assay a second time using the full-length coding sequences of *BPC1* and *BPC2*. The interaction between BPC1/BPC2 and a fragment (90-600 bp) of the *GALS1* promoter was tested by growing on medium lacking Leu and Ura and supplemented with 300 ng/mL AbA. The assay showed that BPC1/BPC2 could bind to the promoter of *GALS1* in yeast (Figure 5A). In *Arabidopsis*, seven BPC proteins are classified into three groups; BPC1, BPC2 and

BPC3 are designated as class I (Monfared et al., 2011). However, we found that BPC3
could not directly bind to the *GALS1* promoter in yeast (Figure S10).

303 BPC proteins can bind to sequences rich in GA repeats (Kooiker et al., 2005; Meister et al., 2004; Mu et al., 2017b; Sangwan and O'Brian, 2002; Santi et al., 2003; 304 Simonini et al., 2012). We then searched the putative BPC protein binding elements in 305 the sequences 600 bp upstream of the coding region of GALS1 and identified one 306 GA-rich motif (-92 to -100) in the promoter region of GALS1 (Figure 5C). To 307 determine whether BPC1/BPC2 binds this GA-rich motif, an electrophoresis mobility 308 shift assay (EMSA) was performed. His-BPC1 or His-BPC2 was expressed in 309 *Escherichia coli* BL21 and purified from the soluble fraction. BPC1/BPC2 directly 310 bound to the probes labeled with biotin, whereas BPC1/BPC2 could not bind to the 311 312 mutated probes labeled with biotin. In addition, the binding activity between BPC1/BPC2 and the probe was almost abolished by the addition of unlabeled 313 competitive probes (Figure 5B). 314

To determine whether BPC1/BPC2 directly binds to the promoter of *GALS1 in vivo*, a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay using *Pro-BPC1-BPC1-Myc* and *Pro-BPC2-BPC2-Myc* plants was performed. ChIP-qPCR assay showed that BPC1/BPC2 protein could strongly bind to the P1, but not the P2 region of the promoter, which does not have a GA-rich motif (Figure 5C). Taken together, our results clearly suggest that BPC1/BPC2 directly binds to the *GALS1* promoter *in vitro* and *in vivo*.

### 323 BPC1/BPC2 positively affects salt tolerance by repressing *GALS1* expression

Since BPC1/BPC2 could directly bind to the *GALS1* promoter *in vivo* and *in vitro* (Figure 5), we wanted to test whether BPC1/BPC2 is involved in plant response to salt stress. Two T-DNA insertion lines (*bpc1-1* and *bpc2*) (Monfared et al., 2011) were used for salt sensitivity assays. Four-day-old seedlings of the wild type, *bpc1-1* and *bpc2* mutants were transferred to 1/2 MS medium with or without 125 mM NaC1. Unexpectedly, there were no obvious differences in primary root length between wild type and *bpc1-1* or *bpc2* mutants in the presence of NaCl (Figure S11). Monfared et al.

(2011) showed that there was a high level of redundancy among the BPC genes in 331 Arabidopsis development and growth. Thus, we hypothesized a functional 332 redundancy of BPC1 or BPC2 gene in plant response to salt stress. To test our 333 hypothesis, we obtained the bpcl-1 bpc2 double mutant (Monfared et al., 2011). As 334 shown in Figure 6A and Figure S11, the primary root length of *bpc1-1 bpc2* double 335 was shorter than that in the wild type. To further investigate the function of 336 BPC1/BPC2, two independent BPC1 overexpressor lines (OE-BPC1#1 and 337 OE-BPC1#3) and two independent BPC2 overexpressor lines (OE-BPC2#2 and 338 *OE-BPC2#3*) were confirmed by qRT-PCR (Figure S12), and investigated in salt 339 sensitivity assays. Under NaCl-free conditions, no differences in primary root growth 340 were observed among the tested genotypes. Under NaCl treatment, compared to wild 341 342 type, BPC1 overexpressors and BPC2 overexpressors showed a longer primary root length (Figure 6B). Moreover, we analyzed the BPC1 and BPC2 expression in 343 response to salt stress and found that salt stress significantly reduced the expression of 344 both genes (Figure 6C). These results clearly suggest that BPC1/BPC2 positively 345 346 regulates salt tolerance.

To further test whether BPC1/BPC2 directly regulates GALS1 expression, we 347 quantified the expression of GALS1 in the bpc1-1 bpc2 double mutant, BPC1 348 overexpressors and BPC2 overexpressors with or without NaCl treatment. The 349 350 transcript of GALS1 was markedly increased in bpc1-1 bpc2 double mutant, while GALS1 expression was repressed in BPC1 overexpressors and BPC2 overexpressors 351 both in the absence and presence of NaCl (Figure 6D). Furthermore, we analyzed the 352  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan level in these genotypes. In agreement with the *GALS1* expression, the 353  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan level was increased in the *bpc1-1 bpc2* double mutant, while the 354  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan level was significantly suppressed in *BPC1* and *BPC2* overexpressors 355 (Figure 6E and 6F). These results demonstrate that BPC1/BPC2 positively affects the 356 salt tolerance by repressing the *GALS1* expression to modulate the  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan 357 358 accumulation.

To further determine the number of other genes regulated by BPC1/BPC2 proteins in response to salt stress, an RNA sequencing assay for both the wild type and the

*bpc1-1 bpc2* mutant exposed to salt stress was performed. The RNA-seq data showed 361 362 that 1838 genes were up-regulated and 1646 genes were down-regulated in the wild type after NaCl treatment. In the *bpc1-1 bpc2* double mutant, 1839 genes were 363 up-regulated and 1422 genes were down-regulated after NaCl treatment (Figure 364 S13A). Subsequent comparisons identified 629 genes (365 activated genes and 264 365 repressed genes) that were differentially expressed in NaCl-treated bpc1-1 bpc2 366 mutant roots compared with NaCl-treated wild type roots (Figure S13B and Data S1). 367 According to a gene ontology (GO) analysis the BPC1/BPC2-dependent 629 368 differentially expressed genes in response to NaCl treatment could be classified into a 369 diverse range of categories: 182 genes (28.9%) in response to stimulus, 334 genes 370 (53.1%) in cellular process, 15 genes (2.4%) associated with growth, 11 genes (1.7%) 371 372 in immune system process, and so on (Figure S13C and Data S1). Therefore, these results reveal that BPC1/BPC2 have a significant impact on the global gene 373 expression profile under salt stress. 374

### 376 *GALS1* is epistatic to *BPC1/BPC2*

Our results indicate that the BPC1/BPC2-GALS1 transcriptional cascade is crucial for the salt sensitivity via accumulating  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan in *Arabidopsis* roots. To confirm the genetic relationship between BPC1/BPC2 and GALS1, we crossed the *bpc1-1 bpc2* double mutant to the *gals1-1* single mutant to obtain the *bpc1-1 bpc2 gals1-1* triple mutant (Figure S14). The transcripts of *BPC1*, *BPC2* and *GALS1* were all disrupted in *bpc1-1 bpc2 gals1-1* triple mutant (Figure 7A).

Next, we investigated the salt sensitivity of wild type, gals1-1, bpc1-1 bpc2 and 383 384 bpc1-1 bpc2 gals1-1 mutants. As shown in Figure 7B, the primary root growth of *bpc1-1 bpc2 gals1-1* triple mutant was comparable to that of *gals1-1* single mutant 385 under NaCl treatment, indicating that BPC1/BPC2 is an upstream regulator of GALS1 386 and that both genes act in the same pathway. After detecting the  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan 387 fluorescence in different genotypes, we found that the  $\beta$ -1.4-galactan level in *bpc1-1* 388 *bpc2 gals1-1* triple mutant was also as low as that of the *gals1-1* single mutant, even 389 though the *bpc1-1 bpc2* double mutant showed a high  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan level (Figure 7C 390

and 7D). Therefore, we conclude that *GALS1* is genetically epistatic to *BPC1/BPC2* with respect to the control of salt sensitivity as well as accumulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan in the root.

### 395 **Discussion**

 $\beta$ -1,4-galactan is one of the major cell wall polysaccharides and generally found as a 396 side chain of rhamnogalacturonan I. A few studies have shown that  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan 397 play a role in plant growth and development (Harholt et al., 2010; McCartney et al., 398 2003; Moneo-Sánchez et al., 2019; Øbro et al., 2009; Roach et al., 2011; Ulvskov et 399 al., 2005). However, *Arabidopsis* mutants essentially devoid of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan show 400 surprisingly normal growth and development (Ebert et al., 2018), suggesting that the 401 402 important function of this polysaccharide would be more obvious under specific growth conditions, e.g. abiotic stress conditions. Here, we demonstrated a specific 403 function of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan in salt hypersensitivity. 404

Based on the modulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan occurrence by growth inhibitors in 405 406 Arabidopsis roots, McCartney et al. (2003) proposed a hypothesis that  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan might function in cell elongation. A recent study found that  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan turnover 407 occurred during cell elongation in Arabidopsis etiolated hypocotyls and floral stem 408 internodes (Moneo-Sánchez et al., 2019). These studies could suggest a link between 409 the amount of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan and the root elongation in response to salt stress. In this 410 study, we found that salt stress resulted in an increase in the amounts of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan 411 detected in the root tip of Arabidopsis (Figure 1). Epitope detection in 412 immunofluorescence microscopy can be affected by masking by other polymers 413 414 (Verhertbruggen et al, 2017). However, the detection of the LM5 epitope, the sugar composition analyses of root cell walls, and the GALS1 expression levels, were 415 always correlated, and we did not see evidence of a change in other pectic 416 polysaccharides that could have affected masking effects. To date, three GALACTAN 417 SYNTHASE genes, GALS1, GALS2 and GALS3, were reported to synthesize 418  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan (Ebert et al., 2018; Liwanag et al., 2012). However, only *GALS1* was 419 induced by salt stress and the NaCl-induced accumulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan was 420

dependent on the GALS1 expression (Figure 3). Thus, our data is consistent with a 421 422 salt-induced accumulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan resulting from increased GALS1 expression, which ultimately resulted in a negative effect on salt tolerance (Figure 2 423 and Figure 3). Nevertheless, the endo- $\beta$ -1,4-galactanase digestion (Figure 1B) and 424 LM5 epitope detection (Figure 1D) might be affected by accessibility, we cannot 425 exclude that there are also effects on accessibility of the  $\beta$ -1,4-galactans to enzymes 426 and antibodies. The application of D-Gal, which increased  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan levels in 427 428 Arabidopsis, indeed aggravated the suppression of primary root growth caused by salt stress (Rösti et al., 2007) (Figure 3 and Figure S5). These results clearly suggest that 429  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan synthesized by GALS1 aggravates salt sensitivity. 430

 $\beta$ -1,4-galactan served as the matrix polysaccharides entrapped by cellulose 431 432 microfibrils during crystallization (Mellerowicz and Gorshkova, 2012) and trimming of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan by  $\beta$ -1,4-galactosidase is important for final cellulose crystallization 433 (Roach et al., 2011). Ebert et al. (2018) found that loss or accumulation of 434  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan led to minor changes in crystalline cellulose in *Arabidopsis* seedlings 435 436 under normal conditions. In our study, we found that GALS1 affected the crystalline cellulose synthesis in Arabidopsis under salt stress (Figure 4). However, how 437 NaCl-induced  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan affect the crystalline cellulose synthesis is presently 438 unclear. Sorek et al. (2015) found that the esterification levels of pectin affect the 439 440 cellulose synthesis, possibly through modifying the interaction of pectin and cellulose. Pectic polymers could directly tether or coat the cellulose microfibrils through neutral 441 sugar side chains containing  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan (Lin et al., 2015; Zykwinska et al., 2007). 442 It is possible that  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan somehow directly affect the synthesis/deposition of 443 444 the cellulose at the plasma membrane in response to salt stress. Notably,  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan can bind to cellulose extensively and preferably, therefore, limiting the 445 access of other matrix polysaccharides including xyloglucan to cellulose microfibrils 446 (Lin et al., 2015). Another possible explanation is that  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan induced by salt 447 stress modulates the crystalline cellulose via altering the cellulose interaction with 448 other matrix polysaccharides such as xyloglucan. Moneo-Sánchez et al. (2019) 449 showed that  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan remodeling affected the degree of interaction between 450

cellulose and xyloglucan, and the xyloglucan structure during elongation. Our 451 452 previous study has showed that the modified-xyloglucan synthesis potentially affected 453 crystalline cellulose synthesis in response to salt stress (Yan et al., 2019). Sustained cellulose biosynthesis was crucial for plants to maintain root growth under salt stress 454 condition (Kesten et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2016). Based on our 455 study and what was previously reported, we present a model that  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan 456 induced by salt stress inhibits the root growth via disrupting the crystalline cellulose 457 synthesis. Further studies are required to fully understand the exact mechanism of 458 how the accumulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan induced by salt stress disrupts cellulose 459 synthesis in plants. 460

Transcriptional regulation is an important mechanism in plant response to salt stress. 461 462 The transcript level of GALS1 was significantly induced by salt stress (Figure 2), therefore, it is expected to be affected largely by transcriptional regulation. Here, we 463 identified two transcriptional factors, BPC1 and BPC2, which directly bind to the 464 GALS1 promoter (Figure 5). BPC1 functions in ovule and embryo development by 465 466 directly inhibiting the expression of several key transcription factors, such as INO (INNER NO OUTER), STK (SEEDSTICK) and FUSCA3 (Kooiker et al., 2005; 467 Meister et al., 2004; Wu et al., 2020). Class I BPCs affect the development of 468 inflorescence meristem suppressing 469 by the expression of STM (SHOOTMERISTEMLESS) and BP (BREVIPEDICELLUS) (Simonini and Kater, 470 2014). The whole members of BPCs protein directly repress ABI4 (ABSCISIC ACID 471 *INSENSITIVE4*) to modulate root development (Mu et al., 2017b). Here, BPC1/BPC2 472 repressed *GALS1* expression and the accumulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan induced by NaCl 473 474 treatment (Figure 6). Genetic analysis further showed that the salt hypersensitivity of bpc1-1 bpc2 double mutant was dependent on GALS1 (Figure 7). However, the 475 *GALS1* transcript levels and the accumulation of  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan in the *bpc1-1 bpc2* 476 double mutant, BPC1 overexpressors and BPC2 overexpressors were still clearly 477 induced by salt stress, which implied that some other TFs modulating GALS1 478 transcript might exist. In addition to BPC1 and BPC2, we also screened some other 479 TFs, including CBF2 and ARF2, which might directly bind to the GALS1 promoter in 480

481 yeast (Table S2). Future work needs to identify additional candidate TFs in regulating
482 *GALS1* expression in response to salt stress.

483 Based on these results, we propose a new regulatory mechanism by which  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan regulated by BPC1/BPC2-GALS1 module aggravates salt sensitivity in 484 Arabidopsis. When plants are under NaCl-free conditions, BPC1/BPC2 binds to the 485 486 GA rich sequence of GALS1 promoter and represses GALS1 transcription to maintain a low  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan level with no effect on crystalline cellulose synthesis. When 487 plants are subjected to salt stress, the transcription level of BPC1/BPC2 is rapidly 488 reduced, their suppression of GALS1 transcription is relieved, and then synthesis of 489  $\beta$ -1,4-galactan is increased. This in turn cause disruption of the normal crystalline 490 cellulose synthesis, and finally the plants with compromised crystalline cellulose 491 492 biosynthesis show salt hypersensitivity (Figure 7E). With this mechanism in play, it is somewhat counterintuitive that GALS1 expression is induced under salt stress, since 493 the change leads to increased salt sensitivity. One possibility is that the reduced 494 growth observed under salt stress is in fact an adaptive response that ultimately 495 496 improves the survival and fitness of the plants. Future studies with detailed analysis of plant response throughout the life cycle of the plant to different salt regimes will be 497 required to fully understand the relationship between cell wall properties, root growth 498 and plant performance. 499

### 552 Cell Wall Preparation

Four-day-old seedlings were transferred to 1/2 MS plates containing 125 mM NaCl 553 for another three days. The root materials were harvested into 96% ethanol and 554 incubated for 30 min at 100°C to inactivate cell wall-degrading enzymes. The tissue 555 556 was homogenized using a Retsch MM200 mixer mill and centrifuged. The pellet was washed with 100% ethanol and twice with a mixture of chloroform and methanol 557 (2:1), followed by four successive washes with 100% ethanol and acetone. The starch 558 559 in the samples was degraded with  $\alpha$ -amylase, amyloglucosidase, and pullulanase (Megazyme, Ireland) as described previously (Liwanag et al., 2012). The pellet was 560 air-dried overnight and was referred to as AIR (alcohol-insoluble residue). 561

### 563 Cell Wall Fractionation and Composition Analyses

Pectic polysaccharides were extracted from AIR by combining three hot water extracts at 100°C for 1 h each. Subsequently, hemicellulose polysaccharides were extracted by treating the remaining pellet three times with 4% (w/v) KOH containing 0.02% (w/v) NaBH<sub>4</sub> at 25°C for 12 h. The uronic acid content in the pectic polysaccharides was determined by M-hydroxy-diphenyl assay (Blumenkrantz and Asboe-Hansen, 1973). The total polysaccharide content in the hemicellulose fraction was determined using the phenol sulfuric acid method (Dubois et al., 1956). About 2 mg of AIR was hydrolyzed in 2 M TFA at 121°C for 1 h. The insoluble material was further hydrolyzed with Updegraff reagent at 100°C for 30 min. Then, the crystalline cellulose content was quantified by the anthrone assay (Updegraff, 1969).
