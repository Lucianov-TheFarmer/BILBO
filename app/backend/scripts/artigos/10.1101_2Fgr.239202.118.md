#### 1 **Abstract**

2 Diverse RNA 5′ ends are generated through both transcriptional and post-transcriptional processes. These 3 important modes of gene regulation often vary across cell types, and can contribute to the diversification 4 of transcriptomes and thus cellular differentiation. Therefore, the identification of primary and processed 5 5′ ends of RNAs is important for their functional characterization. Methods have been developed to 6 profile either RNA 5′ ends from primary transcripts or the products of RNA degradation genome-wide. 7 However, these approaches either require high amounts of starting RNA or are performed in the absence 8 of paired gene-body mRNA-seq data. This limits current efforts in RNA 5′ end annotation to whole 9 tissues and can prevent accurate RNA 5′ end classification due to biases in the datasets. To enable the 10 accurate identification and precise classification of RNA 5′ ends from standard and low-input RNA, we 11 developed a next-generation sequencing based method called nanoPARE and associated software. By 12 integrating RNA 5′ end information from nanoPARE with gene-body mRNA-seq data from the same 13 RNA sample, our method enables the identification of transcription start sites at single-nucleotide 14 resolution from single-cell levels of total RNA, as well as small RNA-mediated cleavage events from at 15 least 10,000-fold less total RNA compared to conventional approaches. NanoPARE can therefore be used 16 to accurately profile transcription start sites, noncapped RNA 5′ ends and small RNA targeting events 17 from individual tissue types. As a proof-of-principle, we utilized nanoPARE to improve *Arabidopsis*  <sup>18</sup>*thaliana* RNA 5′ end annotations and quantify microRNA-mediated cleavage events across five different 19 flower tissues.

#### 1 **Introduction**

2 Diverse RNA 5′ ends are generated during and after transcription as the result of a variety of gene 3 regulatory functions. Alternative transcription start sites (TSS) can generate RNA isoforms that 4 differentially impact cellular activities. Alternative TSS have also been demonstrated to affect 5 downstream translation and protein function through inclusion or exclusion of regulatory N-terminal 6 peptides such as upstream open reading frames or protein localization sequences (Haberle et al. 2014; 7 Cheng et al. 2018; Ushijima et al. 2017). Post-transcriptional maturation of non-coding RNAs such as 8 those involved in splicing (snoRNA) or translation (rRNAs, tRNAs) also generates diverse RNA 5′ ends 9 (Granneman et al. 2011; Wang et al. 1988; Henras et al. 2015; Filipowicz and Pogacić 2002). Moreover, 10 small regulatory RNAs such as microRNAs (miRNAs) and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) can mediate 11 endonucleolytic cleavage of target RNAs and are important regulators of development, genome stability 12 and defense (Bartel 2004; Borges and Martienssen 2015). Therefore, the identification of RNA 5′ ends 13 derived from transcriptional and post-transcriptional processes is important for the functional 14 characterization of RNA molecules. 15 Next-generation sequencing (NGS) based methods have recently been used to identify RNA 5′ 16 ends genome-wide. For example, TSS profiling using Cap Analysis of Gene Expression (CAGE) led to 17 the annotation of TSS from polyadenylated mRNA and long non-coding RNA (Andersson et al. 2014;

18 Hon et al. 2017). TSS profiling has also provided fundamental insights into how RNA isoforms with

19 different 5′ ends modulate gene function, as recently demonstrated in *Arabidopsis thaliana* (Arabidopsis)

20 where the phytochrome photoreceptor regulates alternative promoter usage in a light-dependent manner

21 that ultimately leads to gene products with distinct functions and subcellular localization (Ushijima et al.

22 2017). Methods referred to as PARE (Parallel Analysis of RNA Ends), or degradome, sequencing enrich

23 for 5′ monophosphorylated RNAs, which include small RNA (sRNA) mediated cleavage products. PARE

24 methods therefore enable the genome-wide profiling of sRNA target sites and have been instrumental in

25 characterizing the mechanistic basis of sRNA-mediated developmental and physiological processes

26 (Addo-Quaye et al. 2008; German et al. 2008; Gregory et al. 2008).

#### 14 **Results**

#### 15 **RNA 5**

′ **end enrichment from low-input RNA samples**  16 To profile RNA 5′ ends genome-wide from low amounts of total RNA, we developed an NGS based 17 method called nanoPARE (Parallel Analysis of RNA 5′ Ends from low-input RNA; Fig. 1; see Methods). 18 First, we followed the Smart-seq2 protocol through cDNA preamplification to produce full-length cDNAs 19 with template switching oligonucleotide (TSO) sequences at their 5′ ends (Picelli et al. 2013). Tn5 transposase was then used to fragment the cDNA and ligate adaptors for NGS library preparation (Ramsköld et al. 2012). To selectively amplify the 5′ ends of the cDNAs, we performed PCR on the tagmented products using primers complementary to TSO and inserted transposase adapter sequences. The resulting amplicons were then used for final PCR amplification with indexed Illumina-adapter primers for next generation sequencing. Additionally, tagmented products corresponding to nonterminal, or body, regions of transcripts were amplified according to the Smart-seq2 method (Picelli et al. 2013).

By combining the 5′ end and body sequence information from the cDNA of a single sample, the 5′ ends of RNA can be precisely identified at single-nucleotide resolution as demonstrated below.

### 3 **Identification of capped and noncapped 5**′ **end features**

4 Template switching readily occurs at the 5′ ends of RNA templates with or without 7-methylguanosine 5 (m7G) cap structures (Cloonan et al. 2008; Harbers et al. 2013). We reasoned that template switching 6 could be used to identify RNA 5′ end features genome-wide regardless of their cap structure. However, 7 spurious 5′ ends could be produced by a variety of technical artifacts, including random fragmentation of 8 RNA in vitro, stalling of the reverse transcriptase enzyme, PCR amplification bias, or "strand invasion" 9 by the template-switching oligo (Cocquet et al. 2006; Tang et al. 2012), which make it difficult to 10 distinguish biological signal from noise. A major source of nonrandom bias is internal template switching 11 at sites complementary to the TSO 3′ end, and signal at these sites can be removed in silico (Tang et al. 12 2012). For other sources of noise, we developed a "scaling factor" for comparing nanoPARE libraries to a 13 counterpart Smart-seq2 library from the same cDNA (see Methods). The scaling factor estimates the 14 expected ratio of 5′ end containing cDNA fragments to gene body fragments after tagmentation with the 15 assumption that all RNA is full-length. After applying this scaling factor, genomic regions are identified 16 that produce more terminal signal than nonterminal signal, and these regions are isolated as 5′ end 17 features using the software EndGraph (see Methods).

18 We applied EndGraph to a paired collection of nanoPARE/Smart-seq2 libraries prepared from 5 19 ng of total RNA isolated from Arabidopsis floral buds in biological triplicate and reproducibly identified 20 a total of 22,852 5′ end features from polyadenylated RNA in at least 2 of the 3 biological replicates 21 (Supplemental Data S1; Supplemental Data S2). Reverse transcription produces untemplated cytosines at 22 the template 5′ terminus that can base pair with a m7G cap to yield an untemplated upstream guanosine 23 (uuG) in the cDNA between the TSO and genome-matching sequence (Cumbie et al. 2015; de Rie et al. 24 2017). These uuGs can be used to filter 5′ end data produced by template-switching protocols and isolate 25 5′-capped transcription start sites (Cumbie et al. 2015). Indeed, uuG consistently appeared in roughly 15% 26 of nanoPARE reads per library, occurring from 4 to 10 times more frequently than other nucleotides

1 (Supplemental Fig. S1). We analyzed the total proportion of reads in all 5′ end features that contained 2 uuG, and we could observe a striking bimodal distribution, with most 5′ end features (20,679; 90.5%) 3 containing >10% uuG reads (Fig. 2A). We hypothesized that this bimodal distribution indicated two 4 populations of RNA 5′ ends: high-uuG "capped" features that contain an m7G cap, and low-uuG 5 "noncapped" features that contain alternative 5′ end structures, predominantly monophosphates. The yeast 6 Xrn1 exoribonuclease specifically degrades 5′-monophosphorylated RNA in the 5′-to-3′ direction, leaving 7 capped RNA intact (Nagarajan et al. 2013). We performed nanoPARE on the same total RNA samples 8 after subjecting them to exonucleolytic digestion by Xrn1 and found that, as expected, nearly all capped 9 features showed no significant change in abundance after Xrn1 digestion (Fig. 2B). In contrast, a global 10 decrease was observed in the abundance of putative noncapped features after Xrn1 digestion, with a mean 11 reduction of 19.5%, and a statistically significant reduction in 314 of 2,173 noncapped features (14.5%; 12 Fig. 2C).

### 13 **Genomic distributions of capped and noncapped 5**′ **features**

14 Capped and noncapped features have distinct distributions in the genome. Capped features have an even 15 distribution across the non-centromeric region of the nuclear genome, whereas noncapped features are 16 highly concentrated on the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes, and more sparsely dispersed in the 17 nuclear genome (Supplemental Fig. S2). The 5′ ends of chloroplast and mitochondrial RNA do not 18 possess m7G caps (Monde et al. 2000; Grohmann et al. 1978; Legen et al. 2002). Accordingly, the 5′ 19 features mapping to chloroplasts and mitochondria were classified as 94% and 95% noncapped, 20 respectively. Moreover, capped and noncapped features localized to different gene types (Fig. 2D). 21 Consistent with caps being associated only with RNA polymerase II (polII) transcripts, nanoPARE 22 identified predominantly capped features for mRNA, long noncoding RNA, and primary microRNA 23 (Supplemental Fig. S3). Several noncapped features mapping to pre-tRNA and rRNA loci, which are not 24 transcribed by polII, could also be identified (Supplemental Fig. S4, S5). While these products normally 25 do not possess a poly(A) tail, a subset will be transiently polyadenylated by the TRAMP complex prior to 26 their degradation (Hopper et al. 2010), and nanoPARE may be detecting this subset. Even within a class

1 of RNA, the behavior of capped and noncapped features is quite distinct. For example, a majority of 2 snoRNAs in Arabidopsis are transcribed in tandem arrays of two or more species from a single polII 3 precursor (Dieci et al. 2009). EndGraph identified both a noncapped feature at the mature 5′ terminus and 4 a capped feature upstream of many annotated snoRNAs (Fig. 2D). Several primary snoRNAs contained 5 additional noncapped features not predicted by TAIR10 annotations, and these are supported by genome-6 wide profiling of noncoding RNAs (Supplemental Fig. S3) (Wang et al. 2014).

7 Protein-coding mRNA also displays a distinct distribution of capped and noncapped 5′ end 8 features. Among capped features overlapping nuclear protein-coding genes, 95% mapped to the annotated 9 5′ UTRs (Fig. 2E). Noncapped features showed an opposite trend, accumulating closer to the 3′ termini of 10 protein-coding genes, particularly just upstream of the stop codon. This pattern closely resembles the 11 reported pattern of co-translational decay detected in PARE data, which is mediated by the major 12 cytoplasmic exonuclease EXORIBONUCLEASE4 (XRN4) (Yu et al. 2016; Hou et al. 2016). To test 13 whether we detect the steady state by-products of XRN4 digestion with nanoPARE, we generated 14 nanoPARE and Smart-seq2 libraries for floral buds of *xrn4-5* mutants and performed *de novo* 5′ feature 15 identification. All features identified in wild-type floral buds (with or without Xrn1 digestion) and/or <sup>16</sup>*xrn4-5* floral buds were combined to produce a unified set of 5′ end features (Supplemental Data S2). 17 Because XRN4 is restricted to the cytoplasm (Kastenmayer and Green 2000), non-cytoplasmic 5′- 18 monophosphorylated ends should not be increased in *xrn4-5* loss-of-function mutants. As predicted, non-19 cytoplasmic 5′ end features associated with mature or primary noncoding RNAs including nuclear 3′ 20 cleavage products of pri-snoRNA, pre-miRNA, as well as mitochondrial and chloroplast pri-tRNA 21 (Supplemental Data S3) were not increased in *xrn4-5* floral buds relative to wild-type. In contrast, these 22 features were sensitive to Xrn1 digestion in vitro (Supplemental Fig. S6). 23 Consistent with wild-type XRN4 digesting full-length decapped mRNA, noncapped features 24 upstream and adjacent to stop codons were globally decreased in relative abundance in *xrn4-5* mutants, 25 concomitant with a relative increase in reads contained by capped features (Supplemental Fig. S7).

26 Together, these trends predict an increase in full-length transcripts with 5′-monophosphates in *xrn4*

1 mutants. Indeed, the accumulation of full-length decapped mRNA has been reported for some transcripts 2 in *xrn4* mutants (Gregory et al. 2008). We reanalyzed public PARE data from wild-type and *xrn4-5* 3 inflorescences (German et al. 2008) and found a global average increase by more than three-fold in the 4 proportion of 5′ monophosphorylated RNA ends mapping to all capped features defined by nanoPARE 5 (Supplemental Fig. S7). Overall, the capped and noncapped 5′ features identified with nanoPARE support 6 the existing model of XRN4 as a general RNA decay factor that acts downstream of decapping.

#### 7 **TSS characterization**

8 To test the reproducibility of nanoPARE to detect 5′ end features from low-input RNA, we generated 9 nanoPARE libraries from a dilution series of the original floral bud total RNA in triplicate: 1 ng, 100 pg, 10 and 10 pg of total RNA input, which is typically less than or equal to the amount of total RNA found in a 11 single cell (Brennecke et al. 2013; Ramsköld et al. 2012). We performed *de novo* feature identification 12 using the Smart-seq2 libraries from 5 ng of RNA as a background model. To assess the sensitivity of the 13 method at recovering genuine capped transcription start sites, we compared the capped features of the 14 dilution series to a set of 9,326 transcription start sites identified by a cap-specific 5′ sequencing protocol 15 (Paired-End Analysis of Transcription Start Sites; PEAT) applied to whole Arabidopsis roots (Morton et 16 al. 2014). As a baseline, we tested whether the Arabidopsis reference annotations, TAIR10 and 17 Araport11, contained a transcript model with a 5′ end within 50 bp of a given PEAT peak. TAIR10 18 detected 75% of PEAT peaks under this definition (Fig. 3A). The more recent Araport11 annotations 19 performed much worse at accurately detecting transcription start sites. Only 2,239 (24%) of PEAT peaks 20 fell within 50 bp of any transcript 5′ ends defined in Araport11, which is likely due to the systematic 21 overextension of transcript UTRs during transcript model assembly. Despite the different tissue type and 22 preparation method, nanoPARE outperformed both reference annotations at detecting experimentally 23 validated transcription start sites, down to 1 ng of total RNA (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, when comparing the 24 precision of peaks, all nanoPARE dilutions, including those generated from 10 pg of total RNA, had a 25 higher likelihood of agreeing with the PEAT data on the exact nucleotide position of the transcription 26 start site peak (Fig. 3A, B). Finally, we examined the sensitivity of the method by comparing identified

1 capped features with the transcript abundance as measured by Smart-seq2 (Fig. 3C). Remarkably, 2 nanoPARE reproducibly identified at least one capped feature overlapping 76.6% of all protein-coding 3 transcripts detected at or above 0.1 transcripts per million (TPM) when libraries were generated from 5 ng 4 of total RNA (18,295/23,900 genes). This value increased to 91.9% for transcripts detected at a threshold 5 of 1 TPM (18,111/19,706 genes), and 99.0% for transcripts of at least 10 TPM (11,165/11,294 genes). In 6 contrast, only 0.4% of transcripts not detected with Smart-seq2 (0 TPM, 20/5,419 genes) were assigned a 7 capped feature. Overall, the 5 ng, 1 ng, and 100 pg samples performed similarly well, especially for 8 robustly detected transcripts. Sensitivity reduced substantially between 100 pg and 10 pg of total RNA 9 without affecting the precision of the capped features identified (Fig. 3A, B). Multiple distinct TSS could 10 even be identified at all dilutions for certain highly expressed genes (Fig. 3D; Supplemental Data S2). 11 Therefore, nanoPARE capped features represent genuine transcription start sites, and can be used for 12 transcription start site annotation with as little as 10 pg of input RNA. 13 **Detecting sRNA-mediated cleavage sites**  14 In plants, Argonaute-bound sRNAs recognize highly complementary 20-22 nt target sites and mediate target RNA cleavage precisely between the 10th and 11th 15 nucleotides of the sRNA-target duplex (Jones-16 Rhoades and Bartel 2004; Llave et al. 2002; Kasschau et al. 2003). #### 3 **Tissue-specific miRNA-mediated cleavage sites**

4 To test whether nanoPARE can detect small RNA-mediated cleavage sites that occur in specific tissue 5 types, we applied the method to five different tissues dissected from whole flowers immediately after 6 anthesis (Fig. 5A). The flower is comprised of four concentric whorls of tissues, which are specified by 7 three transcription factor groups functioning in overlapping domains within the developing primordia. 8 The coordinated action of these genes is described as the "ABC model" of flower development (Bowman 9 et al. 1991b; Coen and Meyerowitz 1991). These transcription factors are known as group A, B, or C 10 genes if they are expressed in the outer two whorls, middle two whorls, or inner two whorls of the 11 developing flower, respectively. Arabidopsis possesses two A genes, two B genes, and a single C gene 12 whose transcript spatial distributions are maintained through late flower development except for 13 *APETALA2* (*AP2*) mRNA, which has been observed in all four whorls of mature flowers (Jofuku et al. 14 1994; Mandel et al. 1992; Jack et al. 1992; Bowman et al. 1991a; Goto and Meyerowitz 1994). Upon 15 comparing the relative abundance of 5′ capped transcript ends, we observed that nanoPARE faithfully 16 recapitulated the expected spatial transcript patterns of all five homeotic genes (Fig. 5B). Therefore, these 17 datasets can be used to quantify tissue-specific variation in RNA abundance.

18 Tissue-specific variation of miRNA-target interactions on a genome-wide scale has not been 19 reported in flowers, but individual studies indicate that miRNAs can suppress their targets in a tissue-20 specific manner (Wollmann et al. 2010; Wu et al. 2006; Liang et al. 2014). Upon performing nanoPARE 21 on 10 ng of total RNA from either whole flowers or five individual floral tissues, we identified 41 22 miRNA target sites in at least two biological replicates of the same floral tissue (high-confidence sites; 23 Fig. 5C and Supplemental Data S4). While the target cleavage sites directed by three miRNAs (miR160, 24 miR171, and miR173) are robustly detected across all tissues examined, over half of the high-confidence 25 interactions were enriched in specific tissues. #### 19 **Discussion**

20 NanoPARE can accurately profile RNA 5′ ends genome-wide from low amounts of total RNA. Because 21 TSS partition the genome into transcribed and cis-regulatory regions, their accurate identification is 22 critical for transcriptome assembly and prediction of regulatory binding sites. Moreover, TSS can vary 23 among cell types and thus their identification from low-input RNA samples can increase our 24 understanding of diverse RNA processing events and corresponding functions. NanoPARE's integrative 25 approach of combining RNA 5′ end enrichment and full-length Smart-seq2 datasets from the same sample 26 enables TSS identification at single-nucleotide resolution from single-cell to standard levels of total RNA.
