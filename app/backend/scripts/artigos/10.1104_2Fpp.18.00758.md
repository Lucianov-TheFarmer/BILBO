- **One-sentence summary:** Consequences of transcription out of bounds: a retrograde signal can
- trigger RNA Polymerase II read-through, upregulating the expression of downstream genes.

**Short title:** Pol II read-through and retrograde signaling

# Abstract

 In plants, the molecular function(s) of the nuclear localised 5′–3′ EXORIBONUCLEASES (XRNs) are unclear; however, their activity is reported to have a significant effect on gene expression and SAL1-mediated retrograde signaling. Using Parallel Analysis of RNA Ends (PARE) we documented a dramatic increase in uncapped RNA substrates of the XRNs in both *sal1* and *xrn2xrn3* mutants. We found that a major consequence of reducing SAL1 or XRN activity was RNA Polymerase II (Pol II) 3′ read-through. This occurred at 72% of expressed genes, demonstrating a major genome-wide role for the XRN-torpedo model of transcription termination in Arabidopsis. Read-through is speculated to have a negative effect on transcript abundance; however, we did not observe this. Rather, we identified a strong association between read-though and increased transcript abundance of tandemly orientated downstream genes, strongly correlated with the proximity (<1,000 bp) and expression of the upstream gene. We observed read-though in the proximity of 903 genes upregulated in the *sal1-*8 retrograde signaling mutant; thus, this phenomenon may directly account for up to 23% of genes upregulated in *sal1-*8. Using *APX2* and *AT5G43770* as exemplars, we genetically uncoupled read-through loci from downstream genes to validate the principle of read-through mediated mRNA regulation, providing one mechanism by which an ostensibly post-transcriptional exoribonuclease that targets uncapped RNAs could modulate gene expression.

*Keywords:* Retrograde signaling; RNA degradation, transcription, RNA polymerase, gene regulation, transcription termination; gene expression; plant; Arabidopsis.

# Introduction

 Retrograde signaling is the process by which the chloroplasts of plant cells communicate with the nucleus to direct changes in gene expression. This mechanism is required to coordinate chloroplast function during cell differentiation and development (biogenic control) and response to stressful environments (operational contro[l; Pogson et al., 2008; Woodson and Chory, 2012;](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/RbnwC+0W5Wf+Fdc68)  [Chan et al., 2016b\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/RbnwC+0W5Wf+Fdc68) In this way, the chloroplast can act as an environmental stress sensor and utilize retrograde signaling pathways to initiate acclimation responses. Previously, we have described the SAL1-PAP-XRN retrograde signaling pathway [\(Estavillo et al., 2011; Chan et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/K7CH3+U5Fe+z6OcW)  [2016a; Pornsiriwong et al., 2017\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/K7CH3+U5Fe+z6OcW) in which the redox-regulated SAL1 protein controls the levels of the signaling molecule 3′-phosphoadenosine 5′-phosphate (PAP). PAP can move from the chloroplast to the nucleus where it is it thought to attenuate the activity of the nuclear 5′-3′ exoribonucleases (XRNs), which in turn leads to changes in gene expression. However, the mechanism by which this pathway functions in the nucleus is unclear, particularly how an ostensibly post-transcriptional exoribonuclease that targets uncapped RNAs could modulate gene expression.

 The XRNs are a family of 5′-3′ exoribonucleases highly conserved and unique to eukaryotes that are responsible for 5′ processing or turnover of a broad range of uncapped RNAs. The XRNs display highly processive 5′-3′ activity toward 5′ monophosphorylated RNA substrates [\(Zuo and Deutscher, 2001; Nagarajan et al., 2013\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/1IKXR+PNkN7) In Arabidopsis there are three XRN: nuclear-localised XRN2 and XRN3, and cytoplasmic XRN4 [\(Kastenmayer and Green, 2000\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/n1ao0) Investigations in Arabidopsis and yeast have established that nuclear 5′ exoribonuclease activity is essential for viability [\(Gy et al., 2007; Zakrzewska-Placzek et al., 2010\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/LPsU+lf1h) However, beyond knowledge of their 5′ exonuclease activity, the function and substrates of the nuclear XRNs is enigmatic in plants. In a screen to identify repressors of Post Transcriptional Gene Silencing

 (PTGS), Gy et al. [\(2007\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/LPsU/?noauthor=1) discovered that loss of XRN2 and XRN3 (and SAL1) promotes transgene silencing in a manner similar to that previously reported for XRN4 [\(Gazzani et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/9DVMw)  [2004\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/9DVMw) In the same study it was also demonstrated that XRN2 and XRN3 degrade hairpin loops excised during pre-miRNA processing. XRN2 also plays a role in rRNA processing [\(Zakrzewska-Placzek et al., 2010\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/lf1h)

 In Arabidopsis, limited accumulation of intergenic transcripts downstream of several thousand genes was observed in *sal1* and *xrn* mutants and was hypothesised to be due to impairment of a surveillance mechanism for aberrant transcripts [\(Kurihara et al., 2012\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/xd449) Analogous observations have been reported in yeast for a variety of Xrn1-sensitive unstable transcripts (XUT[s; van Dijk et al., 2011\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/NB2du) It is well established that RNA surveillance can take place in the nucleus, where it involves both Rat1/Xrn2 and the nuclear exosome [\(LaCava et al., 2005;](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/GzEMx+8hMmv+mcnDK+nP4j3+HJFBo+mrJ2B)  [Wyers et al., 2005; Houseley and Tollervey, 2009; Jiao et al., 2010; Jimeno-González et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/GzEMx+8hMmv+mcnDK+nP4j3+HJFBo+mrJ2B)  [2010; Davidson et al., 2012\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/GzEMx+8hMmv+mcnDK+nP4j3+HJFBo+mrJ2B) .

 Intriguingly, in addition to the possibility that the XRNs are involved in turnover of intergenic transcripts, they may also be involved in their production. This hypothesis is based on reported interactions of Xrn2/Rat1 with RNA polymerase II in yeast, humans, and worms [\(Kim et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/hbFLU+QS2Vt+mcWjE)  [2004; West et al., 2004; Miki et al., 2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/hbFLU+QS2Vt+mcWjE) In this torpedo model of termination [\(Proudfoot,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/TXhRw)  [1989\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/TXhRw) following cleavage and polyadenylation of the mRNA, a 3′ nascent RNA is produced as Poll II continues transcription downstream of the polyadenylation (poly(A)) site. This RNA is cotranscriptionally decayed by Xrn2/Rat1 faster than Pol II can synthesise it, ultimately dislodging Pol II and removing the nascent non-coding RNA. However, the prevalence of the torpedo mechanism varies between species [\(Fong et al., 2015; Baejen et al., 2017; Miki et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/iBtON+9fQDV+mcWjE)  [2017\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/iBtON+9fQDV+mcWjE) and its functionality in plants is only now being tested. Moreover, the potential consequences of putative transcriptional read-through or proliferation of non-coding RNAs have  not been addressed in a systematic manner. These may include the production of small RNAs (sRNAs), which in turn could lead to Post Transcriptional Gene Silencing (PTGS) or RNA directed DNA Methylation (RdDM) [\(Gazzani et al., 2004; Gregory et al., 2008; Matzke and](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/9DVMw+uYMxf+s7U2T)  [Mosher, 2014\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/9DVMw+uYMxf+s7U2T) Likewise, read-though may cause generally wasteful or harmful transcription of intergenic space, or the interference of transcription of downstream genes due to Pol II competition, occlusion, or collision [\(Shearwin et al., 2005\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/og4l5) or even be linked to the activities of Pol IV and Pol V [\(Erhard et al., 2015; McKinlay et al., 2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/pxpzv+OTBjP)

 Whether or not the appearance and accumulation of intergenic non-coding RNAs in *xrn* and *sal1* mutants is due to alterations in Pol II termination is unresolved. Here, using a combination of total RNA, mRNA, and degradome short-read sequencing, bioinformatics, and genetic approaches, we elucidate the cause of intergenic non-coding RNA accumulation in *sal1* and *xrn* retrograde signaling mutants and the consequences for the expression of surrounding genes.

# Results

## **XRN-sensitive transcripts identified using PARE**

 Previously, mRNA profiling and correlation analysis has been used to infer the activities of the XRNs and the role of SAL1 in the accumulation of aberrant genic [\(Estavillo et al., 2011\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/U5Fe) and intergenic [\(Kurihara et al., 2012\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/xd449) transcripts. However, neither prior strategy evaluated whether there was an increase in uncapped genic and intergenic transcripts that can be directly linked to impaired 5′ degradation. To directly identify and characterise the substrates of the nuclear XRNs in Arabidopsis, we profiled mRNA and total RNA using RNA-seq, and the 5′ RNA degradome using PARE in the *xrn2-*1*xrn3-*3 (herein referred to as *xrn2xrn3*) and *sal1-*8 (also known as *alx8*) mutant backgrounds. PARE specifically captures 5′ uncapped, mono-phosphorylated RNA molecules which are the canonical substrates of 5′-exoribonucleases [\(Zuo and Deutscher, 2001;](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/1IKXR+PNkN7)  [Nagarajan et al., 2013\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/1IKXR+PNkN7) PARE libraries were constructed by first capturing polyadenylated RNA molecules [\(Zhai et al., 2014\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/x8sRX) indicating that both the genic and intergenic RNAs observed in these PARE libraries are polyadenylated. We observed an order of magnitude increase in the abundance of the degradome in *sal1-*8 and *xrn2xrn3* mutants relative to total RNA abundance (Fig. 1, A and B, Supplemental Fig. S1A) throughout the length of the chromosomes (Supplemental Fig. S1B), consistent with the accumulation of XRN substrates.

#### **Validating and quantifying intergenic transcript accumulation**

 PARE reads were frequently observed at the gene terminus and downstream thereof, as shown for *AT5G20630* (Fig 1B). The meta profile of all genes revealed this to be a genome-wide phenomena in *sal1-*8 and *xrn2xrn3* (Fig. 2A). Read accumulation in the 3′ genic region is consistent with 5′–3′ direction of RNA turnover and likely represents a snapshot of the cytoplasmic degradome, comprised of mRNAs undergoing decay (it should be noted that this may also arise in part due to polyA selection). In comparison, detection of intergenic reads may reflect a nuclear process such as transcriptional termination.

Intergenic reads were barely detectable in WT plants. An increase in intergenic transcript

abundance in the *sal1* and *xrn* mutants was observed in both PARE, poly(A) mRNA-seq

profiling (Fig. 2B), and to even greater levels in total RNA-seq (compare Fig. 2, B and C). This

suggests that intergenic transcripts of *sal1-*8 and *xrn2xrn3* are comprised of both poly(A) and

non-poly(A) RNAs.

 Given that intergenic transcripts were most abundant near the transcription termination site (TTS) declining to near undetectable levels by 1,000 bp downstream (Fig. 2A–C) we sought to test the hypothesis that perturbation to Pol II termination by SAL1-PAP-XRN signaling alters production of intergenic RNAs. First, to establish a baseline, we surveyed all detectable

 aberrant transcripts downstream of coding sequences in the *sal1-*8 and *xrn2xrn3* mutants. Using the total RNA-seq data set, for WT plants we could detect transcription products (>0.1 CPM) at 912 genes using an overlapping 500-bp window directly downstream of representative TAIR10 gene models. By contrast, a presence-absence test revealed that 10,243 genes in *sal1-* 8 and 3,205 in *xrn2xrn3* had 3′ intergenic fragments. Sequencing depth could affect detection of intergenic RNAs; however, each genotype had similar total mapped reads with 125 million for WT, 118 million *xrn2xrn3*, and 117 million for *sal1-*8, thus the datasets for each genotype are highly comparable (Supplemental Table S1).

#### **Defining putative read-through loci**

 To identify a high-confidence set of putative read-through loci (RTLs), significant changes in intergenic transcript abundance immediately downstream of annotated genes were calculated with reference to both genic abundance [\(Baejen et al., 2017; McKinlay et al., 2017\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/OTBjP+iBtON) and abundance in WT plants (false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05, absolute fold-change [FC] over WT > 2). Combining this analysis across total RNA-seq, mRNA-seq, and PARE datasets, we found evidence that impairment of SAL1 or XRN2 and XRN3 led to the accumulation of downstream intergenic RNAs at 14,718 genes, designated the putative RTL set (Fig. 2D, Supplemental Table S2). This strategy captured 84% of the ~2,000 prior reported loci [\(Kurihara et al., 2012\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/xd449) and more than 12 thousand additional loci. There was strong concordance with 99% of the 4324 *xrn2xrn3* RTLs overlapping with 14,675 *sal1*-8 RTLs. We observed that 97% of the mRNA-seq RTLs were also found in the total RNA-seq data and there were >5,000 more RTLs in the total RNA-seq data. Comparatively, only 22% of the total RNA-seq RTLs were also identified by mRNA-seq, initially suggesting that most loci produce predominantly non-poly(A) transcripts. Surprisingly, when we compared the abundance of the 2,107 RTLs that were detected in both the *sal1-*8 mRNA-seq and total RNA-seq data we observed slightly higher abundance in the mRNA fraction (Supplemental Fig. S2A). Together, these observations suggest that there are

 two classes of RTLs: firstly, poly(A) RTLs that are equally detectable by either total RNA-seq and mRNA-seq; and secondly, a large class of non-poly(A) specific loci only reliably detectable through total RNA-seq.

#### **Arabidopsis SAL1, XRN2, and XRN3 affect Pol II termination**

 To assess whether the 14,718 putative RTLs were genuinely associated with Pol II read-though, we analysed Pol II occupancy using genome-wide Pol II ChIP-seq. For RTLs, the meta profile of the region downstream of the TTS revealed a clear and significant increase in Pol II occupancy in *sal1-*8 and substantial and consistent increase in *xrn2xrn3* compared to WT (linear mixed effects model, ANOVA, p < 0.01). This increase was observed in the combined RTL dataset (Fig. 2E) and also when each sequencing method was profiled individually (Supplemental Fig. S2, B–D). In contrast to the intergenic region, Pol II occupancy over the genic region, including the 3′ end, was nearly identical in all genotypes. In comparison to RTLs, at all other genes Pol II levels were far less diverged among the genotypes between 0 to ~600 bp (Fig. 2F, *p* > 0.05), although beyond this Pol II levels in *sal1*-8 were somewhat elevated. The ChIP-seq protocol was independently validated using RT-qPCR (Supplemental Fig. S3, A and B) and we also profiled H3K36me3, as both a positive ChIP control and a mark of active transcription which could potentially mirror any change in Pol II density. For example, mutations affecting human Set2D, an H3K36me3 methyltransferase, can also lead to Pol II read-though [\(Grosso et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/VkEh5)  [2015\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/VkEh5) In contrast to Pol II ChIP profiles, the distribution of H3K36me3 was similar in all genotypes (Supplemental Fig. S3C). The *sal1-*8 mutant displayed a higher level of Pol II read- through compared to *xrn2xrn3*, which is consistent with prior work pointing to the increased severity of the *sal1-*8 mutation [\(Estavillo et al., 2011; Pornsiriwong et al., 2017\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/K7CH3+U5Fe) and the hypomorphic nature of the *xrn3-3* allele [\(Gy et al., 2007; Zakrzewska-Placzek et al., 2010\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/LPsU+lf1h) These results strongly suggest that a major explanation for the increased abundance of

 intergenic transcripts in *xrn2xrn3* and *sal1-*8 is due to deficient Pol II termination and increased Pol II activity in the intergenic regions downstream of genes.

 In *S. cerevisiae* the XRNs are globally required for Pol II termination [\(Baejen et al., 2017\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/iBtON) whereas in *C. elegans* the XRNs appear to be only required at a subset of loci [\(Miki et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/mcWjE)  [2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/mcWjE) In total, 14,718 RTLs were identified in the integrated *sal1-*8 total RNA-seq, mRNA-seq, and PARE datasets, which were correlated with increased Pol II occupancy at the meta level (Fig. 2, D and E). These RTLs occur at 72.4% of all expressed genes (> 0.3 CPM, 20,334 genes) suggesting that Pol II read-through is pervasive across the transcriptome. In fact, a feature of the identified RTLs was that they are disproportionately detected at highly expressed genes; for instance, as measured in the total RNA-seq data set (Supplemental Fig. S3D). This suggests that for genes that are expressed at a low level, read-though may be occurring below our limit of detection. Indeed, the frequency of genes in the top expression decile (> 42 FPKM) that exhibited read-though in the total RNA-seq was > 70%. These results are consistent with a global requirement for SAL1 and XRN activities for efficient Pol II termination of most genes in Arabidopsis.

 To further interrogate the characteristics of RTL transcripts and their consequences, we focused on a single sequencing data set. These subsequent analyses required mate paired reads and correlations with downstream gene expression, ruling out the single end PARE data. Given that total RNA-seq captures both capped/uncapped and poly(A)/non-poly(A), and also captures 97% of the mRNA-seq RTLs, we focused on this subset comprising 9,965 distinct RTLs identified in *sal1-*8 or *xrn2xrn3* (Supplemental Fig. S2, B and E).

#### **RTL transcript structure**

 We hypothesised that transcriptional read-through could produce two general transcript classes at the 3′ end of genes. If processing of the primary transcript does not take place, an extended "Class 1" transcript comprising the mRNA and the read-through extension will be produced, whereas if processing and cleavage remains functional, a physically separate "Class 2" intergenic transcript will result (Fig. 3A). Identifying the prevalence of Class 1 transcripts is important because this may impact the proper functioning of the canonical protein coding gene by affecting transcription, RNA stability, or translation. In Arabidopsis, it was previously concluded that putative read-through transcripts in the *SAL1* mutant *fry1-6* were Class 2 based on two genes exhibiting cleaved read-through transcripts [\(Kurihara et al., 2012\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/xd449) To examine this on a global scale, we estimated the relative abundance of Class 1 and Class 2 putative isoforms using the pattern of mate-paired reads in total RNA-seq data (Fig. 3A, Methods). Globally, we estimate that 73.0% of read-through transcripts are characteristic of Class 2 and 27.0% are Class 1 for *xrn2xrn3* (Fig. 3B); and for *sal1-8*, 63.5% are characteristic of Class 2 and 36.5% are Class 1 (Fig. 3C).

 The next question is: for a given gene are transcripts all Class 1, 2, or some proportion of both? Calculating the proportion of Class 2 transcripts on a per-gene basis for *xrn2xrn3* (Supplemental Fig. S4A), 79.1% of RTLs produced predominantly Class 2 transcripts (%CLASS 2 > 50), 8.7% predominantly Class 1 transcripts (%CLASS 2 < 50), and the remainder were unclassified due to a deficit of intergenic mates indicative of either a mis-annotated TTS or very short read-through. For *sal1*-8 (Supplemental Fig. S4B), 63.9% of RTLs produced predominantly Class 2 transcripts (example in Supplemental Fig. S4C) and 22.4% predominantly Class 1 transcripts (example in Supplemental Fig. S4D). Of note, the two loci examined by Kurihara et al. (2012) were *AT1G16410* and *AT3G45160*, which we found to have %CLASS 2 values in *sal1-*8 of 61% and 99%, respectively, consistent with the prior report and lending support to our bioinformatic

 approach. Loci which exhibited a greater proportion of Class 2 cleaved read-through products typically had more abundant read-through relative to both their respective upstream protein coding gene and to wild-type plants, though this trend was clearest for *sal1-*8 (Fig. 3, D–G). Thus, based on this analysis the majority of read-through products are cleaved, physically separate intergenic transcripts. However, the prevalence of Class 1 uncleaved continuous read-through products is still substantial across the genome and for a given gene.

**Pol II read-through affects downstream mRNA expression proportional to proximity** 

 Given Pol II read-through declines with distance from the TTS, the question arises as to whether it impacts downstream gene expression either positively by enhancing transcription or negatively by interfering with Pol II, transcription factor binding or through generation of antisense transcripts? The *sal1*-8 mutation was originally isolated in a genetic screen for upregulation of *APX2*. When we examined this locus we found that *APX2* was downstream of a strong RTL, namely *SAC56* (Fig. 4A). In the genome, 45% of genes occur in a tandem orientation on the same strand as their nearest upstream gene. All of the 50 most highly upregulated genes in *sal1*-8 (50/50, 100%) occur in a tandem orientation, compared to only 25% (13/50) of the 50 most down-regulated genes. Globally, of the 3,889 upregulated genes in *sal1-*8 (Supplemental Tables S3 and S4), 43% were associated with an upstream RTL. In the kernel density plots (Fig. 4, B and C) the median distance for each gene from its upstream neighbour in the genome is 2.8 kb. This reduces to 1.4 kb for *xrn2xrn3*-upregulated genes and 1.1 kb for *sal1-*8 upregulated genes (purple dashed line), which is significantly and disproportionately closer than the genome average (brown) or the downregulated set (yellow; p < 0.001 one-sample bootstrap).

 The normal unimodal distribution for the genome and down-regulated set contrasts with a bimodal distribution for the upregulated set with the major peak associated with shorter

 intergenic distance (Fig. 4, B and C). If read-though were causative of downstream gene activation, then we would anticipate an association between intergenic distance and upregulation. To test this, we assessed whether the bimodal distribution could be deconvoluted based on expression by dividing the gene sets into four quartiles of expression from low (1st quartile) to high (4th 289 quartile). Indeed, the four quartiles revealed that the distribution of gene expression was associated with the bimodality (Fig. 4, D and E), with the highest expressed quartile being closely associated with the most proximal genes (p < 0.05 Wilcoxon rank sum test).

 Furthermore, we also observed a strong genome-wide correlation between differential expression in *xrn2xrn3* and *sal1-*8 and an interaction of gene orientation and intergenic distance (Fig. 4, F–I). When the gene downstream of an RTL was in a tandem orientation, the frequency of expression upregulation in *sal1-*8 increased significantly from ~20% at distances > 2000 bp to ~55% at distances < 200 bp, with a corresponding decrease in downregulated and unchanged expression (p < 0.001 Pearson's Chi-squared test; Fig. 4I). By contrast, if the downstream gene was in a convergent orientation to the RTL, there were no changes in differential expression as intergenic distance decreased (p > 0.05; Fig. 4H). The smaller number of RTLs in *xrn2xrn3* led to more decile-to-decile variability, with proportional change that was not statistically significant (Fig. 4, F and G). However, expression of tandem downstream genes was more likely to be upregulated than downregulated. These results suggest that Pol II read-though can lead to upregulation of tandem gene pairs, while having no clear association with downregulation nor a strong effect on genes in colliding orientation.

 The association between expression upregulation in *sal1-*8 and neighbouring gene proximity could potentially be explained by clustering of active gene expression. Recent reports have

identified genes that cluster together in local chromatin domains, such as topologically

 associating domains (TADs; [Liu et al., 2017\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/ilsmh) which may have similar expression levels [\(Rennie](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/gltYU)  [et al., 2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/gltYU) However, examination of our WT RNA-seq data demonstrated that actively expressed genes were not disproportionately close to upstream genes (p > 0.05, pairwise Wilcoxon rank sum test; Supplemental Fig. S5A).

 We subsequently examined genes differentially expressed under drought stress using RNA-seq data [\(Crisp et al., 2017\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/itKBw) which partially overlap with genes differentially expressed in *sal1*-8, to determine if upregulation of gene expression was generally linked to short intergenic distances. To isolate *sal1*-independent effects, upregulated genes in this data set were divided into those either upregulated or unchanged in *sal1-*8. A clear difference was observed, with the *sal1*-8 set of upregulated genes exhibiting a significantly shorter median distance than the *sal1*-8 set of unchanged genes (Supplemental Fig. S5B; Wilcoxon rank sum test, p < 0.05). This confirmed that the shift was a specific feature of *sal1*-8 and not a general characteristic of gene upregulation.

 Collectively, Figures 2, 3, and 4 indicate that a potential threshold for read-through that is able to significantly influence the expression of a downstream gene is in the order of 1000 bp. In total, 903 upregulated genes in *sal1-*8 (23.2% of all upregulated genes) and 20 upregulated genes in *xrn2xrn3* (1.2% of all upregulated genes) fall within 1000 bp of an upstream gene that exhibits read-through.

## **Defining and disrupting RTL-activated downstream mRNA expression**

 An important question is whether the upregulated transcripts that are downstream of the RTLs have 5′ extensions or whether the 5′ ends map to the canonical transcription start site (TSS). We specifically estimated the proportion of genic transcripts with a 5′ extension spanning the TSS (Class 3) vs canonical transcripts (Class 4) using the mapping location of mate-paired

 reads. In *xrn2xrn3*, 9.71% of upregulated transcripts associated with upstream read-through were characterised as Class 3 (Fig. 4J), meaning they exhibited 5′ extensions, in contrast to 0.95% of WT transcripts at these loci (Fig. 4K). The *sal1-*8 mutant exhibited the same pattern with 11.55% of upregulated transcripts associated with upstream read-through characterised as Class 3 (Fig. 4L), in contrast to 2.57% of WT transcripts (Fig. 4M). Thus, read-through does generate some transcripts continuous between the intergenic region and downstream genes; however, by this measure the large majority of transcripts are canonical and physically separate from the intergenic read-through transcript itself.

 To experimentally verify Pol II read-through as a mechanism capable of promoting downstream gene transcription, we sought to interrupt this process in *sal1*. T-DNA insertion lines were obtained for genes upstream of two of the most highly upregulated loci in the set of 903 genes possibly regulated by read-through in *sal1-*8*.* These were *APX2,* which is the most upregulated gene in *sal1-*8 and was the basis of the screen that identified a role for SAL1 in retrograde signaling [\(Wilson et al., 2009\);](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/3r87) and an uncharacterised proline-rich gene, *AT5G43770*, which is induced by abiotic stresses, such as drought and osmotic shock [\(Kilian et al., 2007\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/xuaQT) and developmentally expressed to its highest level in dry seed, another tissue high in PAP [\[\(Schmid](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/7dG3n)  [et al., 2005\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/7dG3n) visualised in the eFP-BAR [\(Winter et al., 2007\)\]](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/i5vs2). For the gene 432 bp upstream of *APX2,* namely *SAC56,* the T-DNA insertion lines *sac56*-1 and *sac56*-2 were characterised (Fig. 5A). Similarly, the insertion line *kcs20*-1 has a T-DNA insertion in the gene *KCS20*, 192 bp upstream of *AT5G43770* (Fig. 5F). The *SAC* mutations, *sac56*-1 and *sac56*-2*,* and the *KCS20* mutation, *kcs20*-1*,* were each confirmed to substantially and significantly decrease *SAC56* and *KCS20* mRNA levels, respectively (Fig. 5, C and G; ANOVA, Tukey's HSD, *p* < 0.001), before these lines were crossed with those containing *sal1*-6 or *sal1*-8 alleles to obtain homozygous double mutants. Both *sal1-6* and *sal1*-8 lack SAL1 activity and accumulate PAP [\(Wilson et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/U5Fe+3r87)  [2009; Estavillo et al., 2011\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/U5Fe+3r87) and *sal1*-6 has phenocopied *sal1*-8 in all traits reported to date. We  confirmed that read-through levels were highly comparable for the *APX2* RTL in lines carrying both *sal1* alleles (Supplemental Fig. S6).

 In each double mutant, the insertion of an upstream T-DNA was associated with significantly attenuated expression of the downstream gene relative to the parental *sal1-*8 line (Fig. 5, E and I; *p* < 0.001). We also confirmed that intergenic transcription of the RTL was significantly disrupted (Fig. 5, D and H; *p* < 0.001). To confirm these effects were associated with perturbing read-through and not due to a general disruption of the surrounding chromatin, the gene diverging from *SAC56* was also assayed. A non-significant change in the expression of this gene (*AT3G09620*) was observed (Fig. 5B). Thus, the major attenuation of intergenic and downstream gene expression can be attributed to impairment of read-through.

 Collectively, the results of our analysis of the structure of RTL transcripts and the expression of downstream genes lead us to propose a model whereby read-through can promote the upregulation of downstream gene expression (Fig. 6).

### **Physiological significance and potential applications for read-through**

 The potential for Pol II read-through to influence the expression of downstream genes in SAL1- PAP mutants raises the possibility that read-though could operate as a novel gene regulatory mechanism under conditions where PAP is elevated. This could include environmental or developmental situations such as during drought and light stress, or in dry seed [\(Estavillo et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/K7CH3+U5Fe)  [2011; Pornsiriwong et al., 2017\);](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/K7CH3+U5Fe) or could occur as a result of exogenous application of PAP or PAP analogs. Consistent with this hypothesis, the genes upregulated in *sal1-*8 and *xrn2xrn3*  show a significant overlap (p < 0.001, hypergeometric test) with genes upregulated under drought (38% and 34%, respectively; Supplemental Fig. S7) and excess light (41% and 53%, respectively). As a second test of this hypothesis, the prevalence of read-through was examined  after pharmacological treatments designed to increase cellular PAP levels. Treatment of plant tissues with lithium-containing PAP solutions increases intracellular PAP levels because lithium is a potent inhibitor of PAP catabolism [\(Murguia et al., 1996; Albert et al., 2000; Estavillo et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/K7CH3+U5Fe+ywhI3+nIBs9)  [2011; Pornsiriwong et al., 2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/K7CH3+U5Fe+ywhI3+nIBs9) We have previously reported that both PAP-lithium and lithium treatments significantly increase cellular PAP levels [\(Pornsiriwong et al., 2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/K7CH3) Here, using paired replicate samples from the same experiment, we profiled the transcriptome using RNA- seq. Lithium and PAP+lithium treatments lead to the upregulation of ~1,500 genes (Table I, Supplemental Tables S5 and S6), which significantly overlapped with genes upregulated in *sal1*-8 (Table I; p < 0.001 hypergeometric test); however, the downregulated genes sets did not significantly overlap. No genes were differentially expressed under PAP treatment alone (FC >1.5; FDR < 0.05). Exogenous PAP application was associated with a global increase in Pol II read-through (Fig. 7A). As expected, treatment with PAP alone did not trigger read-though, likely because of the rapid catabolism of PAP in the absence of lithium. In total, for the tissue samples with increased PAP levels, we identified 62 loci with transcription downstream of the TSS characteristic of RTLs (Table I, Supplemental Table S7), and in nine cases the downstream gene was upregulated. Finally, to examine the possibility of environment-induced read-through we analysed the RTL upstream of *APX2* (Fig. 7B) in drought-stressed plants using RT-qPCR. After 10 days of soil-drying drought, PAP levels increased moderately from below detectable levels to 15 pmol/mg dry weight, and *APX2* mRNA levels increased 55 fold (Fig. 7D). This was accompanied by induction of read-though upstream of *APX2* at the RTL to 6-fold the level in control plants (Fig. 7C). Transcript abundance of two additional *sal1-*8 read-though loci was quantified under drought conditions using RT-qPCR, and both loci exhibited a ~5- and ~70- fold increase, respectively (Fig. 7, E and F).

# Discussion

 Understanding how the nuclear XRN2 and XRN3 can affect gene expression has important implications generally for gene regulation and specifically for retrograde signaling. In this study, we combined degradome profiling, transcriptome sequencing, Pol II ChIP sequencing, and genetic interrogation of Pol II termination in SAL1- and XRN-deficient mutants to elucidate the function of PAP and the XRNs. In particular, we extended the original applications of PARE [miRNA analysi[s \(Addo-Quaye et al., 2008; German et al., 2008; Gregory et al., 2008\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/sIkWZ+ZnzXL+uYMxf) and ribosome footprinting [\(Hou et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2016; Crisp et al., 2017\)\]](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/NQa6C+itKBw+2nTW2) to identification of XRN substrates. PARE revealed that in the XRN-deficient *sal1*-8 and *xrn2xrn3* mutants, there was a global increase in the RNA degradome, particularly at the 3′ end of genes and into downstream intergenic regions (Fig. 1, A and B, Fig. 2A). This observation is consistent with the known role of XRN2/XRN3 homologs in Pol II termination in yeast, humans, and worms [\(Kim et](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/hbFLU+QS2Vt+mcWjE)  [al., 2004; West et al., 2004; Miki et al., 2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/hbFLU+QS2Vt+mcWjE) Importantly, whereas prior reports have demonstrated correlations between mRNA levels in *sal1* and *xrn* mutants, we show here on a global scale that *sal1* mutants accumulate extremely high levels of characteristic XRN substrate RNAs, in particular XRN2 and XRN3 substrates.

### **Arabidopsis XRN2 and XRN3 are required genome-wide for Pol II termination**

 Under the combined allosteric-torpedo model of termination in eukaryotes [\(Luo and Bentley,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/1ENYT+KRk5J+wZjZe)  [2004; Schreieck et al., 2014; Proudfoot, 2016\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/1ENYT+KRk5J+wZjZe) the XRNs play a role in terminating Pol II. The torpedo operates globally in *S. cerevisiae* [\(Baejen et al., 2017\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/iBtON) but is restricted in *C. elegans* [\(Miki et al., 2017\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/mcWjE) and until recently, was only hypothesised to occur in plants [\(Kurihara et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/xd449)  [2012\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/xd449) Genome structure both between and within species can vary a great deal, including gene density and chromatin regulation from yeast's compact genome to larger plant genomes with relatively vast intergenic spaces. Here we find evidence of read-through transcripts for the

 majority of expressed genes (> 14,000 or 72%). We demonstrate a global functionality by profiling Pol II occupancy genome-wide in *xrn2xrn3* and *sal1* mutants and reporting increased levels of Pol II downstream of protein coding genes. We also extend this to a demonstration that retrograde regulation of XRNs via SAL1-PAP signaling can also impact Pol II activity. Consistently, we observed higher levels of read-though and related phenotypes in *sal1*-8 compared to that in *xrn2*-1*xrn3*-3. This is consistent with prior results suggesting that *xrn3-*3 is a relatively weak hypomorphic allele [\(Gy et al., 2007; Zakrzewska-Placzek et al., 2010\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/LPsU+lf1h) and that the *sal1-*8 mutation likely leads to a greater impairment of XRN activity in comparison to that caused by *xrn2*-1*xrn3*-3 [\(Estavillo et al., 2011; Pornsiriwong et al., 2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/K7CH3+U5Fe) In parallel with our observations, a very recent corroborative study in Arabidopsis also concluded that the XRNs are required for Pol II termination, and that a more severe *xrn3*-8 mutant allele resulted in a corresponding increase in Pol II read-through in *xrn3-*8 compared to the levels we observed in *xrn3-*3 [\(Krzyszton et al., 2018\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/wZZay) Together, these two parallel investigations independently demonstrate the role of the XRNs in Pol II termination in Arabidopsis.

#### **Pol II read-through upregulates expression of downstream genes**

 Theoretically, read-through has the potential to negatively affect gene expression, in particular by disrupting transcription of neighbouring genes, thus reducing their mRNA levels [\(Shearwin et](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/og4l5)  [al., 2005\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/og4l5) In this context, we were surprised to discover that the effect of read-through was upregulation of tandemly oriented downstream genes without any significant or observable changes that could be associated with downregulation of adjacent genes. This does not preclude the existence of the later, but it is clearly a minor effect at a global level (Fig. 4, D and E). We also found that upregulation correlates with distance from an RTL. As intergenic distance increases, the likelihood of the Pol II complex destabilising or terminating by alternative mechanisms increases. Read-through into downstream genes is consistent with prior reports in *S. cerevisiae*, where in XRN-deficient strains around 35% of non-terminated Pol II enzymes are

 estimated to transcribe into downstream genes [\(Baejen et al., 2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/iBtON) Likewise, in *C. elegans*, reporter constructs were employed to demonstrate a capacity for read-through to activate downstream gene expression. In that study, it was also found that promoter elements affect the mode of downstream termination, pointing to mechanisms regulating termination.

 Our investigation extends upon prior correlative studies in *S. cerevisiae* and *C. elegans* [\(Baejen](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/iBtON+mcWjE)  [et al., 2017; Miki et al., 2017\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/iBtON+mcWjE) by decoupling the expression of tandem gene pairs–an important test of causality–by using T-DNA insertional mutants. While the insertion of a large T-DNA could have unanticipated effects on gene expression at an off-target locus, in the case of *APX2*, only the downstream neighbouring gene and not the divergent upstream gene was substantially affected, thus suggesting the direct impact of the T-DNA was largely localised to the gene it was inserted in. These experiments were performed in *sal1* mutant backgrounds because the read- through phenotype was more readily detectable in a *sal1* background compared to the hypomorphic *xrn2xrn3* and a loss-of-function XRN3 mutant is unavailable because it is embryo lethal [\(Gy et al., 2007; Zakrzewska-Placzek et al., 2010\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/LPsU+lf1h) Using this approach, we demonstrated that disruption of upstream genes precluded intergenic read-though and restored the expression of the downstream gene to WT levels at the two independent loci examined.

**A functional role for read-through, implications for retrograde signaling and beyond**  While read-through can lead to increased transcript levels of downstream genes, an important consideration is whether these read-through transcripts are cleaved or continuous with the coding sequence. Under the 'torpedo' model of termination, the XRNs theoretically operate after cleavage and polyadenylation of the upstream pre-mRNA, which exposes a 5′ unprotected nascent RNA for degradation from the 5′ end. An earlier study demonstrated cleavage for two genes [\(Kurihara et al., 2012\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/xd449) Extending this to a global analysis, we estimate that the majority of RTL transcripts are cleaved. Nevertheless, extensions were found at detectable levels at

 many RTLs, and globally up to 36% of all RTL transcripts could be 3′ extension products (Fig. 3, A and B). RTL transcripts were found to be both poly(A) and non-poly(A). The reason we observe cleaved and continuous read-though products in a poly(A)- derived RNA-seq is not clear. One possibility is that polyadenylation may be part of a mechanism for targeting nuclear RNAs for decay, which has been reported in animals [\(LaCava et al., 2005; Wyers et al., 2005;](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/GzEMx+8hMmv+mcnDK)  [Houseley and Tollervey, 2009\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/GzEMx+8hMmv+mcnDK) Alternatively, the transcribing Pol II may have reached an alternate/cryptic poly(A) signal either in the intergenic region or in a downstream gene, potentially generating chimeric or extended gene read-though products. There are potential limitations to using short read sequencing to infer transcript structure, and in the future these conclusions might be tested by full-length cDNA sequencing, for instance using Nanopore or PacBio technology.

 Extensions to the 3′ end of mRNAs could affect gene expression; however, extensions are anticipated to have a much greater effect at the 5′ end. For the 903 RTL downstream genes upregulated in *sal1*-8, we estimate that 88% of the time they are initiated at the canonical TSS, with 11.6% frequency of 5′ extensions (compared to 2.6% in wild-type). We did not observe evidence of the 5′ extensions leading to gene downregulation as for yeast; specifically, yeast Ndc80 is downregulated by a 5′ extended long undecoded *NDC80* transcript isoform [\(Chia et](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/nx3Gi)  [al., 2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/nx3Gi) While evaluation of translation rates for 900 genes is well beyond the scope of this study, for a number of the RTL-activated downstream genes, there is demonstrated physiological and biochemical evidence of increased expression. For example, in the case of the RTL-associated *APX2*, *sal1* mutants have significantly reduced levels of the substrate of APX2, hydrogen peroxide [\(Rossel et al., 2006; Wilson et al., 2009; Estavillo et al., 2011;](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/3Ugzq+3r87+U5Fe+K7CH3)  [Pornsiriwong et al., 2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/3Ugzq+3r87+U5Fe+K7CH3)

 Is read-through restricted to *sal1* and *xrn* mutants? In this context, the *sal1-*8 and *xrn2xrn3* transcriptomes significantly overlap with transcripts upregulated by drought and excess light, and both SAL1 and XRNs have demonstrated roles in retrograde signaling of oxidative stress in vivo via ROS and redox regulation of chloroplast-localised SAL1 [\(Wilson et al., 2009; Estavillo](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/3r87+U5Fe+K7CH3+z6OcW)  [et al., 2011; Chan et al., 2016a; Pornsiriwong et al., 2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/3r87+U5Fe+K7CH3+z6OcW) In total, 23% of the *sal1-*8 upregulated gene set overlaps with the RTL-activated upregulated set. We also found a strong correlation between the accumulation of PAP during drought and the increased expression of the three RTLs examined, including the RTL upstream of *APX2*. Several other situations and conditions are also reported to stimulate read-through at specific loci, such as transcription of Piwi-interacting RNA (piRNA) clusters of Drosophila [\(Mohn et al., 2014\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/uRlYG) osmotic stress in human cells [\(Vilborg et al., 2015\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/E0em8) cancer [\(Grosso et al., 2015\),](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/VkEh5) and lithium and sodium stress in Arabidopsis [\(Kurihara et al., 2012\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/xd449) In Arabidopsis, continuous 3′ read-through products from small nuclear RNAs (snRNA) can activate the expression of downstream genes, and this mode of read-through activated expression was found to be activated by salt stress [\(Fukudome et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/2V7Pl)  [2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/2V7Pl) Here, the authors proposed that read-through transcription to produce 3′-extended snRNA is not a mere transcriptional abnormality, but is a part of transcriptional regulatory strategies in plants. We also found that pharmacological manipulation of PAP levels induced read-though, raising the possibility that exogenous treatments could be applied to plants to manipulate this process. Thus, there is some limited evidence read-through can occur in response to treatments and drought stress, but whether or not this is physiologically important or wide-spread is an open question.

 A core goal of this study was to investigate a candidate mechanism by which PAP and the XRNs could modulate gene expression during retrograde signaling. By characterising *sal1* and *xrn2xrn3* mutants we have shown how the SAL1-PAP-XRN pathway could potentially increase transcript abundance via a read-through mechanism (Fig. 6) of up to 903 genes, which is 23%

 of the *sal1-*8 upregulated transcriptome. While we propose that this read-though model is widespread applying to many genes, it is likely that there are other direct and indirect regulatory mechanisms affecting the expression of many other genes in parallel. We demonstrated that read-though expression of neighboring genes was affected in a distance-dependant manner up to ~1000 bp. Contrary to scenarios where the production of antisense transcripts downregulate genes, there was no significant change to downstream genes on the complementary strand. Instead, the major effect of read-through in *sal1-*8 and *xrn2xrn3* was to increase mRNAs of genes in a tandem orientation. Significantly, our study moves beyond correlation demonstrating that 88% of transcripts of the downstream loci are predicted to be transcribed from the canonical TSS, and disruption to upstream gene expression causes attenuation of intergenic and downstream gene expression.

# Methods

#### **Plant material**

 For all experiments *Arabidopsis thaliana* (Arabidopsis) Col-0 plants were cultivated in soil under a 12- or 16-h photoperiod, 100 (±25) μ mol photons m−<sup>2</sup> s−<sup>1</sup> 554 photosynthetically active radiation [PAR] and 23/22°C (±2°C) day/night temperatures. The SAL1 mutant alleles *sal1-*8 (*alx8*) and *sal1-*6 (*fry1-*6), and the *xrn2*-1*xrn3*-3 double mutant, were as previously described [\(Crisp et al.,](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/itKBw)  [2017\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/itKBw) The *suppressor of acaulis 56*-1 (*sac56*-1) mutant (SALK-027492), *sac56*-2 mutant (SALK-130595), and *3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase 20*-1 *(kcs20*-1*)* mutant (SALK-019727) were recovered in the SALK sequence-indexed T-DNA insertion collections [\(Sessions et al., 2002;](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/rmKxo+xUTfr)  [Alonso et al., 2003\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/rmKxo+xUTfr) obtained from the The Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center, Ohio State University (ABRC). Primer sequences used for genotyping are provided in Supplemental Table S8. Drought treatments were performed as described in Crisp et al. (2017) by withholding water from five-week-old soil-grown plants for 10 days. PAP and lithium treatments were performed as  described in Pornsiriwong et al. (2017) using a petiole feeding technique to administer solutions of 100 mM LiCl, 10 mM ATP, or 1 mM PAP (Sigma-Aldrich) alone or in different combinations, prepared in infiltration buffer (1 mM PIPES KOH pH 6.0, 1 mM Sodium citrate, 1 mM KCl, 15 mM Sucrose) modified from a cordycepin infiltration buffer [\(Seeley et al., 1992\).](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/A5FZW) Solutions were administered through the petioles of excised leaves. The samples used in this study for transcript profiling were paired replicates of samples from the same experiment in Pornsiriwong et al. (2017) [\(https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.23361.007,](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.23361.007) Fig. 2 to Supplemental Fig. S1).

### **RT-qPCR analysis**

 Total RNA was extracted using the Ambion PureLink RNA mini kit with Trizol (ThermoFisher) and treated on-column with DNase (Sigma). Quantification and RNA quality was determined using a LabChIP GXII (PerkinElmer) and 1 μg RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Invitrogen SS III First Strand cDNA synthesis kit and oligo(dT18VN) primers. qPCR was performed in technical duplicate on a LightCycler480 using SybrGreen I (Roche Diagnostics). Data was analysed using LinReg PCR [\(Ramakers et al., 2003; Ruijter et al., 2009\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/eiTvR+uamCU) and normalised to the reference gene *PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 2A SUBUNIT* (*PP2AA3*). Primers for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplemental Table S8. Statistical significance was assessed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) between genotypes, with post-hoc multiple comparison testing using Tukey's honest significant difference (HSD) test. Statistically significant differences were defined as pairwise comparisons with an adjusted P-value (padj) of less than 0.05.

### **Identification of read-through loci (RTLs)**

 All reads were associated with their nearest gene using *BEDTools* v2.26.0 *genomecov* and coverage depth summarised in a window up to 500 bp (depending on intergenic distance) either side of the annotated transcription termination site for genes with CPM > 0.3, excluding intergenic loci < 50 bp in length or with a mean coverage < 2x. Mean intergenic coverage was normalised to mean genic coverage [\(McKinlay et al., 2017\)](https://paperpile.com/c/gQeatu/OTBjP) and loci with mean intergenic coverage > 8 times than of genic coverage were excluded to prevent unannotated loci within the intergenic region confounding the results. Significant differences between genotypes were identified using *limma* (FDR < 0.05, FC >2).

### **Cleavage and structural analysis of RTL transcripts**

- Paired-end reads that overlapped with a region comprised of the TTS + 50 nt were identified
- and categorised as either intergenic or genic according to the location of their mate reads. For
- reads originating from Class 1 transcripts, no bias in either direction would be expected, while
- for reads originating from Class 2 transcripts a bias toward intergenic mate reads would be
- expected. The proportion of Class 2 transcripts was calculated as 100 genic mates x 100 /intergenic mates
- (see SI- Supplementary Methods for derivation of formula). As short Class 2 transcripts (less
- than the average fragment size of ~175 nt) are lost during RNA-seq library preparation, this
- method necessarily underestimates the number of these transcripts.

**Figure 6.** A model for read-through-mediated transcript upregulation

 A, When an elongating Pol II transcribes to the end of a protein-coding gene a concerted process occurs involving coupled 3′-processing of the pre-mRNA and transcription termination. Under a combined allosteric-torpedo model of termination, transcription over the poly(A) site leads to recruitment of 3′- processing factors and conformational and structural changes that destabilizes Pol II. The pre-mRNA is cleaved and polyadenylated to complete the mRNA and a new 5′ unprotected nascent RNA is generated. Subsequently, an XRN 'torpedo' degrades the nascent RNA from the 5′ end, catches up with and displaces the Pol II complex from the DNA. PAS, polyadenylation signal; CPA, cleavage and polyadenylation complex. B, Read-though occurs when Pol II is not effectively terminated and transcribes beyond the end of canonical gene boundaries. In XRN deficient mutants we observe this at 14,718 genes. Nevertheless, 64% of the time, cleavage is still functional and the read-though locus (RTL) transcript is physically separate from the upstream gene transcript (Class 2). C, Pol II read-though into the intergenic region in the majority of cases is terminated before reaching the transcription start site (TSS) of the downstream gene (Class 4). We only found evidence of 3′ extensions 12% of the time; thus we estimate 88% of downstream genes are transcribed as canonical mRNAs, with their 5′ end mapping to the TSS. We speculate that this intergenic Pol II read-though could lead to upregulation of the

downstream mRNA by altering the chromatin structure or opening the chromatin around promoter

regions.

#### **Figure 6.** A model for read-through-mediated transcript upregulation

A, When an elongating Pol II transcribes to the end of a protein-coding gene a concerted process occurs involving coupled 3′-processing of the pre-mRNA and transcription termination. Under a combined allosteric-torpedo model of termination, transcription over the poly(A) site leads to recruitment of 3′ processing factors and conformational and structural changes that destabilizes Pol II. The pre-mRNA is cleaved and polyadenylated to complete the mRNA and a new 5′ unprotected nascent RNA is generated. Subsequently, an XRN 'torpedo' degrades the nascent RNA from the 5′ end, catches up with and displaces the Pol II complex from the DNA. PAS, polyadenylation signal; CPA, cleavage and polyadenylation complex. B, Read-though occurs when Pol II is not effectively terminated and transcribes beyond the end of canonical gene boundaries. In XRN deficient mutants we observe this at 14,718 genes. Nevertheless, 64% of the time, cleavage is still functional and the read-though locus (RTL) transcript is physically separate from the upstream gene transcript (Class 2). C, Pol II read-though into the intergenic region in the majority of cases is terminated before reaching the transcription start site (TSS) of the downstream gene (Class 4). We only found evidence of 3′ extensions 12% of the time; thus we estimate 88% of downstream genes are transcribed as canonical mRNAs, with their 5′ end mapping to the TSS. We speculate that this intergenic Pol II read-though could lead to upregulation of the downstream mRNA by altering the chromatin structure or opening the chromatin around promoter regions.
