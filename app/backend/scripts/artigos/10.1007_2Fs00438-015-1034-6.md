**Abstract** *Ipomoea nil* is widely used as an ornamental plant due to its abundance of flower color, but the limited transcriptome and genomic data hinder research on it. Using illumina platform, transcriptome profiling of *I. nil* was performed through high-throughput sequencing, which was proven to be a rapid and cost-effective means to characterize gene content. Our goal is to use the resulting information to facilitate the relevant research on flowering and flower color formation in *I. nil*. In total, 268 million unique illumina RNA-Seq reads were produced and used in the transcriptome assembly. These reads were assembled into 220,117 contigs, of which 137,307 contigs were annotated using the GO and KEGG database. Based on the result of functional annotations, a total of 89,781 contigs were assigned 455,335 GO term annotations. Meanwhile, 17,418 contigs were identified with pathway annotation and they were functionally assigned to 144 KEGG pathways. # **Introduction**

The Japanese morning glory (*Ipomoea nil*), a member of *Convolvulaceae*, is one of the most popular ornamental plants around the world (Wang et al. [2014\)](#page-11-0). It has an extensive history of genetic studies, and about one-third of >200 spontaneous mutants exhibit altered flower pigmentation (Iida et al. [1999\)](#page-10-0). As a typical absolute short-day (SD) plant, *I. nil* has been widely used as a model species to study photoperiodic flowering response for about 80 years (Imamura [1967\)](#page-10-1). Despite a long history of attempts, there is still not enough information on the regulatory mechanisms underlying flower color formation and SD response in *I. nil*, owing to the limited transcriptome and genomic data. Thus, transcriptome sequencing provides a highly efficient way to discover the molecular information on the whole genome scale, which will undoubtedly benefit relevant research. Additionally, it can also provide initial genetic resources that will be valuable for other species in the *Ipomoea* genus.

The genus *Ipomoea* includes more than 600 species worldwide, and its genetic bases of flower color formation have been extensively studied (Austin and Huáman [1996](#page-10-2)). As suggested by the common name, the Japanese morning glory (*I. nil*) flowers open early in the morning, and as a successfully domesticated ornamental plant, its flowers are found in diverse colors including white, pink, blue and dark blue (purple) (Hoshino et al. [2009\)](#page-10-3). It is now known that most flower colors in *I. nil* are the result of the presence of flower pigments, referring to anthocyanins or other flavonoids. For instance, the wild-type *I. nil* plants display bright blue flowers, containing a polyacylated pigment called heavenly blue anthocyanin (HBA), whereas reddish flowers accumulate pelargonidin derivatives known as wedding bells anthocyanin (WBA) (Hoshino et al. [2003](#page-10-4)). Thanks to the research of flower pigmentation in a number of spontaneous mutants of *I. nil*, several structural genes in the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway of *I. nil* have been extensively studied, and also the regulatory genes for the pathway, *InMYB1* and *InWDR1* (Morita et al. [2006\)](#page-11-1), but yet a global view of this pathway in *I. nil* is still needed.

*I. nil* is also well studied for its response to SD signal. As an SD plant, *I. nil* is highly sensitive to day length and it is ideal for the study of early events in photoperiodic inductive flowering. Young, light-grown seedlings of *I. nil* can be induced to flower by exposure to a single dark period of 16 h, whereas under continuous light (LL) or long-day (LD) conditions, they remain vegetative. Several attempts have been made to identify genes involved in flowering of *I. nil*. For example, *PnCO*, an ortholog of *Arabidopsis CONSTANS* (*CO*), was isolated using a differential display screen of mRNA (Liu et al. [2001\)](#page-11-2). In 2007, two orthologs of *FLOWERING LOCUS T* (*FT*), *PnFT1* and *PnFT2*, were found in *I. nil*, and their expression correlating with *PnCOs* under darkness was analyzed as well (Hayama et al. [2007](#page-10-5)). Furthermore, some other genes involved in photoperiodic flowering have been identified, such as *PnGI* (Higuchi et al. [2011](#page-10-6)), *PnPHY* (Zheng et al. [2009\)](#page-11-3), *PnMADS1* (Kikuchi et al. [2008\)](#page-11-4), and *PnLFY*, *PnAG*, *PnCRC* (Parfitt et al. [2004](#page-11-5)). However, an understanding of how *I. nil* responds to daylength at molecular level is still limited, and the relevant floral transition pathways in *I. nil* have not been fully documented.

Until now, few genomic resources are available for *I. nil* in general. As of March 26, 2014, there are only 2067 nucleotide sequences, 99,478 ESTs and 25,110 proteins from *I. nil* deposited in GenBank. Earlier expressed tag (EST) sequencing has long been the core technology for the discovery of novel genes, and a limited number of robust molecular markers like simple sequence repeats (SSRs) and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) have also been developed from some of the publicly available ESTs (Iorizzo et al. [2011\)](#page-10-7). To date, only 514 unique microsatellite-containing ESTs have been identified in 92,662 ESTs of *I. nil* and their polymorphisms estimated among seven accessions that originated from five regions of the world (Ly et al. [2012\)](#page-11-6), but it is inefficient to cover the whole transcriptome of *I. nil*. Therefore, extensive genomic and transcriptomic sequence data are needed for *I. nil*, which can be used for SSR markers mining, novel gene discovery, gene localization, etc.

Due to the large efforts that are required to sequence even these medium-sized eukaryotic genomes, only a few plant genomes have been sequenced as of 2014. Fortunately, with the advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology in recent years, genomic information in previously uncharacterized systems can be generated (Grabherr et al. [2011](#page-10-8)). Currently, high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) has emerged as a powerful and cost-efficient tool for transcriptome analysis, and it has also been used for transcript profiling in various nonmodel plant species, including potato (*Solanum tuberosum L.*) (Zhang et al. [2014\)](#page-11-7), pennycress (*Thlaspi arvense*) (Dorn et al. [2013](#page-10-9)), sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*) (Firon et al. [2013\)](#page-10-10) and radish (*Raphanus sativus L.*) (Wang et al. [2012a](#page-11-8)).

In the present study, we adopted Illumina paired-end sequencing to analyze the transcriptome of *I. nil*, whose normalized cDNA library was derived from a pooled sample of four representative tissue types, including roots, leaves, shoots and flowers. In total, 26.8 gigabase pairs were obtained by deep sequencing; transcripts involved in most metabolic pathways were detected by our procedures. This is the first report of a trancriptome database for this flowering plant. We provide a public dataset for genetic analysis and biological study of *I. nil.*

# **Materials and methods**

## **Plant materials and RNA extraction**

*Ipomoea nil* Chosiy cv. Violet (the Japanese moring glory; *Convolvulaceae*) was used for all experiments. Roots, leaves, shoots and flowers were collected from ten individual plants from this *Ipomoea* strain at Sichuan Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Chengdu, China. All tissues were frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after harvest and stored at −80 °C. Total RNA was extracted from these materials using the Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and treated with DNase I (Fermentas, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA quality and purity were assessed with OD260/230 ratio, and the RNA integrity was analyzed by capillary electrophoresis on an Agilent Bio-Analyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, [http://www.home.](http://www.home.agilent.com/agilent/home.jspx?cc=CN&lc=chi) [agilent.com/agilent/home.jspx?cc](http://www.home.agilent.com/agilent/home.jspx?cc=CN&lc=chi)=CN&lc=chi).

## **cDNA library construction and high‑throughput RNA sequencing**

A pooled sample containing equal amounts of purified total RNA from each of the four tissue samples was submitted to the Macrogen sequencing facilities in Seoul, South Korea. The cDNA libraries were constructed according to the manufacturer's instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Briefly, after total RNA extraction and DNase I treatment, the mRNAs were enriched using oligo (dT) magnetic beads and then fragmented into short pieces (about 200 bp). These fragments were used to synthesize dsDNA using random hexamer primer for the first strand and DNA polymerase I for the second strand. Double-stranded cDNA was purified with magnetic beads and subjected to end reparation and 3′ single adenylation. Sequencing adaptors were then ligated to the adenylated fragments, which were subsequently enriched by PCR amplification. The Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System were used to qualify and quantify the sample library during the quality control (QC) step. After quality assessment on an Agilent Technologies 2100 Bioanalyzer, the cDNA library products were sequenced on a paired-end (PE) flow cell using an Illumina Hiseq™ 2000 platform.

#### **Data filtering and de novo assembly**

Using in-house perl scripts, weak signals and low-quality sequences were removed; read ends were also screened and trimmed for Illumina adaptor sequences. The remaining Illumina sequences were first assessed by FastQC (v0.10.0) ([http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/](http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) [fastqc/\)](http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and then de novo assembled using Trinity (v2012- 10-25) (Grabherr et al. [2011](#page-10-8)) under default parameters. Trinity assembly output comprised a single contig file (Trinity fasta), which was used in further analyses.

The initial assembly quality was evaluated using the following metrics: total number of contigs, maximum contig length, mean contig length and N50 (Martin and Wang [2011](#page-11-9)). Initial assembly statistics and contig length distribution analysis was done by custom perl scripts as described (Tao et al. [2013](#page-11-10)). Moreover, to further investigate the assembly quality, we used the Trinity utility (transcripts\_ to\_best\_scoring\_ORFs.pl) to predict ORFs of at least 100 amino acids from the reconstructed contigs.

#### **Functional annotation and pathway assignments**

The assemblies were aligned with the NCBI non-redundant (nr) database using BLASTX (Altschul et al. [1990](#page-10-11)) with an *E* value of 1E-3 and taking the best 15 hits for annotation. The resulting BLAST output was fed into publicly available Blast2GO software (v.2.5.0) (Götz et al. [2008](#page-10-12)) to retrieve associated gene ontology (GO) terms describing biological processes, molecular functions and cellular components (Ashburner et al. [2000\)](#page-10-13). Then WEGO [\(http://wego.](http://wego.genomics.org.cn/cgi-bin/wego/index.pl) [genomics.org.cn/cgi-bin/wego/index.pl\)](http://wego.genomics.org.cn/cgi-bin/wego/index.pl) software was used to obtain GO functional classifications at the macro level. To determine metabolic pathways, the sequences with corresponding enzyme commission (EC) numbers obtained from Blast2GO were mapped to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes And Genomes (KEGG) Web server ([http://www.](http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) [genome.jp/kegg/\)](http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) (Kanehisa et al. [2004\)](#page-11-11).

#### **Expression level analyses**

We used RSEM (Li and Dewey [2011](#page-11-12)) to estimate expression levels as fragments per transcript kilobase per million fragments mapped (FPKM) using the script (run\_RSEM\_ align\_n\_estimate.pl) available within the Trinity software package.

#### **Identification of EST‑SSRs**

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were detected using the MISA tool (MIcroSAtellite; [http://pgrc.ipk-gatersleben.](http://pgrc.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/) [de/misa/](http://pgrc.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/)), which identified both perfect and compound repeats. We searched for SSRs with motifs ranging from dito hexanucleotides in size. The parameters were adjusted for identification of perfect di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide motifs with a minimum of 9, 6, 5, 4 and 3 repeats, respectively. Adjacent microsatellites ≤10 nt apart were considered as compound repeats (Iorizzo et al. [2011](#page-10-7)).

#### Sequence composition of the transcriptome

All transcripts with ORFs  $\geq$  300 bp were uploaded to the Galaxy website (http://main.g2.bx.psu.edu/) for GC percentage statistics and codon usage analysis. The "cusp" program from EMBOSS bioinformatics software packages of Galaxy was used. As a result, the GC content of the coding regions was 43.71 %, with 50.57 % for the first letter of the codon, 40.09 % for the second and 40.47 %

for the third. Results also indicated that the most frequently used stop codon in *I. nil* is TGA (occurs in 40.6 % of all transcripts) followed by TAA (35.4 %) and TAG (24.0 %). According to the codon usage analysis, we found that the most abundant amino acids in *I. nil* are non-polar amino acids (43.45 %) and polar neutral amino acids (30.41 %), while basic amino acids (13.89 %) and acidic amino acids (12.03 %) are relatively few in number (Table S2) (Tao et al. 2012, 2013).

#### Functional annotation and classification

Annotation of contigs was achieved based on searches of specific databases for sequence similarity. All of the contigs were compared to the sequences in the NCBI (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) nonredundant protein (nr) database, using BLASTX with a cutoff *E* value of  $10^{-3}$ . A total of 137,307 contigs (62.38 % of all contigs) returned a significant BLASTX result and matched 67,167 unique protein accessions.

The contigs homologous to known sequences in nr were further annotated with GO terms using Blast2GO. A total of 89,781 (40.79 %) contigs were assigned 455,335 GO term annotations, which could be classified into three categories: biological processes, molecular functions and cellular components. Among biological processes, "metabolic process" represented the most abundant subcategory followed by "cellular process" and "biological regulation". Within the molecular function category, contigs were assigned to "catalytic activity", "binding", "transporter activity" and "molecular transducer activity". Regarding cellular components, the major GO terms were "cell" and "cell part". Furthermore, a relatively large number of contigs were clustered into terms of "reproduction" and "pigmentation" as well. ## **Expression analysis of candidate enzymes in the anthocyanins biosynthesis**

Anthocyanins are a large class of flavonoids that are responsible for various colors of flowers, fruits and other tissues. As flowers of *I. nil* are a rich source of anthocyanins, contigs participating in the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway were selected and studied in detail. Eleven gene families, represented by 17 published protein sequences, were compared to our assembly using TBLASTN. This search revealed nine contigs with significant matches to seven gene families and to all the *I. nil* anthocyanin reference genes from GenBank (*CHS*-*D, CHS*-*E, CHI, F3H, F3′H, DFR, ANS, UF3GT* and *In3GGT*) (Table S4). In most instances, more than one contig was annotated as the same gene, and for these transcripts the longest theoretical translation was obtained to explore protein sequence conservation.

Considering plant secondary metabolites, anthocyanin compounds are a major class of flavonoids issuing from the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. The first three biosynthetic reactions in phenylpropanoid metabolism, referred to as the general phenylpropanoid pathway, produce *p*-coumaroyl CoA, which is a major branch-point metabolite between the production of the flavonoids and the production of the three lignin monomers called monolignols (Schilmiller et al. [2009](#page-11-18)). The biochemical pathway of anthocyanins starts with the synthesis of chalcone, which is controlled by chalcone synthase (*CHS*) and proceeds along a single path up to anthocyanins.

To study whether high anthocyanin percentage results from high expression levels of anthocyanin biosynthesisrelated genes in *I. nil*, the expression of transcripts encoding for key enzymes for anthocyanins and lignins was analyzed. As a result, nearly all genes involved in the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway exhibited a relatively high expression abundances, while genes involved in lignin biosynthesis had a low expression level. For example, transcript comp80198\_c0\_seq 1, annotated as CHS, an enzyme condensing three molecules of malonyl CoA and 4-coumaroyl CoA into a chalcone, was 358.33 FPKM. On the contrary, transcript comp141362\_c0\_seq 3, encoding laccases, an enzyme which catalyzes the final step of lignification, presented a very low expression level (9.02 FPKM). These findings impel us to hypothesize that the repression of lignin biosynthesis might lead to the redirection of the metabolic flux into anthocyanins. #### **Mining database for SSRs**

SSRs are highly informative and widely used for genetics, evolution and breeding research. In the plant genome, approximately 3–7 % of expressed genes contain putative SSR motifs, mainly within the untranslated regions of the mRNA (Liu et al. [2012\)](#page-11-22). To evaluate the usefulness of this database for marker development, all of the assembled sequences were mined for SSRs. Using the MISA Perl script, a total of 15,537 SSRs were identified in 14,048 sequences, with 1304 sequences containing more than 1 SSR and 269 SSRs presenting in compound form.

In addition, the distribution, frequency and type of the potential SSRs were also analyzed in this work. On average, we found 1 SSR per 13 Kb in *I. nil* transcriptome sequences, and the frequency of SSRs was 7.06 %. ## **Discussion**

Although *I. nil* has been used intensively for genetic studies, its nuclear genome and transcriptome sequence are still unrevealed. With a long history of attempts, cross-breeding aimed at producing different kinds of flower colors in *I. nil* cultivars had been conducted (Yamamizo et al. [2010](#page-11-26)), which might lead to high heterozygosity in *I. nil*'s genome. In the last 5 years, with sequencing throughput expanded and read costs decreased, the advancement of sequencing technologies has been a driver for genomic-based inquiries in biology (Gongora-Castillo and Buell [2013](#page-10-17)). However, as *I. nil* might have a high degree of heterozygosity,

<span id="page-8-1"></span>**Fig. 9** Frequency distribution of SSRs based on motif types. The AG/CT di-nucleotide repeat motif was the most abundant motif detected in our SSRs

it is not feasible to consider whole genome sequencing of *I. nil* because of the high costs and time consumption. Fortunately, RNA sequencing provides a more powerful approach for mapping and quantifying the transcriptomes (Zhang et al. [2014\)](#page-11-7). Compared with traditional large-scale EST sequencing, RNA sequencing provides more abundant information for biological studies and offers more fundamental insights into biological processes, etc. Prior to our work, there was no sequencing information on *I. nil* in public databases. Therefore, we carried out de novo transcriptome assembly of *I. nil* using short-read (Illumina) sequencing. Due to the heterozygosity of alleles at each locus in *I. nil*, accurate representation of gene and gene models can be challenging (Gongora-Castillo and Buell [2013](#page-10-17)). Therefore, we selected Trinity software as the de novo assembler in the present study. De novo assemblies produced by the Trintity assembler provide additional information about the contigs generated by grouping alleles, isoforms and paralogs into 'components' (Haas et al. [2013\)](#page-10-15). In the absence of a reference genome, this approach might swell the overall contig numbers in de novo assemblies, but the additional information will undoubtedly benefit the genome sequencing project in the future. Additionally, the cDNA library was constructed using pooled RNA samples from four representative tissue types. Since the aims of this project were to generate as many cDNA sequences as possible, pooling was a cost-effective strategy based on statistical and practical considerations (Sloan et al. [2012](#page-11-27); Xu et al. [2012\)](#page-11-28).

In this study, we constructed a comprehensive 228 M transcriptome of *I. nil* by assembling approximately 27 Gbp RNA-Seq PE reads data. The draft transcriptome consists of 220,117 contigs, of which 137,307 were annotated using the Blast2GO pipeline. Due to lack of genomic resources, the proportions of contigs to known proteins in GenBank were considered as a very useful metric (Ness et al. [2011](#page-11-29)). Nearly, 62.38 % of our contigs matched with the nr protein database, which demonstrated the effectiveness of the assembly in capturing a large portion of the transcriptome (Hao da et al. [2011](#page-10-18); Tian et al. [2013\)](#page-11-17).

Quality of the de novo assembly was also confirmed by sequence comparison, CDS predictions and marker validation. Comparison of assembled contigs with full-length of cloned *I. nil* gene sequences confirmed the high quality of the assembly. Seventy-one percent of the contigs aligned nearly completely with mRNA reference sequences, whose results were similar to those previous reports (Iorizzo et al. [2011](#page-10-7)). Additionally, based on the CDS predictions, the majority of our assembled contigs (48.41 %) belong to protein-coding genes with an ORF ≥100aa. This number (106,552) is lower than the predicted number of proteincoding genes (137,307) in the Blast2GO annotation, mainly attributable to the fact that many relatively short contigs (ORF ≤ 100aa) were annotated as well (Ramilowski et al. [2013\)](#page-11-30). Most of these assignments were based on marginal BLAST hits near the significance threshold and often involved low-complexity sequences, which are prone to producing false-positive hits (Sloan et al. [2012](#page-11-27)). There are still contigs without significant matches to the existing databases, which could reflect either novel, specific genes of *I. nil* or non-coding RNAs or fragments of longer RNAs, etc.

Further study with GO analysis revealed that *I. nil* plant genes are involved in many biological processes and many genes were classified as 'metabolic process' and 'catalytic activity', which in turn suggests a large diversity of enzymes involved in various syntheses of primary metabolites. This is consistent with the KEGG analysis that identified 'starch and sucrose metabolism' and 'purine metabolism' to be the first and second most represented pathway, with a total number of 1949 (11.19 %) and 1824 (10.47 %) genes mapped to it, respectively (Ramilowski et al. [2013\)](#page-11-30).

For most plant flowers, anthocyanins are the principal flower pigments, and their metabolic pathway has been extensively studied (Grotewold [2006](#page-10-19)). However, the mechanisms that control anthocyanin catabolism in *I. nil* flowers are far from conclusive. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that such abundance estimation of candidate enzymes in the biosynthesis of anthocyanins will facilitate further research on the underlying mechanisms. Here, as revealed by biochemical pathway mapping, total structural genes in the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway were covered by our sequence data and we detected that all structural genes were highly expressed in *I. nil*. In the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, *p*-coumaroyl CoA is the common substrate of two enzymes, chalcone synthase (CHS), which catalyzes the formation of the flavonoid skeleton, and hydroxycinnamoyl transferase (HCT), which leads to the biosynthesis of two major lignin-building units (Besseau et al. [2007](#page-10-20)). Considering the expression analysis results in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, we hypothesize that the repression of lignins biosynthesis might leads to the redirection of the metabolic flux into anthocyanins, which could be the reason of high anthocyanins content in *I. nil* flowers.

The genetic mechanisms controlling the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth have been widely studied in *Arabidopsis* and rice. Nevertheless, it is still necessary to study the mechanistic basis of flowering response in a wider range of plant species (Hayama et al. [2007\)](#page-10-5). It is our understanding that flowering is controlled by a complex network of signaling pathways, and hormones are included in the essential elements that control the induction of flowering. In *I. nil*, ethylene is thought to be a strong inhibitor of flowering and that the inhibitory effect may depend on its influence on the ABA level (Wilmowicz et al. [2008](#page-11-31)). It was reported that IAA also inhibited *I. nil* flowering, probably resulting from its stimulatory effect on ethylene production (Frankowski et al. [2009\)](#page-10-21). Though effects on flowering time control were established for GA, its roles in *I. nil* flowering have not been elucidated. Out of these concerns, to investigate GA's roles in *I. nil* flowering, we have listed the transcripts with putative functions for controlling the GA metabolic pathway and GA-signal transduction in "[Results](#page-2-0)". Our findings lead us to hypothesize that GA might also play an important role in flowering in *I. nil*, which is mediated through the GA-signaling pathway. Moreover, sequence homologs for the floral integrator pathway genes related to *FT*, *FLC*, *SOC1* and *TFL*, together with floral meristem identity genes including *LFY*, *AP1* and *AP2*, were all identified in our *I. nil* transcriptome database. To summarize, all the above information would accelerate more detailed studies on the mechanism of floral differentiation for *I. nil*.

Here, we conducted Illumina sequencing to profile the transcriptome of *I. nil*. To the best of our knowledge, this study presents the first next-generation sequencing effort and transcriptome annotation of a non-model flowering plant (*I. nil*), and this resource should lay an important foundation for future genetic or genomic studies on morning glory species. Using functional annotation and sequence comparison with *Arabidopsis* or other phylogenetically related species, we have identified a large amount of putative *I. nil* homologs that are probably involved in four major flowering pathways and the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway. A large number of SSRs were also identified and ready for marker development. The information uncovered in this study should serve as a valuable resource for understanding the relevant research in *I. nil* and will undoubtedly benefit future breeding work.
