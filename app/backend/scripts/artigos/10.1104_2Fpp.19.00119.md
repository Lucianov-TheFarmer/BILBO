### 34 **Abstract**

35 Salinity impairs seed germination and seedling establishment. We investigated the role of 36 Arabidopsis (*Arabidopsis thaliana)* CALMODULIN-BINDING TRANSCRIPTION ACTIVATOR 37 6 (CAMTA6) in salinity stress responses during early germination. Compared with wild type (WT), 38 the *camta6-4* and *camta6-5* mutants were more tolerant to NaCl and abscisic acid (ABA), and accumulated less Na<sup>+</sup> 39 . In contrast, 4- to 11-day-old *camta6* seedlings were more sensitive to NaCl. In *camta6*, expression of HIGH-AFFINITY K+ 40 TRANSPORTER 1 (*AtHKT1;1)*, encoding a Na+ /K<sup>+</sup> 41 transporter, was restricted to the radicles and was not enhanced by NaCl or ABA. During 42 germination, the *camta6 hkt1* double mutant was as sensitive as WT and *hkt1* to NaCl, suggesting 43 that HKT1;1 is crucial for the salt tolerance of *camta6*. An abscisic acid response element (ABRE) 44 in the *HKT1;1* promoter was found to be indispensable for the enhanced expression of the gene in 45 response to NaCl and to ABA. Transcriptome analysis of WT and *camta6-5* with and without salt 46 treatment, revealed 1020 up- and 1467 down-regulated salt-responsive genes in the WT. Among 47 these, 638 up- and 1242 down-regulated genes were classified as CAMTA6-dependent. Expression 48 of several known salt stress-associated genes, including *SOS1* and *NHX1*, was impaired in *camta6* 49 mutants. Bioinformatics analysis of the 5' upstream sequences of the salt-responsive CAMTA6- 50 dependent up-regulated genes revealed the CACGTGTC motif as the most prominent element, 51 representing an ABRE and a potential CAMTA -binding site. We suggest that CAMTA6 regulates, 52 directly or indirectly, the expression of most of the salt-responsive genes in germinating seeds, including genes that are crucial for Na<sup>+</sup> 53 homeostasis and salt-stress tolerance.

## 56 **Introduction**

57 High salt concentrations in the growth medium arrest seed germination and impair seedling 58 establishment. In view of enhanced salinization of arable lands world-wide (Wang et al., 2003), 59 there is an urgent need to better understand the mechanisms governing plant responses to salinity at 60 this early stage of plant development. Moreover, crop production in marginal lands has been 61 suggested to have great potential to mitigate food shortages and improve food security (Tester and 62 Langridge, 2010). *Arabidopsis thaliana* has been used as a model plant to elucidate the mechanisms 63 underlying salinity stress responses (Zhu, 2000; Yang and Guo, 2018). Typically, exposing seeds to 64 ≥75 mM NaCl in the growth medium significantly decreases the germination rate of wild type (WT, 65 Col-0) seeds (Xiong and Zhu, 2002). Subsequently, several Arabidopsis mutants were isolated 66 based on their improved tolerance to high NaCl levels during germination: *salt resistant (rs*) (Saleki 67 et al., 1993), *reduced salt sensitivity* (*rss*) (Werner and Finkelstein, 1995), *salobreno* (*san*)

68 (Quesada et al., 2000), *low expression of osmotic stress-responsive genes 5* (*los5*), also referred as 69 *abscisic acid deficient 3* (*aba3*) (Xiong, 2001), and *los6/aba1* (Xiong et al., 2002). Interestingly, the seeds of two alleles of *rs* (*rs17* and *rs20*) accumulated less sodium ions (Na+ 70 ), compared with 71 WT seeds, when sown on media containing 150 mM NaCl (Saleki et al., 1993), suggesting that the improved tolerance in these mutants is achieved, at least in part, by adjusting the Na<sup>+</sup> 72 levels, which allows germination under such unfavorable conditions. Prevention of excess Na+ 73 accumulation in seedlings or mature plants could be achieved by reducing Na+ 74 entry into the plant tissue to minimize 75 transport to the shoot, which is more sensitive than the root (Munns, 2002; Essah, 2003; Shkolnik-76 Inbar et al., 2013), by compartmentation into the vacuoles of specific tissues (Apse et al., 1999), or by export of Na+ to the external medium (Shi et al., 2000; Shi et al., 2002). However, how Na+ 77 78 transport systems are regulated and coordinated with seed metabolism and growth during 79 germination remains to be elucidated.

80 The phytohormone abscisic acid (ABA) plays an essential role in abiotic stress responses 81 during seed germination (Nakashima and Yamaguchi-Shinozaki, 2013). ABA levels were elevated 82 dramatically in response to salt or osmotic stress (Ribaut and Pilet, 1991; Park et al., 2008; Zhang 83 et al., 2016), which is required for activating a cascade of genes (Shinozaki and Yamaguchi-84 Shinozaki, 1997; Yuan et al., 2011). The reduced sensitivity to NaCl during germination of *san5*, 85 an allele of the ABA-regulated APETALA2 (AP2) transcription factor (TF) *ABSCISIC ACID*  86 *INSENSITIVE 4* (*abi4*), which is impaired in ABA perception (Finkelstein, 1994), and the ABA 87 biosynthesis defective *aba1* and *aba3* mutants, suggest a salt tolerance mechanism that involves 88 ABA signaling. However, numerous dehydration and salt-induced genes were found not to be 89 responsive to exogenous ABA treatment, suggesting the existence of ABA-independent signaling 90 pathways (Yoshida et al., 2014). Moreover, the ability to germinate in the presence of relatively 91 high NaCl concentrations does not mean that the developing seedlings will survive the stress 92 (Saleki et al., 1993; Quesada et al., 2000).

93 Interestingly, *abi4* seeds display improved tolerance to salinity during germination (Quesada et al., 2000) and maturity (Shkolnik-Inbar et al., 2013). ABI4 was found to limit Na<sup>+</sup> 94 95 transfer from the root to the shoot via the xylem, by direct negative regulation of the transcript level of the Na+ /K<sup>+</sup> 96 transporter AtHKT1;1 (Shkolnik-Inbar et al., 2013). Indeed, in Arabidopsis, 97 *hkt1* seedlings are hypersensitive to salinity, a phenotype that was attributed to the role of HKT1;1 in unloading sodium ions (Na+ 98 ) from the root vascular tissues, and thus limiting their accumulation 99 in sensitive shoot tissues (Mäser et al., 2002; Rus et al., 2004; Sunarpi et al., 2005; Horie et al., 2006). In wheat (*Triticum aestivum*), improved tolerance to NaCl, associated with reduced Na+ 100 101 uptake, was obtained by silencing the *HKT1* gene by antisense RNA (Laurie et al., 2002), 102 consistent with the suggested ability of AtHKT1;1 and wheat HKT1 to facilitate Na<sup>+</sup> entry into 103 plant cells (Gassmann et al., 1996; Uozumi et al., 2000). Another important Na<sup>+</sup> homeostasis 104 regulator is the plasma membrane Na<sup>+</sup>/H<sup>+</sup> antiporter SALT OVERLY SENSITIVE 1 (SOS1) (Shi 105 et al., 2000; Shi et al., 2002). Nevertheless, how these and other transport mechanisms function in 106 maintaining Na<sup>+</sup> homeostasis during early germination in conjunction with other salt stress 107 responses is currently not fully understood.

Calcium  $(Ca^{2+})$  was reported to promote salt tolerance during germination and in the early 108 stages of seedling development of many plant species. It was suggested to improve the selectivity of 109 110 potassium ion ( $K^+$ ) uptake, as well as the  $K^+/Na^+$  ratios when  $Na^+$  levels in the medium are high (Lahaye and Epstein, 1969; Bernstein et al., 1970; Lahaye and Epstein, 1971; Kent and Läuchli, 111 1985; Bliss et al., 1986). NaCl induces a rapid elevation of cytosolic  $Ca^{2+}$  levels ( $[Ca^{2+}]_{cyt}$ ) in root 112 cells (Choi et al., 2016).  $Ca^{2+}$  functions as a second messenger that regulates the expression of many 113 114 salt-responsive genes (Knight et al., 1997; Tracy et al., 2008) and modulates other cellular functions including Na<sup>+</sup> transporters (Zhu, 2002). Bioinformatics analysis of transcriptome changes in 115 response to artificially generated  $[Ca^{2+}]_{cvt}$  transients revealed 230  $Ca^{2+}$ -responsive genes, with a 116 high occurrence of ABA-related *cis*-regulatory elements in their upstream sequences: an ABA-117 118 responsive element (ABRE; CACGTG[C/T/G]) and its coupling element (ABRE-CE; 119 [C/A]ACGCG[T/C/G]) (Kaplan et al., 2006; Whalley and Knight., 2013). Therefore, the ABA and Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling pathways in response to abiotic stress, including salt, most likely merge at specific 120 121 key points (Reddy et al., 2011).

Previously, while investigating the mechanisms of  $Ca^{2+}$  signaling under abiotic stress, a 122 family of six genes encoding calmodulin (CaM)-binding transcription activators (CAMTAs; also 123 124 referred to as SRs) was identified in Arabidopsis (Bouché et al., 2002; Yang and Poovaiah, 2002; 125 Mitsuda et al., 2003; Finkler et al., 2007a; Doherty et al., 2009; Shen et al., 2015), all with similar 126 DNA-binding domains. These genes were suggested to mediate responses to various external and 127 hormonal stimuli, including abiotic stresses and ABA (Yang and Poovaiah, 2002; Finkler et al., 128 2007a). Similar CAMTAs are present in other plant species including crops such as maize (Zea 129 mays; Yue et al., 2015), tomato (Solanum lycopersicum; Li et al., 2014), rice (Oryza sativa; Choi et 130 al., 2005; Rahman et al., 2016), and others. To date, in Arabidopsis, the major focus has been on investigating the roles of CAMTA1 (Kaplan et al., 2006; Galon et al., 2010; Kim et al., 2013; 131 132 Pandey et al., 2013), CAMTA3 (Galon et al., 2008; Du et al., 2009; Laluk et al., 2012; Kim et al., 133 2013; Kim et al., 2017; Jacob et al., 2018) and to a lesser extent CAMTA2 (Kim et al., 2013; Kim 134 et al., 2017) and CAMTA5 (Kidokoro et al., 2017), whereas the functions of Arabidopsis CAMTA4 135 and CAMTA6 remain unclear. In Medicago truncatula, overexpression of the CaM-like protein 136 MtCML40 resulted in down-regulation of the expression of *MtHKT1;1* and *MtHKT1;2* (Zhang et al., 2019), both of which encode proteins associated with Na<sup>+</sup> 137 removal from the shoot, and caused enhanced sensitivity to salt at germination. Thus Ca2+ signaling is involved in maintaining Na+ 138 139 homeostasis during germination, partly by regulation of *HKT1* expression. However, the TFs 140 mediating the regulation of salt-responsive genes at early stages of germination have not been 141 reported. The research presented herein reports on the importance of CAMTA6 (also designated SR3; At3G16940) in maintaining Na+ 142 homeostasis in germinating Arabidopsis seeds by mediating 143 both ABA-dependent and -independent signaling pathways, including the regulation of At*HKT1*. 144

### 146 **Results**

147 *camta6* **mutants display reduced sensitivity to salt and ABA during germination** 

148 In order to determine the germination behavior of *camta6* mutants in the presence of salt (NaCl), 149 seeds of WT (Col-0) and two alleles of *camta6* mutants (*camta6-4* and *camta6-5*) were sown on 150 0.25 x Murashige and Skoog (MS)/ agar-solidified medium, supplemented with 0 (control) or 200 151 mM NaCl. When sown on 200 mM NaCl-containing media, Col-0 seeds displayed a 1.7 ± 1.5% 152 germination rate, in contrast to 46.3 ± 4.5% germination for *camta6-4* and 30.3 ± 8% for *camta6-5* 153 (Fig. 1A, B). All genotypes displayed germination rates of ~100% when no NaCl was added to the 154 medium (Fig. 1B). Alternatively, when sown on mannitol-containing medium, Col-0 and *camta6* 155 seeds showed similar germination rates (Supplemental Fig. S1), indicating that the *camta6* salt-156 tolerance phenotype is not due to a general tolerance to osmotic stress. Complementation assays, 157 performed by introducing a *CAMTA6pro:CAMTA6-EGFP* construct to *camta6* mutants, resulted in 158 full restoration of the sensitivity to 200 mM NaCl (Supplemental Fig. S2), confirming that the 159 enhanced salt tolerance of the mutants is indeed due to the lack of active CAMTA6.

To further study the effect of Ca2+ 160 on germination in the presence of NaCl, Col-0 and 161 *camta6* seeds were sown on a medium supplemented with 10 mM CaCl2, or with 1 mM of the highly selective Ca2+ 162 chelator ethylene glycol-bis(β-aminoethyl ether)-N,N,N',N'-tetraacetic acid 163 (EGTA), or with combined treatments of NaCl with CaCl2 or NaCl with EGTA. Interestingly, in the 164 presence of CaCl2, the germination rate on NaCl-containing medium was similar for all genotypes (Fig. 1B; ~83%), confirming the previously described effect of Ca2+ 165 on improving salt tolerance 166 during germination (Kent and Läuchli, 1985; Bliss et al., 1986). In contrast, the addition of EGTA 167 increased seed sensitivity to NaCl with a significantly more severe effect on *camta6* seeds (Fig. 168 1B). As controls, the addition of CaCl2 or EGTA at the same concentrations, but without NaCl, did 169 not impair seed germination (Fig. 1B).

170 To broaden our understanding of CAMTA6 function in NaCl stress tolerance, we examined 171 *camta6* mutants at later stages of development (4- to 11-day-old, grown on agar solidified medium; 172 materials and methods), supplemented with 0 (control) or 250 mM NaCl (Fig. 1C, D). Both *camta6* 173 alleles displayed hypersensitivity to NaCl compared to Col-0. Although root growth was similarly 174 arrested in all lines, the shoots of *camta6* seedlings appeared more wilted and bleached as a result of 175 enhanced chlorophyll degradation in the presence of NaCl (Fig. 1C, D). No significant phenotypic 176 differences were observed among the three genotypes grown under control conditions (Fig. 1C, D). 177 Observation of 5- to 6-week-old, soil-grown plants revealed earlier senescence of rosette and 178 cauline leaves of *camta6* mutants compared to leaves of Col-0 (Supplemental Fig. S3 A, B and C). 179 Interestingly, the phenotype of early senescence was more pronounced in cauline leaves

180 (Supplemental Fig. S3 D and E), in agreement with data indicating highest mRNA levels of 181 *CAMTA6* in cauline leaves (Supplemental Fig. S3 F and G; http://bar.utoronto.ca/efp/cgi-182 bin/efpWeb.cgi).

183 To determine whether CAMTA6 activity is associated with ABA signaling during early 184 germination, seeds of Col-0 and *camta6* mutants were sown on medium supplemented with 0, 1, 185 1.5, 2, or 10 µM ABA. Seed germination of all genotypes in the presence of 0, 1 or 10 µM ABA 186 was ~100, 50 or 0 %, respectively (Fig. 1E). However, in the presence of 1.5 or 2 µM ABA, the 187 germination rate of Col-0 seeds was very low (~10%), whereas both *camta6* alleles maintained 188 significantly higher germination rates, above 25% (Fig. 1E).

189 The phenotypes of Arabidopsis *camta6* mutants at different stages of development, from 190 germination to maturity, with or without NaCl or ABA, suggest various key roles of CAMTA6 in 191 response to stress, phytohormone signaling and development.

## 193 *CAMTA6* **expression is enhanced by salt during germination**

194 *CAMTA6* (*SR3*) transcript levels were reported to be enhanced by salt in three-week-old Col-0 195 plants (Yang and Poovaiah, 2002). Prasad et al. (2016) suggested that CAMTA3 (SR1) acts as a 196 negative regulator of salt tolerance. However, the expression pattern during the early stages of 197 germination, under control and salt stress conditions, has not been reported. Therefore, *CAMTA6* 198 transcript levels were first examined by RT-qPCR in pre-germinating Col-0 seedlings ejected from 199 seeds that were treated with 200 mM NaCl for 16 hrs. *CAMTA6* transcript levels rose by 2.3-fold 200 and ~2-fold in response to NaCl and ABA treatments, respectively (Fig. 2A). Examination of 201 *CAMTA6* promoter activity *in planta*, performed by GUS staining of pre-germinated transgenic 202 seedlings harboring the *CAMTA6pro*:*GUS* construct, showed significant increases of expression 203 levels in response to NaCl and to ABA treatments compared to controls, mainly in the cotyledon 204 margins (Fig. 2B). Enhanced expression levels of *CAMTA6* in response to NaCl and ABA were 205 confirmed by fluorometric GUS assays of seed extracts, showing ~2-fold increase of *CAMTA6* 206 promoter activity following exposure to NaCl or to ABA (Fig. 2C), consistent with the RT-qPCR 207 results. Thus, salt-enhanced expression of the *CAMTA6* gene during germination is consistent with 208 its involvement in the response to salt stress at this early developmental stage and at later stages (4- 209 11 day-old; Fig. 1C, D).

## 213 *camta6* **mutants accumulate less sodium ions during germination under salt stress**

One mechanism of salt tolerance relies on limiting Na+ 214 accumulation in cells (Munns and Tester, 2008; Almeida et al., 2017). To quantify the Na+ 215 accumulation in Col-0 and *camta6* pre-germinated 216 seedlings, extracts of very young germinating seedlings that were ejected from seeds treated with 0

(control) or 200 mM NaCl for 16 hrs (as shown for day 1 in Fig. 1A) were analyzed for Na+ 217 content 218 by Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES). Col-0 seedlings were found to accumulate 1.5 ± 0.2 µg Na<sup>+</sup> 219 /seedling under control conditions and 2.9 ± 0.2 µg Na+ 220 /seedling when exposed to 200 mM NaCl during germination (Fig. 3). Interestingly, both *camta6* alleles accumulated slightly (albeit not statistically significant) more Na+ 221 than the WT when sown on control medium (Fig. 3), whereas, strikingly, no increase in Na+ 222 content was found in these

223 mutants after salt treatment (Fig 3). These data strongly suggest that CAMTA6 is involved in the regulation of factors that maintain Na+ 224 homeostasis in germinating seedlings under salt stress. Moreover, the reduced accumulation of Na+ 225 in the *camta6* mutants may explain, at least in part, 226 their higher tolerance to NaCl compared to WT.

## 228 **CAMTA6 regulates the expression of** *HKT1;1*

229 To further assess the function of CAMTA6 in germinating seedlings in response to ABA or NaCl, the relative transcript levels of several key Na+ 230 transporters were determined in Col-0 and *camta6* 231 mutants using RT-qPCR. In Col-0, the expression of the *HKT1;1* antiporter gene was enhanced by 232 either salt (5.1-fold) or ABA (1.9-fold) treatments, compared with control conditions (Fig. 4A). 233 Interestingly, the basal relative *HKT1;1* transcript level in the background of *camta6-4* or *camta6-5* 234 alleles was found to be higher than in Col-0 (1.8- and 1.9-fold, respectively). In contrast, the 235 expression of *HKT1;1* was not increased by ABA or NaCl treatments in *camta6* mutants (Fig. 4A). 236 To better understand the nature of the interaction between HKT1;1 and CAMTA6 in

237 response to salt stress during germination, we crossed the *hkt1* mutant with the two alleles of 238 *camta6* to create *camta6 hkt1* double mutants*.* Germination assays with and without NaCl were

239 performed with the double mutants alongside Col-0 and the *camta6* and *hkt1* single mutants (Fig. 240 4B). Interestingly, while *camta6* seeds displayed the aforementioned improved tolerance to salt, 241 *hkt1*, *hkt1-4*, and the *camta6 hkt1* double mutant displayed similar germination rates as WT 242 (Fig.4B). These data suggest that HKT1 is necessary for salt tolerance in the absence of a functional 243 CAMTA6, even though *HKT1* expression levels are not enhanced by NaCl or ABA in the *camta6* 244 mutant.

245 One possible explanation for these observations is a change in *HKT1's* spatial expression 246 pattern in *camta6*. To test this possibility, we assessed the *HKT1;1* promoter activity in Col-0 and 247 *camta6* by generating transgenic plants that express the *GUS* reporter gene under the transcriptional 248 regulation of the native *HKT1;1* promoter (2000 bp or 500 bp upstream of the translation start 249 codon, *HKT1;1pro*: GUS). GUS staining in *HKT1;1pro*:*GUS* (500 bp promoter segment) 250 transgenic pre-germinated seedlings, under control conditions, showed expression mainly in the 251 cotyledons and radicle, excluding the radicle tip and the shoot meristem (Fig. 4C). In agreement 252 with the RT-qPCR results, ABA or NaCl treatments significantly enhanced the activity of the 253 *HKT1;1* promoter (Fig. 4C). In contrast, in the *camta6* genetic background, *HKT1;1* expression was 254 restricted to the radicle and was not affected by ABA or NaCl (Fig. 4C), consistent with the 255 aforementioned RT-qPCR findings (Fig. 4A). The change in spatial expression of *HKT1;1* in the 256 *camta6* mutant may be a key factor in the apparent enhanced tolerance to NaCl in the *camta6* 257 mutant. Similar GUS expression profiles were obtained with a 2000 bp promoter segment in Col-0 258 (not shown). Further analysis proceeded with the 500 bp promoter segment.

259 Bioinformatics analysis of the *HKT1;1* promoter sequence revealed an ABA Response 260 Element (ABRE; ACGTGT) located 75 bp upstream of the translation start codon (Fig. 4C). In 261 order to assess the importance of this ABRE in the responsiveness of *HKT1;1* to ABA or NaCl 262 treatments, the ABRE was replaced by the TTTAAA sequence (the recognition site of the *Dra*I 263 restriction enzyme). As a result of this substitution, and in contrast to the WT promoter, GUS 264 activity regulated by the mutated ABRE (*HKT1;1[ABRE mutation]pro*:GUS) was restricted to the 265 cotyledons' upper parts, around the vascular tissues, and was not significantly enhanced or spread 266 to other leaf parts following ABA or NaCl treatments (Fig. 4C). These results suggest that the 267 ABRE mediates the expression of *HKT1;1,* both in response to salt and to ABA and under non-268 stressful (control) conditions.

269 Interestingly, under control conditions, the spatial GUS expression pattern driven by the 270 *HKT1;1* promoter differed in the genetic backgrounds of Col-0 and the *camta6* mutant. In Col-0, 271 expression was almost evenly spread throughout the germinating seedling, whereas in the mutant it 272 was confined (and even enhanced) to the radicle. These results suggest that CAMTA6 is important 273 for the regulation of *HKT1;1* expression in germinating seeds, both under salt stress and under non-274 stressful conditions. Finally, comparing the expression of *HKT1;1pro*:*GUS* with that of the 275 *HKT1;1[ABRE mutation]pro*:GUS in the genetic background of *camta6,* revealed no major 276 differences in the spatial expression pattern under control conditions, NaCl, or ABA treatment. 277 This confirms that CAMTA6 is required for the enhanced expression of *HKT1;1* in response to 278 NaCl and ABA. However, basal expression of *HKT1;1* likely involves other *cis*-elements that are 279 not dependent on CAMTA6. We note that the mechanism by which CAMTA6 regulates *HKT1;1*  280 expression (e.g., by direct binding to its promoter, or indirectly through another TFs, is yet to be 281 determined.

# 284 **CAMTA6 mediates the expression of the majority of the salt-responsive genes in germinating** 285 **seeds**

12

286 To further assess the extent of CAMTA6 involvement in salt stress responses in germinating seeds, 287 transcriptome analysis was performed on WT and the *camta6-5* mutant under control or salt stress 288 conditions (3 biological repeats of each genotype for each treatment). Expression reads higher than 289 10 were filtered for differential expression using Partek Genomics Suite (Partek GS v 6.6; 290 http://www.partek.com/pgs). Differentially expressed gene lists of WT-salt vs. WT-control (cutoff 291 fold change (FC)  $\geq$  |3| and pFDR < 0.05) and *camta6-5*-salt vs. *camta6-5*-control were extracted. 292 Venn diagrams were created to compare differentially expressed gene lists (cutoffs  $FC \ge |3|$  and 293 pFDR < 0.05) (Supplemental Fig. S4; Materials and Methods). Using these selected cutoffs, 1020 294 up- and 1467 down-regulated salt-responsive genes were obtained for WT (Supplemental Table 295 S1), whereas only 404 up- and 240 down-regulated salt-responsive genes were found in the *camta6*-296 5 mutant (Figure 5; Supplemental Table S2). These results imply that the expression of the majority 297 of the salt-responsive genes (up- and down-regulated) in the WT are affected by CAMTA6 (62% 298 and 84%, respectively). To further dissect the role of CAMTA6 in the salt stress response, we 299 defined two groups of genes: those whose expression was responsive to salt in the WT, but not in 300 the camta6 mutant, were designated as CAMTA6-dependent (638 up- and 1242 down-regulated 301 genes; Supplemental Fig. S4; Supplemental Table S3), whereas those with common salt-regulated 302 expression in both WT and *camta6-5* were designated as CAMTA6-independent (382 up- and 225 303 down-regulated; Supplemental Fig. S4; Supplemental Table S4).

304 Heat maps of differentially expressed genes of WT-salt vs. WT-control (cutoffs  $FC \ge |3|$ 305 and pFDR < 0.05) and *camta6-5*-salt vs. *camta6-5*-control were made using Partek Genomics 306 Suite hierarchical clustering (Supplemental Fig. S5). Pearson's correlation of 2524 differentially expressed gene lists of salt vs. control of both WT and *camta6-5* (cutoffs  $FC \ge |3|$  and pFDR <307 308 0.05) was performed using Partek (Supplemental Fig. S6). Both the Pearson's correlation diagram 309 and the heat maps indicate that the WT and the *camta6-5* mutant are very similar under control 310 (non-stressed) conditions (99%; Supplemental Table S5; using FC > |3| and p-value < 0.05), 311 whereas the transcriptomes of salt-treated WT and *camta6-5* have a similarity of only 85%. The 312 similarity of WT-control vs. WT-salt-treated is 41%, whereas for the mutant control vs. the mutant 313 salt-treated it is 77%. This again shows that CAMTA6 mediates a substantial change of the 314 transcriptome profile in response to salt stress.

Dot plot analysis of the expression levels of some genes of interest, known to be associated with salt stress, is depicted in Supplemental Fig. S7. These include *SOS1*, cation/proton exchanger 3 (*CAX3*), Na<sup>+</sup>/H<sup>+</sup> antiporter (*NHX1*), Ca<sup>2+</sup>-ATPase 12 (*ACA12*), *ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE 5* (*ABI5*), and *DEHYDRATION RESPONSE ELEMENT-BINDING PROTEIN 19* (*DREB19*). This plot validates the observation that major differences occurred when the WT-control and WT-salt-

320 treated samples were compared; moreover, it demonstrates that the biological repeats have been 321 accurately handled. However, although the replicates were found to be reproducible, the transcript 322 levels of *CAMTA6* and *HKT1;1* were below the detection threshold of the transcriptomic analysis, 323 in contrast with the confirmed expression revealed by RT-qPCR and promoter activity (Figs. 2 and 324 4, respectively). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CAMTA6 is indispensable for salt-325 responsive modulation of the transcriptome during early germination, including some of the well-326 known salt stress-responsive genes. However, we note that this analysis does not discern the direct 327 CAMTA6 target genes from the other salt-responsive genes that may be regulated by other TFs, 328 some of which may be direct CAMTA6 targets.

## 330 **Promoter analysis of salt-responsive genes suggests a key role for ABA-dependent signaling**

331 In order to better understand the signaling pathways involved in salt stress responses in 332 germinating seeds, we analyzed 1000 b of genomic sequence (representing the presumed 333 promoters upstream of each up- and down-regulated salt-responsive gene in the WT and *camta6-5* 334 mutant and in the CAMTA6-dependent and -independent groups (Fig. 5; Supplemental Tables S1, 335 S2, S3, S4). First, a specific motif search (targeted approach) in the differentially expressed genes 336 was performed by using Promomer (http://www.bar.utoronto.ca/ntools/cgi-337 bin/BAR\_Promomer.cgi; Materials and Methods). Based on Choi et al. (2005); Finkler et al. 338 (2007a) and Finkler et al. (2007b), we first tested the occurrence of the following ABRE and 339 CAMTA-binding *cis*-elements in the differentially expressed genes relative to their respective 340 distribution in the genome: C/ACGCGC/G/T, representing the CAMTA-binding sites (CGCG-341 core), C/ACGTGT, representing ABRE/CAMTA (CGTGT-core), and ACGTGG/C, representing 342 the ABRE/non-CAMTA-binding motifs (Fig. 5). ## 380 **Promoter analysis of up- and down-regulated salt-responsive genes: an unbiased motif scan**

381 To learn more about the possible involvement of other *cis*-elements mediating the salt response, 382 we analyzed the predicted promoters of the salt-responsive genes by using an unbiased motif scan 383 tool (Amadeus/Allegro http://acgt.cs.tau.ac.il/allegro/download.html; Materials and Methods). 384 Analysis of 1000 bp of genomic sequences of the presumed promoter regions of the corresponding 385 genes was carried out in order to detect over-representation of *cis*-elements for TF-binding. The 386 JASPAR *cis*-element database was used for motif detection. In the WT (Fig. 6; left), the unbiased 387 motif analysis of up-regulated salt-responsive genes revealed a motif that consists of the palindromic G-box *cis*-element CACGTG (p-value: 4.4E-68 388 ), which is also the scaffold for the 389 ABRE motif ACGTGG/T. This element may serve as the binding site for numerous TFs, such as 390 bZIP (Hobo et al., 1999) and bHLH TFs such as ABI5, ABF1, PIF4, HY5, MYC, and others (Choi 391 et al., 2000; Uno et al., 2000). Indeed, analysis of these promoters using Pscan 392 (http://159.149.160.88/pscan/) identified those TFs with a significant p-value (Supplemental Table S6). The other detected motif with a consensus AGTCGGTA and a p-value of 2.6E-27 393 is DRE-like, 394 known to mediate salt and drought responses through binding of TFs including several DREBs 395 (Chen et al., 2002). Analysis of 1467 salt-responsive down-regulated genes in the WT revealed an 8-bp motif with a consensus of CCACGTGC and a p-value of 2.2E-22 396 , which represents a G-box 397 and an ABRE (non-CAMTA) motif for the binding of bHLH TF family members such as PIFs, 398 SPT, MYC, and others. The other motif contains the consensus ACTAGTAC with a p-value of 2.9E-20 399 , which may represent promoter motifs involved in light-regulated gene expression, such as 400 SORLREP1 (Hudson and Quail, 2003).

# 486 **Phenotypes of** *camta6* **mutants: salt tolerance during early germination and dependence on Ca2+** 487

488 The early stages of germination are critical for establishing the emerging seedling; therefore, 489 stressful growth conditions in the vicinity of the germinating seed could be detrimental to the 490 germination rate, the establishment of seedlings, and the yield in the case of crops. Surprisingly, in 491 spite of ample investigations of salt stress responses in plants and the identification of crucial 492 mechanisms involved in these responses, the effects of salt stress at the specific stage of early 493 germination have not been thoroughly investigated. Here we sought to assess the possible role of 494 CAMTA6 at the stage of early germination. CAMTA6 is a member of a 6-gene family of TFs in 495 Arabidopsis (Bouché et al., 2002; Yang and Poovaiah, 2002; Finkler et al., 2007a). Like all CAMTAs, it contains a Ca2+ 496 -dependent CaM-binding domain and is therefore assumed to function *in vivo* in response to Ca2+ 497 signaling. Analysis of germinating *camta6* mutants (two alleles and 498 several complementation lines tested) revealed that the mutants are more tolerant than the WT to 499 salt stress (Fig. 1 A, B). Moreover, the mutants also appear to be more resistant to exogenous 500 ABA (Fig. 1 E), suggesting that at least some of the CAMTA6-dependent pathways that are 501 relevant to salt stress responses are mediated by ABA, consistent with promoter analysis of the salt-responsive CAMTA6-dependent genes. Interestingly, in the presence of exogenous Ca2+ 502 , germination rates in the presence of NaCl were much higher (> 80%) than those without Ca2+ 503 , and 504 were similar in the WT and the *camta6* mutants (Fig. 1 B). This responsiveness of *camta6* seeds indicates the existence of more than one signaling pathway in response to Ca2+ 505 and NaCl. In 506 contrast, in the presence of NaCl and exogenous EGTA, germination rates in the *camta6* mutants 507 were significantly lower than those in the WT (Fig. 1 B). These results suggest that the role of CAMTA6, as a salt stress regulator of gene expression, is associated with Ca2+ 508 signaling, as expected for a CaM-binding protein, and consistent with early studies that revealed the role of Ca2+ 509 510 in salt stress tolerance (LaHaye and Epstein, 1969). Consistent with the enhanced tolerance of germinating *camta6* mutants to salt stress, the mutants accumulated less Na+ 511 than did WT seeds when exposed to high concentrations of NaCl (200 mM) (Fig. 3). The reduced Na<sup>+</sup> 512 accumulation 513 in the *camta6* mutants could be the result of impaired transcription levels (or spatial and / or temporal profiles) of genes encoding Na<sup>+</sup> 514 transporters, including *SOS1, NHX1, HKT1,* or others, 515 in response to NaCl (Supplemental Fig. S7; and Fig. 4). However, *sos1* germinating seeds were 516 reported to display hypersensitivity to salt during germination (Wu et al., 1996), suggesting that 517 the attenuated induction of *SOS1* transcription in response to NaCl in *camta6* is not related to the 518 enhanced tolerance of *camta6* germinating seeds to salt. The enhanced expression of *CAMTA6* in 519 response to salt stress (Fig. 2) and the extent of CAMTA6-dependent salt-responsive genes 520 revealed by transcriptomic analysis support the involvement of CAMTA6 in salt stress responses. 521 Finally, the observed hypersensitivity of *camta6* older seedlings to salt (Fig. 1 C, D) suggests 522 different roles of CAMTA6 in response to salt at different stages of development, which is 523 reminiscent of the previously described hypersensitivity of *hkt1* seedlings to salt (Mäser et al., 524 2002; Rus et al., 2004; Sunarpi et al., 2005; Horie et al., 2006).

## 526 **Regulation of salt-responsive genes by CAMTA6: The** *HKT1;1* **gene as a case study**

HKT1;1 is a Na+ /K<sup>+</sup> 527 transporter that plays an important role in plant responses to salt stress (Rus et 528 al., 2001; Laurie et al., 2002; Rus et al., 2010), and whose expression is regulated by both NaCl 529 and ABA (Shkolnik-Inbar et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2019). Here we tested the importance of 530 CAMTA6 in the regulation of *HKT1;1*. Both transcript analysis and promoter activity tests *in vivo*  531 confirmed that CAMTA6 is indispensable for NaCl- and ABA-regulated expression of *HKT1;1*. 532 Moreover, based on *HKT1;1* promoter:GUS activity, CAMTA6 is also likely important for the 533 basal expression levels and in determining the spatial pattern of *HKT1;1* expression under control 534 conditions (Fig. 4 C). Furthermore, we identified an ABRE (ACGTGT) in the promoter of *HKT1;1*  535 as a crucial element in the response of the gene to both NaCl and ABA. This ABRE could be a 536 target for different types of TFs, including CAMTA (Finkler et al., 2007b). However, we do not 537 know whether CAMTA6 regulates *HKT1;1* by directly binding to it, or by mediating other TFs, 538 perhaps related to ABA signaling. To further asses the nature of the interaction between CAMTA6 539 and *HKT1;1,* we examined the germination rate of *camta6* and *hkt1* single mutants and *camta6 /*  540 *hkt1* double mutants in the presence of NaCl. Interestingly, *hkt1* and *hkt1 / camta6* seeds 541 germinated at the same rate as Col-0 seeds in the presence of 200 mM NaCl in contrast to the 542 tolerance observed in *camta6* germinating seeds (Fig. 4B). Examination of the promoter activity of 543 *HKT1;1* in *camta6-5* indicates an exclusive expression in the radicle, that was not affected by salt 544 treatment and might explain the improved salt tolerance of germinating *camta6* mutants and the 545 sensitivity of wild type, *hkt1* and *hkt1* / *camta6* (Fig. 4C). In this case, it is possible that HKT1;1, that facilitates the removal of Na+ 546 from the xylem sap to reduce its mobilization to the salt 547 sensitive shoot tissues (Sunarpi et al., 2005; Davenport et al., 2007; Moller et al., 2009), promotes the removal of Na<sup>+</sup> 548 from the non-differentiated radicle tissue of a germinating seedling, which results in the observed reduced Na+ 549 accumulation (Fig. 3) and enhanced tolerance (Fig. 1 A and 550 B). The lack of expression of *HKT1* in the cotyledons of the germinating seeds of the *camta6* mutant (Fig. 4) may also contribute to the enhanced tolerance to Na<sup>+</sup> 551 . However, analysis of the 552 double mutants compared to the single mutants indicates that in the absence of a functional 553 CAMTA6, HKT1;1 is necessary for the salt tolerance phenotype even though the overall 554 expression levels of *HKT1;1* are not enhanced by NaCl in the *camta6* mutant (Fig. 4A). Finally, 555 the possible contribution of other mechanisms to salt tolerance in germinating *camta6* mutants cannot be ruled out, possibly by regulation of other Na+ 556 transporters. However, the downregulation of the potential regulators of Na+ 557 cellular level, *NHX1* and *SOS1,* in *camta6* (Supplemental Fig. S7) is unlikely to result in Na<sup>+</sup> 558 accumulation, as *nhx1 nhx2* double mutants were found to accumulate more Na<sup>+</sup> , and SOS1 overexpressors were reported to accumulate less Na<sup>+</sup> 559 (Shi et al., 560 2002; Bassil et al., 2011; Barragán et al., 2012), further supporting the suggested role of HKT1;1 in regulation of Na+ 561 levels in germinating seedlings.

## 563 **CAMTA6 is a master regulator of salt-responsive genes in germinating seeds**

564 Comparison of the transcriptomes of WT and the *camta6* mutant under control conditions and 565 under salt stress revealed that in the WT, 62% and 84% of the salt-responsive up- and down-566 regulated genes, respectively, are CAMTA6-dependent. Moreover, the most prominent *cis*-567 elements in the salt-responsive genes are ABREs, consistent with the known role of ABA in salt 568 stress responses and with the phenotypes of the *camta6* mutants regarding their tolerance to both 569 NaCl and ABA. The majority of the salt-responsive genes contain the core element for the 570 ABRE/CAMTA (C/ACGTGT) and ABRE/non-CAMTA (ACGTGG/C) binding sites, but no 571 enrichment of CAMTA-binding motifs (A/CCGCGG/C/T). However, a substantial and significant 572 depletion of CAMTA-binding motifs in the *camta6* salt-treated mutant suggests a prominent role 573 in salt stress regulation by CAMTA6 (Fig. 5). Therefore, CAMTA6 regulates the transcription of 574 many of the salt-responsive genes through ABA signaling and other TFs. Consistent with this 575 suggestion is the fact that TFs are the most prominent class of salt-responsive genes in the WT and 576 in the CAMTA6-dependent group (Fig. 8; Supplemental Fig. S8; Supplemental Table S9). Indeed, 577 20% and 30% of the TF genes, which are CAMTA6-dependent in response to salt stress, contain 578 CAMTA-binding sites and ABRE/CAMTA binding sites, respectively (Supplemental Table S9). 579 Moreover, 40% of the CAMTA6-dependent upregulated genes in response to salt were identified 580 as associated with seed development processes (Supplemental Table S10). Collectively, these data 581 indicate that CAMTA6 operates upstream of several TFs that regulate the expression of numerous 582 downstream salt-responsive genes. The extent of gene regulation by CAMTA6 in response to salt 583 stress is not surprising in view of reports that CAMTA1, CAMTA2, and CAMTA3 are involved in 584 regulating thousands of genes in response to cold stress (Kim et al., 2013). Furthermore, 585 identifying the rapid stress response element (RSRE) by Walley et al. (2007), as a CAMTA-

binding *cis*-element CGCGTT, and the role of both Ca2+ 586 and CAMTA in activation of the RSRE 587 (Benn et al., 2014) collectively imply that CAMTAs are master regulators of gene expression in 588 response to environmental cues.

589

## 590 **CAMTA6-dependent and –independent ABA signaling in response to salt stress**

Coinciding *cis*-elements for Ca2+ 591 - and ABA-responsiveness have been previously described 592 (Kaplan et al., 2006; Finkler et al., 2007b; Galon et al., 2010; Whalley and Knight., 2013). 593 Moreover, whereas the core sequence for CAMTA-binding is CGCG, CAMTAs may also bind to 594 the ABRE sequence C/ACGTGT (Choi et al., 2005; Finkler et al., 2007a; Finkler et al., 2007b). 595 Other ABREs are not CAMTA-binding sites (e.g., ACGTGG/C) (Finkler et al., 2007a). Thus, it is 596 not surprising that among the salt-responsive genes in the *camta6* mutant the most prominent motif 597 in both the up-regulated (33%) and down-regulated (20%) genes is the ABRE/non-CAMTA 598 (ACGTGG/C) (Fig. 5), whereas, the ABRE/CAMTA motif is the most prominent in the 599 CAMTA6-dependent class, both in the up-regulated (31%) and down-regulated genes (24%).

600 The transcriptome analysis of salt-treated Arabidopsis germinating seeds strongly suggests 601 that salt stress signaling is mediated by ABA-dependent and ABA-independent pathways. Further 602 evidence supports cross-talk between these pathways, which are involved in the regulation of 603 osmotic/salt stress, but thus far, very little is known about the mechanisms underlying this cross-604 talk (Yoshida et al., 2014). The role of ABFs and DREB2 TFs in mediating salt stress signaling via 605 ABA-dependent and ABA-independent pathways, respectively, was further expanded by the 606 analysis of *camta6-5* and WT salt-treated differentially expressed genes. The identification of 607 numerous CAMTA6-dependent genes encoding TFs, such as the AP2 family (DREB2G, 608 DREB2B, DREB2C, DREB19, DREB1B, and DREB1C), the ABFs (ABF1), MYBs/MYCs, and 609 NACs, all known to mediate the regulation of salt stress gene expression (Boudsocq, 2005; 610 Yamaguchi-Shinozaki and Shinozaki, 2006), suggests a role for CAMTA6 as an upstream master 611 regulator of salt stress signaling.

How Ca2+ 612 signaling modulates CAMTA6 function is not known. From the studies of 613 CAMTA3 (SR1) in conjunction with defense responses to pathogens, it is clear that CAMTA3 614 functions as a repressor of gene expression of some of its targets, which is removed upon stimulation. Yet, if and how Ca2+ 615 and CaM are involved in the repression and activation stages is 616 not clear (Fromm and Finkler, 2015). Du et al. (2009) suggested that CaM binding to CAMTA3 is 617 required for repression of its target gene *EDS1* in response to biotic defense. On the other hand, 618 Kim et al. (2017) recently reported that repression of the CAMTA3 target genes operates through a 619 CAMTA3 module that does not involve CaM binding. However, gene activation in this case does require the binding of Ca2+ 620 /CaM (Kim et al., 2017). Activation of CAMTA-repressed genes may 621 also involve ubiquitin-mediated degradation of CAMTA, as described for CAMTA3 (Zhang et al., 622 2014). Therefore, different modes of regulation and signaling pathways may regulate each 623 CAMTA under different physiological conditions. These pathways remain to be elucidated.
