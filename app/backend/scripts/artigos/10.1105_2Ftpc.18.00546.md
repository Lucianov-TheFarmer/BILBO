Short Title: A CACTA element regulates ufol epialleles

**One-sentence summary:** Comparative sequence-based analysis reveals the causal gene of maize *Ufo1-1*, a dominant mutation that modifies *pericarp color1* expression, more than 50 years after this allele's discovery.

# ABSTRACT

The maize (*Zea mays*) mutant *Unstable factor for orange1* (*Ufo1*) has been implicated in the epigenetic modifications of *pericarp color1* (*p1*), which regulates the production of the flavonoid pigments phlobaphenes. Here we show that the *ufo1* gene maps to a genetically recalcitrant region near the centromere of chromosome 10. Transcriptome analysis of *Ufo1-1* mutant and wild-type plants identified a candidate gene in the mapping region using a comparative sequence-based approach. The candidate gene, GRMZM2G053177, is overexpressed by >45-fold in multiple tissues of *Ufo1-1*, explaining the dominance of *Ufo1-1* and its phenotypes. In the mutant stock, GRMZM2G053177 has a unique transcript originating within a CACTA transposon inserted in its first intron, and it is missing the first four codons of the wild-type transcript. GRMZM2G053177 expression is regulated by the DNA methylation status of the CACTA transposon, explaining the incomplete penetrance and poor expressivity of *Ufo1-1*.

Transgenic overexpression lines of GRMZM2G053177 (*Ufo1-1*) phenocopy the *p1*-induced pigmentation in coleoptiles, tassels, leaf sheaths, husks, pericarps, and cob glumes. Transcriptome analysis of *Ufo1* vs. wild type-tissues revealed changes in several pathways related to abiotic and biotic stress. Thus, this study addresses the enigma of *Ufo1* identity in maize, which had gone unsolved for more than 50 years.

# **INTRODUCTION**

 In maize (*Zea mays*), genes involved in pigment biosynthesis have been used in genetic studies for over 100 years (Anderson, 1924; Emerson, 1917). Pigmentation in maize is a relatively simple trait, and the easy-to-score phenotype makes it ideal for use as a marker for genetic studies. One locus of particular interest is *pericarp color1* (*p1*), which controls the production of red phlobaphene pigments in floral tissues such as cob glumes, tassel glumes, pericarps, and husks. *P1* encodes an R2R3 MYB transcription factor that directly regulates the structural genes needed for phlobaphene biosynthesis (Grotewold et al., 1994, 1991). *P1* has been identified as a major quantitative trait locus of an insecticidal compound in silks, a c-glycosyl flavone called maysin (Byrne et al., 1996), and it has been demonstrated directly that *p1* can induce maysin production (Cocciolone et al., 2005). Transcriptome experiments done in maize have provided a genome-wide regulatory framework of downstream targets regulated by *p1,* which includes over 1000 genes (Morohashi et al., 2012). Over 100 alleles of *p1* have been collected for study, with phenotypes ranging from colorless to dark red pericarps, combined with or without pigmented cob glumes, as well as many variegated alleles (Brink and Styles, 1966). Alleles of *p1* are named for their pigmentation pattern in the pericarp and cob glumes, such as *P1-wr* for *w*hite pericarp and *r*ed cob glumes. A comparative study of two alleles with different pigmentation patterns, *P1- wr* and *P1-rr*, revealed that these alleles contain similar regulatory and coding sequences but have different gene structures (Chopra et al., 1998, 1996). *P1-rr* has a single copy gene structure, while *P1-wr* has at least six copies in a tandem head-to-tail array. Additionally, the *P1-wr* allele is hypermethylated compared to *P1-rr* (Chopra et al., 1998). Transgenic experiments have reinforced the idea that different patterns of pigment produced by *p1* are epigenetically regulated (Sidorenko et al., 2000).

 There is longstanding interest in understanding regulation of *p1* and the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway (Grotewold et al., 1998; Casas et al., 2016). One aspect of *p1* gene regulation includes *trans*-acting modifiers, such as *Unstable factor for orange1* (*Ufo1*), a dominant, mutation which is unlinked from *p1* and induces phlobaphene pigment production throughout the plant body, causing plants to appear orange or red. *Ufo1* in the presence of a non-functional *p1* allele does not produce pigments, but it interacts with various functional alleles of *p1*. For example *Ufo1- 1;P1-wr* plants have the most striking phenotypes, since these plants produce ectopic pigments throughout the plant body, tassels, husks, pericarps and cob glumes due to increased expression of *p1* (Figure 1) (Chopra et al., 2003). Notably, *Ufo1-1* can release epigenetic gene silencing of a number of *p1* alleles and epialleles, including *P1-wr*, *P1-wr\**, *P1-ww DP* , *P1-pr*, *P1-rr'*, and *P1 prtp* (Chopra et al., 2003; Sekhon et al., 2007; Rhee et al., 2010; Goettel and Messing, 2013; Sekhon et al., 2012). These epigenetic modifications have been attributed to reduced DNA methylation of an upstream enhancer region as well as in the second intron of *p1*. Although *Ufo1-1* is dominant, *p1* plants carrying *Ufo1-1* may not have any obvious phenotype (incomplete penetrance) or can have a range of pigmentation from very weak to very strong (poor expressivity), making its genetic study difficult (Sekhon and Chopra, 2009). Additionally, *Ufo1- 1* plants can display a range of pleiotropic defects, such as short stature and bent plants (Chopra et al., 2003). Robbins *et al.* (2013) demonstrated that some of the bending defects of *Ufo1-1* plants could be due to reduced lignin content in the plant body. It has been suggested that the excess pigments may have a deleterious effect on plant growth, causing defects (Chopra et al., 2003). However, *Ufo1-1;P1-pr* plants are just as short as *Ufo1-1;P1-wr* plants, even though they have significantly less pigment in their plant bodies (Goettel and Messing, 2013). Furthermore, plants lacking a functional *p1* gene also display defects, albeit less severe, in the presence of *Ufo1-1* (S. Chopra, K. Wittmeyer personal observation). Thus, *Ufo1-1* seemingly affects several processes and accordingly, *ufo1* (refers to the wild-type gene) may have a broader role than just regulating the epigenetic status of *p1*, but its exact nature is unknown.

The mutant allele *Ufo1-1* was identified as a spontaneous mutation by Charles Burnham, circa

the 1960's. The mutant allele was later passed on to Derek Styles (Supplemental File 1, Early

history of '*Ufo*'). Styles noted its interaction with *p1* and the instability of *Ufo1-1* phenotypes,

thus providing the name of the controlling locus in a 1987 report (Styles, 1982; Styles et al.,

1987). In subsequent decades, work with *Ufo1-1* has proceeded slowly, due in part to the

pleiotropic defects and epigenetic components described above, as well as incomplete penetrance

of the pigment phenotype, making its genetic mapping impossible. Because of these challenges,

in the 40+ years since the original reports of this unusual allele, the molecular basis of *Ufo1-1*

has been unclear. In this report, we characterize the genetic basis of *Ufo1-1,* identify a novel

protein specific to the Poaceae, and link the pleiotropic *Ufo1* phenotypes to the insertion of a

CACTA transposon at this locus.

# **RESULTS**

# *Ufo1-1* **plants are stunted, exhibit delayed flowering, and show variable penetrance**

Maize plants carrying the *Ufo1-1* allele are affected in growth and development. We observed

several defects in the form of plant stunting, leaf rolling, and reduced plant height. We

previously showed that plant bending could be due to reduced lignin accumulation in *Ufo1-1*

plants (Robbins et al., 2013). *Ufo1-1* plants were approximately 10 inches shorter on average

than their wild-type siblings in a backcross population (Figure 1C). The reduced height was due

to reduced internode length, while the number of internodes remained the same in *Ufo1-1* and the

wild type. Overall, *Ufo1-1* plants have reduced aboveground and belowground biomass.

Additionally, silk emergence was delayed by ~3 days in *Ufo1-1* plants from the same population,

indicating that there is some effect on flowering time (Figure 1D). *Ufo1-1* plants also show

poorly penetrant phenotypes for *p1* expression (Figure 1E).

# *Ufo1-1* **lies within a non-recombining region surrounding centromere 10**

We used fine linkage mapping to identify candidate genes underlying *Ufo1-1*. In our previous

mapping study using a small population, *Ufo1-1* was placed on chromosome 10 linked to the

simple sequence repeat (SSR) marker umc1367 (Chopra et al., 2003). *Ufo1-1-*induced

phenotypes show poor penetrance and low expressivity, indicating that *Ufo1-1* is not fully

dominant. This was evidenced by examining F1 progeny of *Ufo1-1* crossed with W23, wherein

only 26% of plants had a pigmented phenotype (Sekhon and Chopra, 2009). In order to

circumvent the problem of poor penetrance, we performed more extensive fine mapping using

~4000 plants and a backcross mapping strategy, choosing only plants expressing a *Ufo1*-induced

ear (refers here to kernel and cob) pigment phenotype. These plants must be heterozygous for the

 causal locus. Initial screens identified border markers umc1785 and umc2016. Further screens of 4032 plants with internal markers did not yield any recombinants (Figure 2). We found several markers linked to the *Ufo1-1* phenotype: CAP5, CAP45, ARP6-8, and umc1381 (Not all shown). The final mapping region spans ~38 Mb around the centromere and contains about 650 gene models (B73 reference genome V3, 5b+) with 321 expressed genes (Figure 2B). A comparison of *Ufo1-1* SNPs generated from the Illumina MaizeSNP50 BeadChip revealed a region of identity by descent (IBD) shared among 13 other inbred lines, which overlaps nearly the entire mapping region (Supplemental Figure 1). We concluded that further mapping efforts would not narrow down the mapping region. Mapping and cloning efforts were continued after candidate genes were selected based on the RNA-seq data (see Candidate gene identification through k-mer analysis, below).

# **Identification of** *Ufo1-1 silent* **epialleles**

 The identification of markers linked to *Ufo1-1* allowed us to perform genotypic selection of *Ufo1-1* as opposed to relying on phenotype alone. Using the linked markers, it was confirmed that the *Ufo1-1*-linked region can be present in sibling plants that do not produce an obvious enhanced ear pigmentation phenotype. Plants that appear to be *Ufo1-1* genotypically but not phenotypically will henceforth be referred to as *U-S* for *Ufo1-1 silent*, while those with a *Ufo1-1* pigment phenotype will be referred to as *U-E* for *Ufo1-1 expresser* (Figure 1E). *U-S* plants can appear spontaneously, and their frequency is background dependent. For example, maize inbred line B73 has particularly low *Ufo1-1* penetrance and after several generations of backcrosses produces mostly *U-S* plants. By contrast, in maize inbred line W23, the frequency of silencing decreases as the backcross generations increase (Sekhon and Chopra, 2009). We isolated stable lines of *U-E* and *U-S*. Crosses of *U-S* with naïve *P1-wr* continued to have white pericarps and red cob glumes, indicating that changes of state occurred at *Ufo1-1* and not at *P1-wr*. *Ufo1-1* "silencing" appears to resemble an epigenetic phenomenon, as in very rare cases, some *U-S*  plants revert back to expressing a *Ufo1-1* phenotype.

# *ufo1* **candidate gene identified through comparative sequence analysis of RNA-seq data**

 In order to understand the function of *ufo1*, we performed RNA-seq experiments on three different tissues (Supplemental Table 1 and 2). *Ufo1-1* was introgressed into the inbred line B73 (*P1-wr*) or W23 (*P1-wr*) and whole aboveground seedlings, mature leaves, and 18 DAP pericarp were sampled (see Methods for details). Given the large mapping region of *Ufo1-1* (containing 321 expressed genes), it was not feasible to narrow the candidate gene search by mapping region alone. We therefore performed RNA-seq experiments for candidate gene(s) identification using a comparative sequence-based approach, modeled after a study used to find sex-linked sequences in persimmons (Akagi et al., 2014). Briefly, RNA-seq reads for libraries from 12 wild type, 12 *Ufo1-1*, and three *U-S* samples were broken into 25 bp k-mers (*k* bp long sequences, with a 1 bp sliding window along each read such that each read is broken into *read length – k + 1* k-mers and each k-mer overlaps the previous k-mer by *k – 1*); each k-mer sequence was counted in each library (Figure 2C, Supplemental Figure 2). This can be thought of as a collection of every 25 bp sequence combination found in the transcriptomes, enabling the detection of sequences present in some libraries but not others. *Ufo1-1*-specific k-mers were then identified as having a count less than two in all of the wild-type and *U-S* libraries and a count greater than two in all of the *Ufo1-1* libraries. The opposite parameters were used to search for wild-type-specific k-mers, but none were found. This analysis does not rely on reads that map to a reference genome, enabling it to identify sequences not found in the reference as well as novel sequence polymorphisms. The inclusion of *U-S* samples was critical to control for non-causal sequences linked to the large *Ufo1-1* mapping region.

 The analysis identified two candidates with high levels of expression in *Ufo1-1* compared to the wild type and *U-S* samples (Figure 2). Both of the candidates were within the mapping region. The first candidate does not appear to be a protein-coding gene, instead resembling a long non- coding (lnc)RNA, and it lacks a gene model in the maize reference genome. There are a number of ways lncRNAs can function, with many functions dependent upon homology to other sequences in the genome. The lncRNA in question has some homology with retrotransposon sequences present throughout the genome but has no homology within *P1-wr* sequence. Additionally, there was no correlation between lncRNA-homologous small RNAs (sRNAs) and *Ufo1-1*. Furthermore, four recombinant plants with a wild-type allele for the lncRNA still showed *Ufo1-1* phenotypes (Figure 2A). On this basis, we ruled out the lncRNA as a candidate for *Ufo1*.

The second candidate gene was GRMZM2G053177. In the RNA-seq data, the transcripts of this

gene were significantly more abundant in *Ufo1-1* seedlings, leaves, and pericarps than in wild

type (46-, 269-, and 325-fold higher than wild type, respectively) (Figure 2B).

GRMZM2G053177 is also the only protein-coding gene in the mapping region that is

differentially expressed in all three tissues in *Ufo1-1*, as compared to the wild type. Additionally,

GRMZM2G053177 appears to be expressed at normal or low levels in the *U-S* samples,

indicating that the strong increase in expression in *U-E* is associated with the *Ufo1-1* phenotype

(Figure 2B).

# **GRMZM2G053177 contains a CACTA transposon from which transcription starts in** *Ufo1-1* **plants**

 Assembly of the *de novo* transcript of GRMZM2G053177 revealed that a novel transcript, with a different 5' UTR (untranslated region) and the loss of the first four codons, is present in *Ufo1-1*; this transcript differed from the annotated gene model in the B73 reference genome (Figure 3). Using long read (PacBio) whole-genome sequences generated from the *Ufo1-1* genetic stock (introgressed into B73), the GRMZM2G053177 genomic sequence was obtained (Figure 3). Comparison with the B73 reference genome showed that GRMZM2G053177 in *Ufo1-1* contains a CACTA transposon within the first intron of the gene that is absent in the reference genome. The novel transcript present in *Ufo1-1* begins within the transposon, with the transposon sequences serving as the 5' UTR in the assembled transcript (Figure 3B). The first ATG codon of the *Ufo1-1* transcript lies within exon 2 and is an in-frame start codon, so the encoded protein is nearly identical to the wild type, except that it is missing the first four amino acids (Met-Glu- Gln-Gln encoded by the small exon 1. Using publicly available genomic sequence data (from panzea.org), inbred lines sharing the common region of identity by descent (IBD) on chromosome 10 with *Ufo1-1* were searched for the CACTA insertion, but no lines containing the CACTA within GRMZM2G053177 were found. This indicates that the CACTA insertion is unique to *Ufo1-1* genetic stock.

 In order to establish a phenotype-genotype linkage between *Ufo1-1* and the CACTA element, we screened several F2 populations from *Ufo1-1* crossed to B73, W23, or W22. Genotyping of these

 F2 populations was done with previously established *Ufo1-*linked markers (see Figure 2A) as well as a marker developed from the CACTA border and flanking GRMZM2G053177 sequences. We found 69 plants with a *Ufo1-1* phenotype, all of which carried the CACTA element, while there were no *Ufo1-1* plants without a CACTA element (Table 1). Additionally, *U-S* materials were screened for the CACTA to determine if excision of the transposon could return wild-type function. All but one of the *U-S* plants screened carried the CACTA (Table 1). The lone plant that was genotypically *Ufo1-1* but lacked a CACTA appears to be a recombination rather than a transposition event, as some linked markers were heterozygous for *Ufo1-1* while others closest to GRMZM2G053177 were homozygous for the wild-type allele. We performed sequencing across the insertion site for this plant and no excision footprint could be found, further supporting a recombination event.

# *Ufo1-1* **penetrance is correlated with DNA methylation of the CACTA insertion**

 The finding that *U-S* plants carried the CACTA but have reduced GRMZM2G053177 expression levels compared to *Ufo1-1* suggested that an enhancer-suppression phenomena could be acting on the CACTA and controlling the expression of GRMZM2G053177. We therefore performed bisulfite sequencing of the 5' end of the CACTA and its border to confirm or rule out this hypothesis. It should be noted that this region has been identified as a promoter sequence for another member of the CACTA superfamily, the *En/Spm* transposon (Raina et al., 1993). A stark contrast was found with *Ufo1-1* plants having very low levels of DNA methylation compared to sibling *U-S* plants, which had very high levels of DNA methylation comparable to that of dense heterochromatin (Figure 4, Supplemental Figure 3). We also tested the CACTA methylation of a third type of plants, which had reverted from silent to expressing. These revertant plants had intermediate levels of DNA methylation compared to *Ufo1-1* and *U-S* plants, indicating that the reversion of *U-S* to *U-E* is associated with a partial loss of DNA methylation. Additionally, the levels of siRNAs homologous to the 5' end of the CACTA were higher in *U-S* compared to *Ufo1-1* (Figure 4B).

# **Confirmation of the aberrant expression of GRMZM2G053177 in** *Ufo1-1*

 The expression of GRMZM2G053177 is normally limited to the endosperm in wild-type plants, according to the maize gene atlas (Stelpflug et al., 2016; Sekhon et al., 2011). We measured the expression of GRMZM2G053177 and the reporter gene *p1* using qRT-PCR in leaves, 10 days after pollination (DAP) seeds, 18 DAP pericarps, 18 DAP endosperm, and 18 DAP embryo from *U-E*, *U-S*, and related wild-type plants (Figure 4C). In the *U-S* and wild-type samples, GRMZM2G053177 was expressed at a level less than 1% that of the *actin1* gene in leaves, 10 DAP seeds, 18 DAP pericarps, and 18 DAP embryos, while its expression level rose to ~1.5% that of *actin1* in wild-type 18 DAP endosperm. Interestingly, compared to the wild type, this gene was expressed at a level ~15-fold higher in each of the tissues in *U-E*, except for pericarps, in which it was over ~200-fold upregulated. In addition, *p1* was expressed in each of the tissues in *U-E*, with the highest expression levels in leaves, 10 DAP seeds, and 18 DAP pericarp. This is in contrast with the RNA-seq data, in which *p1* expression was below the level of detection in leaves.

 Temporally, the earliest expression of this gene was found in 6 DAP kernels and reached a maximum between 8 and 12 DAP in the endosperm, followed by a modest decrease in transcript levels through ~30 DAP (Chen et al., 2014; Li et al., 2014). Spatially, GRMZM2G053177 has the highest expression level in a sub-region of the endosperm called the conducting zone (Zhan et al., 2015). The conducting zone is found right above the basal endosperm transfer layer (BETL) and is thought to help transport solutes to the central endosperm (Becraft, 2001). The conducting zone genes (including GRMZM2G053177) are enriched in the gene ontology (GO) categories response-to-stress, cadmium ion binding, and carbohydrate metabolism (Zhan et al., 2015).

# **Transgenic expression of GRMZM2G053177 phenocopies the** *Ufo1* **mutant**

To further test the hypothesized identity of the candidate gene, we designed an overexpression

- construct using the GRMZM2G053177 coding sequence and the 3' UTR from the *Ufo1-1*
- transcript, driven by the maize *ubiquitin* promoter. T0 plants were then crossed with different
- inbreds carrying *P1-wr* (B73, W23, and W22). We developed an assay to screen seedlings for an
- active *Ufo1-1* phenotype by assessing the production of flavan-4-ols (the precursor of *p1* induced

 phlobaphenes) in the coleoptile. In this assay, flavanols are extracted in acidic butanol and then the absorbance spectra are measured. This method can distinguish the two predominant pigment precursors, flavan-4-ols, with a peak ~565 nm and flavan-3,4-diols (anthocyanin precursors), with a peak between 530 and 545 (Grotewold et al., 1998). Further confirmation of the pigment precursors comes from boiling the extract, which leaves the flavan-3,4-diol peak intact but removes the major flavan-4-ol peak at ~565 nm and shifts it to ~480–510 nm. We screened the coleoptiles of several wild-type maize lines including B73, W23, W22, and Hi-II, as well as *Ufo1-1* related plants (Supplemental Table 3). We found that B73 and Hi-II both produce light- dependent anthocyanin in coleoptiles, while W23 and W22 do not produce any flavonoid pigments in the coleoptiles. The presence of phlobaphene precursors was only found in *Ufo1-1* lines but not in *U-S* or segregating wild-type lines. This demonstrates that ectopic expression of *p1* in the coleoptile is associated the mutant *Ufo1-1* allele.

 Next, we screened the T1 (T0 x *P1-wr*) transgenic seedlings overexpressing GRMZM2G053177 for the production of flavan-4-ols in the coleoptile. Of the 11 independent events examined, we detected flavan-4-ols in five of them (20 plants total) (Supplemental Table 4, Figure 5). The six other events may also have small amounts of flavan-4-ols that were masked by flavan-3,4-diols. The presence of flavan-4-ols was limited exclusively to plants carrying the transgene. We also phenotyped these plants through harvest time. Of the five events, two had plants containing increased levels of phlobaphene pigments in tassels, while one event produced plants with dark red husks and pericarps, just like *Ufo1-1* plants (Figure 5B). Additional transgenic events grown in the field also had several of the pleiotropic defects found in *Ufo1-1* plants, including dwarf/stunted plants, rolled leaves, and chlorotic/yellow stalks. These results were consistent with the notion that GRMZM2G053177 is *ufo1* because the overexpression of this gene phenocopied the ectopic expression of the *p1* pigment as well as the pleiotropic defects found in the mutant. However, because a gene sequence encoding a slightly truncated version of the protein lacking the first four amino acids was used to make the transgene, these results do not rule out the possible role of these first four amino acids in *Ufo1-1* phenotypes. The loss of these amino acids could change the localization of the protein if these amino acids are part of a signal peptide, or these could change the confirmation of the entire protein. Further work is needed in this regard.

**Specificity of GRMZM2G053177 to the Poaceae**

 No known annotation has been ascribed to the 326-amino-acid protein encoded by GRMZM2G053177. Extensive BLAST searches were used to identify putatively orthologous genes in other plant species. Homologs were found in a few Poaceae species including *Sorghum bicolor*) (Sobic.007G066566.1, 58% identity and Sobic.007G066200.1, 54% identity), foxtail millet (*Setaria italica*) (LOC101754388, 35% identity, discontinued in current annotation), and from domesticated and wild rice varieties (*Oryza sativa* and wild *Oryza* species), but each only has limited identity with GRMZM2G053177\_P02 (Supplemental Figure 4). In each of these species, the homologous genes were found in the syntenic regions of the respective species, which is consistent with a single, common origin (Figure 6, Supplemental Figure 4). It appears from sequence alignments that the gene has undergone rapid sequence divergence among the different grass species. Additionally, sorghum contains two copies of the gene that appear to have arisen from duplication after the split from maize. Rice also has two gene copies according to the current gene model predictions. Finally, in both *Setaria* species, a single copy of the gene in the syntenic region is present; however, both species lack a gene model for this gene in current versions. The record XM\_004974324.1 from *Setaria italica* has been removed, but it did contain the predicted mRNA for the gene. Careful examination of the genomic sequences shows that the full-length open reading frame (ORF) from the predicted gene can be found without any polymorphisms or stop codons, and that proper splice sites can be found for each of the introns, indicating that the gene at least has the potential to be properly transcribed in these species. Internally, the gene appears to contain several partially homologous repeats. One surprising aspect is that we found no evidence for this gene in other close relatives within the Poaceae, indicating that it may have been lost or has diverged too far to be identified using BLAST searches. Taken together, we find that GRMZM2G053177 first arose in the Poaceae, but it is unclear if it arose from another gene or what that gene may have been. It has rapidly diverged in sequence among different species and has expanded through duplications of internal sequences as well as at least one whole-gene duplication within sorghum.

 This maize gene and its orthologs are located in syntenic regions and are present within a cluster of cupin genes (Figure 6A). BLASTP of Sobic.007G066200.1 revealed homology with a DnaJ

 domain in the middle of this ortholog from position 163 to 301 (E-value 4.16e-08). However, this region is largely unique to Sobic.007G066200.1, with only ~40 amino acids overlapping with GRMZM2G053177 towards the end of the region. Additionally, the overlapping sequence is not well conserved, and BLASTP using GRMZM2G053177 did not yield a match to the DnaJ domain. It did show homology with an adenylate kinase (ADK) superfamily domain from position 39-88, however, the e-value of 1.80e-03 is somewhat low and neither sorghum ortholog has hits for this domain.

# *Ufo1-1* **transcriptomes have increased representation of stress-response genes**

 We next sought to characterize the function of *Ufo1-1* using RNA-seq analysis. In the vegetative tissues (seedling and leaf), there were two broad categories of enriched GO terms among highly upregulated genes: response-to-stress and transcription (Figure 7). There is also coordinated regulation of gene enriched for several categories, such as response to high-light intensity (GO:0009644), heat (GO:0009408), hydrogen peroxide (GO:0042542), cold (GO:0009409), and hormone stimulus (GO:0032870). The *Ufo1-1* plants appeared to be under stress even though they were grown under the same conditions as the wild-type controls. The similarity of upregulation of the 'response' GO categories and the transcription GO categories suggests that upregulation of transcription factor gene could be a part of the response to stress.

 The broader GO category of response-to-stress (GO:0006950) includes many genes encoding transcription factors, such as MYB, bHLH, ERF, bZIP, WRKY, and NAC transcription factors, as well as heat shock proteins, peroxidases, serine/threonine protein kinases, NB-ARC disease resistance proteins, and several genes involved in carbohydrate/sugar metabolism, among others (Figure 8). Among the transcription factors, the bHLH family is large and has diverse functions, including abiotic stress tolerance, anthocyanin biosynthesis, phytochrome signaling, and globulin expression (Wang et al., 2018). Likewise, MYBs are a large and diverse family with members involved in secondary metabolism, cell morphogenesis, the cell cycle, defense, and stress responses (Dubos et al., 2010). Ethylene response factors (ERFs) are of particular interest because these are involved in responses to environmental stimuli (Xu et al., 2008). The bZIP members are involved in pathogen defense, light and stress signaling, seed maturation, and

 flower development (Jakoby et al 2002). Additionally, WRKY transcription factors play a role in responses to abiotic and biotic stress as well as development (Wu et al 2005).

#### **Soluble sugar levels are elevated in** *Ufo1-1* **leaves and in light-dependent lesions**

 Increased soluble sugar levels are a normal part of the stress response in plants (Couée et al., 2006). We therefore measured total starch, fructose, glucose, and sucrose levels in mature leaves of *Ufo1-1* and *U-S* plants. Compared to *U-S* plants, the starch content was significantly lower in *Ufo1-1*, while the soluble sugar (fructose, glucose, and sucrose) levels were significantly higher (Supplemental Figure 5). The sugar levels were elevated in *Ufo1-1* compared to the control samples but were still within the normal range for wild-type leaves that has been reported previously (Braun et al., 2006). These metabolite data are also supported by the RNA-seq data from leaf tissue. The levels of several amylases involved in the breakdown of starch were upregulated in *Ufo1-1*, as well as the levels of many invertases needed for conversion of sucrose into simple sugars (Supplemental Figure 5B).

 Interestingly, we observed the formation of light-induced lesions on the leaves of *Ufo1-1* plants. In some populations of *Ufo1-1,* lesions formed on the upper-most leaves of plants just before the initiation of tasseling. Lesions first appeared as small, light green discolored areas and quickly spread as the lesion area died. Within a week or so, most of the affected leaf has died. The prevalence of the lesions was greater during the hotter, dry parts of the season, indicating there could be some environmental influence. Leaves containing lesions when stained with DAB showed enhanced ROS levels due to the presence of H2O2 (Figure 9). *U-S* and wild-type plants did not show these lesions.

 We also measured the soluble sugar and starch contents from *Ufo1-1* light-induced lesions and the green tissue around the lesion during early lesion formation. At this stage, lesions are light green/yellow and somewhat resemble yellow patches found in *tie-dyed* mutants, which are defective in loading sucrose into the phloem (Slewinski et al., 2012; Braun et al., 2006). We found that sucrose and starch levels within the lesions were highly elevated compared to the green tissue around them as well as the B73 control leaves (Figure 9). Sucrose levels were also elevated in the green *U-E* tissue compared to the B73 control, while starch levels were not significantly different from the control. The levels of soluble sugars and starch within the lesions  were comparable to those of the *tie-dyed1* and *sut1* mutants, which have defects in sugar transport (Braun et al., 2006; Slewinski et al., 2009).

#### **DISCUSSION**

# *Ufo1* **results from aberrant and ectopic expression of GRMZM2G053177**

 The identification of the causal gene of *Ufo1-1* has been hindered by the inability to sufficiently narrow the mapping region to a reasonable size because of poor penetrance. However, several lines of evidence show that GRMZM2G053177 is the causal gene of *Ufo1-1*. First, the comparative k-mer analysis only identified two candidates that were unique in the *Ufo1-1* transcriptome. Both are highly overexpressed in *Ufo1-1,* and GRMZM2G053177 contains the only polymorphic transcript. This also means that no other polymorphisms were identified in any other genes. The other candidate, a lncRNAs, has been ruled out, as it has been found to be unlinked from the *Ufo1-1* phenotype in some recombinant plants of the mapping population (Figure 2). The wild-type GRMZM2G053177 is only expressed in developing kernels, mostly the endosperm from 6 DAP through the end of development (Stelpflug et al., 2016). The finding that it is expressed in seedling and leaf tissue in *Ufo1-1* is aberrant. Secondly, GRMZM2G053177 contains a CACTA transposon inserted in the first intron of the gene, which is unique to the *Ufo1-1* allele compared to 13 maize inbred lines that share a region of identity by descent around this gene. Importantly, the overall behavior of *Ufo1-1* can be explained by the notion that the CACTA transposon acts as an enhancer and that it has been silenced through DNA methylation. Similar mechanisms have been reported for the *En/Spm* transposons, which can influence the expression of nearby genes depending on the methylation status and presence of other transposable elements (Fedoroff, 2013; Ong-Abdullah et al., 2015). Other copies similar to the CACTA inserted in *Ufo1-1* can be found in the reference maize genome. These copies can contribute to homology-dependent gene silencing and copy number differences between different inbreds, and may help to explain differences in *Ufo1-1* penetrance among different genetic backgrounds. Interestingly, no excision events of the CACTA were found, and its unknown whether an autonomous element exists or even if this CACTA is capable of excision in the genetic background tested so far.

Overexpression can explain the dominant phenotype of *Ufo1-1* for the reporter *p1*. Similarly,

- loss of expression due to DNA methylation of the CACTA explains the poor penetrance and
- expressivity of *Ufo1-1*. We observed a classical tight correlation between DNA methylation and

small RNA abundance at this CACTA element. Epialleles that are silent (*U-S*), as well as those

that revert from *U-S* to *U-E* (see DNA methylation, Figure 4), add to the validation of

GRMZM2G053177. Finally, transgenic lines overexpressing GRMZM2G053177 also had

ectopic expression of *p1* just like the mutant *Ufo1-1* plants.

 The lack of annotated orthologs, lack of conservation, unusual gene structure, and limited expression make it difficult to determine the function of this *ufo1* gene. One explanation could be that GRMZM2G053177 acts as a transcription factor for *p1* or for a gene closely upstream of *p1*. Another possibility (based on the wild-type expression pattern of this gene) is that it plays a role in seed development, maturation, and preparing the seed for desiccation. This could explain the increased expression of antioxidant and osmoprotectant genes in *Ufo1-1* plants, which ectopically and constitutively express this candidate gene at levels 50- to 200-fold that of the wild type in different tissues.

## *Ufo1-1* **plants are constitutively under stress in the absence of exogenous stress**

 Several stress phenotypes are seen in *Ufo1-1* expressing plants, and in the transcriptome data, we also found enrichment for several categories of transcription factors associated with stress responses. Seedlings and leaves in particular show phenotypes that can be linked with clear upregulation of stress response genes. The transcriptome indicates that *Ufo1-1* plants are under continuous stress, which may be reflected by the hypersensitivity of *Ufo1-1* plants to heat/water stress as well as lesion formation (Figure 9). These *Ufo1-1* lesions have elevated sugar levels. Soluble sugars play a central role in stress responses (Couée et al., 2006; Van Den Ende and Valluru, 2009), and soluble sugar levels are also elevated in response to many types of stress, including chilling and drought (De Roover et al., 2000; Shi et al., 2015; Hodges et al., 1997). Several genes involved in sugar metabolism are upregulated by *Ufo1-1,* including *α-amylase, β-amylase*, and several invertases (Supplemental Figure 5). However, further analyses are needed

 to directly implicate increased sugar levels in the developmental defects observed in *Ufo1-1*  plants.

- Of particular interest is the effect of sugar on the anthocyanin and flavonoid pathways. The induction of anthocyanin production by sucrose has been well studied in Arabidopsis and includes the MYB-BHLH-WRKY (MBW) complexes (Solfanelli et al., 2006; Van den Ende and El-Esawe, 2014), whose function is replicated by the C1/R1 B1/Pl1 or P1 transcription factors of maize. There is also evidence that sucrose can activate anthocyanin production in maize (Kim et al., 2006; Ma et al., 2009). Unlike anthocyanin production, the expression of *p1* has not been shown to be responsive to sugar in maize, although it has been shown to be induced by UV-B treatment in high altitude landraces (Ferreyra et al., 2010; Rius et al., 2012). It will be interesting to find out if UV-B treatment or other stresses involve sugar accumulation and how they relate to the expression of *p1*.
- How the overexpression of GRMZM2G053177 causes stress is still unknown; a functional
- characterization will help decipher this issue. One hypothesis for its function is based on its
- expression in the endosperm conducting zone, which is also enriched for stress response genes,
- cadmium ion binding, and carbohydrate metabolism genes (Zhan et al., 2015). A recent study
- (Xiong et al., 2017) using gene regulatory network (GRN) analysis found that
- GRMZM2G053177 is one of the five unidentified genes that play critical roles in maize kernel
- development.
- Previous studies have linked decreases in DNA methylation and H3k9me2 of *p1* enhancer
- elements with *Ufo1-1*-induced activation of *p1* (Sekhon et al., 2007; Sekhon and Chopra, 2009;
- Sekhon et al., 2012); however, further studies will be needed to reveal the mechanism(s)
- underlying how these epigenetic changes are directed by *Ufo1-1* at *p1* and any other maize loci.
- The overexpression of *ufo1* in transgenic plants induces enhanced flavonoid pigmentation
- phenotypes in different tissues (similar to *Ufo1-1*). Further analysis of these transgenes will
- allow us to dissect the epigenetic mechanisms involved.

# **METHODS**

#### **Genetic Stocks**

Original *Zea mays Ufo1-1* genetic stocks in an unknown background were obtained from Derek

- Styles as described previously (Chopra et al., 2003) and maintained through repeated selfing or
- introgressed into other maize inbred lines. Inbred lines B73, W23, and W22 were obtained from
- the maize genetic stock center (Maize co-op). Mapping populations were developed through
- repeated backcrosses of *Ufo1-1* with the inbred parent B73 or W23 to create near isogenic lines
- carrying *Ufo1-1*. The backcross population BC5 was used for B73 and BC10 was used for W23.
- F2 populations of *Ufo1-1* crossed to B73, W23, and W22 were also used for linkage studies. The
- *Ufo1-silent* (*U-S*) genetic stocks used were isolated from crosses of *Ufo1-1* (*U-E*) and B73,
- which were then selfed to the F5 generation with selection for red *Ufo1-1* pericarp pigment. The

F5 progenies were genotyped for *Ufo1-1* plants homozygous for either *Ufo1-1* or *ufo1* (wild type)

and were then selfed. The homozygous *Ufo1-1* F6 plants were found to have sibling plants with

and without *Ufo1-1*-inudced pigment. Related *Ufo1-1*/*Ufo1-1* expressing, silent, and wild-type

 plants were then collected for the experiments. All plant materials were grown at the Penn State Agronomy farm (Rock Springs, PA).

# **Flavonoid pigment analysis**

 Young husk tissue was collected at the time of silk emergence for each of the different genotypes. At least four samples from different plants were used per genotype. ~30 mg of tissues was incubated in HCl:Butanol (3:7) for 1 hour at 37°C. The absorption spectra (from 450 nm to 700 nm) of each sample were measured in duplicate on a spectrophotometer. The average of duplicates was used as the value of a sample. The extracts were boiled and measured again on the spectrophotometer. Boiling shifts the peak absorption wavelength of flavan-4-ols from ~565 nm to ~500 nm, but it has no effect on the peak absorption wavelength of flavan-3,4- diols (max peak ~530 nm) (Boddu et al., 2005). The average and standard error were calculated for each genotype, and statistical differences were assessed using Student's t-test.

To screen seedlings, the coleoptiles of seven-day-old plants were removed using forceps and

assayed using acid butanol as described above. T1 plants (HiII tg+ x *P1-wr*) all produce

anthocyanin in the coleoptile, depending on light exposure. Transgene+ (tg+) plants that

 produce phlobaphene usually contain a mixture of both anthocyanin and phlobaphene, which produce a spectrum with a very broad peak or a peak around ~530 nm and a shoulder around ~565 nm. Spectra were carefully examined by eye to discern the presence of flavan-4-ols. Initial determinations were confirmed after boiling the extracts, as the plants producing a mixture of both would have their peaks shifted to the left, confirming the presence of flavan-4-ols, while those containing predominantly anthocyanin would remain the same or shift slightly to the right (by 5-10 nm). Examples are provided in Figure 5.

# **Phenotypic characterization**

Plant height and silking dates were measured in BC10 W23 plants segregating 1:1 for *Ufo1-1/+*:

*+/+*. Height was measured from the ground to the beginning of the tassel at full maturity. 46

*Ufo1-1* plants and 53 wild-type plants were measured for height and silking date. Statistical

differences were measured using Student's t-test.

#### **SNP50 and development of CAPs markers**

High density SNP data were generated from several *Ufo1-1* samples in different backgrounds

including the original *Ufo1-1* stock, BC8(W23), BC11(W23), BC5(B73), *Ufo1-1* x *p1-*

*ww*(*4Co63*), and F6 (*Ufo1-1* x B73). Data were generated using the MaizeSNP50 BeadChip

(Illumina, WG-500-1001). SNPs on chromosome 10 were compared with those available for a

282 line diversity panel (Cook et al., 2012). Regions of identity were calculated by counting the

number of shared SNP genotypes between *Ufo1-1* and the diversity lines in 5 MB windows.

Regions that shared >95% SNP genotypes were considered to be identical by descent. Some

room for error was allowed, as some SNP calls can be wrong, and errors in the reference genome

(RefGenV3) can lead to inaccurate placement of some SNPs.

CAPs markers were designed using SNPS2CAPS software (http://pgrc.ipk-

gatersleben.de/snp2caps/). Briefly, the software takes as input a fasta file of the SNPs with

flanking sequences and a database of restriction enzymes. It then reports SNPs whose different

alleles are differentially cut by the given restriction enzymes. A list of suitable sites and

enzymes was chosen and primers designed using Primer3 (http://bioinfo.ut.ee/primer3-0.4.0/).

Finally, markers were tested using PCR and enzyme digestion to ensure they are truly

polymorphic between *Ufo1-1* and control lines.

# **Mapping**

 A population of 10,000 backcross plants was grown in the field in 2013. Plants were phenotyped at around the V6 stage, and plants appearing to be *Ufo1-1* were collected for genotyping. Initially, 94 plants were screened with the markers umc1367 and umc2016, giving three and one recombinants, respectively. An additional 96 plants were screened with umc2016, giving an additional three recombinants. Finally, all 4032 *Ufo1-1* plants were screened with markers (CAP5 and CAP45) inside the established boarders. Zero recombinants were found at either marker. Final phenotyping of pericarp color was performed at the time of harvest to confirm the phenotype. Plants with a wild type appearance were discarded from further analysis.

 Additional linkage studies using F2 populations were also carried out. Samples were genotyped with several markers throughout the mapping region (Supplemental Table 5). Markers for the CACTA transposon were developed based on sequence polymorphisms found in the PacBio sequence of *Ufo1-1*.

# **Construct design and transformation**

 To validate GRMZM2G053177 as *ufo1,* transgenic maize plants were developed with constitutive overexpression, as is found in *Ufo1-1* plants. The construct was designed using the pMCG1005 vector (McGinnis et al., 2005) obtained from Kan Wang at the Iowa State Plant Transformation Facility. The full construct contains the coding sequence and 3' UTR from the *Ufo1-1* allele of the gene, which is missing the first exon (four codons). The coding sequence was fused to the maize *ubiquitin* promotor and the rice *adh1* first intron to drive strong constitutive expression of the gene. Coding sequences were synthesized by IDT, Inc. (Skokie, IL). The construct also contains the *BAR* gene driven by the 35S CaMV promotor to provide

 herbicide resistance to bialaphos using Liberty herbicide (Bayer, USA). The plasmid construct was sequenced from both strands prior to transformation to ensure accuracy.

*Agrobacterium* transformation of maize Hi-II (*p1-ww*) embryos was performed by the Iowa State University Plant Transformation Facility as described (Frame et al., 2002, 2006). Transformed plantlets were then transferred from tissue culture into the greenhouse, where they received 14 hour daylight using a 3:1 ratio of 1000 watt metal halide and 1000 watt high pressure sodium lamps, providing photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) of approximately 400 µmole/m<sup>2</sup> /sec at a temperature maintained between 70 and 78° F. T0 plants (*p1-ww*) were crossed to a *P1-wr* inbred line (B73, W23, or W22). T1 plants were grown in the greenhouse and screened for the presence of phlobaphene pigments (see above) to validate the *Ufo1-1*-like phenotypes.

#### **Identification of orthologs and phylogeny**

 Nucleotide and protein sequences from GRMZM2G053177 were used for BLAST searches of plant genomes available in Phytozome (Goodstein et al., 2012), Gramene (Tello-Ruiz et al., 2018), and NCBI in order to identify genes with similar sequences. Genes identified in sorghum, *Setaria*, and rice were then also used in BLAST searches to try to identify more divergent genes, but additional genes could not be identified. Chromosomal-level synteny was checked, and it was confirmed that the putative orthologs were found among the syntenic chromosomes of the various species. Local synteny was constructed manually as follows. Protein sequences of all of the gene models upstream and downstream ~200 kb of the putative GRMZM2G053177 ortholog was obtained from either Phytozome or Gramene using their biomaRt functions. Gene models from different species were then compared by BLAST against each other using BLASTP on the NCBI website. Genes with at least 70% sequence identity were considered to be orthologous for our purposes.

# **Phylogeny**

 Nucleotide sequences for GRMZM2G053177 and its orthologs were aligned using Clustal Omega under default settings (Sievers et al., 2011). Model selection was then performed using  the model selection function of MEGA7 (Kumar et al., 2015). The evolutionary history was inferred using the Maximum Likelihood method based on the Hasegawa-Kishino-Yano model (Hasegawa et al., 1985). The tree with the highest log likelihood (-1037.5538) is shown (Figure 6B). Bootstrapping was performed with 1000 replicates, and the percentage of trees in which the associated taxa clustered together is shown next to the branches (Felsenstein, 1985). Initial tree(s) for the heuristic search were obtained automatically by applying Neighbor-Joining and BioNJ algorithms to a matrix of pairwise distances estimated using the Maximum Composite Likelihood (MCL) approach and then selecting the topology with superior log likelihood value. A discrete Gamma distribution was used to model evolutionary rate differences among sites (5 categories (+G, parameter = 4.2223)). The tree is drawn to scale, with branch lengths measured in the number of substitutions per site. The analysis involved 15 nucleotide sequences. Codon positions included were 1st+2nd+3rd+Noncoding. All positions containing gaps and missing data were eliminated. There were a total of 192 positions in the final dataset. Evolutionary analyses were conducted in MEGA7 (Kumar et al., 2015).

# **High-throughput DNA extraction and genotyping**

 Seedlings were sampled and genotyped using linked PCR-based markers. For DNA extraction, 2 leaf punches per sample were ground in 96-well plates containing 400 µl urea buffer (0.9 M urea, 0.5 M NaCl, 0.0625 M Tris pH=8). Following grinding, 100 µl sarcosine buffer was added (0.17 M n-lauroyl sarcosine), followed by incubation at 65° C for 15 minutes. The plates were then spun at a minimum of 3000 rpm (~1,500 g) for 10 minutes on a Beckman GS-6KR centrifuge (Indianapolis, IN). 200 µl supernatant was transferred to a new plate. 200 µl of a 4.4 M ammonium acetate and isopropanol (1:10 ratio) mix was added. The plates were shaken before centrifugation at 3000 rpm (~1,500 g) for 10 minutes. The supernatant was discarded and the pellet was washed with 70% ethanol, followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes. The ethanol was discarded and the plates were incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes to allow excess ethanol to evaporate before dissolving the pellet in 100 µl H2O. 2 µl of DNA was used per PCR. The primers used for genotyping are listed in the Supplemental Table 5.

#### **RNA-seq design**

- Stocks used for RNA-seq were as follows: F6 (B73 x *Ufo1-1*) leaf tissue was collected from the
- uppermost leaves of plants at the V10 stage. Two replicates of both *Ufo1-1* and B73 plants were
- used. One set of pericarp samples was collected from the same material as the leaves (two reps of
- each genotype). For additional pericarp samples, F5 (B73 x *Ufo1-1*) plants were genotyped, and
- wild-type and *Ufo1-1/Ufo1-1* plants were selected and carried to the next generation. Pericarps
- of F6 plants carried on from the previous selection were collected from wild-type or *Ufo1-*
- *1/Ufo1-1* plants. Additionally, for the *Ufo1-1/Ufo1-1* plants, two sets of material were collected,
- those with a *Ufo1-1* phenotype and those without (termed *Ufo1-silent* or *U-S*). Three replicates
- of each type were collected for the second set of pericarps, and this material was also used for
- small RNA sequencing. For seedlings, BC11 (W23) plants were collected at the V2 stage. Total
- aboveground tissue was collected for five replicates each of *Ufo1-1* and wild type.

# **RNA extraction/library prep/sequencing**

Total RNA was isolated from the tissues described above, followed by DNase I treatment

- (Sigma-Aldrich, AMPD1-1KT) and purification with RNA Clean & ConcentratorTM -5 (Zymo
- Research, R1015). sRNA and RNA-seq libraries were constructed using a TruSeq Small RNA

Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, RS-200-0012) and a TruSeq RNA library Preparation kit v2

- (Illumina, RS-122-2001), respectively, and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 instrument at
- the Delaware Biotechnology Institute at the University of Delaware. Raw sequencing data were
- first trimmed of adapter sequences.

# **RNA-seq analysis**

RNA-seq analysis was performed following cleaning of the data. HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2015)

- was used to align the raw reads to the maize reference genome (refgenv3) (Schnable et al.,
- 2009). The htseq python package was used to produce raw counts for each gene model (Anders
- et al., 2015). Raw counts were analyzed with the edgeR (Robinson et al., 2010; McCarthy et al.,
- 2012) package using the SARtools pipeline (Varet et al., 2016). GO enrichment analyses (SEA

and PAGE) were performed using the agriGO tool (Tian et al., 2017). K-means clustering and

heatmaps were created using multi-experiment viewer software (Howe et al., 2011). Gene

names and descriptions were downloaded from maizegdb.org, gramene.org, and

phytozome.jgi.doe.gov. Transcription factor families were downloaded from the plant

transcription factor database (Guo et al., 2008). Sugar pathways were visualized using MapMan

software (Usadel et al., 2009).

Small RNA abundance within the CACTA insertion was measured from pericarp samples of

*Ufo1-1* and *U-S.* Averages of three replicates were measured from alignments of the sRNAs

against the CACTA sequence using Bowtie under default settings with no mismatches allowed

(Langmead et al., 2009).

#### **K-mer analysis**

 K-mer analysis was performed essentially as described (Akagi *et al.* 2014). First, cleaned unaligned RNA-seq reads are cut into smaller 25 bp "k-mers" using Jellyfish (Marçais and Kingsford, 2011). K-mers are made with a 1 bp sliding window, meaning that every 25 bp sequence within the read is extracted and counted. Counts for each k-mer sequence were then tallied for every RNA-seq library. The presence of *Ufo1-*specific k-mer sequences was then assessed by requiring a count of at least three in each *Ufo1-1* library and a count of 1 or less in each wild-type and *U-S* library. The opposite was done to find k-mers absent in *Ufo1-1*, but no k-mers meeting the criteria were found. *Ufo1-1-*specific k-mers were then mapped back to their reads and full-length transcripts using BLAST. This method does not require a reference genome or gene models, meaning it can find novel transcripts not found in the reference. Additionally, this method should find any sequence polymorphisms present in *Ufo1-1* that are absent from the wild type and *U-S* samples. Finally, it should identify the presence/absence of transcripts between the comparisons.

# **PacBio sequencing**

 Whole genome sequencing was performed on BC5F3 (B73) *Ufo1-1* plants using single molecule sequencing technology (SMRT) from Pacific Biosciences. Additionally, optical mapping was performed on the same material using the Irys system from BioNano. Sample prep, sequencing, and *de novo* assembly of hybrid scaffolds were performed as described by Jiao *et al.* 2017. Sequences of the candidate GRMZM2G053177 were then extracted by BLAST. Sequence polymorphisms on chromosome 10 were found using the NUCmer alignment tool from the MUMmer3 software package (Kurtz et al., 2004) on the B73 refgenV4 (Jiao et al., 2017). The parameters used were: nucmer –mum –c 2000 –g 100000. The output was first converted to a human readable format using the show-coords tool and then converted to a bed file using a custom script. Polymorphisms were then visualized using the IGV genome browser software (Thorvaldsdóttir et al., 2013).

#### **Bisulfite sequencing**

 Genomic DNA was extracted from pericarps of two *Ufo1-1* and two *U-S* plants using the CTAB method (Porebski et al., 1997). Bisulfite treatment was performed using a EZ DNA Methylation- Gold Kit (Zymo Research, D5005) following the standard protocol. Bisulfite-treated DNA was then amplified using nested primers with one end of the PCR product inside the CACTA and one on the flanking DNA. The PCR product was TA-cloned using the pGEM-T Easy Vector (Promega, A1360). A minimum of 22 clones per sample were sent for Sanger sequencing. Sequences were manually cleaned and sorted by strand before being aligned using Clustal Omega (Sievers et al., 2011). The CyMate program was then used to analyze the DNA methylation at each site and by context (Hetzl et al., 2007). Methylation was calculated as the number of methylated bases divided by the total number of bases at each cytosine. The methylation was calculated for each context as the average of two biological replicates.

# **RNA preparation and synthesis of first-strand cDNA**

RNA samples were extracted from mature leaves, 10 days after pollination (DAP) developing

seed, 18 DAP pericarp, 18 DAP endosperm, and 18 DAP embryo tissues. Sets of tissues were

collected from three different plants of each genotype (*Ufo1-1-Expressor, Ufo1-1-silent*, or wild-

type). The tissues were manually ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and

pestle. Total RNA was then isolated using RNAzol following the manufacturer's protocol

- (Molecular Research Center, Inc. Cincinnati, OH). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using
- High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA).

## **qRT-PCR**

 qRT-PCR was performed using 10 ng of cDNA and the FastStart Universal SYBR Green RT- PCR Master Mix kit as the detector (Roche Applied Science, Indianapolis, IN). The qRT-PCR reaction mix contained 2 µl of DNA, 3 µl of 333 nM forward and reverse primer mix, and 5 µl of SYBR Green in a total volume of 10 µl. The default program for the qRT-PCR was as follows; pre-incubation at 95°C for 10 minutes followed by 40 cycles of 95°C (15 sec) and 60°C (1 min). After each cycle, a dissociation curve was run consisting of 95°C (15 sec), 60°C (1 min), and 95°C (15 sec).

#### **DAB staining for ROS**

 H2O2 staining was performed using DAB. Briefly, leaves were incubated in 1 mg/ml DAB in ddH2O (pH=3.8) overnight. The leaves were then washed with water twice before bleaching the leaves to remove chlorophyll. For bleaching, leaves were boiled in a solution of ethanol:acetic acid:glycerol at 3:1:1. Bleaching was sometimes repeated to fully remove chlorophyll. Finally, the leaves were washed and dried before taking photographs.

# **Quantification of sugar and starch contents**

 For soluble sugar (Suc, Glc, and Fru) and starch quantification, leaves were collected from field- grown plants at the V7 stage in the afternoon, before the end of the photoperiod. For the *Ufo1-1* lesions, leaf tissue was collected at VT (the time when lesions usually form), and lesion and green tissue from the same leaf was separated for analysis; green leaf tissue from B73 control plants was collected at the same time. Four independent plants were sampled for each genotype, and each sample was measured twice, with the average of the two measurements being used as the value for that sample. Sugar and starch were extracted from the tissue as described by Leach

- and Braun (2016). Quantifications were done using commercial assay kits for Saccharose
- (Sucrose)/D-Glucose/D-Fructose and Starch (R-Biopharm 10716260035 and 10207748035)
- according to the manufacturer's instructions as described (Leach and Braun, 2016).
