BACKGROUND: New modes of action are needed for herbicides. The flavonoid synthesis intermediate *t*-chalcone causes apoptosis-like symptoms in roots and bleaching of shoots of Arabidospsis, suggesting a unique mode of action as a phytotoxin.

RESULTS: Using RNA-Seq, transcriptome changes were monitored in Arabidopsis seedlings during the first 24 h of exposure (at 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 h) to 21 μM *t*-chalcone (I50 dose), examining effects on roots and shoots separately. Expression of 892 and 1000 genes was affected in roots and shoots, respectively. According to biological classification, many of the affected genes were transcription factors and genes associated with oxidative stress, heat shock proteins, xenobiotic detoxification, ABA and auxin biosynthesis, and primary metabolic processess. These are secondary effects found with most phytotoxins. Potent phytotoxins usually act by inhibiting enzymes of primary metabolism. KEGG pathway analysis of transcriptome results from the first 3 h of *t*-chalcone exposure indicated several potential primary metabolism target sites for *t*-chalcone. Of these, *p*-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase (HPPD) and tyrosine amino transferase were consistent with the bleaching effect of the phytotoxin. Supplementation studies with *Lemna paucicostata* and Arabidiopsis supported HPPD as the target, although *in vitro* enzyme inhibition was not found.

CONCLUSIONS: *t*-Chalcone is possibly a protoxin that is converted to a HPPD inhibitor *in vivo*.

# **1 INTRODUCTION**

The widespread evolution of resistance to currently used herbicides has become a global problem that is causing substantial economic losses. 1, <sup>2</sup> Resistance management often involves use of diverse modes of action, so that selection pressure with one mode of action over extended perions does not occur. Unfortunately, resistance has evolved to most available modes of action, and a new mode of action has not been introduced for about 30 years.<sup>3</sup> Discovery of new, natural product-based herbicides with new modes of action is one approach to ameliorating this problem. 4-6 Plant secondary metabolites are a source of natural phytotoxins that can provide new herbicides with novel modes of action. In fact, the last new herbicide mechanism of action introduced, inhibition of hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase (HPPD, EC 1.13.11.27), was a result of the study of the triketone phytochemical leptospermone.<sup>3</sup>

Chalcones are precursors of flavonoids in plants, 7 and some of them accumulate as yellow flower pigments in some species. 8 These secondary metabolites are noted for their wide range of biological activities, including bactericides, insecticides, fungicides and pharmaceuticals. 9, <sup>10</sup> In particular, *trans*-chalcone (1,3-diphenyl-2-propen-1-one) consists of two phenolic rings connected by a three carbon bridge with an α,β-double bond, forming a C6-C3-C<sup>6</sup> structure. *t*-Chalcone is nematicidal and nematostatic, <sup>11</sup> is slightly fungicidal, 12 inhibits the enzymes phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL, EC 4.3.1.24) and 4-coumarate: CoA ligase (4CL, EC 6.2.1.12), <sup>13</sup> and is phytotoxic to a variety of plants. 14, <sup>15</sup> This secondary metabolite induces programmed cell death (PCD) in *Arabidopsis thaliana* (Arabidopsis) roots and causes increased membrane permeability and complete bleaching of the shoot. 16, 17 Knowledge of its mode of action and molecular site of action might causee interest in its use in weed management.

Transcriptome analysis technologies such as RNA-Seq are useful tools that can assist in the elucidation of the mode of action and molecular target(s) of a phytotoxin.<sup>18</sup> Such technologies provide considerable information on transcription of thousands of genes. However, at phytotoxic doses, many genes that are associated with general stress responses are affected, making it difficult to identify the molecular target(s). <sup>18</sup> Hence, determination of the specific site of action of a phytotoxin is usually an arduous task, requiring validation with other experimental procedures such as enzymatic studies or gene mutations or knockouts.

Here we report the transcriptome changes in Arabidopsis seedlings during early exposure to *t*-chalcone. This study is focused on the analysis of transcriptome profiling

during the first 24 h of exposure, examining effects on Arabidopsis roots and shoots separately. We hypothesized that use of RNA-Seq methods to generate the transcriptome response over a time course with metabolic pathway analysis of the results would provide indications of the molecular target site of *t*-chalcone. Genes of several essential primary biosynthetic pathways were affected, leading us to examine three potential molecular target sites, acetolactate synthase (ALS, EC 2.1.1.6), tyrosine aminotransferase (TAT, EC 2.6.1.5), and HPPD as potential targets with physiological and biochemical methods.

# **2 MATERIALS AND METHODS**

### **2.1 Plant material and culture conditions**

*Arabidopsis thaliana* (L.) Heynh. ecotype Columbia (Col-0) seeds were sterilized with ethanol (70%) and NaOCl (3%) and then imbibed in 0.1% agar (Ultra Pure Agarose, Invitrogen) at 4 ºC for 72 h to stratify the seeds. Sterile square Petri dishes (100x15 mm) were prepared with 0.44% macro-and micronutrients (w/v; Murashige-Skoog, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) + 1% sucrose (pH adjusted to 6.0 with KOH) in 0.8% agar (35- 40 mL/plate; Phytagar, Life Technologies) one day before sowing. Twenty-four seeds were sown per plate, which were sealed with tape (Leukopor) and placed in a growth chamber at 22 ºC and 16/8 h light/ dark photoperiod under light intensity of 120 µmol/m<sup>2</sup> sec.

### **2.2 Dose-response curve**

A dose-response curve was conducted to calculate IC<sup>50</sup> *t*-chalcone (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) concentration for 16/8 h light/ dark photoperiod. Agar solutions of *t*chalcone were prepared at concentrations of 0, 12.5, 25, 50 and 100 µM in 0.1% ethanol (five replicates per concentration). Square dishes were placed vertically in a growth chamber for 14 days. After that time, root length was measured and IC<sup>50</sup> was calculated to be used in transcriptome experiments.

### **2.3 Transcriptome study**

Plants were grown on agar plates for 14 days in a growth chamber at 22 ºC and 16/8 h light/ dark photoperiod under light intensity of 120 µmol/m<sup>2</sup> sec. They were treated at 7 h after the beginning of the photoperiod by adding 8 mL per plate of the IC<sup>50</sup> concentration (21 µM) of *t-*chalcone in 0.1% ethanol or 8 ml of 0.1% ethanol. After treatment, plants were incubated

for 0, 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 h. At each time point treated and control were removed, washed, separated into roots and shoots, weighed, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 ºC for RNA extraction. Three replicate samples were collected from control plants (0.1% EtOH) and treated plants for each time point.

### **2.4 RNA isolation for sequencing**

Total RNAs were isolated from flash frozen Arabidopsis tissues using an RNeasy plant mini kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNAs were then treated with RNase-free DNase I kit to remove residual DNA contamination and repurified with RNeasy MinElute Cleanup Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's procedures. RNA recovery and purity were determined spectrophotometrically using a NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific), and sample integrity was also assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. The quality and quantity of prepared total RNAs were accessed according to the MIQE Guidelines. 19, 20

### **2.5 Library construction and sequencing**

Total RNA quality was determined using an RNA Nano Chip on a Bioanalyzer (Agilent). Library construction, quantification and qualification were performed by Global Biologics (Columbia, MO) using the TruSeq RNA Library Preparation Kits (Illumina, San Diego, CA). The libraries were sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq™2500 (Illumina, http://www.illumina.com/) platform with single-end 75 bp reads.

### **2.6 Data processing**

Raw RNA-Seq reads of the cDNA libraries were trimmed and filtered to remove adaptor sequences and low-quality reads containing poly-N using the CLC Genomics Workbench 7.0 (CLC bio, Denmark). Briefly, the raw reads for all samples underwent trimming in the following sequential order: quality trimming, ambiguity trimming, adapter trimming, and length trimming. First, only reads with PHRED (Phil's Read Editor) quality scores of Q30 (or base call accuracy of 99.9%) or higher were retained. The sequence ends were then trimmed based on the presence of ambiguous nucleotides (typically N) so that end regions contained no more than two ambiguous nucleotides and any contaminating Illumina adapter sequences and associated 5' sequences were removed. All nucleotides 3' of the adapter match were preserved as a trimmed read. Finally, terminal bases at the 3' end of the reads were removed and reads below 50 base pairs in length were discarded.

Trimmed reads of each sample in the experiment were mapped to the *Arabidopsis thaliana* reference genome (Col-0 TAIR 10) and counted using the RNA-Seq mapping algorithm in the CLC Genomics Workbench and computational tools allowing for gene expression prediction that included fragments that might map to multiple genes were employed. Expression values (fragments per kilobase per million) for all known genes were calculated for each sample. Both the raw data and the normalized expression data have been deposited at NCBI (RNA-seq accession number GSE127759).

Gene counts were tested for differential expression using the R package *DESeq2*  (version 1.14.1) (Love *et al*., 2014). <sup>21</sup> Samples taken at 12 h from shoot tissue were not used due to one replicate being a clear outlier for unknown reasons. Tissue types, root and shoot, were processed separately. Two separate differential expression analyses were performed. In the first, *time + treatment + time:treatment* (an interaction term) was tested against the reduced model of *time + treatment* (without interaction). Adjusted p-values reflect the probability of a time-by-treatment interaction after correcting for multiple tests. Since 0 h represents a pre-treatment state and all time-points have a control, any gene that is responding differentially to the treament should be detected in this approach. To look at time-point specific treatment responses, *time* and *treatment* were combined into a single "group" factor and fold-changes were evaluated between treatments separately for each time-point. All cluster and PCA plots were based on the *rlog* adjusted counts as implemented in *DESeq2*. 21

### **2.7 Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis**

For KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, the R package *keggseq* (version 0.99.0) was used (https://github.com/w-korani/keggseq). Given the exploratory nature of this analytical component and the sparceness of genes with KEGG annotations, this analysis was based on genes differentially expressed between treatments at a single time point with a liberal threshold of p-value <0.1 after correcting for multiple tests. *q*-Values for pathway enrichment were assessed using *keggseq* as well. Plots were generated using the R package *pathview* . 22

### **2.8 Supplementation Studies**

Treatment of *Lemna paucicostata* with *t*-chalcone and metabolites*: Lemna paucicostata* L. plants were grown as previously described.<sup>23</sup> A dose of *t*-chalcone that strongly inhibited growth was provided with or without homogentisic acid, hydroxyphenylpyuvate, or a mixture of branched chain amino acids (valine, leucine, and isoleucine) (all from Sigma). Frond

growth was determined with a Lemnatec Scanalyzer PL (Aachen, Germany) image analysis instrument that measures frond area periodically, as described by Michel *et al.*<sup>24</sup> . Results are shown as % of control of duckweed frond growth.

*Treatment of the Arabidopsis seedlings with* t*-chalcone and metabolites:* An autoclaved half strength MS (Sigma) medium containing 0.6% phytagel (Sigma) was cooled down to 55° C and appropriate amount of *t*-chalcone (Sigma) and/or homogentisic acid (Sigma) and/or hyrdoxyphenyl pyruvate was added with or without 30 μM *t*-chalcone. One ml of medium solution was transferred into each well. Three 24 well plates were used during the experiment. Five of sterile and stratified (for 4 days) Arabidopsis seeds (Col-0) were planted into each well. Plates were kept in a growth chamber for 16 h at 24° C (light) and 8 h at 22° C (dark). After 14 days of incubation, the Arabidopsis seedling leaf areas were measured as discussed above for *L. paucicostata*.

### **2.9 4-Hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase (HPPD) assays**

Recombinant HPPD from *Arabidopsis thaliana* was overexpressed in *E. coli* and tested in total soluble protein extracts. Enzyme activity was measured as described previously. 25 The enzyme assay was done with no inhibitor, with sulcotrione, and with *t-*chalcone. The HPLC system used to quantify enzyme activity was composed of a Waters Corporation system (Milford, MA) including a Model 600E pump, Model 717 autosampler, Millenium 2010 controller and Model 996 photodiode detector equipped with a 7.8 mm 100 mm X-Terra C18 (5 µm) reversed phase column. Solvent A was 0.1% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid in ddH2O and solvent B was 0.08% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid in 80% (v/v) HPLC-grade acetonitrile. The solvent system consisted of a linear gradient at 0% (100% A) to 70% B from 0 to 2 min, 70% to 100% B from 2 to 4 min, 100% B from 4 to 6 min, 100% to 0% B from 6 to 7 min and 0% B from 7 to 8 min. The flow rate was 3 mL min-1 and the injection volume was 100 µL. HGA was detected by UV absorbance at 288 nm. <sup>26</sup> A calibration curve was established by injecting various concentrations of HGA.

# **3 RESULTS**

## **3.1 Dose-response curve**

Both root length and shoot growth of *Arabidopsis* seedlings were inhibited by *t*-chalcone treatment (Fig. 1). There was a large decrease of root growth between the two lowest *t*chalcone concentrations (12.5 and 25 µM), with an IC<sup>50</sup> value of 21 µM (R<sup>2</sup> = 0.941). Due to

slightly different growth conditions used in these experiments, the IC<sup>50</sup> value obtained here is slightly different from the IC<sup>50</sup> value (35 µM) published by Díaz-Tielas *et al.*<sup>16</sup> . The doseresponse curves are also very similar. This IC<sup>50</sup> value is low for a natural product. For example the IC<sup>50</sup> value for the allelochemical benzoxazolin-2(3*H*)-one in a similar bioassay is 540 µM. 27

### **3.2 Transcriptomic profile**

The abundance of 1154 and 1189 transcripts in shoots and roots, respectively, was found altered at one time or another during the 24 h *t*-chalcone treatment (P ≤ 0.01) (Table 1; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Some genes were altered more than once at different times during the *t*-chalcone treatment. Without these repetitions of genes, the total number of transcripts altered during *t*-chalcone treatment was 892 in roots and 1000 in shoots (Fig. 2A). Using the gene ontology (GO) term enrichment tool from the TAIR database, these genes were assigned to biological process, cellular component or molecular function as shown in Fig. 2. The GO analysis (Fig. 2B-C) was similar for roots and shoots, but the precise *t*chalcone-affected transcripts were somewhat different in roots and shoots. Among all the transcripts differentially expressed, 151 were found altered in both roots and shoots. *t-*Chalcone treatment induced a more rapid response in roots, with many up- and downregulated genes after 1 and 3 h, while shoots showed a slower response with fewer genes affected at these time points (Fig. 3). The maximum response in roots was at 3 h, with 271 up-regulated genes and 213 down-regulated genes, and at 6 h in shoots, with 291 upregulated and 338 down-regulated genes. The number of differentially expressed genes in the roots decreased from 6 to 24 h after exposure, while the effect in the shoot was still pronounced at 24 h. As mentioned in the materials and methods, the 12 h shoot data could not be statistically analyzed because of an obvious outlier. To summarize, gene transcription effects were more pronounced in roots than in shoots for the first 3 h of phytotoxin exposure, after which, the effects on the shoot were more pronounced.

We classified most of the affected non-primary metabolism genes into five major groups according to biological function: 1) transcription factors, 2) oxidative stress and programmed cell death, 3) heat shock proteins, 4) xenobiotic detoxification, and 5) ABA and auxin biosynthesis, response and transport. A general trend of the expression profiling of these groups of genes was detected in roots, where most of the genes were up-regulated during the first 3 h of treatment, followed by a 'silent period', and down-regulation by 24 h of treatment. In contrast, the general trend in Arabidopsis shoots was a broad down-regulation during the first 3 h, followed by up-regulation at 6 h, and fewer alterations after 24 h of treatment.

### **3.3 Non-primary metabolism genes**

### *3.3.1 Transcription factors*

The oxidative stress response transcription factors (TFs) WRKY, DREBA, MYB and HSF were differentially expressed mainly in the roots, being mostly over-expressed at the beginning of *t*-chalcone treatment, and suppressed at 24 h (Table S3). In the contrast, DREBA-TFs were down-regulated in the shoot after the first hour of exposure, and only MYB domain TFs were up-regulated at 6 h. There was downregulation of MYB3, DREB2C and MYBL2 at 6 h. By 24 h, only MYB29 was downregulated.

### *3.3.2 Oxidative stress and programmed cell death-related genes*

This group of genes (Table S4) was strongly affected by *t*-chalcone treatment. The general trend was the same as in the other groups, with the strongest effects (a greater number of genes both up- and down-regulated) in the roots at 1 and 3 h and in the shoots at 6 and 24 h. Peroxidase genes were all down regulated in the roots at 1 and 3 h. Apoptosis-related genes in the root were both up and down-regulated, depending on the gene at 1 and 3 h, but by 6 h affected genes were all upregulated. Relatively few genes in these families were affected in the shoot. For example, only one gene associated with cell death was affected, and it was affected at only one time point. No peroxidase genes were affected, but unlike the root, thioredoxin and ferredoxin genes were affected (upregulated at 6 h and downregulated at 24 h).

### *3.3.3 Heat shock proteins (HSPs)*

Numerous HSPs and heat shock-related genes were highly over-expressed in Arabidopsis seedlings during *t*-chalcone exposure (Table S5). Major up-regulation in HSP transcription factors, HSPs and HSP20-like chaperones superfamily proteins occurred in roots at 1 and 3 h. None of these genes were affected in the root after 3 h. In the shoot, three different HSP genes were up-regulated at 1 and 3 h, with down-regulation of two of them at 24 h. Three others were only up-regulated at 6 h, and four chloroplast-targeted HSP-related genes were up-regulated at 6 h, with two of these down-regulated at 24 h (Table S5).

### *3.3.4 Xenobiotic detoxification related genes*

Table S6 shows that numerous glutathione-*S*-transferase (GST) genes were highly upregulated in roots after 1 and 3 h of *t-*chalcone treatment. At these times, the majority of UDP-glucosyltransferases (UGT), ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, and multidrug and toxin efflux family (MATE) genes were also up-regulated in roots at 1 and/or 3 h of treatment. Root hair-specific genes were down-regulated at 1 h or both 1 and 3 h. By 24 h, only a few of these genes were affected in the root, and in each case they were downregulated. In the shoot, UGTs and glutathione-conjugate and xenobiotic transporter genes were up-regulated at 1, 3, or 6 h of treatment.

### *3.3.5 Auxin and ABA transport and response related genes*

Among all the phytohormone-related genes differentially transcribed during chalcone treatment, there was a clear predominance of genes associated with auxin and abscisic acid (ABA) synthesis, hormone transport, and cellular responses to hormones. Table S7 shows the transcriptome profile response of genes related to both hormones. Six genes associated with auxin synthesis, responses and signaling were affected in roots. The uridine diphosphate glycosyltransferase 74E2 (UGT74E2) gene was up-regulated in roots at 1 and 3 h of treatment. The transcript levels of this enzyme are induced by H2O<sup>2</sup> and may allow integration of ROS (reactive oxygen species) and auxin signaling.<sup>28</sup> Nitrilase 2 (NIT2) was over-expressed in roots after 3 h. It catalyzes the hydrolysis of indole-3-acetonitrile (IAN) to indole-3-acetic acid (IAA). Four genes related to ABA transport, synthesis, response and signaling were up-regulated at 1, 3 or both 1 and 3 h of treatment.

Only six genes related to auxin or ABA were affected in the shoot, with most of them were affected only at 6 h. Three of the affected genes were only affected in the shoot: two methyl IAA esterase genes and an ABA response/signaling gene that was down-regulated only at 1 h.

### *3.3.6 Genes related to t-chalcone metabolism*

One might expect that *t-*chalcone would affect expression of genes related to chalcone biosynthesis or metabolic conversion of chalcone. At the p = 0.01 level of confidence only one chalcone metabolism gene (chalcone isomerase, AT5G05270) was affected in shoots at the only the 24 h time point. However, in the root, genes for enzymes downstream from this enzyme were affected at 1 and 3 h after treatment. The gene for flavonol 7-Oglucosyltransferase was up-regulated after 1 and 3 h by 1.9- and 1.5-fold, respectively, after

chalcone treatment. As well, the gene for a protein similar to isoflavone reductase was upregulated 1.1-fold.

### **3.4 Kegg pathway and system analysis**

Only timepoints 1 and 3 h were used for pathway analysis, as we were most interested in initial effects and not in secondary or tertiary effects that might occur later. Table 2 provides a list of pathways and processes that were significantly affected in roots and shoots. Some of the pathways that were greatly affected are those associated with stress (*e.g*., those associated with secondary metabolism, glutathione metabolism, and hormone signal transduction). However, a strong phytotoxin is much more likely to impact an enzyme of primary metabolism.<sup>3</sup> The pathways with the most prominent effects in roots at 1 h were: pentose and glucuronate interconversions (Fig. S1); glycine, serine and threonine metabolism (Fig. S2); cysteine and methionine metabolism (Fig. S3); tyrosine metabolism (Fig. 4a); phenylalanine metabolism (Fig. S4); β-alanine metabolism (Fig. S5); glutathione metabolism (Fig. S6a); sulfur metabolism (Fig. S7a); and phenylpropanoid metabolism (Fig. S8a). The valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 5a) also had genes of interest that were significantly affected. At 3 h, the following pathways of the roots were enriched in significant effects: alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism (Fig. S9); valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation (Fig. 6); glutathione metabolism (Fig. S6b); sulfur metabolism (Fig. S7b); phenylpropanoid metabolism (Fig. S8b), and 2-oxycarboxylic acid metabolism (Table 2). Proteasome function, ER protein processing, and peroxisome function genes were also significantly affected. At 1 h, there were fewer pathways affected in the shoots than in the roots: tyrosine metabolism (Fig.4b) and glutathione metabolism (Fig. S10a). Again the valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 5b) was affected similarly to the effect in the root as this time point. Hormone signal transduction and ER processing genes were also affected. At 3 h in the shoot, gluatahione metabolism was still affected (Fig. S10b), and α-linolenic acid metabolism (Fig. S11) and carbon fixation (Fig. S12) were affected too. Also, expression of genes associated with the light reactions and energy transduction of photosynthesis were affected (Fig. 7a and 7b).

Effects on the tryrosine metabolism and branched chain amino acid pathways (Figs. 4 and Figs. 5 and 6, respectively) were of particular interest because genes for well-established herbicide target enzymes were strongly affected. In the case of tyrosine metabolism, this was the gene for HPPD, and for branched chain amino acid synthesis the gene of interest was for

ALS. We were particularly interested in these genes because of the rapid effects in the shoots and the commonality between the effects in roots and shoots.

### 3.5 Effects of *t*-chalcone on HPPD and TAT

As mentioned earlier, *t*-chalcone causes complete bleaching of Arabidopsis shoots.<sup>16, 17</sup> HPPD inhibition stops plastoquinone (PQ) biosynthesis, and thereby stops carotenoid biosynthesis because PQ is a phytoene desaturase cofactor.<sup>2</sup> The gene for HPPD was upregulated in both roots and shoots at 1 and 3 h treatment (Figs. 4 and 8). Inhibition of HPPD results in loss of chloroplast function, which would be expected with the effects seen in the shoots on genes associated with chloroplast light reactions and energy transduction at 3 h chalcone treatment (Fig. 7). However, HPPD gene expression was only up-regulated at 1 and 3 h in the roots (Fig. 8b) and shoots (Fig. 8a). Note that expression level is much higher in the shoot than the root, as would be expected in tissue with chloroplasts. Using an *in vitro* assay for HPPD enzymatic activity, we found no effects of *t*-chalcone on Arabidopsis HPPD activity at 100  $\mu$ M. Sulcotrione, a herbicidal HPPD inhibitor gave 100% inhibition at 10  $\mu$ M.

Cases in which the applied compound is converted to an active pesticide *in vivo* are common.<sup>30</sup> In such cases, there is no *in vitro* effect on the enzyme. So, we used the assay of Schultz *et al.*,<sup>31</sup> which bypasses HPPD by providing the plant with exogenous homogentisic acid (HGA), the enzymatic product of HPPD to determine if there is an *in vivo* effect of *t*-chalcone on HPPD. They found that 1 mM HGA could completely reverse the growth inhibitory effect of a HPPD inhibitor on *Lemna* plants. We tried this experiment on *t*-chalcone-treated *L. paucicostata* and Arabidopsis and found reversal in both cases (Fig. 9), although HGA was somewhat phytotoxic to Arabidopsis. Either HGA or 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate (HPP) will reverse the effects of TAT inhibitors.<sup>32</sup> So, we supplied HPP to *t*-chalcone-treated *L. paucicostata* and Arabidopsis, but got little reversal (data not shown).

### 3.6 Effects on branched chain amino acid metabolism

ALS, is a key enzyme of branched chain amino acid biosynthesis and is a very good herbicide target site.<sup>33</sup> The gene for ALS was up-regulated in *t*-chalcone-treated roots at 1 and 3 h and in *t*-chalcone-treated shoots at 1h and 6 h (Figs. 5 and 10). Furthermore, in *t*-

chalcone-roots at 3 h, genes for enzymes of branched chain amino acid degradation were strongly up-regulated (Fig. 6). These results indicate that *t*-chalcone might inhibit ALS. We tested this by determining if the effect of *t*-chalcone on *L. paucicostata* could be prevented by feeding the plants with non-toxic levels of branched chain amino acids.<sup>34</sup> We found no reversal of the effect of t-chalcone in this experiment (data not shown).

# **4 DISCUSSION**

A general trend on the expression patterns of each group of genes was found during *t*chalcone treatment. Significant up- and down-regulation of genes in roots after 1 and 3 h was followed by a 'silent period' until 24 h, when down-regulation of gene expression was more common in roots. However, the response to *t-*chalcone was slower in shoots, with an increasing number of affected genes at 1, 3 and 6 h and fewer affected few genes at 24 h. Our results show a clear correlation between the expression of TFs and the expression of genes related to detoxification of xenobiotic chemicals, HSPs, redox homeostasis, and cell death after *t*-chalcone treatment. These are all effects that are commonly associated with any phytotoxin-caused stress. The more rapid and different effects seen in roots than in shoots could be the result of slower movement of a lower dose of the phytotoxin to shoots than experienced by the roots and/or indirect effects on shoots, due to primary or direct effects in roots.

Numerous genes involved in the detoxification of xenobiotic compounds, such as GSTs, UDP-glucosyltransferases and drug/xenobiotic transporters, were up-regulated during *t-*chalcone treatment. GSTs are widely known to contribute to the detoxification of toxins such as herbicides, both by inactivation through glutathione conjugation and by targeting for trans-membrane transport. *e.g.*, 27 As well, *t*-chalcone treatment resulted in the up-regulation of a phenolic glucoside malonyltransferase gene in roots. Glycosylation, glutathione conjugation, and sulfonylation are xenobiotic modifications involved in detoxification. Sometimes, multiple modifications may be introduced sequentially, such as the glycosylation followed by malonylation. In fact, malonylation is a key reaction in the metabolism of xenobiotic phenolic glucosides. <sup>35</sup> These xenobiotic modifications are also common with modifications of plant secondary metabolites that alter the properties and functions of the modified compounds. Although 20 GST genes were differentially expressed in roots, no GST transcript was found altered in Arabidopsis shoots during the 24 h *t*-chalcone treatment. As well, compared to the roots, few transcripts involved in the detoxification of xenobiotic compounds were up-regulated in the shoots.

Additionally, some GSTs have been implicated in oxidative stress responses, and found to play a role in the cellular responses to auxins.<sup>36</sup> Wagner et al.<sup>37</sup> determined that *At*GSTF6, *At*GSTF8, and *At*GSTU19 expression was strongly up-regulated after treatment with hydrogen peroxide and the auxinic herbicide 2,4-D. Also, *At*GSTF6 mRNA was also strongly induced by salicylic acid and the herbicide metolachlor. Our results have shown that *At*GSTF6 was down-regulated in roots at 24 h, while *At*GSTF8, and *At*GSTU19 were upregulated in roots at 1 and 3 h.

According to Mittler,<sup>38</sup> the H2O<sup>2</sup> produced during abiotic stress activates calmodulin and a MAP-kinase cascade, resulting in the activation of WRKY, DREBA, MYB and HSF transcription factors, among others. These TFs regulate the response of plants to oxidative stress (HSPs, ROS scavenging and NADH supply), and suppression of photosynthesis. Only one TF was up-regulated in the shoots, *MYB111*. *MYB111* participates in the control of flavonol biosynthesis in the shoot apical meristem and its expression has been suggested to be under redox control.<sup>39</sup> The numerous transcripts from genes involved in response to oxidative stress and programmed cell death found up-regulated in roots at 1 and 3 h could indicate that strong oxidative stress is rapidly induced by *t*-chalcone, which could lead to the induction of programmed cell death. Genes related to apoptosis and oxidative stress cell death were found up-regulated only in roots at 1 to 6 h, and one of them in the shoot at 3 h.

The expression profiles of the genes related to ROS-dependent cell death and apoptosis suggest that the Arabidopsi*s* roots rapidly responded to *t*-chalcone, with the strongest gene expression for 1 to 3 h after treatment. *t*-Chalcone treatment reduced the expression of one flavin mono-oxygenase gene (*FMO1*) after 1 h. The flavin monooxygenase transcripts accumulate in response to superoxide anion (O<sup>2</sup> - ) and ROS involved in regulating programmed cell death, suggesting that it can serve as a marker for plant cell death.40, 41 However, the early observed down-regulation with no effects later does not support this view. Other transcripts related to cell death protection such as alphadioxygenase (DOX1) were found up-regulated in roots after 3 h.

Other ROS-producing mechanisms are involved in ROS signaling in response to different stimuli, such as the mitochondrial alternative oxidase, chloroplastic ferredoxins, thioredoxins and aldo-keto reductase, and the dehydroascorbate reductase and glutathione peroxidase involved in the glutathione-ascorbate cycle. 42 Expression of all of these genes was found altered during *t*-chalcone treatment. The up-regulation of transcripts of chloroplastic heat shock proteins and other proteins related to stress response in chloroplasts, as the stress enhanced protein 2 (SEP2), which may be involved in non-photochemical quenching to maintain the balance between dissipation and utilization of light energy minimizing generation of oxidizing molecules and protecting the thylakoid membrane in response to stress, suggests an effect on chloroplasts already after 6 h *t*-chalcone treatment that would be in concordance with the strong effects on chloroplast structure and function previously observed by Díaz-Tielas and collaborators on Arabidopsis shoots after a longer treatment with *t*-chalcone.<sup>17</sup>

ROS can affect biosynthesis, degradation, metabolism, transport or perception of auxin.<sup>43</sup> As well, oxidative stress-related genes are differentially regulated by auxins, and these phytohormones are able to induce ROS generation in cells. 44 The simultaneous increased expression of redox signaling and auxin-related genes may indicate the occurrence of interactions between the two processes to regulate the cellular responses against *t*chalcone-induced stress, possibly contributing to the spreading of such cellular responses to the whole plant. Furthermore, no responses to ABA were found up-regulated in shoots at any time of *t*-chalcone treatment, while auxin responsive elements were over-expressed at 6 h in the shoot. This suggests that auxin, and not ABA, play a role in spreading responses to the phytotoxin from the root to the shoot.

Heat stress transcription factors may be also activated under a number of stress conditions, including osmotic stress, oxidative stress, heavy metals, genotoxic stress, and ultraviolet light, among others.45-47 Prolonged exposure to stress inevitably affects cell homeostasis, having negative effects on the biochemical stability of proteins, which can unfold, misfold, or aggregate. In such situations, the heat shock response is activated, leading to an increase of molecular chaperones and proteases that repair the damaged proteins and assist in the recovery of the cell.<sup>45</sup> <sup>48</sup> The heat shock response has been considered a bioindicator of environmental toxicology, because its induction is much more sensitive to stress than other traditionally used indices.<sup>49</sup> Many of the HSPs found up-regulated after *t*chalcone treatment are small HSPs that range in size from approximately 16–42 kDa.46, 50 They are characterized for being able to refold denatured proteins in an ATP-independent manner; while HSP60 and HSP70 are ATP-dependent.<sup>51</sup>

Herbicide action leads to stress effects that are manifested by many of the gene family effects described above in this paper and others. e.g., 27 Since *t-*chalcone is a metabolic intermediate of all higher plants, it might seem surprising that it is so phytotoxic, but a high level of phytotoxicity of some other metabolic intermediates is known, such as protoporphyrin IX,<sup>52</sup> phytosphingosine,<sup>53</sup> and 2-aminobutyric acid.54, 55 These are all metabolic intermediates that are normally found at very low *in vivo* levels and are apparently under strong regulation to avoid accumulation to toxic levels. Although we found some effects on expression of genes of the flavonoid pathway, it is doubtful that inhibition of this pathway would result in phytotoxicity, especially since Böger<sup>56</sup> reported that a compound (*R*metolachlor) that inhibits chalcone synthase is not phytotoxic. However, chalcone synthase has been reported to be involved in more than synthesis of *t*-chalcone, as up-regulation of the chalcone synthase gene is associated with resistance to the herbicide dicamba, a benzoic acid analog herbicide.<sup>57</sup> Nguyen *et al*. 58 reported *t*-chalcone to be a weak inhibitor of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC), with increasing activity upon hydroxylation of either or both rings of the molecule. PEPC from C4 plants was more sensitive than that from C3 plants. However, this mode of action does not fit the hypersensitivity of the roots that we report here, and the gene for PEPC was only slightly down-regulated in the shoot at 3 h. Furthermore, because PEPC is the most abundant protein in green cells, the amount of even a good inhibitor needed to poison a plant would be much higher than the micromolar level we have reported for *t*-chalcone.

Our results are consistent with rapid *t*-chalcone-induced oxidative damage on roots, which may be modulated and spread by ROS signaling and auxins, which participate in the implementation of cellular responses and detoxifying mechanisms by the plant. As well, over-expression of genes related to ROS-dependent cell death and apoptosis after 3 h of *t*chalcone treatment may be indicating an early induction of PCD within the *Arabidopsis* roots in response to the presence of high levels of ROS, <sup>16</sup> whereas after 24 h many of these genes were repressed, possibly reflecting the inception of cell death. The induction of HSPs, found after 1 h of *t*-chalcone treatment, is indicative of long-term stress conditions. This all indicates that *t*-chalcone reaches its site of action very rapidly and exerts toxicity leading to ROS production quickly since stress responses were detected very early. Previously found effects of *t*-chalcone on *Arabidopsis* shoots, especially early plasma membrane depolarization and dramatic effects on chloroplasts structure and function, pigment degradation, reduced photosynthetic activity, bleaching, and eventually cell death would be related to secondary effects consequences of the oxidative stress generated by *t*-chalcone on *Arabidopsis* metabolism.<sup>17</sup>

The discussion above is mostly on secondary effects of *t*-chalcone. Effective herbicides and other phytotoxins usually target a critical enzyme of a primary metabolic pathway.<sup>3</sup> We therefore analyzed these pathways with KEGG software. KEGG analysis of metabolic pathways suggested several primary target sites of *t*-chalcone. Of these, ALS, HPPD, and TAT were examined by metabolite supplementation studies, similar to those of Schultz *et al.*, <sup>30</sup> Duke *et al*., 23 , and Grossmann *et al*. <sup>31</sup> In our studies, only the product of HPPD, HGA could rescue the plants (*L. paucicostata* and Arabidopsis) from much of the phytoxicity symptoms caused by *t-*chalcone (Fig. 9). The bleaching of Arabidopsis shoots caused by *t*-chalcone is a typical symptom of HPPD inhibitors, in that it indirectly inhibits carotenoid synthesis.<sup>28</sup> Even though the symptomology and supplement reversal studies are consistent with HPPD as the molecular target site of *t*-chalcone, we found no *in vitro* effect on the enzyme. Not all HPPD inhibitors have an *in vitro* effect on the enzyme, as isoxaflutole must be metabolically converted to an active inhibitor after application to the plant.<sup>29</sup> *t*-Chalcone could be converted to a HPPD inhibitor *in vivo*. Indeed, a structure very similar to a chalcone is found in several predicted HPPD inhibitors, and the one most like *t*chalcone inhibited HPPD *in vitro*. <sup>59</sup> Another possibility is that t-chalcone inhibits homogentisic solanesyltransferase (HST), the next enzyme after HPPD in the sythessis of plastoquinone.Symptoms caused by a herbicide that inhibits this enzyme (cyclopyrimorate) are very similar to those of HPPD inhbitors.<sup>60</sup> Cylopyrimorate is a proherbicide that must be metabolized to be a good HST inhibitor. HST mRNA level in the experiment described in the present paper were unaffected except for shoots at 24h, when the expression level was significantly (p = 0.002) downregulated by about one third by *t-*chalcone (data not shown). Reconciling the reversal of the effect of *t-*chalcone on Arabidopsis and duckweed by HGA with a mode of action other than inhibition of HPPD is difficult. Further research is needed to determine the role of HPPD in the mode of action of *t*-chalcone.
