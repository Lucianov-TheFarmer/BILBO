## 27 Highlight

## 33 Abstract

In arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbiosis, key components of nutrient uptake and exchange
 are specialized transporters that facilitate nutrient transport across membranes. As

36 phosphate is a nutrient and a regulator of nutrient exchanges, we investigated the effect of 37 P availability to extra-radical mycelium (ERM) on both plant and fungus transcriptomes and 38 metabolomes in a symbiocosm system. By perturbing nutrient exchanges under the control 39 of P, our objectives were to identify new fungal genes involved in nutrient transports, and to 40 characterize in which extent the fungus differentially modulates its metabolism when 41 interacting with two different plant species. We performed transportome analysis on the 42 extraradical (ERM) and intraradical mycelium (IRM) of the AM fungus Rhizophagus irregularis 43 associated to Populus trichocarpa and Sorghum bicolor under high and low P availability in 44 ERM, using quantitative RT-PCR and Illumina mRNA-sequencing. We observed that 45 mycorrhizal symbiosis induces expression of specific phosphate and ammonium transporters 46 in both plants. Furthermore, we identified new AM-inducible transporters and showed that 47 a subset of phosphate transporters is regulated independently of symbiotic nutrient 48 exchange. mRNA-Sequencing revealed that the fungal transportome was not similarly 49 regulated in the two host plant species according to P availability. Mirroring this effect, many 50 plant carbohydrate transporters were down-regulated in P. trichocarpa mycorrhizal root 51 tissue. Metabolome analysis revealed further that AM root colonization led to a modification 52 of root primary metabolism under low and high P availability and to a decrease of primary 53 metabolite pools in general. Moreover, the down regulation of the sucrose transporters 54 (SUT) suggests that the plant limits carbohydrate long distance transport (ie from shoot to 55 the mycorrhizal roots). By simultaneous uptake/reuptake of nutrients from the apoplast at 56 the biotrophic interface, plant and fungus are both able to control reciprocal nutrient fluxes.

#### 67 **1. Introduction**

68 Phosphorus (P), and nitrogen (N), are among the most essential nutrients for plants. As P is 69 present in many biological compounds and is involved in many key metabolic processes, it 70 can make up to 0.2% of the dry weight of a plant (Schachtman et al., 1998). In living plants, 71 the cellular inorganic phosphate (Pi) concentration ranges between 1-10mM whereas the soil 72 water Pi concentration, is about 10,000-times less (Rausch and Bucher, 2002; Ai et al., 2009; 73 Branscheid et al., 2010): Due to its negative charge and its low mobility, Pi is rapidly 74 sequestered by cations and organic substances in the soil (e.g. clay) and therefore only barely 75 accessible to plants (Poirier and Bucher, 2002; Aung et al., 2006; Chiou et al., 2006; Javot et 76 al., 2007a; Tatry et al., 2009). To prevent mineral nutrient deficiency, and P deficiency in 77 particular, a majority of land plants form symbioses with the so called arbuscular mycorrhizal 78 (AM) fungi. In the AM symbiosis the AM fungus provides macro and micro nutrients to the 79 plants and in return receives carbohydrates from the host plant (Smith and Read, 2008). AM 80 fungi have a broad host range: each fungal species, each genet can colonize several plant 81 individuals, also from different species, connected each other and forming a so called 82 common mycorrhizal network (Walder et al., 2012; Fellbaum et al., 2014). The extraradical 83 mycelium (ERM) of the AM fungi engaged in such a network is able to acquire nutrients that 84 are out of reach or not accessible to the plant partners. For a given plant, this makes the 85 mycorrhizal uptake pathway often more effective than the direct uptake pathway (Wipf et 86 al., 2019). The nutrients taken up by the ERM are transported to the hyphal network inside 87 the host root via the intraradical mycelium (IRM), which forms highly branched tree-like 88 structures (arbuscules) inside the root cortical cells. The arbuscules are still surrounded by 89 the plant cell-derived periarbuscular membrane and the inter-membrane interstice, the 90 periarbuscular space. Within this structure, the mineral nutrients acquired by the AM fungus 91 are exported to the periarbuscular space, and taken up by plant nutrient transporters of the 92 periarbuscular membrane (Smith and Smith, 2011).

93 With respect to Pi, the extent to which plants cover their Pi-demand through the AM fungus 94 ranges from only a small percentage to 100 % (Paszkowski, 2006; Javot et al., 2007a). The Pi 95 taken up by the ERM is incorporated into poly-P, transported to the arbuscules, hydrolyzed 96 and translocated to the periarbuscular space (Ezawa et al., 2002; Javot et al., 2007a; Kikuchi 97 et al., 2016). Essential key players in this process are transporters and permeases that 98 facilitate uptake and transport of nutrients across membranes. The expression of 99 transporters is regulated by nutrient availability. In this way, a steady and efficient 100 translocation of nutrients adapted to given environmental conditions can be guaranteed 101 (Smith and Smith, 2011; Courty et al., 2015; Garcia et al., 2016; Wipf et al., 2019).

102 In AM fungi, only few phosphate transporters (PT) were characterized so far: one in 103 Funneliformis mosseae (GmosPT, Benedetto et al., 2005) and Glomus versiforme (GvPT, 104 Harrison and Buuren 1995), and seven in Rhizophagus irregularis (formerly Glomus 105 intraradices, RiPT1-RiPT7; Maldonado-Mendoza et al., 2001; Walder et al., 2016). Expression 106 of the three high affinity transporters, *RiPT1*, *GvPT* and *GmosPT*, is dependent on external Pi 107 concentrations in the ERM (Harrison and Buuren, 1995; Maldonado-Mendoza et al., 2001; 108 Benedetto et al., 2005). Reduced expression of GmosPT in the intraradical mycelium (IRM) 109 compared to the extraradical mycelium (ERM) suggested a concentration-dependent 110 regulation of PTs in the symbiotic root tissue.

111 In plants, the family of PTs can be divided into three subfamilies. Subfamily I transporters 112 (Pht1) are membrane bound  $H^+/P$  symporters driven by an  $H^+$  gradient. They are a subgroup 113 of the major facilitator superfamily to which most of the PTs known to date belong (Pao et 114 al., 1998). Subfamily II members are located in the plastids and function as antiporters 115 (Poirier and Bucher, 2002; Rausch and Bucher, 2002; Javot et al., 2007b); members of the 116 subfamily III are located in the mitochondrial inner membrane and are predicted to function 117 as  $H^{+}/P$  symporters or as  $P/OH^{-}$  antiporters (Takabatake et al., 1999; Javot et al., 2007b). In 118 mycorrhizal plants, some Pht1 phosphate transporters are specifically induced during 119 symbiotic interaction. The first mycorrhiza-inducible PT was identified in Solanum tuberosum 120 (StPT3) and was localized in arbusculated root sections (Rausch et al., 2001). Then, more 121 mycorrhiza-inducible transporters were identified in several other plants (Harrison et al., 122 2002; Glassop et al., 2005; Nagy et al., 2005; Loth-Pereda et al., 2011). StPT3 and MtPT4 from 123 Medicago truncatula were present in the periarbuscular membrane only. Furthermore, it was 124 demonstrated that MtPT4-deficient plants accumulated Pi as poly-P in the arbuscules, which 125 resulted in a premature collapse of the arbuscules and in inefficient symbiosis (Javot et al., 126 2007a; Breuillin-Sessoms et al., 2015).

127 For N, it was long assumed that AM symbiosis plays only a minor role in plant nutrition. In 128 the soil, inorganic N is mostly present as nitrate or ammonium, both of which are readily 129 mobile in the soil. Therefore, it was assumed that AM fungi take up N with the same efficiency 130 as plants (Marschner and Dell, 1994; Hodge et al., 2010; Smith and Smith, 2011). But, it was 131 shown that AM fungi can contribute up to 42% of the plant N demand (Frey and Schüepp, 132 1993; Mäder et al., 2000; Govindarajulu et al., 2005). In addition to the uptake of ammonium 133 and nitrate, it was shown that AM fungi can take up N in the form of organic molecules (*i.e.* 134 small peptides and amino acids) (Bago et al., 1996; Hawkins et al., 2000; Govindarajulu et al., 135 2005; Jin et al., 2005) and possibly also in the form of more complex organic compounds 136 (Leigh et al., 2009; Hodge et al., 2010). However, in plants as well as in AM fungi, it was shown 137 that ammonium is the preferred N source, as it can be directly incorporated into glutamine 138 by the glutamine synthetase/glutamine oxoglutarate aminotransferase (GS/GOGAT) 139 pathway whereas nitrate needs to be reduced to ammonium before N assimilation in the 140 GS/GOGAT pathway (Villegas et al., 1996; Hawkins et al., 2000; Toussaint et al., 2004). In the 141 ERM of AM fungi, the glutamine is further metabolized into amino acids such as arginine, 142 alanine and asparagine for transport. Studies using <sup>15</sup>N showed that arginine was the most 143 common labelled amino acid in the ERM of AM fungi (Govindarajulu et al., 2005). Arginine is 144 then transported from the ERM to the IRM where it is cleaved by arginases in the arbuscules. 145 The released ammonium is transported to the periarbuscular space where it can be taken up 146 by the plant ammonium transporters (AMT). So far, only six transporters have been identified 147 and functionally characterized in Glomeromycotina, three in R. irregularis GintAMT1, 148 GintAMT2 and GintAMT3 (López-Pedrosa et al., 2006; Pérez-Tienda et al., 2011; Calabrese et 149 al., 2016), and three in Geosiphon pyriformis (GpAmt1, GpAmt2, GpAmt3; Ellerbeck et al., 150 2013).

151 In plants, the family of AMT can be divided into two subfamilies: subfamily I and subfamily II 152 (reviewed in Courty et al., 2015). While members of the subfamily I were found to be mostly 153 expressed in roots, members of the subfamily II were preferentially expressed in shoots 154 (Couturier et al., 2007). Several mycorrhiza-inducible AMTs have been identified through 155 gene expression analysis in several plant species (Gomez et al., 2009; Guether et al., 2009; 156 Kobae et al., 2010), including *PtAMT1.2* in poplar (Selle et al., 2005; Couturier et al., 2007) 157 and *SbAMT3.1* and *SbAMT4* in sorghum (Koegel et al., 2013).

158 In return for the mineral nutrient, the AM fungi receives photosynthetates from the plants. 159 In absence of gene encoding fatty acid synthase in their genome (Wewer et al., 2014; Tang 160 et al., 2016), AM fungi are dependent to host fatty acids. Although the mechanism of transfer 161 remains unknown, it was demonstrated that palmitic acid is transferred from plant roots to 162 symbiotic mycelium, putatively esterified as mono-acyl-glycerol (Bravo et al., 2017; Jiang et 163 al., 2017; Keymer et al., 2017; Luginbuehl et al., 2017). Research on sugar transporter (SUT) 164 expression in plants is not as consistent. Mycorrhization caused either increased or 165 decreased expression of SUTs in root and shoots of the host plants (Ge et al., 2008; Boldt et al., 2011; Doidy et al., 2012a). A new class of SUT, the SWEETs, was identified. These 166 167 transporters are located in the plasma membrane and have been shown to function as 168 bidirectional sugar uniporters (Chen et al., 2010). Due to their involvement in rhizobial 169 symbiosis it is assumed that they may also play a role in other biotrophic plant symbioses 170 such as the AM symbiosis (Gamas et al., 1996; Doidy et al., 2012b).

171 In the AM fungus *R. irregularis,* four carbohydrate transporters have been identified (Helber 172 et al., 2011). The gene encoding the monosaccharide transporter *RiMST2* was the most highly 173 expressed transporter in symbiotic tissue, and it could be localized in the arbuscules and in 174 the IRM. Within this line, An et al. (2019) recently showed that the expression of the gene 175 encoding *M. truncatula SWEET1b* transporter is strongly enhanced in arbuscule-containing 176 cells compared to roots and localizes to the peri-arbuscule membrane.

177 A comprehensive view of symbiosis under environmental and nutritional stress is important 178 in times of climate change and resource shortening, as AM symbiosis is a key component of 179 nutrient uptake and exchange through their transport systems. However, despite 180 accumulating knowledge about transporter expression and metabolic activity in AM 181 symbiosis, we still lack a precise understanding about their regulation and their importance 182 for metabolism. Here, our objectives were to define the effects of P availability on two plants 183 connected through a mycorrhizal network. Therefore, we analyzed the effects of 184 mycorrhization and contrasting P nutrition on the transporter expression and metabolite 185 accumulation in Populus trichocarpa (poplar) and Sorghum bicolor (sorghum) when colonized 186 by the AM fungus R. irregularis. Our main focus was on the regulatory role of the 187 mycorrhization and P nutrition on the expression of the Pht1 PTs in the plants and in the AM 188 fungus. Further we assessed the effect of the applied conditions on AMTs and carbohydrate 189 transporters. In the AM fungus R. irregularis, we determined expression of PTs, AMTs, 190 monosaccharide transporters (MST) and fatty acid metabolism in the ERM and in the IRM of 191 colonized poplar and sorghum roots. We identified new specific mycorrhiza-inducible PTs 192 and AMTs in poplar and sorghum. Moreover, our data allowed us to gain further insight into 193 symbiotic carbon exchange.

## 195 **2. Material and Methods**

# 196 **2.1 Experimental set-up**

197 Experiments were performed with P. trichocarpa cuttings (clone 10174, Orléans, France) and 198 S. bicolor (L.) Moench, cv Pant-5. Sorghum seeds were kindly provided by I.G.F.R.I. (CSS 199 Agriculture University of Hissar, Harvana, India) and G.B. Pant University of Agriculture and 200 Technology (Pantanagar, Uttaranchal, India). Seeds were surface-sterilized in 2.5% KCIO for 201 10 min, rinsed several times with sterile deionized water and soaked overnight in sterile 202 deionized water. Seeds were germinated in the dark at 25°C for three days. Plants were 203 inoculated with 1 ml liquid inoculum of *R. irregularis*, isolate BEG75 (Inoculum Plus, Dijon, 204 France), in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6) with about 110 spores/ml. The microcosms were set 205 up in tripartite compartments (mycorrhizal treatment) or single compartments (non206 mycorrhizal treatments). Compartments were filled with an autoclaved (120°C, 20 min) 207 quartz sand (Alsace, Kaltenhouse, Trafor AG, Basel): zeolite (Symbion, Czech Republic) 208 substrate (1:1, w:w). In the tripartite compartment system poplar cuttings were planted in 209 the left compartment and sorghum seedlings in the right compartment. Both plants were 210 inoculated with R. irregularis to create a common mycorrhizal network and to increase poplar 211 root colonization (Figure S1). Compartments were separated by two 21 µm meshes and one 212 3 mm mesh, to allow the AM fungus to grow from one compartment to the other but to avoid 213 plant roots protruding the neighboring compartment. As control, non-inoculated poplar and 214 sorghum plantlets grew in single compartments receiving the Pi containing fertilizer 215 treatments directly to their roots. Plants were fertilized once a week with 10 ml Hoagland 216 solution without P, until all plants showed signs of P depletion, indicated by anthocyan 217 accumulation. From the  $22^{nd}$  week high-P (560  $\mu$ M) or low-P (28  $\mu$ M) containing Hoagland 218 solution was applied to the first compartment for 9 weeks, to obtain ERM and to ensure that 219 P was delivered via the mycorrhizal uptake pathway. Control plants received fertilizer 220 treatment directly to their root systems.

## 222 2.2 Harvest

223 The ERM was harvested by dispersing the substrate with tap water and fishing it from the 224 surface using a 32  $\mu$ M mesh. These steps were repeated several times. Afterwards the 225 cleaned ERM samples were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C.

For RNA extractions, two leaves from the top of Poplar plants and two young leaves of Sorghum plants were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C. The rest of the shoots was harvested and dried in an oven at 55°C for 4 days for total P measurement.

Roots were removed from substrate under tap water and cut into ~1 cm small pieces. Two
subsamples of about 100 mg were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C.
One subsample of about 100 mg was taken for root colonization measurements. The
remaining roots were placed in a paper bag and dried at 55°C for 3.5 days for determination
of total P and N content.

## 235 **2.3 Colonization measurements and P extraction**

Roots were immersed in 10% KOH and stored at 4°C overnight. The next day, the roots were rinsed with tap water and immersed in 2% HCl for 1 hour at room temperature. Then, the roots were rinsed with tap water, immersed in 0.005% trypan blue (w:v in lactic-acid: glycerol: water, 1:1:1, v:v:v) and stored at 4°C o/n. The next day, the roots were rinsed and immersed in lactic-acid glycerol water (1:1:1, v:v:v) for destaining (Brundrett *et al.*, 1984). Total colonization count was performed using the magnified intersection method (McGonigle *et al.*, 1990). Differences between means of variables were assessed by t-test (p<0.05), using</li>
Microsoft Excel 2010.

For determination of P concentration in the plants, dried root and shoot samples of six biological replicates were ground using a ball mill. Up to 500mg were used for the modified P extraction method by Murphy and Riley (1962).

## 248 2.4 RNA extraction

#### 256 **2.5 Reverse transcription and qRT-PCR**

cDNAs from three biological replicates were obtained using the iScript<sup>™</sup>cDNA Synthesis Kit 257 258 (BIO RAD Laboratories, Paolo Alto, CA, United States), using 200 ng of total RNA per reaction, 259 using same RNA extracts as for mRNA sequencing. For quantification a two-step quantitative 260 RT-PCR approach was used. Gene specific primers were designed in Primer 3 261 (http://frodo.wi.mit.edu/cgi-bin/primer3/primer3 www.cgi) and tested as well in amplify 262 3.1 (http://engels.genetics.wisc.edu/amplify). Target gene expressions were normalized to 263 the expression of the reference gene ubiquitin in Poplar (Potri.015G013600) and Sorghum 264 (Sb10g026870) and translation elongation factor in R. irregularis, respectively. All primers 265 used are listed in Table S1. gRT-PCRs were run in a 7500 real-time PCR system (Roche) using 266 the following settings: 95°C for 3 min and then 40 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 60°C for 1 min and 267 72°C for 30 s. There were three biological and three technical replicates per treatment. 268 Differences in gene expression between applied conditions were tested by a one-way ANOVA 269 using SPSS Statistics, version 22 (IBM, Chicago, USA).

## 271 **2.6 RNA sequencing and data analysis**

Total RNA sequencing was done for three biological replicates per condition. Eighteen
libraries were prepared and paired-end Illumina HiSeq mRNA sequencing (2x100bp RNA-Seq)
was performed by Beckman Coulter Genomics France (Grenoble, France), which produced
around 2x80 million reads per library in average. After quality check using FastQC, adaptor

276 sequences were removed using FASTX-Toolkit. Only inserts of at least 30-nt were conserved 277 for further analysis. Reads were mapped with CLC Genomics Workbench 11 (CLC Bio 278 workbench, Qiagen, Aarhus, Denmark) using manufacturer's recommendations on Poplar 279 genome and gene annotation Ptrichocarpa 210 v3.0 (Tuskan et al., 2006), on Sorghum 280 genome and gene annotation Sbicolor 313 v3.1 (McCormick et al., 2017), and on R. 281 irregularis gene annotation Rhiir2-1 (Morin et al., 2019). The mapped reads for each 282 transcript were calculated and normalized as RPKM for calculating gene expression (reads 283 per kilobase of transcripts per million reads mapped—Mortazavi et al., 2008). Intact and 284 broken pairs were both counted as one. The RPKMs of each transcript in different conditions 285 were compared using proportion-based test statistics (Baggerly et al., 2003) implemented in 286 CLC genomic Workbench suite. This beta-binomial test compares the proportions of counts 287 in a group of samples against those of another group of samples. Different weights are given 288 to the samples, depending on their sizes (total counts). The weights are obtained by assuming 289 a Beta distribution on the proportions in a group, and estimating these, along with the 290 proportion of a binomial distribution, by the method of moments. The result is a weighted t-291 type test statistic. We then calculated False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction for multiple-292 hypothesis test (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). Only genes showing a difference of 10 reads 293 between compared conditions were considered as significantly expressed. Genes were 294 considered as differentially expressed when meeting the requirements of fold change  $\geq |2|$ 295 and FDR  $\leq$  0.05.The expression was then normalized using RPKM. Raw RNAseq data and 296 global mapping data analyses were deposited at GEO (GSE138316).

## 298 **2.7 Metabolite profiling and data analysis**

299 For extraction of soluble metabolites about 90 mg of deep frozen poplar root and ERM 300 samples (three biological replicates per condition) were pulverized in liquid nitrogen. 301 Metabolite profiling was performed as described in (Dethloff et al., 2014) by gas 302 chromatography coupled to electron impact ionization/time-of-flight mass spectrometry 303 (GC-EI/TOF-MS) using an Agilent 6890N24 gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, 304 Böblingen, Germany; http://www.agilent.com). Guidelines for manually supervised 305 metabolite identification were the presence of at least 3 specific mass fragments per 306 compound and a retention index deviation < 1.0% (Strehmel et al., 2008). For quantification 307 purposes all mass features were evaluated for best specific, selective and quantitative 308 representation of observed analytes. Laboratory and reagent contaminations were evaluated 309 and removed according to non-sample control experiments. Metabolites were routinely 310 assessed by log2-transformed relative changes expressed as response ratios. Statistical testing, namely 2-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney testing of significance were performed using relative abundances or log2-transformed ratios. Statistical assessments and data visualizations were performed using the multi-experiment viewer software, MeV (Version 4.9; http://www.tm4.org/mev.html; Saeed et al., 2006) and the Microsoft-Excel 2010 program.

## 317 3. Results

## 318 **3.1 Colonization, and N and P measurements**

AM colonization of roots was between 79% and 87% (poplar) and about 93% (sorghum) (**Table** S2). Non-AM plants were not colonized. The hyphal colonization and the percentage of vesicles were not significantly different between low-P and high-P treatments in sorghum and poplar plants. However, significantly, sorghum roots contained three times more arbuscules in the low-P treatment, indicating that P starvation supported mycorrhization.

P treatment had significant effects on P content in the shoots and roots of poplar and sorghum (**Figure** S2). High-P treatment on ERM increased P content in roots and shoots, except for the roots from non-AM sorghum plants. Under the same P supply conditions, non-AM poplar accumulated more P than AM poplar whereas in AM sorghum, P accumulation was comparable to non-AM plants.

P treatment had significant effects on N content in the shoots of poplar, but marginally on
 sorghum (Figure S2). High-P treatment increased N content in poplar shoots. Under the same
 P supply conditions, AM poplar accumulated more P than non-AM poplar whereas in AM
 sorghum, N accumulation was comparable to non-AM plants.

# **334 3.2** Patterns of ERM and IRM expressomes in the symbiocosm

335 *3.2.1 Incidence of phosphate availability* 

336 The symbiotic part of the mycelium in roots (Intra Radical Mycelium, IRM) showed different 337 gene expression patterns according to phosphate concentration and plant host. The total 338 number of differentially expressed genes (DEG) differs around 10 fold between the two 339 plants with 298 genes differentially regulated in S. bicolor plants against 3162 in P. 340 trichocarpa in response to phosphate treatment (Figure 1A). High phosphate concentration 341 has a fewer incidence on the IRM transcriptome in S. bicolor than in P. trichocarpa whereas 342 the fungus is connected to both plants, indicating that the allocation of mineral nutrients is 343 more tightly regulated in *P. trichocarpa* than in *S. bicolor*. In both plants, the number of up 344 regulated fungal genes is 10 times the number of down regulated fungal genes in high-P 345 condition compared to low-P. This indicates that the fungus is metabolically more active in 346 high-P condition, consistent with the fact that adding a high-P solution in the CMN 347 compartment enhances the fungal-dependent Pi acquisition pathway. Only two fungal genes 348 were significantly up regulated in both plants in response to high-P treatment and are 349 involved in vesicular cargo. In Sorghum, the few up regulated genes were involved in 350 vesicular trafficking, lipid binding, nitrate reduction and general transport. In P. trichocarpa, 351 the genes up regulated by high-P belonged to vesicle metabolism, fatty acid metabolism and 352 transport of nitrogen, Pi, sugars, and water. Interestingly, only one gene (a short chain 353 dehydrogenase) was significantly up regulated in both plants and in the ERM in response to 354 high-P treatment.

355 It is noteworthy that on the one hand, while cultivated in high-P, 3408 genes were 356 overexpressed in IRM (either in S. bicolor, or in P. trichocarpa or in both host plants) against 357 73 in the ERM compartment. On the other hand, while grown in low-P, the number of up 358 regulated genes in planta dropped to 294 against 209 in the ERM. These overall expressome 359 patterns suggest that when Pi is abundant in soil, the fungus invests little energy in recruiting 360 the Pi but is highly active in exchanging this nutrient with the plants. On the opposite, when 361 Pi is scarce in the medium, the extra radicle mycelium has to put a lot of energy in recovering 362 the mineral in the soil but has fewer nutrients to exchange with the plants.

#### 549 **4. Discussion**

In this study, we described the effects of P supply and AM colonization on PTs, AMTs,
carbohydrate transporters and lipid genes in poplar and sorghum, and in the ERM and IRM
of the AM fungus *R. irregularis.*

#### 553

#### 554 **4.1 Differential resource allocation in the CMN**

555 In the symbiocosm, we observed disequilibrium in fungal DEG patterns according to sorghum 556 and poplar symbiotic tissues in response to high-P concentration supplied to ERM. Sorghum 557 is an annual grass with a C4 metabolism, and poplar a perennial tree with a C3 metabolism. 558 The fungal transportome in the fast growing Poaceae is always more important than in poplar 559 in any given P concentration. It has been shown that the fungus is able to adapt its resource 560 allocation to the best symbiont available (Fellbaum et al., 2014; Whiteside et al., 2019). Our 561 data provide same conclusion at the transcriptomic level. However, we have no information 562 about the extent of the ERM developed from roots of each of the two host plants, so 563 differences observed between the two hosts may depend also on a differential ability of the 564 symbiont to form functional ERM. Anyway, we could hypothesize that the perennial poplar 565 plant is less mycorrhiza-dependent than the annual sorghum.

566

#### 567 **4.2 Symbiotic phosphorus exchange**

568 In our study, we confirmed the expression of *PtPT1.10* in Poplar AM roots only (Figure S3) as 569 already reported (Loth-Pereda et al., 2011). In addition, PtPT1.8 was also expressed in AM 570 roots only. In sorghum roots, AM colonisation induced the specific expression of SbPT1.8, 571 SbPT1.10 and partially of SbPT1.11 (Figure S5). The specific induction of PtPT1.8 and PtPT1.10 572 in poplar and of SbPT1.8, SbPT1.10 in sorghum upon mycorrhization strongly suggested that 573 there is a symbiosis-dependent Pi uptake system. In M. truncatula, it has been shown that 574 MtPT4 is specifically expressed in the periarbuscular membrane (Harrison et al., 2002). The 575 specific induction of PtPT1.8 and PtPT1.10 in poplar and of SbPT1.8, SbPT1.10 in sorghum 576 suggested that these transporters are localized at the periarbuscular membrane (Figure 6).

577 On the fungal side, the PTs of *R. irregularis* were expressed in the ERM and in the IRM of 578 poplar and sorghum roots. The high affinity transporter *RiPT1* was previously found to be 579 regulated in the ERM by external Pi concentration (Maldonado-Mendoza et al., 2001) and 580 expressed at the arbuscular side (Fiorilli et al., 2013). Here, RiPT1 was up-regulated by 581 external low-P concentrations in the ERM but also in the IRM. High expression of RiPT1 582 suggests that *RiPT1* is the main transporter for Pi uptake and symbiotic Pi transfer in our 583 conditions. Maldonado-Mendoza et al. (2001) predicted the existence of other PTs operating 584 at high external P concentrations. Indeed, RiPT3 and RiPT7 were expressed but not affected 585 by the nutrient conditions in the ERM. It might be that higher Pi concentrations are needed 586 to increase expression of these possible high affinity transporters. Clearly, RiPT3 and RiPT7 were induced in the IRM with sorghum, with a similar tendency in poplar, suggesting thatthey participate also in Pi transfer/exchanges in the symbiosis.

589

## 590 **4.3 P-dependent regulation of PT expression**

591 Low-P conditions induced expression of PtPT1.1, PtPT1.2, PtPT1.4 and PtPT1.11 in poplar 592 roots, showing that expression and regulation of PTs is dependent on Pi availability (Figure 593 S3). In the mycorrhizal microcosm, the only Pi source was the AM fungal symbiont, and the 594 absence of a direct Pi source further increased expression of these four transporters. As we 595 still observed a Pi-dependent regulation of transporter expression, our data suggest that 596 these PTs are regulated by external and internal Pi-concentrations and are regulated 597 independently by the mycorrhizal pathway. Increased expression of PTs in low-P condition 598 further suggests that the AM fungus supplies the host plant with more Pi if the fungus itself 599 has increased access to Pi. By regulating these PTs independently poplar ensures a P nutrition 600 uncoupled from the AM symbiont. Symbiosis-independent P-nutrition is necessary for 601 perennial plants as mycorrhizal abundance varies in nature with the seasons (Courty et al., 602 2008; Dumbrell et al., 2011).

The fact that *PtPT1.2*, *PtPT1.4* and *PtPT1.11* expression was also induced upon P-limiting conditions in the shoots suggests that they function in Pi uptake at the root-soil interface as well as in intercellular distribution and translocation of Pi from root to shoot. *PtPT1.9* was mainly expressed in the shoot, which suggests that it is mainly responsible for Pi allocation in the shoots.

Sorghum on the other hand turned out to be more mycorrhiza-dependent than poplar. Under mycorrhization PTs were equally low expressed as under a non-mycorrhizal high-P condition, indicating that the AM fungus was able to cover the Pi needs of sorghum. Induction of PT in the shoots in the non-mycorrhizal low-P condition further showed that the plant suffered of P deficiency and therefore probably reallocated Pi from old leaves (source) to young leaves (sink). The stronger dependency of sorghum to the AM fungus was also indicated by Piaccumulation in mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal sorghum (**Figure S2**).

## 616 4.4 Symbiotic nitrogen exchange

As symbiotic N transfer is also an important aspect of AM symbiosis we analyzed the effects
of mycorrhization and Pi availability on plant and AM fungal AMT expression (Figure 6). Pcontent has a stronger effect on poplar N contain than mycorrhization. Sorghum N nutrition
is not affected by Pi availability. In poplar, mycorrhization induced expression of three AMTs.
Our results are supported by previous studies which showed that *PtAMT1.1* and *PtAMT1.2*

622 were mycorrhiza-inducible when poplar was mycorrhiza, suggesting a sensor role I with the 623 ectomycorrhizal fungi P. involutus (Couturier et al., 2007) and Amanita muscaria (Selle et al., 624 2005) (Figure S6). In addition, we found that AMT3.1 is as well a mycorrhiza-inducible 625 transporter in the roots, which is in contrast to the data of Couturier et al. (2007) who 626 detected PtAMT3.1 solely in senescing leaves. In shoots, the expression of PtAMT1.1 is 627 mediated by Pi availability under mycorrhizal conditions. Further, increased expression of 628 PtAMT1.2 and PtAMT3.1 suggests an increased ammonium transfer when the fungal needs 629 of Pi are accomplished. Analysis of the transcriptome dataset revealed that PtAMT4.1, 630 PtAMT4.2 and PtAMT4.3 were also induced upon mycorrhization independently from the P 631 supply of the fungus as it was the case in our previous study where mycorrhizal poplar was 632 set under N stress (Calabrese et al., 2017).

633 As there is an ongoing debate on whether amino acids as an organic N source can be taken 634 up by AM fungi and transferred from the fungus to the plant (reviewed in Hodge and Storer, 635 2015) we screened the transcriptome data of poplar and identified several amino acid 636 transporters and H<sup>+</sup>/oligopeptide symporters that were either induced or repressed upon 637 mycorrhization (Table S3). Specific induction of amino acid transporters and one of the 638 H<sup>+</sup>/oligopeptide transporters indicate that amino acids are transferred from the AM fungus 639 to the plant as an alternative N source. However, our metabolome analysis on mycorrhizal 640 and non-mycorrhizal poplar roots showed that mycorrhization reduced the abundance of 641 most metabolites including amino acids in the colonized roots tissue (Table 2, Table S7), 642 suggesting high rates of metabolic turnover by the fungus or the host roots or, alternatively, 643 transport to the shoots. Interestingly, an accumulation of relevant metabolites might be 644 detectable in the shoots as it was demonstrated by Whiteside et al. (2012) for the amino 645 acids phenylalanine, lysine, asparagine, arginine, histidine, cysteine, methionine and 646 tryptophan with quantum dot analysis.

647 In the well-established mycorrhizal symbiosis described here, *GintAMT3* was significantly 648 induced in the IRM in both hosts, poplar and sorghum (Figure 4), indicating a major 649 participation of this transporter in the exchanges of ammonium at the arbuscule (Calabrese 650 et al., 2017). High GinAMT2 expression levels independent of Pi-supply and localization in the 651 ERM and IRM indicate that it is a low affinity transporter for ammonium. Moreover, *GinAMT2* 652 displays high sequence similarity to GintAMT3 which is a low affinity transporter (Calabrese 653 et al., 2017). GintAMT1 on the other hand, a high affinity transporter, was expressed at low 654 levels in the ERM and IRM independent of the P availability. Together, these findings indicate 655 that GintAMT1 and GintAMT2 are mainly involved in the uptake of ammonium in the ERM and in the IRM, and that GintAMT3 is mainly involved in the exchanges/competition of/forammonium at the biotrophic interface between the AMF and its host plant.

658

659 In sorghum, only SbAMT3.1 was induced in mycorrhizal roots (Figure S7). In contrast to 660 Koegel et al. (2013) SbAMT4 was not expressed in our experimental conditions. Induced 661 expression of SbAMT1.1 and SbAMT1.2 in the low-P condition suggests that upon sensing of 662 nutrient stress the plant activates a general nutrient uptake program to avoid running short 663 on one or more essential nutrients. In addition our data show that regulation of sorghum 664 AMTs is less mycorrhiza-dependent than nutrient-dependent. Under mycorrhization the 665 plant is supplied with sufficient P and N to keep the P and N level constant within the plant. 666 This scenario may explain the unchanged expression of the SbAMTs. Interestingly, 667 mycorrhization induced expression of SbAMT1.1 and SbAMT1.2 in the shoots in low-P and of 668 SbAMT3.3 in high-P conditions. An increased translocation rate of ammonium from root to 669 shoot upon mycorrhization may be a possible explanation. Moreover, SbAMT1.1 and 670 SbAMT1.2 expression levels have a tendency to be also concentration dependent. Strong 671 induction of SbAMT3.3 in the shoots indicated an increased ammonium flux during high-P 672 and mycorrhizal condition.

#### 674 **4.5 Symbiotic carbon exchange**

675 In order to shed some light on symbiotic carbon exchange we used gRT-PCR and mRNA-Seq 676 analysis to identify possible transporters that enable carbohydrate transport from the plant 677 to the fungus. Interestingly, quantitative RT-PCR expression analysis and mRNA-Seq analysis 678 revealed downregulation of carbohydrate transporters in poplar (Figure S8, Figure 6) and 679 sorghum (Figure S9), which might indicate that carbohydrates are actively sequestered by 680 the fungus. But we also identified two carbohydrate transporters induced upon 681 mycorrhization (Table S3). The UDP-galactose transporters are intracellular transporters 682 situated in the cisternae of the Golgi lumen where UDP-galactose is used for synthesis of non-683 cellulosic polysaccharides and glycoproteins (Norambuena et al., 2002). Only recently, it was 684 shown that they also transport rhamnose (Rautengarten et al., 2014). In the Golgi-network 685 and the early endosome the newly synthesized proteins are sorted either for the secretion 686 to the plasma membrane, the extracellular matrix or for degradation (Feraru et al., 2012; 687 Brandizzi and Barlowe, 2013; McFarlane et al., 2014). Induced expression of UDP-galactose 688 therefore indicates an increased transport activity of proteins to colonized cells which goes 689 along with increased plasma membrane synthesis. In M. truncatula, it has been shown that 690 arbuscule development goes along with the synthesis of the periarbuscular membrane which is distinct from the plasma membrane (Pumplin and Harrison, 2009). Further it has been
shown that AM-induced transporters are specifically directed to the periarbuscular
membrane and an involvement of the trans-Golgi for PT has been implicated (Pumplin et al.,
2012). The other transporter induced upon mycorrhization is a sugar transporter/spinster
transmembrane protein, a member of the major facilitator proteins, which makes it a
candidate for being localized at the cell membrane eventually stimulating carbohydrate
transport to the AM symbiont.

698 Interesting is also that a Glucose-6-phosphate/phosphate and phosphoenolpyruvate/ 699 phosphate antiporter was induced. Glucose-6 phosphate/phosphate antiporters are 700 normally expressed in non-green plastids and serves as carbohydrate importer. In 701 amyloplasts glucose-6 phosphate/phosphate antiporters are located at the site of starch and 702 fatty acids synthesis (Kammerer et al., 1998; Flügge, 2001). Phosphoenolpyruvate/ 703 phosphate antiporter mediates the transport of the transport of Phosphoenolpyruvate into 704 the organelles as in amyloplasts, for the synthesis of aromatic amino acids and several 705 secondary compounds. Increased activity of this transporter suggests a decrease of freely 706 available sugars, of storing starch and of aromatic amino acids synthesis. These results shed 707 light the link between carbon, phosphorus, but also nitrogen.

708 In the AM fungi *Geosiphon pyriformis* and *R. irregularis* several carbohydrate transporters 709 were identified. For GpMST1 and RiMST2, it was shown that they specifically transport 710 monosaccharides. RiMST2 was found to be expressed at the arbuscular site and in the 711 intraradical hyphae (Schüßler et al., 2006; Helber et al., 2011). Helber et al. (2011) proposed 712 a model in which the absorbed sugars might derive from cell wall degradation but this is 713 contradictory to previous observations in which carbohydrate transfer varied with the source 714 strength of the host plant (Olsson et al., 2010; Fellbaum et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2015). We 715 demonstrated that RiMST2 was induced in the IRM of its host plants poplar (Figure 3) and 716 sorghum (Figure 3) and that its expression level remained unchanged by Pi availability. The 717 metabolome analysis revealed further that mycorrhization significantly decreased the 718 abundance of monosaccharides (i.e. glucose, rhamnose and ribose) in AM poplar roots. In 719 AM fungi it has been shown that hexoses received from the host plants are transformed to 720 glycogen, trehalose. Our observations are consistent with these findings that 721 monosaccharides are taken up mainly by RiMST2 from the apoplast, converted into 722 disaccharides (*i.e.* trehalose) for transport and hydrolyzed in the ERM into monosaccharides, 723 ready for direct usage or for storage. The mycorrhizal-dependent downregulation of 724 carbohydrate transporters and monosaccharide abundance in poplar roots, along with reduced RiMST2 expression at the arbuscular site and in the IRM suggests that the fungusactively sequesters the sugars on its own demand.

Taken together our data suggest that the plant strictly regulates carbohydrate allocation on
mycorrhizal roots. With the Sugar transporter/spinster transmembrane protein a possible
candidate for symbiotic carbon transfer from the plant to the periarbuscular space is found.
The fungus may take up sugars from there by RiMST2.

AMF are fatty acid (FA) auxotrophs as their fatty acids are provided by their host plant (MacLean et al., 2017). To date, mechanisms of FA transport are unknown. We observed that fungal lipid metabolism is highly expressed in the IRM. While many lipases, elongases and desaturases are expressed both in the ERM and IRM, they might play a role in the storage and use of triglycerides in lipid droplets. However, their over-expression *in planta* could be a clue of the recovering process of FA from the plant.

737

#### 738 Conclusion

739 Here, we demonstrated that mycorrhization leads to specific induction of a variety of 740 transporters. As P and N are key elements of mycorrhizal symbiosis, we tested the expression 741 of PT and AMT and show that mycorrhization specifically induces expression of a selection of 742 PT and AMTs in poplar and in sorghum. Further, we identified one carbohydrate transporter 743 specifically induced in mycorrhizal root tissue that might be a possible candidate for 744 symbiotic carbon exchange. By contrast, other carbohydrate transporters were down-745 regulated upon mycorrhization indicating that the plant may not volunteer provision of 746 carbohydrates in exchange for mineral nutrients. We further showed that some nutrient 747 transporters are more strongly expressed in the IRM compared to the ERM, which indicates 748 that they are directly involved in nutrient transfer at the arbuscular membrane or, as in the 749 case of MST, in nutrient uptake into the intraradical hyphae (Figure 6). Nevertheless, despite 750 its importance, the release of major nutrients taken up by the ERM into the root apoplasm 751 occurs through widely unknown mechanisms, which must include the differentiation and 752 polarisation of the fungal membrane transport functions. Transport processes across the 753 polarised membrane interfaces are of major importance in the functioning of the established 754 mycorrhizal association as the symbiotic relation is based on an apparent 'fair-trade' between 755 fungus and host plant. PTs and AMTs are active transporters and they are able to transport 756 nutrients against the direction of concentration gradients using electrochemical potential 757 differences build up by proton-pumping ATPases. In fact, the reality is probably much more 758 complex, with reuptake of nutrients at the biotrophic interface allowing both partners to, at 759 least partially, control the exchanges (Figure 6).

In agriculture, vast amounts of mineral fertilizers are applied to the field to increase crop yield. Indeed, the amount of applied fertilizer might actually exceed the plant needs for mineral nutrients. Collecting data such as ours will help to further deepen our understanding of the plant-AM symbiosis and may lead to development of a more sustainable agriculture with a reduced fertilizer input and improved adaptation to changing environmental conditions.

1147 Figure 6. Schematic representation of the mycorrhizal nutrient uptake pathway and the 1148 direct nutrient uptake pathway in our model systems poplar and sorghum when colonized 1149 by R. irregularis. In the direct uptake pathway (right hand side) nutrients, i.e. inorganic 1150 phosphate (Pi) and nitrogen (N) are taken up from the rhizosphere by phosphate transporters 1151 (PT) and ammonium transporters (AMT) and are transported to the shoot. In symbiotic 1152 interaction, the AMF partially takes over nutrition of the plant. Nutrients are taken up by 1153 specialized transporters in the extraradical mycelium (ERM) and are further transported to 1154 the intraradical mycelium (IRM) where they are transferred to the periarbuscular space, to 1155 be taken up by plant transporters. N has been suggested to be additionally taken up from the 1156 soil in form of amino acids (AA) by predicted amino acid transporters (AAT). In exchange for 1157 the transfer of the mineral nutrients the mycorrhizal fungus is rewarded with essential 1158 carbohydrates from the plant. As some transporters were specifically induced by 1159 mycorrhization a possible localization at the periarbuscular membrane was assumed for the 1160 plant transporters. High induction of mycorrhizal transporters in the IRM compared to the 1161 extraradical mycelium ERM suggest that these transporters are mainly involved in nutrient 1162 exchange at the symbiotic interface. The reality is probably much more complex, with 1163 reuptake of nutrients (double-headed arrow) at the biotrophic interface, allowing both 1164 partners to, at least partially, control the exchanges.
