# *Arabidopsis thaliana* miRNAs promote embryo pattern formation beginning in the zygote

Alma Armenta-Medina<sup>1</sup>, Daniel Lepe-Soltero<sup>1</sup>, Daoquan Xiang<sup>2</sup>, Raju Datla<sup>2</sup>, Cei Abreu-Goodger<sup>1</sup>, C. Stewart Gillmor<sup>1\*</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Laboratorio Nacional de Genómica para la Biodiversidad (Langebio), Unidad de Genómica Avanzada, Centro de Investigación y Estudios Avanzados (CINVESTAV), Irapuato, Guanajuato, México

<sup>2</sup>Plant Biotechnology Institute, National Research Council, Saskatoon, Saskatchewan, Canada

\*Correspondence. stewart.gillmor@cinvestav.mx

#### ABSTRACT

miRNAs are essential regulators of cell identity, yet their role in early embryo development in plants remains largely unexplored. To determine the earliest stage at which miRNAs act to promote pattern formation in embryogenesis, we examined a series of mutant alleles in the Arabidopsis thaliana miRNA biogenesis enzymes DICER-LIKE 1 (DCL1), SERRATE (SE), and HYPONASTIC LEAVES 1 (HYL1). Cellular and patterning defects were observed in dcl1, se and hyl1 embryos from the zygote through the globular stage of embryogenesis. To identify miRNAs that are expressed in early embryogenesis, we sequenced mRNAs from globular stage Columbia wild type (wt) and se-1 embryos, and identified transcripts potentially corresponding to 100 miRNA precursors. Considering genome location and transcript increase between wt and se-1, 39 of these MIRNAs are predicted to be bona fide early embryo miRNAs. Among these are conserved miRNAs such as miR156, miR159, miR160, miR161, miR164, miR165, miR166, miR167, miR168, miR171, miR319, miR390 and miR394, as well as miRNAs whose function has never been characterized. Our analysis demonstrates that miRNAs promote pattern formation beginning in the zygote, and provides a comprehensive dataset for functional studies of individual miRNAs in Arabidopsis embryogenesis.

**Keywords:** *dicer-like* 1 (*dcl1*), *serrate* (*se*), *hyponastic leaves* 1 (*hyl1*), miRNA, *Arabidopsis thaliana*, embryo, seed

#### INTRODUCTION

In the first few days after fertilization, the *Arabidopsis thaliana* zygote undergoes an almost invariant pattern of cell divisions. These oriented cell divisions generate primordia for the root and shoot meristems, the vascular and ground tissue, and the epidermis (reviewed in Palovaara et al., 2016). microRNAs (miRNAs) are important regulators of cell fate in plants (reviewed in Chen, 2009), but studies of their role in embryogenesis have been limited. miRNAs are transcribed from *MIRNA* genes and processed into mature miRNAs primarily through the action of DICER-LIKE 1 (DCL1), an RNase III-like enzyme that trims the miRNA mRNA (known as a pri-miRNA) into a stem loop (known as a pre-miRNA), and then into a miRNA-miRNA\* duplex (Park et al., 2002). The zinc-finger domain-containing protein SERRATE (SE) and the double-stranded RNA-binding protein HYPONASTIC LEAVES 1 (HYL1) cooperate with DCL1 to improve the accuracy of processing of primiRNAs and pre-miRNAs (Vazquez et al., 2004; Yang et al., 2006).

Functional analysis has revealed two roles for miRNAs in Arabidopsis embryogenesis: regulation of pattern formation, and control of the timing of embryo maturation (reviewed in Vashisht & Nodine, 2014). Mutations in *DCL1* and *SE* produce irregularly shaped embryos

with an overproliferated suspensor; loss of shoot, root, and central tissue identity; and altered auxin transcriptional responses (Schwartz et al. 1994; Grigg et al., 2009; Nodine and Bartel, 2010; Willmann et al., 2011; Seefried et al., 2014). Of the few individual miRNAs that have been studied in Arabidopsis embryogenesis, miR156 regulates cell identity in the hypophysis and embryo proper, and embryo maturation (Nodine and Bartel, 2010; Willmann et al., 2011; Seefried et al., 2014). miR160a promotes auxin responses and early pattern formation (Liu et al., 2010), and miR164 regulates cotyledon formation (Mallory et al., 2004). miR165/166 promote shoot apical meristem (SAM) and cotyledon fate, as well as embryo maturation (Liu et al., 2009; Miyashima et al., 2013; Tang et al., 2012; Williams et al., 2005), while miR394 confers stem cell competence to the SAM (Knauer et al., 2013).

Whole genome transcriptome profiling experiments of *dcl1* embryos have been carried out at the globular (Nodine & Bartel, 2010) and torpedo stages (Willmann et al., 2011). RNA sequencing of globular stage *dcl1-5* mutant embryos revealed changes in the mRNA targets of 10 miRNA families (miR156, miR159, miR160, miR166, miR168, miR319, miR393, miR400, miR778, and miR824), suggesting that these 10 miRNAs are expressed and function in early embryogenesis (Nodine and Bartel, 2010). *dcl1* embryos showed precocious upregulation of seed storage gene expression, demonstrating that miRNAs regulate seed maturation (Nodine & Bartel, 2010; Willmann et al, 2011).

To more comprehensively study the role of miRNAs in embryo pattern formation, we examined the phenotypes of an allelic series of *dcl1* mutants, as well as *se-1* and *hyl1-2*. Our analysis revealed cellular defects beginning in the zygote, demonstrating that miRNAs regulate pattern formation beginning at the earliest stage of embryogenesis. To determine which miRNA genes are expressed in early embryos, we sequenced mRNA from globular stage wt and *se-1* embryos, and analyzed this data along with previously published mRNA sequencing data for wt and *dcl1-5* (Nodine & Bartel, 2010). We identified 39 *MIRNA* genes which significantly increased two-fold or more in *se-1* compared to wt, and whose transcripts are unlikely due to neighboring genes. Of these 39 *MIRNAs*, 16 also increased in the *dcl1* globular stage transcriptome. These results demonstrate that miRNAs promote pattern formation from the earliest stages of embryogenesis, and provide a reference dataset for functional studies of the role of miRNAs in Arabidopsis embryogenesis.

#### **MATERIALS AND METHODS**

**Seed stocks, growth conditions, histology and microscopy** Mutant alleles used in this study are listed along with their ecotype and ABRC stock number: *dcl1-2* (Wassilewskija (Ws), CS84714); *dcl1-3* (Columbia (Col), CS6338); *dcl1-4* (Col, CS16068); *dcl1-5* (Col, CS16069); *dcl1-7* ((L*er*) CS3089); *dcl1-10* (Col, CS16071); *dcl1-15* (Ws introgressed into L*er*, CS68113), *se-1* (Col, CS3257) and *hyl1-2* (Col, SALK\_064863). The nature of the *dcl1-2* allele (also known as *emb76-2* and *sus1-2*) is unknown; this mutation was indentified in a T-DNA mutagenized line, but was not found to be linked to a T-DNA insertion (Schauer et al., 2002). *dcl1-3* (also known as *emb60*, *emb76-3*, and *sus1-3*) was induced by X-ray mutagenesis, and corresponds to a ~20kb deletion that removes the 5' end of the *DCL1* gene (Golden et al., 2002; Schauer et al., 2002). *dcl1-4* (*sus1-4*) was induced by T-DNA mutagenesis, and corresponds to a T-DNA insertion in the PAZ domain of *DCL1* (exon 15) (McElver et al., 2001; Schauer et al., 2002). *dcl1-5* (*sus1-5*) contains a T-DNA insertion in the RNA helicase domain of *DCL1* (exon 1) (McElver et al., 2001; Schauer et al., 2002). *dcl1-7* (*sin1-1*) is an EMS-generated mutation, causing a P415S

substitution in the RNA helicase domain of *DCL1* (exon 3) (Golden et al., 2002; Schauer et al., 2002). *dcl1-10* (*sus1-7*) contains a T-DNA insertion in the RNA helicase domain of DCL1 (exon 2) (McElver et al., 2001; Schauer et al., 2002). *dcl1-15* is an EMS-generated allele, causing a G1692E substitution in the RNAse III domain of DCL1 (Willmann et al., 2011). *se-1* contains a T-DNA insertion in the C-terminal portion of the *SERRATE* gene (Prigge & Wagner, 2001), and is a weak allele (as determined by comparing its phenotype with strong alleles such as *se-3* (Grigg et al., 2005). *hyl1-2* contains a T-DNA insertion in the first part of the *HYL1* gene, and was proposed to be null allele, based on its phenotypic similarity to *hyl1-1* (Vazquez et al., 2004). *dcl1-2* is an allele of unknown strength (Schauer et al., 2002), *dcl1-7* is a weak allele (Schauer et al., 2002), *dcl1-15* is an intermediate allele (Willmann et al., 2011), and *dcl1-3*, *dcl1-4*, *dcl1-5*, *dcl1-10* are strong alleles (Schauer et al., 2002).

Plants were grown in soil (vermiculite: perlite: sunshine mix (1:1:3 v/v/v)) in a growth room at 22<sup>o</sup>C with 16h light (100 µmol m-2 ) / 8h dark cycles. Developing seed were dissected from siliques of self-pollinated *dcl1/+, hyl1-2/+,* and *se-1/-* plants, mounted in diluted Hoyer's Solution (7.5 g gum arabic, 100 g chloral hydrate, 5 ml glycerol and 30 ml water, diluted 1:1 with solution of 7.5 g gum arabic, 5 ml glycerol and 30 ml water), and observed using a Leica DM6000B Nomarski microscope.

**Embryo isolation for RNA sequencing, and computational analysis of RNA sequencing data** Wt Col and *se-1* inflorescences were pruned to remove all but the two oldest flower buds, buds were emasculated, and pollinated 4 hours later. Two biological replicates of ~80 globular stage (32-64 cell) wt Col and *se-1* embryos were isolated at 72 hours after pollination, as follows: ovules were placed in petri-dishes containing 4.8% sucrose solution + 0.1% RNAlater (Ambion #AM7020), in a 35-50ml volume. Embryos were dissected from the base of ovule as in Xiang et al. (2011), gently moved to one corner of the Petri dish without disturbing the solution, then moved again to another clean corner of the Petri dish, then withdrawn in less than 5µl of solution (using a pulled glass pipette) and put in another Petri dish containing 50ml of the same solution. Embryos were washed thoroughly by moving in the solution, and finally pipetted into eppendorf tubes sitting on dry ice. To verify embryo developmental stage and absence of maternal tissue and endosperm contamination, representative embryos were placed inside depressions on glass slides made by using a Mini PAP pen (Invitrogen #008877), and observed using a Leica DMR compound microscope and Microfire camera (Optronics, California).

Total RNA was extracted from embryos using the RNAqueous-Micro kit (Ambion, Catalog# 1927). The quantity of RNA isolated was insufficient for preparation of libraries for the RNA-seq experiments, so the mRNA was amplified using the MessageAmp aRNA kit (Ambion, Catalog# 1750). This aRNA was used for preparation of Illumina mRNA-seq libraries using the TruSeq RNA kit (version 1, rev A). For HiSeq 2000 sequencing, eight barcoded libraries were pooled per sequencing lane (including libraries not described in this manuscript), and 101nt paired-end reads were obtained. After removing adapters, sequences were mapped to the *A. thaliana* genome (ARAPORT11) using HISAT2 (Kim *et al.,* 2015). Libraries for two biological replicates were sequenced, yielding 18,894,161 and 19,362,653 reads from wt Col and 18,580,516 and 20,882,004 reads from *se-1* that mapped to protein coding or *MIRNA* genes of the *Arabidopsis thaliana* ecotype Col genome (ARAPORT11) (see Lepe-Soltero et al. (2017) for details). Gene enrichment analysis to detect contamination of RNA from different seed tissues, using the method developed by Nodine & Schon (2017), showed that both wt and *se-1* libraries were enriched for embryo-proper genes, with insignificant contamination of seed coat or endosperm transcripts (Figure S1). Raw data are available in the Gene Expression Omnibus under record number GSE100450.

Raw RNA seq data for single biological replicates for globular stage wt Col and *dcl1-5* (Nodine & Bartel, 2010) were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (accession GSE25404). Adapters as well as polyA and polyT were removed from the 3' and 5' ends (respectively), flanking Ns and low-quality ends were trimmed from individual reads, and reads with more than three internal Ns or shorter than 18nt were excluded. Cleaned reads were mapped to the ARAPORT11 genome using bowtie1 with parameters -l 25 -n 2 -m 1. 5,153,766 reads from the wt Col library, and 4,047,267 reads from the *dcl1-5* library, mapped to protein coding or *MIRNA* genes. These numbers correspond well with the original analysis of the data (Nodine & Bartel, 2010). See Lepe-Soltero et al. (2017) for full details.

#### **RESULTS AND DISCUSSION**

**miRNAs promote pattern formation beginning in the zygote** Previous functional studies of miRNAs in embryos identified morphological mutant phenotypes in *dcl1* at the dermatogen (Nodine & Bartel, 2010) and early globular stages (Schwartz et al., 1994; Seefried et al., 2014), and in *se* at the early globular stage (Grigg et al., 2009). These results suggested that miRNAs do not participate in pattern formation until several days after fertilization. To determine if miRNAs are indeed required for earlier steps of pattern formation, we analyzed embryo morphology for seven alleles of *dcl1* in the Col, L*er*, and Ws ecotypes. We also examined the effect of *se-1* and *hyl1-2,* both in the Col ecotype.

Our analysis demonstrated that *dcl1, se* and *hyl1-2* phenotypes are observable beginning in the zygote, with penetrance varying between alleles and ecotypes (Table 1). In wild type (wt) embryos, the zygote is polarized, with the nucleus at the apical end of the cell, and the vacuole at the basal end (Figure 1A-B). In some *dcl1, se* and *hyl1* embryos, the zygote loses polarity, showing an abnormal morphology, with vacuoles apical and basal of the nucleus (Figure 1H-I). This phenotype was highly penetrant in plants heterozygous for the weak *dcl1-7* allele, where 49.6% of zygotes showed mutant phenotypes (Table 1). The high penetrance of *dcl1-7* compared to other *dcl1* alleles may be due to the L*er* background: all other alleles are in the Col or Ws ecotype, except for *dcl1-15* which is in a mixed Ws/L*er* background. The nearly 50% segregation of zygote phenotypes in *dcl1-7/+* plants suggests a maternal gametophytic requirement for *DCL1*; a maternal sporophytic requirement for *SE* has previously been shown (Prigge & Wagner, 2001). The high penetrance of *dcl1-7* in the zygote might also be due to a lack of wt paternal allele function at this stage: *DCL1* has previously been shown to have a delay in paternal allele function (Del Toro-De León et al., 2014). The penetrance of *dcl1-7* decreases significantly in the 1 cell embryo, suggesting that the *DCL1* paternal allele is active by this stage (Table 1).

The first division of the wt zygote is asymmetric, producing a small apical cell and a larger basal cell, with an average apical: basal cell length ratio of 0.25 (SD 0.04, n=11) (Figure 1C) (Ueda et al., 2017). In *dcl1, se1,* and *hyl1*, this first division can be more symmetric: phenotypically mutant *dcl1* embryos in the Col ecotype had an apical to basal cell length ratio of 0.56 (SD 0.22, n=7), significantly more than wt (unpaired t-test p=0.0006) (Figure 1J). At the 2-cell stage in wt, the apical cell of the embryo proper divides vertically, and the embryo expands isodiametrically, while the cells of the suspensor divide horizontally and expand in the apical-basal axis (Figure 1D). In *dcl1*, *se1* and *hyl1,* the apical cell of the embryo often divides horizontally, producing two cells with a morphology intermediate between the embryo and suspensor (Table 1; Figure 1K).

Octant stage *dcl1*, *se1*, and *hyl1* embryos show aberrant patterns of cell division and cell expansion, with the embryo proper continuing to exhibit a morphology intermediate between the embryo and suspensor (compare wt in Figure 1E with *dcl1* in Figure 1L); *dcl1- 7* (L*er*) and *dcl1-3* (Col) showed nearly complete penetrance for this phenotype (Table 1). At the dermatogen stage, abnormal cell divisions were observed in the hypophysis (as reported for *dcl1-5* by Nodine & Bartel, 2010) for all *dcl1* alleles, as well as *hyl1-2* and *se-1* (Table 1). At the early globular stage, many *dcl1*, *se-1* and *hyl1-2* embryos show a characteristic 'two-tier' phenotype (Fig. 1N), which can be traced back to the octant stage embryo proper with two elongated tiers (Fig. 1L), and to the horizontal division of the apical cell (Fig. 1K). At the mid-globular stage, wt embryos have vascular primordia, ground cells, as well as the lens shaped cell of the root meristem (Figure 1G). At this stage, *dcl1*, *se-1*, and *hyl1-2* show highly penetrant phenotypes such as vertical divisions of the suspensor cells and hypophyseal cell, extra tiers, misoriented cell divisions in the embryo proper, and the early accumulation of lipid and starch bodies, characteristic of late embryogenesis (Table 1; Figure 1O) (Nodine & Bartel, 2010; Schwartz et al., 1994; Seefried et al., 2014).

The *dcl1-15* allele occasionally produces a twin embryo phenotype (2.5% of embryos at the octant stage; n=203) (Figure 1M). Ovules containing these twin embryos have an unfertilized central cell, suggesting that the second embryo arises from the second sperm fertilizing a supernumerary egg cell, reminiscent of the phenotype of loss of function of the *AMP1* gene (Kong et al., 2015), which has recently been shown to promote miRNAmediated translational inhibition (Li et al., 2013). Twin embryos were not observed at later stages, presumably because lack of fertilization of the central cell prevents formation of the endosperm, which is essential for seed development (Berger, 1999).

*MIRNA* **transcripts from globular stage embryos** Previous experiments used RNA sequencing (Nodine and Bartel, 2010) and microarrays (Willmann et al., 2011) to characterize the mRNA transcriptome in *dcl1* mutants. These experiments did not focus on *MIRNA* profiling *per se*- they inferred the presence of miRNAs by determining the effect of *dcl1* on miRNA-targeted mRNAs. To directly determine which *MIRNA* genes are expressed in early embryogenesis, we sequenced mRNAs from two biological replicates of globular stage wt and *se-1* embryos. *se-1* is a weak allele of *serrate* which slows down processing of pri-miRNAs (Yang et al., 2006), allowing miRNA transcripts to be sequenced using standard mRNA sequencing protocols.

Transcripts potentially corresponding to 100 *MIRNA* genes were identified, and classified based on the location of the *MIRNA* relative to other genes (G1, intergenic region; G2, closest protein-coding gene is 1-400 bp away; G3A, overlap of pre-miRNA with proteincoding gene; G3B, overlap of pri-miRNA with protein-coding gene; G4, *overlaps with noncoding gene*) (Table S1). *Bona fide* pri-miRNA transcripts should increase in *se-1* compared to wt, while *MIRNA* genes whose transcripts decrease in *se-1* compared to wt may be due to reads from overlapping (non-*MIRNA*) genes, or where *MIRNA* transcription is regulated by a second miRNA whose processing is also affected by *se-1*. The G1, G2, G3B and G4 classes of *MIRNAs* were significantly upregulated in *se-1* compared to wt (Wilcoxon Rank Sum test: p < 0.05, Figure S2), with 39 individual *MIRNAs* that increased at least two-fold (Likelihood Ratio test: FDR < 0.001, Table 2). These *MIRNAs* correspond to conserved, characterized miRNAs (miR156, miR159, miR160, miR161, miR164, miR165, miR166, miR167, miR168, miR171, miR319, miR390 and miR394) (Rhoades et

al., 2002), as well as less studied or unstudied miRNAs. We conclude that these 39 *MIRNAs* are highly likely to be early embryo-expressed miRNAs, with confidence correlated to their increase in *se-1* compared to wt. The G3A class of *MIRNAs* (31 genes) (Table S1) did not as a class show a significant increase in *se-1* compared to wt (Figure S2), probably because these *MIRNAs* overlap too much with adjacent genes, and the transcripts detected in our experiment are derived from (or dominated by) the adjacent gene.

Like SE, DCL1 is required for processing of pri-miRNAs, and thus mRNA precursors for miRNAs should accumulate in *dcl1* embryos compared to wt. To test this hypothesis, we reanalyzed an RNA sequencing dataset of one biological replicate for globular stage wt Col and *dcl1-5* embryos (Nodine & Bartel, 2010). We identified 116 *MIRNA* genes with 5 or more reads in *dcl1-5* (Table S2). Only the G1 and G2 class *MIRNAs* were significantly upregulated in *dcl1-5* compared to wt (Wilcoxon Rank Sum test: p < 0.05, Figure S3), perhaps because of the lower depth and read quality (Lepe-Soltero et al. (2017). With a single biological replicate, it was also not possible to perform a reliable statistical test for the significance of transcript changes in individual *MIRNAs* between wt and *dcl1-5*. The 16 G1 and G2 class *MIRNA* transcripts which increased in both *dcl1-5* and *se-1* are shown in Table 2. The similar behavior of these 14 *MIRNAs* in *dcl1-5* as well as *se-1* serves as an independent validation of their expression in globular stage embryos.

We analyzed the levels of miRNA-targeted mRNA transcripts in both *se-1* and *dcl1-5* transcriptomes, compared to wt (miRNA-targeted protein-coding mRNAs are shown in Table S4; see Table S3 for complete analysis of mRNA transcriptomes). Due to the loss of pri-miRNA processing in both mutants, miRNA-targeted mRNA transcripts would be expected to increase. Compared to all mRNAs, we observed a slight, but not significant (p=0.12) increase in the level of miRNA-targeted mRNAs in *se-1* vs wt (Figure S4; see also Lepe-Soltero et al. (2017)). By contrast, miRNA-targeted mRNAs do increase significantly in *dcl1-5* compared to wt (p=3.9e-19) (Figure S4). miRNA targets may not increase significantly in *se-1* because *se-1* is a weak allele for an enzyme that promotes (but is not essential for) pri-miRNA processing, while *dcl1-5* is a strong allele of an enzyme that is absolutely required for pri-miRNA processing (Chen, 2009). In addition, SE is known to participate in mRNA splicing, as well as pri-miRNA processing (Laubinger et al., 2008), while DCL1 is required only for pri-miRNA processing. Thus, *se-1* is likely to have a more complicated effect on the mRNA transcriptome than *dcl1-5*.

#### **CONCLUSIONS**

Our analysis of development in wt, *dcl1, se1* and *hyl1* embryos shows that miRNAs promote pattern formation beginning with the polarity of the zygote, earlier than had previously been observed (Grigg et al., 2009; Nodine and Bartel, 2010; Willmann et al., 2011; Seefried et al., 2014). Defects in vacuole positioning, symmetric division of the zygote, abnormal division of the apical cell, and the 'two tier' phenotype observed at the early globular stage in microRNA biogenesis mutants are also seen in *yoda*, *wox8/wox9, wrky2* and *hdg11/hdg12* mutants (Lukowitz et al., 2004; Breuninger et al., 2008; Ueda et al., 2011; Ueda et al., 2017). However, none of these genes are known to be targeted by microRNAs, and none of them decrease in both *se-1* and *dcl1-5* embryos compared to wt (Table S3). The phenotypes mentioned above, as well as aberrant divisions of the hypophysis, are also observed in mutants affecting auxin transport or transcriptional response (Berleth & Jürgens, 1993; Hamann et al., 1999; Friml et al., 2003). Consistent with this, auxin responses are known to be altered in *se* and *dcl1* mutants at the globular stage (Grigg et al., 2009; Nodine & Bartel, 2010; Seefried et al., 2014). Our analysis identified *MIR160B* (which targets *AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR 10 (ARF10*), *ARF16* and *ARF17*) (Liu et al., 2010); *MIR167A* (which targets *ARF6* and *ARF8*) (Jones-Rhoades & Bartel, 2004); and *MIR390A* (required for production of *TAS3*, which targets *ARF3* and *ARF4*) (Hunter et al., 2006) in globular embryos (Table 2). However, none of these *ARFs* increase or decrease significantly in both *se-1* and *dcl1-5* mutants (Table S3 and Table S4), making it difficult to attribute early *se* and *dcl1* phenotypes to alterations in specific miRNA-targeted *ARFs*. *SPL2*, *SPL3*, and *SPL11* are among the few miRNA targeted mRNAs that do increase in both *se-1* and *dcl1-5* (Table S3 and Table S4). Of these, only *SPL11* has been implicated in early embryo development (Nodine & Bartel, 2010), making *SPL2* and *SPL3* interesting targets for future studies.

Based on previous studies, miRNAs have been proposed to promote pattern formation in early Arabidopsis embryogenesis by repressing premature differentiation (Nodine & Bartel, 2010), or alternatively to directly control cell fate decisions (Seefried et al., 2014). A conclusive answer to this question will require a mechanistic understanding of the specific pathways regulated by miRNAs in early embryos. Our histological analysis of the function of miRNAs in pattern formation, and the miRNA dataset reported here, will be valuable resources for this effort.
