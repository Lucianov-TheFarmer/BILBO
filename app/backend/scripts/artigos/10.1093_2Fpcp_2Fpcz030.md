#### **Abstract**

Upon fertilization in angiosperms, one sperm cell fuses with the egg cell to produce a zygote, and, via karyogamy, the parental genetic information is combined to form the diploid zygotic genome. Recently, analyses with parentally imbalanced rice zygotes indicated that parental genomes are utilized synergistically in zygotes with different functions, and that genes transcribed from the paternal or maternal allele might play important roles in zygotic development. Herein, we first conducted SNP-based mRNA-sequencing using intersubspecific rice zygotes. Twenty-three genes, with paternal allele-specific expression in zygotes, were identified, and, surprisingly, their allele dependencies in the globular-like embryo tended to be biallelic. This suggests that the paternaldependent expression of these genes is temporary, occurring during the early stages of zygote development. Of the 23 genes, we focused on *Oryza sativa Apospory-specific Genome Region (ASGR)-BABY-BOOM LIKE (BBML) 1* (*OsASGR-BBML1*), presumed to encode an AP2-transcription factor, due to its reported role in zygotic development. Interestingly, ectopic expression of *OsASGR-BBML1* in egg cells induced nuclear and cell divisions, indicating that exogenously expressed *OsASGR-BBML1* converts the proliferation status of the egg cell from quiescent to active. In addition, the suppression of the function of *OsASGR-BBML1* and its homologues in zygotes resulted in the developmental arrest, suggesting that *OsASGR-BBML1* possesses an important role in initiating zygotic development. Monoallelic or preferential gene expression from the paternal genome in the zygote might be a safety mechanism allowing egg cells to suppress the gene expression cascade toward early embryogenesis that is normally triggered by fusion with a sperm cell.

**Keywords:** AP2 transcription factor, *OsASGR-BBML1*, Paternal allele, rice, zygote

### **Introduction**

In angiosperms, a seed consisting three tissues, embryo, endosperm and maternal seed coat, is produced by double fertilization (Raghavan 2003). Upon double fertilization, one sperm cell fuses with the egg cell, resulting in formation of a zygote with a 1:1 maternal-to-paternal genome ratio (1m:1p), and a triploid primary endosperm cell with a 2:1 maternal-to-paternal genome ratio (2m:1p) is formed by fusion of another sperm cell with the central cell. These zygote and primary endosperm cell develop, respectively, into the embryo that transmits genetic material from the parents to the next generation, and the endosperm that nourishes the developing embryo and seedling (Nawaschin 1898, Guignard 1899, Russell 1992). Among the three tissues of seeds, the endosperm is known to be markedly sensitive to imbalances in the parental genome ratio, caused by ploidy differences between the parents (Haig and Westoby 1991, Scott et al. 1998, Tiwari et al. 2010, Lu et al. 2012).

In addition to effects on the endosperm, the developmental arrest and abnormal development of embryos have been reported in developing seeds produced by interploidy crosses (Scott et al. 1998, Sekine et al. 2013). However, it is not thought that these abnormalities in zygotic development and embryogenesis are due to a parental genome imbalance in the zygotes/embryos, but rather to a side effect of the developmental defects in the endosperm, induced by the parental genome imbalance. This is because the endosperm functions as a possible growth regulator of the developing embryo and is considered to be of primary importance in seed development (Brink and Cooper 1947,

Vijayaraghavan and Prabhakar 1984, Lopes and Larkins 1993, Berger 2003). Recently, however, we addressed the effects on the imbalance of the parental genome ratio on the development of zygotes by the production of polyploid zygotes with parental imbalance using *in vitro* fertilization of isolated rice gametes and subsequent observation of the developmental profiles of such polyploid zygotes (Toda et al. 2018). The results indicated that most polyploid zygotes with an excess maternal gamete/genome developed normally as diploid zygotes, whereas approximately half to three-quarters of polyploid zygotes with a paternal excess showed developmental arrest (Toda et al. 2018). These paternal-excess zygotes showing developmental arrest could not progress beyond the first zygotic division, although karyogamy was completed normally. Remaining half to one-quarter of paternal excess zygotes could divide into two-celled embryos, and further developed into cell masses without developmental arrest.

These results suggest that the early zygotic developmental steps, from karyogamy to first cell division, are highly sensitive to an excess in the paternal genome, but the developmental steps after the first cell division are tolerant to paternal excess. Analyses with parentally imbalanced rice zygotes indicated that parental genomes are utilized synergistically in zygotes with different functions, and that genes transcribed only, or preferentially, from paternal or maternal alleles might play important roles in zygotic development. As for parental contribution to zygotic development, equivalent parental contribution has been reported in maize (Meyer and Scholen 2007) and Arabidopsis (Nodine and Bartel 2012), since the vast majority of transcripts were detected in near-equal amounts from both maternal and paternal alleles during early embryonic development. Alternatively, it was supposed that the zygotic genome appears to be activated during embryogenesis through 'maternal to zygotic transition' as in animals (Pillot et al. 2010, Autran et al. 2011). Moreover, genes showing monoallelic expression and maternally or paternally expressed imprinted genes have been identified in early embryos (reviewed Zhao et al. 2017), suggesting the non-equivalent parental contributions to zygotic development. Recently, Anderson et al. (2017) reported asymmetric activation of parental genomes occurs in rice zygotes. These contradictory conclusions suggest that parental influence during early embryogenesis has not been fully elucidated. This will be partly due to difficulties in directly researching the biology of female gamete, zygote and early embryo that were deeply embedded in ovaries.

Our study aimed to elucidate the mechanisms involved in the synergistic function of parental genomes/gametes in zygotic development, firstly by identifying genes with monoallelic expression in rice zygotes. Intersubspecific rice zygotes were produced by electro-fusion of gametes from Japonica rice (cv. Nipponbare) and Indica rice (cv. Kasalath), and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) based mRNA-sequencing was performed with those zygotes. Twenty-three genes with monoallelic expression in zygotes were identified. Notably, all 23 monoallelic genes were paternal alleledependent. Moreover, in the globular-like embryo, these genes with paternal allele-specific expression with qualification showed tendency of biallelic expression profiles. This suggests that paternaldependent expression of these genes is temporary, occurring during the early developmental stages of zygotes, and may indicate that the genes expressed from paternal alleles in early zygotes function as

male genome-derived initiation triggers for zygotic embryogenesis. Among the 23 paternally expressed genes in rice zygotes, we focused on a gene, *Oryza sativa Apospory-specific Genome Region (ASGR)-BABY-BOOM LIKE (BBML) 1* (*OsASGR-BBML1),* because OsASGR-BBML1 clusters within the eudicot *ANT* (eu*ANT*) lineage of the *APETALA 2/ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR* (*AP2/ERF*) DNA binding domain family of transcription factors (El Ouakfaoui et al. 2010) and the proteins within the euANT subgroups possess critical roles in many developmental processes such as meristem maintenance, cell proliferation, organ initiation and growth, somatic and embryogenesis, embryo differentiation and root formation (Horstman et al. 2014). *BBM*, a euANT protein, was originally identified as a marker for embryogenic cells in *Brassica napus* microsporederived tissue culture, and ectopic expression of *B. napus BBM (BnBBM)* results in spontaneous somatic embryogenesis in *Arabidopsis* and *Brassica* (Boutilier et al. 2002). *PsASGR-BBML1*, an ortholog of *OsASGR-BBML1* in pearl millet, promotes parthenogenesis in sexual tetraploid pearl millet (Conner et al. 2015), and that transformed rice and maize plants expressing *PsASGR-BBML1* under the DD45 promoter, an egg cell-specific promoter in Arabidopsis (Steffen et al. 2007), produce haploid progenies (Conner et al. 2017). Moreover, recently, Khanday et al. (2019) reported that ectopic expression of *OsBBM1*, alternative gene name of *OsASGR-BBML1*, in rice egg cell under the same DD45 promoter is sufficient to generate embryos in the absence of fertilization, and that *OsBBM1*, acting redundantly with other *BBM* genes, triggers the embryonic program in the fertilized egg cell. However, although *OsASGR-BBML1* has been reported to a possible key factor for induction of zygotic embryogenesis, cellular level investigations addressing how ectopic expression of *OsASGR-BBML1* induces egg cell division and how the progression of zygotic development and division are influenced by *OsASGR-BBML1* function are still lacking.

In the study, using isolated rice egg cells and zygotes produced in vitro, *OsASGR-BBML1* were ectopically expressed and overexpressed in egg cells and zygotes, respectively, and the developmental profiles of these cells were monitored. In addition, using SRDX chimeric repressor system (Hiratsu et al. 2003), putative functions of *OsASGR-BBML1* and its homologous were suppressed in zygotes and the developmental profiles of these zygotes were also monitored.

#### **Results**

# **Development of intersubspecific zygotes produced by reciprocal fusion of gametes from Nipponbare and Kasalath rice plants**

In addition to the production of isogenic rice zygotes using gametes from Japonica rice plants (cv. Nipponbare, NB), intersubspecific rice zygotes between NB and Indica rice plants (cv. Kasalath, KS) were produced by electro-fusion of isolated NB and KS gametes. The zygotes produced by the fusion of a KS egg cell with an NB sperm cell expressing H2B-GFP developed into a cell mass via a fourcelled embryo and a globular-like embryo structure (Fig. 1A). Similarly, the zygote produced by the fusion of an NB egg cell and a KS sperm cell developed normally (Fig. 1B). These developmental profiles were equivalent to those of isogenic rice zygotes produced by NB gametes (Uchiumi et al., 2007), suggesting that intersubspecific zygotes develop normally and can be subjected to further analyses. We previously reported that *de novo* gene expression in isogenic NB rice zygotes produced by electro-fusion, is initiated around 4 h after gamete fusion, during which time karyogamy is mostly complete (Ohnishi et al. 2014). Therefore, isogenic or intersubspecific rice zygotes at approximately 4 h after gamete fusion were subjected to transcriptome analyses.

#### **Identification of genes with monoallelic expression in zygotes**

Using the gametes and zygotes as listed in Supplementary Table S1, cDNAs were synthesized and amplified (Supplementary Fig. S1A), and libraries were also constructed successfully (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Next, paired-end sequencing (100 bp) was conducted using an Illumina HiSeq 2000/4000, and total read bases of approximately 1–6 Gbp were obtained for each of the 16 samples (Supplementary Table S2). The Pearson correlation coefficients between the experimental replicates of egg cells (NB and KS), sperm cells (NB and KS) and zygotes (NB X NB, NB X KS, KS X NB) were 0.80–0.97 (Supplementary Fig. S2), suggesting experimental reproducibility even though a small number of gametes or zygotes were used as the starting material for mRNA sequencing.

To identify genes whose expression is induced in zygotes after gamete fusion, the expression level of the transcripts was compared between egg cells (NB) and isogenic zygotes (NB X NB). 1,126 genes were detected as fertilization-induced genes in rice zygotes (Supplementary Table S3). Next,

SNP calling between the genome sequences of the NB genome and the KS genome was conducted. This analytical procedure distinguished 10,335 genes between the NB and KS genomes by the use of SNPs. Figure 1C shows that among the 1,126 fertilization-induced genes, 349 genes were judged as being mapped precisely on the NB or KS genome. RNA-seq reads for these transcripts were obtained from the data sets of intersubspecific zygotes (KS X NB and NB X KS), and mapped onto the NB and KS genome sequences simultaneously. Next, fragments (read pairs) mapped at each SNP site on the NB and KS genome were counted, and genes containing the fragments, which were preferentially mapped to the maternal or paternal allele in both KS X NB and NB X KS zygotes, were identified. As a result, 49 and eight genes were identified as being preferentially expressed from the paternal allele or maternal allele, respectively (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Table S4, S5). Next, to determine which of these 57 genes displayed highly monoallelic expression, genes whose read fragments to SNPs represented more than five counts in all replicates (two replicates of KS x NB zygotes and two replicates of NB x KS zygotes) were selected. Thereafter, fragment counts on each SNP that were biased to the paternal or maternal allele more than 75% in all four replicates were further selected. As listed in Table 1, twenty-three genes were judged as monoallelic genes, and the average percentage of bias to the paternal genome SNPs of these genes was 88–100% (Table 1). Interestingly, all the genes listed in Table 1 were assessed as being expressed from the paternal allele, whereas no maternal allele-dependent expression was detected in any of the genes expressed in zygotes in this study.

# **Allele dependency and expression profiles of monoallelic genes in gametes, zygotes and globular-like embryos**

Among the genes listed in Table 1, the expression profiles of nine genes were observed by RT-PCR in gametes and isogenic zygotes (NB × NB) since they are supposed to encode putative transcription factors and enzymes possibly involving in metabolisms in phytohormone or phosphate groups, or to express with moderate or high level. The suppression and expression in the egg cell and zygote, respectively, was confirmed for these nine genes (Fig. 2A). Expression in the sperm cell was also detected for the genes Os03g0737300 and Os11g0295900. It is possible that zygotes 4 h after gamete fusion may contain sperm cell-derived transcripts from these two genes. However, amount of sperm

#### Page 13 of 43 Plant & Cell Physiology

cell derived transcripts from these genes can be estimated to be very small in zygotes, since cell volume of zygotes is over 100 times larger than that of sperm cells. In addition, our pervious study showed that zygotic genome activation and *de novo* gene expression have already occurred in rice zygotes at 4 h after gamete fusion (Ohnishi et al, 2014). Therefore, the majority of these transcripts detected in the zygotes was considered to be due to *de novo* gene expression.

We then examined the expression of these nine genes in the intersubspecific zygotes by RT-PCR. Expression of these genes was verified in zygotes from both directions of the fusion (NB egg x KS sperm and KS egg x NB sperm) (Fig. 2B). The amplified DNA bands were further subjected to DNA sequencing to check the SNP profiles, resulting in detection of paternal SNPs in both directional zygotes (Fig. 2C).

Next, the expression profiles of these genes in intersubspecific globular-like embryo structures were observed. The globular-like embryos cultured for two days after gamete fusion consisted of approximately 20–30 cells(Fig. 1A, B; Uchiumi et al. 2007). Expression from seven of the nine genes was detected in the embryo structures (Fig. 2D). The allele dependency of these seven genes in the embryo structures was then identified using SNP information from the amplified DNA bands. Notably, the paternal allele-dependent expression in the zygotes of four genes (Os03g0737300, Os06g0507300, Os10g0386300 and Os11g0295900) was detected as biallelic-dependent expression, while that of gene Os01g0185900 showed maternal allele-dependent expression, in the intersubspecific early globular-like embryos (Fig. 2E; Supplementary Table S6). Only one of the seven genes (Os10g0519800) maintained its paternal allele-dependent expression in the globular-like embryos (Fig. 2E; Supplementary Table S6). One remaining gene (Os01g0584900) displayed cultivar-specific (Kasalath allele) expression in the globular-like embryos, rather than parental alleledependent expression (Fig. 2E; Supplementary Table S6).

#### **Expression profiles of** *OsASGR-BBML1* **in gametes and somatic cells/tissues**

Os11g0295900 is thought to encode a member of the AP2-type transcription factor family and is annotated in the Rice Annotation Project Database (RAP-DB) as *Oryza sativa Apospory-specific Genome Region (ASGR)-BABY-BOOM LIKE (BBML) 1 (OsASGR-BBML1)*

(http://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/). Alternatively, the gene is annotated in Phytozome and Oryzabase as *Oryza sativa BABY-BOOM LIKE 1* (*OsBBML1*) [\(https://www.phytozome.net/](https://www.phytozome.net/)) and *Oryza sativa PLETHORA 6 (OsPLT6)* [\(https://shigen.nig.ac.jp/rice/oryzabase/\)](https://shigen.nig.ac.jp/rice/oryzabase/), respectively. The ASGR (Apospory-Specific Genomic Region) is the physically large and dominant genetic locus in *Pennisetum squamulatum* which confers both apomeiosis and parthenogenesis (Ozias-Akins et al. 1998), and it has been reported that exogenous expression of *PsASGR-BBML1*, a BBM-like protein encoded in the ASGR, promotes parthenogenesis in sexual tetraploid pearl millet (Conner et al., 2015) and can induce parthenogenesis in rice and maize, leading to the formation of haploid plants (Conner et al., 2017). *OsASGR-BBML1* is the rice ortholog of *PsASGR-BBML1*, and three additional genes, Os02g0614300, Os01g0899800 and Os04g0504500 were judged to be homologous to *OsASGR-BBML1* (Supplementary Fig. S3).

*OsASGR-BBML1* (Os11g0295900) displays paternal allele-dependent expression in zygotes (Fig. 2 B, C) and its allele-dependency in globular like embryo is biallelic (Fig. 2D, E). Expression profiles of *OsASGR-BBML1* in somatic tissues were further monitored by RT-PCR. Expression of *OsASGR-BBML1* was barely detectable in leaves and roots and very low in anthers and ovaries (Supplementary Fig. S4). In callus and differentiated embryos, its expression was detected at a moderate level, suggesting that *OsASGR-BBML1* is expressed in somatic tissues, in which cell division and growth actively occur.

# **Effects of ectopic expression of** *OsASGR-BBML1* **and its homologue, Os02g0614300, in rice egg cells**

Expression of *OsASGR-BBML1* is undetected in egg cells (Fig. 2A). It can be considered that the *OsASGR-BBML1* transcript is first delivered into the egg cell from the sperm cell upon gamete fusion, and, in zygotes around 4 h after gamete fusion, majority of *OsASGR-BBML1* transcripts is derived from *de novo* expression from the paternal allele of zygotic genome. These let us suspect that *OsASGR-BBML1* is suppressed in egg cells to prevent the egg cell from developing before fusion with a sperm cell. To address this possibility, *OsASGR-BBML1,* was ectopically and transiently expressed in rice egg cells, and the cellular profiles of these cells were observed. Recently, we established a

gene expression system in isolated egg cells using PEG-Ca2+ transfection with plasmid vectors containing an expression cassette of the gene of interest (Koiso et al. 2017). Importantly, PEG-Ca2+ transfection of egg cells with two kinds of plasmids results in their dual expression in the one egg cell with high efficiency. This dual expression is extremely useful for analyzing the function of the gene of interest.

When egg cells were PEG-Ca2+ transfected only with pGFP-ER, encoding the GFP protein tagged with signal peptide (SP) and the carboxy-terminal His-Asp-Glu-Leu (HDEL) sequence under the 35S promoter and HSP70 intron (*35S::HSP70 intron-SP-GFP-HDEL*), and cultured, a GFPderived signal was detectable one day after transfection as described (Koiso et al. 2017), and the fluorescent signal continued until five days after transfection (Fig. 3A). During culture, the egg cells remained as one cell and gradually degenerated (Table 2). Next, rice egg cells were co-transfected with pGFP-ER and pOsASGR-BBML1 (*35S::HSP70 intron-OsASGR-BBML1*) and cultured. A GFPderived signal was detected one day after transfection (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, at two days after transfection, nuclear division and/or cell division had initiated in several transfected egg cells, and the divided egg cells continued to divide further into cell mass-like structures over the five days in culture (Fig. 3B). Subsequently, the cell mass-like structures from the egg cells degenerated. In total, seven out of 20 egg cells co-transfected with pGFP-ER and pOsASGR-BBML1 (approximately 35% of treated cells) showed division profiles, whereas no division was observed in 14 egg cells transfected with pGFP-ER alone (Table 2).

In addition to *OsASGR-BBML1*, three homologues genes exist on rice genome (Kim et al. 2006, Supplementary Fig. S3), and among these three genes, the expression of two genes, Os02g0614300 and Os01g0899800, have been detected in rice zygotes (Supplementary Table S7). Moreover, it has been reported that these genes, termed as *OsBBM2* and *OsBBM3*, function redundantly with *OsASGR-BBML1* (*OsBBM1*) in zygotes (Khanday et al. 2019). Therefore, Os02g0614300, showing higher expression among the two genes, was ectopically expressed in egg cells. In case of PEG-Ca2+ transfection of egg cells with pGFP-ER and pOs02g0614300 and subsequent culture, GFP-derived fluorescence signals were detectable in 13 egg cells at one day after transfection, and among the 13 egg cells, two egg cells showed nuclear division at 3 days after transfection (Supplementary Fig. S5, Supplementary Table S8).

#### **Effects of functional suppression and over-expression of** *OsASGR-BBML1* **in rice zygotes**

To suppress functions of *OsASGR-BBML1* and its homologues in rice zygotes, we employed chimeric repressor silencing technology (CRES-T) in which a putative transcription factor is fused to an EAR repression domain (SRDX), resulting in dominant repression of the target genes of the chimeric protein (Hiratsu et al., 2003). This technology is useful for analyzing the function of redundant genes encoding positive transcription factors because the activities of related positive transcription factors are simultaneously inhibited by the silencing method. In the study, it was expected that ectopic expression of *OsASGR-BBML1-SRDX* under 35S promoter-HSP70 intron in rice zygotes resulting in loss of function situation of *OsASGR-BBML1* and its homologues.

When zygotes produced by electro-fusion of isolated rice gametes were PEG-Ca2+ transfected with pGFP-ER alone and cultured, GFP-derived fluorescence signals were detectable in two-celled embryos one day after transfection (Fig. 4A). The fluorescence signal remained detectable in multicellular embryos four days after transfection, but was barely observed in multicellular embryos around six days after transfection (Fig. 4A). All the zygotes showing the fluorescence signal at one day after transfection developed normally in the same way as non-treated rice zygotes (Table 3, Uchiumi et al., 2007). This suggests that transient GFP expression from the delivered plasmid in rice zygotes continues at least four days after transfection, and that PEG-Ca2+ treatment of zygotes has little inhibitory effect on their developmental profiles.

 Rice zygotes were co-transfected with pGFP-ER and pOsASGR-BBML1-SRDX and cultured. A GFP-derived signal was detected one day after transfection, and zygotes remained in one cell stages (Fig. 4B). Interestingly, after four days of culture, among 6 GFP-positive zygotes, 5 zygotes were in one cell stage without cell division (Fig. 4B, Table 3). One cell situation continued and the zygotes degenerated thereafter. Only one zygote out of 6 GFP-positive zygotes showed first cell division and development into a cell mass (Table 3).

Next, pOsASGR-BBML1 was delivered into rice zygotes to observe the effect of excess OsASGR-BBML1 on zygotic development. Rice zygotes were co-transfected with pGFP-ER and pOsASGR-BBML1 and cultured. GFP-derived signals were detected in nine zygotes. The developmental profiles of these zygotes, in which endogenous and additional exogenous *OsASGR-BBML1*, expressed from the endogenous gene and the delivered plasmid, respectively, were observed. Among these nine zygotes, three did not divide and gradually degenerated during culture (Fig. 4C and Table 3). The remaining six zygotes divided into two-celled embryos (Fig. 4D). However, interestingly, five out of the six two-celled embryos showed abnormal development, ceasing their development (Fig. 4D), and the only one remaining two-celled embryo developed normally into a cell mass (Table 3).

#### **Discussion**

In the study, 23 genes, whose transcripts were completely or preferentially derived from the paternal allele, were identified in rice zygotes (Table 1), whereas no maternal allele-derived genes were detected. This unequal parental distribution among genes with monoallelic expression in zygotes partly explains our previous observations that excess of paternal genome induced the defect of zygotic development, whereas zygotes with excess maternal genome developed normally (Toda et al. 2018).

In *Arabidopsis*, it has been reported that the timing of activation of the paternal allele in the early embryo changes when the direction of the cross is reversed (Del Toro-De Leon et al. 2014), suggesting that direction-dependent changes in allele dependency might also occur in rice zygotes. Therefore, in the study, we screened genes whose allele dependencies were not affected by the direction of fusion of the gametes. As the results, 23 genes were selected from 349 SNP containing genes that are up-regulated in zygotes (Fig. 1C). Therefore, based on the hypothesis that if SNP information for all 1,126 fertilization-induced genes was available and the allele dependency of these genes could be assessed, we estimate that approximately 70 genes could be identified as paternal genes in zygotes. Identification of additional monoallelic genes in rice zygotes will be possible by using gametes from other rice intersubspecies/cultivars to produce zygotes. Recently, Anderson et al. (2017) conducted transcriptome analysis on intersubspecific rice zygotes obtained from Japonica rice (cv. Kitaake) X Indica rice (cv. IR50) and identified six genes with significant paternal bias, although the bias was confirmed in one cross direction only. The difference in the number of identified genes can be explained, in part, by differences in the preparation of the intersubspecific zygotes. Anderson et al. (2017) used zygotes that were isolated from pollinated flowers [\(Anderson](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/?term=Anderson%20SN%5BAuthor%5D&cauthor=true&cauthor_uid=29112853) et al. 2017), in which the timing of fertilization will vary greatly between each pollinated ovary. In our study, zygotes obtained precisely 4 h after gamete fusion, just after karyogamy, were used for transcriptome analysis.

In the study, several genes with paternal allele-specific expression showed biallelic expression profiles in globular-like embryos, in which three to five rounds of zygotic cell division had occurred. This suggests that the paternal allele-dependent gene expression is a temporal event during the early stage of zygotic development. Notably, the timing of the conversion of allele dependency from paternal to bi-parental appears to correspond to a change in sensitivity to excesses in the paternal

#### Page 19 of 43 Plant & Cell Physiology

genome, from sensitive to tolerant, during zygote development in rice (Fig. 5). The conversion of allele dependency during early zygotic development in rice may be explained by changes in the status of genome DNA modification. It has been reported that a *de novo* DNA methyltransference (*OsMET1*) is known to be expressed in rice zygotes (Abiko et al. 2013). In addition, there are reports that both maintenance and *AtMETs* are expressed strongly in the *Arabidopsis* embryo after fertilization, enabling a limited degree of reprogramming of the epigenome (Jullien et al., 2012). Based on these reports, we cannot exclude the possibility that a proportion of genes, expressed from both parental alleles in embryos, initially display paternal allele-dependent expression in zygotes.

Among the 23 paternally expressed genes in rice zygotes, *OsASGR-BBML1* was further investigated because of its possible involvement in zygotic development (Boutilier et al. 2002, Conner et al. 2015, 2017, Anderson et al. 2017, Khanday et al. 2019). Our ectopic expression experiment provides direct evidence that egg cell division is induced by OsASGR-BBML1 and strongly suggests that exogenously expressed *OsASGR-BBML1* converts the proliferation status of the egg cell from quiescent to active. In addition, our *OsASGR-BBML1* homologue also induced egg cell division. Thus, endogenous expression of *OsASGR-BBML1* and its homologous genes in egg cells will be strictly suppressed to avoid autonomous cell division without fusion with sperm cells.

The expression of *OsASGR-BBML1-SRDX* dominant repressor in zygote resulted in development arrest of zygotes at one cell stage. This suggests that *OsASGR-BBML1* and its homologues are essential for initiating zygotic development and division, being consistent the report that embryo formation was not observed in rice triple mutant in which *OsASGR-BBML1* (*OsBBML1*), Os02g0614300 (*OsBBM2*) and Os01g0899800 (*OsBBM3*) were knocked out (Khanday et al. 2019). In PEG-Ca2+-pOsASGR-BBML1-transfected rice zygotes, an excess of *OsASGR-BBML1* was proposed to exist in zygotes, since endogenous *OsASGR-BBML1* expression also occurs in the cells. Interestingly, in contrast to ou**r** expectation that excess *OsASGR-BBML1* enhances the developmental/division profiles of zygotes and early embryos, over-expression of the protein in zygotes caused an inhibitory effect on the progression of zygotic development. This suggests that excess OsASGR-BBML1 is harmful to embryonic development in zygotes and that the expression levels of *OsASGR-BBML1* in zygotes need to be critically regulated for optimal zygotic development.

Combining these results, we suggest that the expression of *OsASGR-BBML1* in egg cells and zygotes is suppressed and properly regulated, respectively, to allow maintenance of their cell fates.

In our study, more than 20 genes expressed in rice zygotes with paternal allele-dependency were identified and their allele dependency appeared to be transient during early zygotic development. In addition, changes in the expression level of a parental monoallelic gene, *OsASGR-BBML1*, in the egg cell altered its cellular state from quiescent to active. We propose that monoallelic or preferential gene expression from the paternal genome in the zygote is a safety mechanism for the egg cell, allowing it to suppress the gene expression cascade toward embryogenesis that is normally triggered by fusion with a sperm cell. To elucidate the gene expression cascade that is triggered by OsASGR-BBML1 in zygote, investigations for identifying genes whose expression levels are regulated by the transcription factors are underway in our laboratories.

### **Materials and Methods**

### **Plant materials, gamete isolation and electro-fusion of gametes**

*Oryza sativa* L. cv. Nipponbare and *O. sativa* L. cv. Kasalath were grown in an environmental chamber (K30-7248; Koito Industries, Yokohama, Japan) at 26 °C under a 13 h light/11 h dark photoperiod. Transformed rice plants expressing the histone H2B-GFP fusion protein were prepared as previously described (Abiko et al. 2013). The isolation of egg cells and sperm cells from rice flowers, and the electro-fusion of isolated gametes for zygote production were conducted as described (Uchiumi et al. 2006; Uchiumi et al. 2007).

### **Microscopic observation**

Gametes, zygotes and embryo-like structures were observed using a BX-71 inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The fluorescence of H2B-GFP proteins in cells was observed under a BX-71 inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus) with 460–490-nm excitation and 510–550-nm emission wavelengths (U-MWIBA2 mirror unit; Olympus). Digital images of gametes, zygotes, and their resulting cell masses were obtained using a cooled charge-coupled device camera (Penguin 600CL; Pixcera, Los Gatos, CA, USA) and InStudio software (Pixcera).

# **Preparation of lysates from gametes, zygotes and globular-like embryos for mRNA-sequencing or RT-PCR**

Egg cells and sperm cells were isolated from flowers of Nipponbare (NB) and Kasalath (KS) rice plants as described (Uchiumi et al. 2007). Isogenic zygotes were prepared by electro-fusion using an NB sperm cell and an NB egg cell as described (Uchiumi et al. 2007). Intersubspecific zygotes were produced by fusion of a KS sperm cell with an NB egg cell or an NB sperm cell and a KS egg cell as above (Uchiumi et al. 2007). These zygotes were incubated in mannitol solution adjusted to 450 mOsmol kg-1 H2O for 4 h, as described (Ohnishi et al. 2014), since karyogamy in zygotes was assumed to be almost complete and *de novo* gene expression occurs approximately 3–4 h after gamete fusion (Ohnishi et al., 2014).

Egg cells, sperm cells and zygotes 4 h after gamete fusion were washed three times by transferring the cells into fresh droplets of mannitol solution adjusted to 450 mOsmol kg-1 H2O on coverslips. The washed cells were then transferred into lysis buffer supplied in the SMARTer Ultra™ Low Input RNA Kit for Sequencing - v3 (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan), and the lysates were immediately used for cDNA synthesis as described below or stored at -80 ºC until use. cDNA syntheses were conducted using 10 egg cells (NB and KS, three replicates each), 101 and 110 sperm cells (NB, two replicates), 66 sperm cells (KS, two replicates), six and 11 isogenic zygotes (NB egg cell × NB sperm cell, two replicates), six and 11 intersubspecific zygotes (KS egg cell × NB sperm cell, two replicates) and 11 and 12 intersubspecific zygotes (NB egg cell × KS sperm cell, two replicates) (Supplementary Table S1).

For the preparation of lysates from intersubspecific globular-like embryos, zygotes produced as above were cultured in N6Z culture medium for two days according to Uchiumi et al. (2007). Four or five resulting globular-like embryos were washed with the mannitol solution on coverslips and transferred into lysis buffer as described above and used for cDNA synthesis.

#### **cDNA synthesis, library preparation and mRNA-sequencing**

cDNAs were synthesized and amplified using the cell lysates above with the SMARTer Ultra™ Low Input RNA Kit for Sequencing - v3 (Takara Bio) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quality and quantity of the amplified cDNAs were examined by Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and the Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer using a High sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Then, amplified cDNAs were purified using the Agencourt AMPure XP beads kit (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The quality and quantity of purified cDNAs were validated by Nanodrop 2000 and the Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer. Prior to generating the final library for Illumina sequencing, the Covaris AFA system was used for DNA shearing to produce DNA fragments 200–500 bp in length using the Covaris M220 (M&S instrument, Osaka, Japan) (Peak power: 50, Duty: 10 %, Burst cycle: 200, Time: 5 min, Mode: frequency sweeping). RNA-seq libraries were prepared from 10 µl of each sheared DNA sample (5 to 10 ngµl-1) using the Low Input Library Prep Kit (Takara Bio) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After verification of the

#### Page 23 of 43 Plant & Cell Physiology

quality and quantity of the amplified libraries with Nanodrop 2000 and the Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer, the library samples were analyzed by paired-end sequencing (100 bp) using the Illumina HiSeq2000/4000 platforms (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at the BGI-Japan (Kobe, Japan) or Macrogen-Japan (Kyoto, Japan).

# **Calculation of numbers of SNPs/INDELs in transcripts from the Kasalath genome against those from the Nipponbare genome**

For detecting SNP and INS/DEL in transcripts between cv. Kasalath (KS) and cv. Nipponbare (NB), high-throughput sequencing reads from the whole KS genome (DRR013714-DRR013716) (Sakai et al. 2014) were mapped to reference NB genome sequences (Kawahara et al. 2013, Os-Nipponbare-Reference-IRGSP-1.0) by the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (Li and Durbin 2009). Before reference mapping, quality control was performed by FastQC

(https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/), and adaptor sequences were trimmed by Cutadapt (https://cutadapt.readthedocs.io/en/stable/). Low-quality reads were also filtered out as described previously (Ohyanagi et al. 2015). Then, SNPs were called and annotated by SAMtools/BCFtools and SnpEff, respectively (Li et al. 2009, Li 2011, Cingolani et al. 2012). Information on putative transcripts from the KS genome corresponding to NB genome-derived transcripts was obtained from http://rice50ks.dna.affrc.go.jp/, and the numbers of transcripts containing SNPs were estimated by an in-house AWK script.

#### **Analyses of transcriptome data**

As reference genome sequences, the NB genome, (Os-Nipponbare-Reference-IRGSP-1.0) and KS genome (Kasalath; downloaded from http://rice50ks.dna.affrc.go.jp/) were used. For preprocessing of the RNA-Seq reads, we performed adapter and poly-A trimming and quality filtering as described above. The preprocessed reads were aligned to the reference genome sequences by TopHat2 (Kim et al. 2013) without mismatch permission (-N 0). For identification of genes induced in zygotes after gamete fusion, the reads obtained from NB egg cells and NB X NB isogenic zygotes were aligned to the NB genome sequences, and FPKM (Fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped

reads) of each gene were calculated by Cufflincs (Trapnell et al. 2010) using the "-u" option, and then differentially expressed genes were identified by Cuffdiff (false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05) (Trapnell et al. 2013). To identify genes with monoallelic expression, the NB and KS genome sequences were concatenated, and the RNA-Seq reads were aligned to the concatenated genome sequences. The read pairs, which are uniquely aligned at the SNP sites in the genes of both Nipponbare and Kasalath, were counted by in-house Perl scripts. Then, to determine if the counts were significantly different (FDR < 0.05) between alleles, a likelihood ratio test was performed using DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014).

# **PCR using cDNA templates for gametes, zygotes and embryo-like structures, and SNP verification**

cDNAs were synthesized as described above. For PCR, 0.3 µl of cDNA (1 ngμL-1) was used as the template in a 20 µl PCR reaction with 0.3 µM of primers using KOD-FX DNA polymerase (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) as follows: 45 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min. The expression of the ubiquitin gene (Os02g0161900) was monitored as an internal control. Primer sequences used for PCR analyses are listed in Supplementary Table S9. For SNP determination, DNA fragments, which were amplified by PCR using cDNA templates from intersubspecific zygotes and globular-like embryos, were purified using the PCR purification kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) or DNA extraction kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA), and then sequenced. Alternatively, amplified bands were subcloned into pGEM-T Easy vector (Promega, Madison, WI), and plasmid DNAs, isolated from 5–10 transformed *Escherichia coli*, were sequenced.

### **Construction of a phylogenetic tree of BBM-like proteins**

The entire OsASGR-BBML1 protein (RAP-DB) was searched in Phytozome ([http://www.phytozome.net/\)](http://www.phytozome.net/) and BLASTP was used individually with *Triticum aestivum*, *Oryza sativa*, *Zea mays*, *Sorghum bicolor*, *Glycine max*, *Arabidopsis thaliana*, *Brassica napus*, and *Pennisetum squamulatum*. The amino acid sequences of proteins showing score values of > 350 to OsASGR-BBML1 were downloaded. Twenty-one amino acid sequences were aligned, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the GENETYX\_Ver10 software based on the Neighbor-Joining method.

**Fig. 5** Schematic illustration of the progression of the early development of rice zygotes. Through gamete fusion and subsequent karyogamy in the zygote, the zygotic nucleus, in which parental chromatin coexists, is formed. *De novo* gene expression from the zygotic genome is initiated during or after completion of karyogamy (Ohnishi et al. 2014). Thereafter, the zygote divides into a two-celled embryo, and the two-celled embryo further develops into a globular embryo-like structure and cell mass (Uchiumi et al. 2007, Sato et al. 2010 ). Excess paternal DNA in rice zygotes affects the early developmental stage of the zygote between karyogamy and first division, but not the subsequent developmental stage after the first cell division (Toda et al. 2018), suggesting that the early and subsequent developmental stages of zygotes are sensitive and tolerant to an excess in the paternal genome, respectively. The present study indicates that, in early zygotes, dozens of genes are paternally-expressed, and the allele dependency of these genes mostly converts to biallelic in the globular-like embryos. The timing of the conversion of allele dependency from paternal to biallelic might be related to the transition of sensitivity to a paternal excess, from sensitive to tolerant. E, egg nucleus; S, sperm nucleus; Z, zygotic nucleus.
