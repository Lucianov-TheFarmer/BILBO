Running title: Characterization of maize *dek*35 mutant

#### Short summary:

# *Dek35* encodes a PPR protein that affects *cis*-splicing of mitochondrial *nad4* intron 1 and seed development in maize

### ABSTRACT

In higher plants, the splicing of organelle-encoded mRNA involves a complex collaboration with nuclear-encoded proteins. Pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins have been implicated in these RNA-protein interactions. In this study, we performed cloning and functional characterization of maize Defective kernel 35 (Dek35). The dek35-ref mutant is a lethal seed mutant with developmental deficiency. *Dek35* was cloned through *Mutator* tag isolation and was confirmed by four additional independent mutant alleles. *Dek35* encodes a new P-type PPR protein that targets mitochondria. The *dek*35 mutation causes significant reduction of DEK35 proteins, and reduced splicing efficiency of mitochondrial nad4 intron 1. Mitochondrial complex analysis in *dek*35 immature seeds indicated severe deficiency of complex I assembly and NADH dehydrogenase activity. Transcriptome analysis of *dek35* endosperm revealed enhanced expression in the alternative respiratory pathway and extensive differentially expressed genes related to mitochondrial function and activity. The results indicated that *Dek*35 is a new PPR protein that affects the cis-splicing of mitochondrial nad4 intron 1 and is required for mitochondrial function and seed development.

Key words: PPR, splicing, mitochondria, NADH, maize

#### INTRODUCTION

Mitochondria are composed of compartments that carry out essential cellular functions, and widely exist in eukaryotic cells. These organelles convert chemical energy into ATP that could be directly used in cellular activities through oxidative phosphorylation. Apart from basic energy supply, mitochondria have been shown to have multiple aspects of participation in apoptosis regulation, ion transmembrane transport and cell homeostasis (Barry and Bleackley, 2002; Russell and Ley, 2002).

During the long-term evolution process, most of the mitochondrial genes have transferred and integrated into the nuclear genome. Except for a small number of functional proteins encoded by mitochondrial circular genomes, the majority of mitochondrial proteins are encoded by nuclear genes. In addition, new proteins are generated to assist and optimize mitochondrial function. also These nuclear-encoded proteins are highly involved in regulating mitochondrial RNA post-transcriptional processes (de Longevialle et al., 2010). The PPR proteins are one kind of these *trans*-acting factors. PPR proteins exist extensively in eukaryotes (Bentolila et al., 2012). It is predicted that Arabidopsis has more than 400 family members of PPR proteins (Aubourg et al., 2000; Barkan and Small, 2014; Rivals et al., 2006). PPR proteins are defined by the feature of tandem degenerate motifs of PPR repeats (Ban et al., 2013; Bentolila et al., 2010). The classical P motif contains 35 amino acids, forming an antiparallel alpha-helix fold, and its N-terminus is involved in combination with the target sequence (Barkan and Small, 2014; Rivals et al., 2006). There are also the L-motif and S-motif, containing long and short amino acid sequences, respectively. P-type and PLS-type comprise the PPR family (Ban et al., 2013). PLS-type PPR proteins are further classified in E, E+ and DYW subgroups, based on having a different C-terminus (Lurin et al., 2004).

PLS-type PPR proteins have been implicated as functioning in RNA editing (Chaudhuri and Maliga, 1996; Choury and Araya, 2006). The process is characterized by the deamination of cytidine to uridine at specific sites (Chateigner-Boutin and Small, 2014). Genetic and biochemical evidence has shown that the *cis*-element targeted by a specific PPR protein was 5-20 nucleotides upstream of the editing site (Choury et al., 2005; Farre et al., 2001). Several editing sites have been found in plastid and mitochondrial transcripts (Fujii and Small, 2011). CRR4 (Kotera et al., 2005), CRR21 (Okuda et al., 2007), MEF19 (Takenaka

et al., 2010), OTP71 and OTP72 (Chateigner-Boutin et al., 2013) are involved in editing processes in the *Arabidopsis* organelle. In maize, four PPR proteins have been reported as participating in editing processes, i.e., PPR2263 (Sosso et al., 2012), EMP5 (Liu et al., 2013), SMK1 (Li et al., 2014) and EMP7 (Sun et al., 2015).

P-type PPR proteins are likely dedicated to RNA stabilization, cleavage, translational activation, splicing and other RNA processes. RNA splicing in mitochondria needs the coordination of PPR proteins because they have lost the ability to perform self-splicing (Bonen, 2008; Brown et al., 2014). Group I and group II introns are two kinds of introns with distinct splicing patterns. In flowering plants, more than twenty group II introns exist in the mitochondrial genome, requiring either *cis*-splicing or *trans*-splicing. Most introns belong to genes encoding subunits in complex I, also known as NADH dehydrogenase, that transfer electrons from NADH to the primary acceptor FMN and then the likely ubiquinone binding site through Fe-S clusters (Berrisford and Sazanov, 2009; Sazanov and Hinchliffe, 2006). The maize NB mitochondrial genome contains 22 identified group II introns, including 15 *cis*-spliced introns in *nad1*, *nad2*, *nad4*, *nad5*, *nad7*, *rps3*, cox2 and ccmFC, and 7 trans-spliced introns in nad1, nad2 and nad5 (Burger et al., 2003; Clifton et al., 2004). To date, five P-type PPR proteins have been shown to be necessary for splicing of Arabidopsis mitochondrial transcripts, functioning in nad1 (de Longevialle et al., 2007), nad2 (Liu et al., 2010), nad5 (Colas des Francs-Small et al., 2014) and nad7 (Hsieh et al., 2015). In maize mitochondria, EMP16 has been reported to be involved in *nad2* intron 4 *cis*-splicing (Xiu et al., 2016). These studies have indicated important roles for PPR proteins in mitochondrial RNA splicing processes and in maintaining normal mitochondrial function and activity.

In our study, we characterized a maize seed mutant *dek35* corresponding to a defective and lethal seed phenotype. It encodes a novel P-type PPR protein that targets mitochondria. The *dek35* mutant shows the reduced splicing efficiency of *nad4* intron 1 in developing seeds, resulting in the deficiency of mitochondrial complex I assembly and NADH dehydrogenase activity, as well as impaired mitochondrial function and seed developmental delay.

#### RESULTS

#### The *dek*35 mutant displays developmental delay during seed development

The maize *dek35-ref* is a lethal-seed mutant obtained from Maize Genetics Cooperation Stock Center under accession 5605F. The *dek35-ref* exhibits small and flat seeds at maturity, along with an empty pericarp at the top. The mutant phenotype can be clearly identified at 15 days after pollination (DAP) with a white and soft texture (Fig. 1A; 1B; 1C). At maturity, the 100-seed weight of mutant seeds is only 20% that of the wild type (WT; P-value <0.01), suggesting a strong impact on seed development in *dek35-ref* (Fig. S1). The analysis of total starch, amylose and total storage protein contents of mature seeds showed no obvious differences between WT and *dek35-ref*, but the seed oil and zein protein contents were significantly lower in mutant seeds (P-value <0.01).

To investigate the developmental status of *dek35-ref*, paraffin sections of immature WT and mutant seeds from the same ear at 15 DAP and 21 DAP were analyzed. The result indicated that embryo differentiation in *dek35-ref* mutant was delayed compared to the WT. Resin sections of immature WT and mutant seeds from the same  $F_2$  ear at 12 DAP, 15 DAP, 18 DAP and 21 DAP were observed (Fig. S2). The result showed that in early periods, the mutant endosperms were cytoplasmic less dense and exhibited fewer starch granules compared to the WT of the same stage, indicating a much delayed development. However, according to the scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis, the formation of starch granules in mature seeds was not affected (Fig. S3).

The basal endosperm transfer layer (BETL) cells were observed at 15 DAP. In WT, BETL cells showed dense and uniform distributions along the base of the endosperm (Fig. 1H; 1J). However, in *dek35-ref* mutant, the BETL cells were less developed, as indicated by a weak formation of wall ingrowths (WIGs), suggesting a developmental delay (Fig. 1I; 1K).

#### Cloning of the Dek35 gene

 $F_2$  seeds of *dek35-ref* exhibiting 3:1 segregation (482:179, P-value <0.05) were used to make two genomic DNA pools, one from wild type seeds (WT/WT or WT/*dek35-ref*) and one from mutant seeds (*dek35-ref/dek35-ref*). Each was extracted from 40 individual seeds. These two DNA pools were analyzed with a maizeSNP3072 genotyping array (Tian et al., 2015), and the mutant locus was mapped to an interval from 254,244,136 bp to 289,337,516 bp, about 35 Mb on chromosome 1 (maize B73 RefGen\_v3).

Because the *dek35-ref* mutant was initially isolated from an active *Mutator* line, we hypothesized the mutant might be caused by a *Mutator* (*Mu*) insertion. Therefore, *Mu* tags were isolated and sequenced (see Methods). Only one unique *Mu* insertion was found in the mapping interval, and it was inserted at 30 bp downstream of the start codon of *GRMZM2G066749* (Fig. 2A; 2B). Thus, *GRMZM2G066749* was considered as the candidate gene.

In addition, two UniformMu insertion lines (UFMu07168, UFMu08695) were identified that carried a *Mu* insertion at 335 bp (*dek35-mu*) and 929 bp (*dek35-mu2*), respectively, downstream of the start codon in *GRMZM2G066749* (Fig. 2B). Another two allelic mutants (BJ14-87, NJ14-150) were also recovered in our own *Mutator* lines, carrying a *Mu* insertion at 74 bp (*dek35-mu3*) and 386 bp (*dek35-mu4*), respectively, downstream of the start codon in *GRMZM2G066749* (Fig. 2B). UFMu07168 (*dek35-mu*) was used for subsequent allelic test. The F<sub>2</sub> ear of *dek35-mu* generated a similar phenotype to defective seeds (Fig. S4A). The allelic test crosses between heterozygous +/*dek35-ref* and +/*dek35-mu* generated ears exhibiting 3:1 segregation (138:43 and 75:21, respectively, P-value <0.05), that confirmed *dek35-mu* is allelic to *dek35-ref* (Fig. S4B; S4C). The results demonstrated that *GRMZM2G066749* is the causative gene for *dek35*.

#### Dek35 encodes a P-type PPR protein

Sequence analysis of *Dek35* revealed it contains a single exon and a 2,298 bp open reading frame that encodes a putative pentatricopeptide repeat protein. Based on annotation of *Arabidopsis* and rice orthologs in FlagDB data (<u>http://urgv.evry.inra.fr/projects/FLAGdb++/HTML/index.shtml</u>), DEK35 was classified as a P-type PPR protein with thirteen P repeat domains (Fig. 2B).

The *dek35* mutant was caused by *Mu* insertion. RT-PCR was performed to examine the existence of *Mu* element in *dek35* transcripts (Fig. S7). To investigate the effect of *dek35*, we examined the *Dek35* transcript in immature seeds of WT and *dek35-ref* at 12 DAP, 15 DAP and 18 DAP by quantitative RT-PCR analysis. Ubiquitin was used as an internal control. Surprisingly, an increased transcript level was detected at three developmental periods in the *dek35-ref* mutant (Fig. 2C).

To further inspect the expression of DEK35 protein, immunoblot analysis was carried out with DEK35 polyclonal antibody (Fig. S8). Total proteins were isolated from the immature seeds of WT, *dek35-ref* and *dek35-mu* at 18 DAP and analyzed by immunoblot. The alpha-tubulin was used as a loading control. The results showed that the DEK35 protein was significantly decreased in both *dek35-ref* and *dek35-mu* (Fig. 2D). The trace amount DEK35 signal in *dek35-ref* and *dek35-mu* samples was likely due to F<sub>1</sub> maternal tissue contamination when dissected immature endosperm tissue from F<sub>2</sub> kernels. Thus, we attributed the molecular basis of the mutant phenotype to the dramatically reduced DEK35 protein that caused a defect of DEK35 function. The increased *dek35* transcript is supposed to be responsible regulation of reduced DEK35 protein.

## *Dek35* is highly conserved and is constitutively expressed in developing seeds

Full-length DEK35 protein and its homologs were used to construct a phylogenetic tree. The DEK35 protein exhibits high sequence similarity with rice and *Arabidopsis* homologs (Fig. S5). A detailed sequence alignment with sorghum, rice and *Arabidopsis* indicated that DEK35 protein shared highly conserved P repeat domains, especially in monocotyledons (Fig. S6).

We further examined the expression patterns of the *Dek35* gene with quantitative RT-PCR. The results showed that *Dek35* was constitutively expressed in all tested tissues, with a relatively higher expression level in the ear, sheath, silk and stem, as well as seeds at 9 DAP (Fig. 3A). Because *dek35* is a seed developmental mutant, total proteins were isolated from different developmental stages of immature seeds, and DEK35 protein was detected by Western blot analysis with the DEK35 polyclonal antibody. The results indicated that DEK35 protein accumulated throughout seed development, with a peak level at 18 DAP (Fig. 3B).

#### DEK35 targets mitochondria

Based on TargetP analysis (<u>http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TargetP</u>), DEK35 was predicted to have a putative mitochondrial localization signal at N-terminus. To examine its subcellular localization, YFP fusion proteins at the C-terminus of full-length DEK35 protein were expressed by transient transformation of tobacco

leaf epidermal cells (Fig. 4A). The results showed that the YFP signals were detected in small patches, were highly overlapped with mitochondria as marked by the mitochondria-mCherry marker, but were not overlapped with chloroplasts as indicated by the autofluorescence signals. A similar co-localized signal of YFP fused DEK35 and mitochondria-mCherry was also observed in onion epidermal cells by transient expression with particle bombardment (Fig. 4A).

To further validate the mitochondrial localization of DEK35, subcellular fractionation was carried out on immature seeds of 18 DAP. The mitochondrial extracts were isolated and 11 components from every centrifugation were collected for Western blot analysis. The antibody against isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) was used as indicator of mitochondrial fractions. The DEK35 specific polyclonal antibody was used to detect DEK35 protein. The results showed the DEK35 signals were consistently associated with the IDH signals among different fractions (Fig. 4B). These results demonstrated DEK35 is a mitochondrion-targeted protein.

#### The dek35 mutant affects cis-splicing of mitochondrial nad4 intron 1

Previous genetic studies have implicated that a PPR protein may interact with a corresponding mitochondrial or chloroplast RNA. Because DEK35 is a mitochondrion-targeting P-type PPR protein, we first examined mitochondrial transcripts of WT and *dek35-ref*. Total RNA was isolated from developing WT and mutant seeds, which was followed by DNase I treatment. Mitochondrial cDNA was generated by reverse transcription with random primers. We used specific primers to amplify transcripts with cDNA template for all mitochondrial protein-encoding genes (Table S3). Among 35 genes, only the *nad4* mature transcript was absent in dek35-ref (Fig. 5A). To further examine the nad4 precursor transcript, we designed three specific primers to amplify fragments containing each of the three introns in nad4 transcript, respectively (Fig. 5B). The results showed the intron 1 (fragment 1F1R) was almost completely spliced in the WT. In addition, the intron 2 (fragment 2F2R) and intron 3 (fragment 3F3R) were partially spliced and the mature transcripts (1F3R) were observed. However, intron 1 was clearly partially spliced in both dek35-ref and dek35-mu. Unspliced intron 2 and intron 3 were not observed, and the mature transcripts were apparently deficient in both dek35 mutants (Fig. 5C).

Maize mitochondria have 22 group II introns, including 3 *cis*-splicing introns of the *nad4* transcript. To further investigate the splicing alterations in *dek35* mutants, specific primers were designed for quantitative RT-PCR to inspect mitochondrial 22 introns in both *dek35-ref* and *dek35-mu*. First, the differences in splicing efficiency between *dek35* mutants and the WT were compared with amplifying primers across adjacent exons and introns. The results showed the common and dramatic reduction of splicing efficiency of *nad4* intron 1 in both *dek35* mutants (Fig. 6A). Additionally, the quantitative differences in spliced exons between *dek35* mutants and the WT were also compared with amplifying primers across adjacent exons. The results showed the common and single reduction of the *nad4* spliced exon 1-2 fragment in both *dek35* mutants (Fig. 6B). These results indicated the splicing efficiency of *nad4* intron 1 was decreased in *dek35* mutants and suggested that DEK35 is required for the *cis*-splicing of mitochondrial *nad4* intron 1 as well as the formation of the *nad4* mature transcript.

# The *dek35* mutant affects mitochondrial complex I assembly and NADH dehydrogenase activity

NAD4 is one of the subunits composing mitochondrial complex I, the NADH dehydrogenase. Blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN-PAGE) was used to detect the potential impact on mitochondrial complexes in *dek35* mutants. The equivalent mitochondria were isolated from immature WT and *dek35* seeds at 18 DAP, and the enriched mitochondria were separated through BN-PAGE (Fig. 7A). As expected, complex I, as well as the super complex I +III<sub>2</sub>, were dramatically decreased in both *dek35-ref* and *dek35-mu*, as compared to the WT. In addition, we also observed the increased bands of complex III in both *dek35* mutants, which might be explained as responsible regulation that was also reported in previous studies (Cohen et al., 2014; Xiu et al., 2016). An in-gel NADH dehydrogenase activity test was further carried out to inspect the activity of complex I and super complex I +III<sub>2</sub> NADH dehydrogenase activity, indicating that complex I and super complex I +III<sub>2</sub> assembly was severely defective in *dek35* mutants.

Furthermore, Western blotting with specific antibodies against proteins in the oxidation respiratory chain was performed (Fig. 7C). NAD7 (complex I /NADH dehydrogenase subunit) was significantly decreased in *dek35* mutants. COX2 (complex IV/cytochrome oxidase subunit) was slightly increased. No difference was observed in Cyt c (cytochrome c). The defect of NAD7 was in accordance with the observed changes with BN-PAGE. The results suggested a severe defect occurred in mitochondrial function in *dek35* mutants.

## The *dek35* mutant exhibits impaired expression of mitochondrial function related genes

To better understand the impact of *dek35* mutants, RNA-seq was performed with isolated total RNA from WT and *dek35-ref* immature endosperms at 18 DAP from the same  $F_2$  ear. High-throughput sequencing using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform resulted in raw and clean reads for each sample (Table S1). Approximately 30 million reads (~75%) were unique and aligned well to the maize genome sequence (Schnable et al., 2009). Among the total 136,770 maize gene models transcribed in WT endosperm, 43,446 genes were detected in both WT and *dek35-ref*. Among them, significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were within a differential expression threshold: fold change >2.0 and P-value <0.05. Under this criterion, 4,720 DEGs were found.

Among 4,720 genes showing significantly different expression, 632 genes could be functionally annotated (BLAST against GenBank database at <u>http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</u>). Using the Gene Ontology (GO) database (<u>http://bioinfo.cau.edu.cn/agriGO</u>), we found the most significant GO classifications were highly associated with the primary mitochondrial function, including the mitochondrial inner membrane (GO:0005743, P-value=2.2E-07), ATP synthesis coupled proton transport (GO:0015986, P-value=0.00043), protein targeting to mitochondrion (GO:0006626, P-value=2.0E-05) and cytochrome-c oxidase activity (GO:0004129, P-value=0.0024) (Fig. 8A; Table S2). All of the DEGs related to these GO classifications exhibited extensively up-regulated transcriptional expression, indicating the function of the oxidation respiratory chain was greatly affected in the *dek35-ref* mutant. In addition, Golgi membrane (GO:000139, P-value=0.0156), translational elongation (GO:0006414, P-value=6.8E-12) and

protein refolding (GO:0042026, P-value=2.2E-06) showed significantly up-regulated genes. However, genes related to nutrient reservoir activity (GO:0045735, P-value=1.5E-04), such as zein proteins, were expressed with significantly decreased regulation that was in accordance with reduced zein protein contents in mature *dek35-ref* seeds (Table S2).

Usually, the alternative respiratory pathway is initiated when inefficient mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation occurs. To verify the RNA-seq data by quantitative RT-PCR, we selected three genes in the alternative respiratory pathway, i.e., Aox1 (AY059646.1), Aox2 (AY059647.1), and Aox3 (AY059648.1), eight functionally annotated genes corresponding to eight GO classifications respectively, i.e., GRMZM2G072415 (mitochondrial inner membrane translocase complex subunit), GRMZM2G041275 (ATPase F1/V1/A1 complex subunit), GRMZM2G030312 (GrpE nucleotide exchange factor), GRMZM2G173833 (cytochrome c oxidase subunit), GRMZM2G007909 (UDP-galactose transporter), GRMZM2G057535 (translation elongation factor EF1A), GRMZM2G458208 (chaperonin) and AF546187.1 FG002 (zein seed storage protein) (Fig. 8B; Table S2). Among these, GRMZM2G072415, GRMZM2G041275, GRMZM2G030312 and GRMZM2G173833 are highly associated with the electron transport chain and mitochondrial activity. The quantitative RT-PCR results were in accordance with RNA-seq data. This indicated a severe disorder of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and cellular activities in both *dek35* mutants.

#### The dek35 mutant causes abnormal mitochondrial morphology

To further inspect the change in subcellular structure, WT and *dek35-ref* developing endosperms at 18 DAP were collected to perform transmission electron microscopy (TEM). In WT, abundant starch granules and protein bodies were filled in the cytoplasm. Aleurone layer cells were arranged closely filled with oil bodies (Fig. 9A). Mitochondria showed regular structures and were well defined (Fig. 9D). However, in *dek35-ref*, the endosperm cells were significantly cytoplasmic less dense. The starch granules were less abundant than in the WT as well as having a severe lack of protein bodies. Aleurone layer cells became large and were filled with less oil bodies (Fig. 9B; 9C). Some mitochondria were enlarged and were observed to have severe deformations and voided internal structures, and some small mitochondria were observed to have disordered assembly (Fig. 9E; 9F).

These results indicated morphological changes of mitochondria occurred in *dek35-ref*, along with the developmental delay of endosperm cells, suggesting that mitochondrial function was severely disordered in the *dek35-ref* mutant during seed development.

### DISCUSSION

# The novel P-type PPR protein DEK35 affects the mitochondrial *nad4* transcript in maize

PPR proteins are the largest family of RNA binding proteins in plant organelles (Andrés et al., 2007: Lurin et al., 2004: Saha et al., 2007: Small and Peeters, 2000). The splicing effect of P-type PPR proteins has been widely studied in *Arabidopsis* mitochondria, i.e., OTP43 (de Longevialle et al., 2007), ABO5 (Liu et al., 2010), OTP439 and TANG2 (Colas des Francs-Small et al., 2014), SLO3 (Hsieh et al., 2015) and MTL1 (Haili et al., 2016). In the Arabidopsis chloroplast genome, E-subgroup and other PLS-type PPR proteins have also shown the ability to either affect splicing in the Arabidopsis promote or chloroplast genome (Chateigner-Boutin et al., 2011; de Longevialle et al., 2008; Yap et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2015).

In maize, a small number of P-type PPR proteins have been well studied regarding their role in chloroplast splicing. The maize nuclear gene *ppr4* was first reported to encode a chloroplast-targeted PPR protein, which was associated *in vivo* with *trans*-splicing intron 1 of the plastid *rps12* transcript (Schmitz-Linneweber et al., 2006). PPR5 was another PPR protein that was bound *in vivo* to the *trnG*-UCC precursor in maize chloroplasts (Beick et al., 2008). THA8 is a short P-type PPR protein with four P repeats, and it associates with introns of chloroplast *ycf3-2* and *trnA* in maize (Khrouchtchova et al., 2012). Nevertheless, a few of the PPR proteins were found to be involved in maize mitochondrial RNA processing. In the maize mitochondrial genome, EMP16 is specifically required for intron 4 *cis*-splicing of the *nad2* transcript and has far-reaching consequences for mitochondrial complex I assembly (Xiu et al., 2016).

To date, no PPR proteins have been found to participate in splicing of the mitochondrial *nad4* transcript. In this study, we identified a novel P-type PPR protein, DEK35, in maize that affects the *cis*-splicing of the *nad4* intron 1. The

maize dek35-ref is a defective and lethal seed mutant caused by Mutator transposon (Fig. 1&2). The mutant showed significantly decreased DEK35 protein and up-regulated *dek35* transcript (Fig. 2). The increased *dek35* transcript was not caused by developmental delay, according to the Dek35 expression data (Fig. 3). The splicing efficiency of the nad4 intron 1 was affected in both dek35-ref and dek35-mu mutants, and this alteration of splicing efficiency was exclusive in all mitochondrial 22 group II introns (Fig. 6). Interestingly, although splicing of the nad4 intron 1 was not completely abolished in dek35 mutants, the mature nad4 transcript decreased dramatically, which was probably due to a loss of stabilization resulting from a splicing defect (Fig. 5). This proposed a possible correlation that splicing processes affect the stability of the mature transcript. Further analysis of mitochondrial complexes showed significant deficiency of complex I and super complex  $I + III_2$ , suggesting a severe defect occurred in mitochondrial function in dek35 mutants as a consequence of a deficient nad4 transcript (Fig. 7). The functional characterization of DEK35 enlarges the list of P-type PPR proteins acting in organelle RNA metabolism as a splicing factor in maize.

## Post-transcriptional processing of the *nad4* transcript is crucial for mitochondrial function

NADH dehydrogenase is the major entrance for transporting electrons in plants (Braun et al., 2014). NAD4 belongs to one of nine mitochondria-encoding subunits (NAD1-4, NAD4L, NAD5-7 and NAD9) constituting NADH dehydrogenase (Clifton et al., 2004). Studies have implicated that the diverse RNA processing of the *nad4* transcript involved different types of nuclear-encoded proteins, i.e., MTSF1 (Haili et al., 2013), RPF1 (Holzle et al., 2011), AHG11 (Murayama et al., 2012) and CSS1 (Nakagawa and Sakurai, 2006). These studies have suggested severe impacts caused by the faulty operating processes of the *nad4* transcript, indicating *nad4* is playing a crucial role in mitochondrial function.

DEK35 is the first studied PPR protein that affects splicing of the *nad4* transcript. In *dek35* mutants, *nad4* intron 1 exhibits affected and reduced splicing processes, resulting in a severe deficiency of mitochondrial assembly and activity as well as seed development. Our study suggested splicing of the *nad4* transcript is

another crucial post-transcriptional process for maintaining normal mitochondrial function.

In *dek35* mutant, the splicing of *nad4* intron 1 was not completely abolished, indicating the splicing process of *nad4* intron 1 might also involve other proteins. For instance, the *nms1* mutant is the nuclear *ms1* recessive mutation in *Nicotiana sylvstris*, that causes defective vegetative and reproductive development resulting in male sterile. The defective splicing of *nad4* intron 1 was also observed in the *nms1* mutant (Brangeon et al., 2000). Therefore, NMS1 might be another factor involved in the splicing of *nad4* intron 1.

#### Mitochondrial function defects significantly affect seed development

PPR proteins have shown similar influences on seed development in maize. PPR2263 is one of the early studied PPR proteins involved in RNA editing and maize seed development. The *ppr2263* mutant lacking mitochondrial complex III causes increased mitochondrial transcript levels and induction of an alternative respiratory pathway, leading to reduced embryo and endosperm growth and small seeds (Sosso et al., 2012). A similar result of increased mitochondrial transcripts was observed in *dek35* mutants (Fig. 8). By RNA-seq with *dek35-ref*, 4,326 genes were up-regulated among 4,720 DEGs, accounting for 91.7%. The large amount of transcriptionally up-regulated genes is not caused by developmental delay, based on the expression data for developing maize seeds (Chen et al., 2014).

Among eight GO classifications, four GO classifications that were highly related to mitochondrial structure and activity exhibited extensive up-regulated DEGs (Table S2). This result indicated that oxidation respiratory chain was severely disrupted in *dek35* mutant. Besides, we also found commonly up-regulated DEGs corresponding to other important cellular processes, including the GO classifications "Golgi membrane", "translational elongation" and "protein refolding" (Table S2). "Golgi membrane" includes major subunits of coatomer and clathrin. These proteins shape membrane-bounded vesicles and mediate intracellular transport, which consuming energy produced by mitochondria (Cosson and Letourneur, 1997; Pearse, 1976). "Translational elongation" and "protein refolding" contain structural subunits of ribosomal proteins, translation elongation and initiation factors, and molecular chaperones, which functioning also under

mitochondrial activity (Frank and Agrawal, 2000; Kusmierczyk and Martin, 2003). Therefore, it was naturally expected that these transcriptional regulation was resulted from the primary defect of energy production in mitochondria.

Apart from seven up-regulated GO classifications, we found only one GO classification "nutrient reservoir activity" showed decreased DEGs (Table S2). Immature *dek35* seeds showed delayed zein protein development (Fig. 9). The mature *dek35* seeds exhibited significantly decreased seed dry weight and zein protein contents (Fig. S1). These phenotype data were in accordance with the decreased DEGs in transcriptome analysis (Table S2). These results demonstrated the expression of zein proteins was significantly affected in *dek35* mutant compared to WT, and resulted in developmental delay of zein proteins sythesis and assembly of protein bodies. The result showed the great necessity of mitochondrial activity for the generation and accumulation of nutrients (Law et al., 2014).

In maize seed endosperm, BETL cells are distributed in the basal surface and are responsible for material transportation and secretion (Offler et al., 2003; Charlton et al., 1995). Previous studies have indicated the transport in BETL cells are energy-dependent process, and defective BETL cells would result in reduced endosperm development (Thompson et al., 2001). In *dek35-ref*, the reduced BETL cell development was observed clearly in 15 DAP exhibiting a weak formation of WIGs, leading to delayed seed development which was further validated by phenotype analysis of mature seeds (Fig. 1&S1). Therefore, the reduced BETL cell development in *dek35-ref* mutant might be the consequence of reduced transporting demand due to lack of energy.

Several PPR proteins have also shown a similar effect on seed development from restricted BETL cells caused by affected mitochondria, i.e., EMP5 (Liu et al., 2013), EMP16 (Xiu et al., 2016), and MPPR6 (Manavski et al., 2012). Although these PPR proteins were characterized by distinct functions, all of these mutants exhibit weak growth of BETL cells, further triggering arrested seed development and generating lethal homozygous mutant seeds with a similar and defective seed phenotype. These results suggested BETL cell growth needs normal functioning mitochondria and also demonstrated that BETL cells are important for seed development.

### **METHODS**

#### **Plant materials**

The 5605F and UFMu07168 mutant lines were obtained from the Maize Genetics Cooperation Stock Center. The wild type seeds of the provided seeds were self-crossed directly to generate the segregating F<sub>2</sub> population. The mature seeds were used for genomic DNA extraction in liquid nitrogen using the phenol-chloroform extraction method. The immature seeds were harvested for RNA and protein extraction, paraffin and resin section preparation, as well as TEM observation and RNA-seq. All plants were cultivated in the field on the campus of Shanghai University.

#### SNP genotyping array and *Mu* tag isolation

Genomic DNA of mature  $F_2$  seeds were extracted and two equivalent genomic DNA pools were prepared. One was from mutant seeds (*dek35-ref/dek35-ref*) and the other was from wild type seeds (WT/WT and WT/*dek35-ref*). Crude genomic DNA was second-extracted with a Qiagen DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Lot. 69104) and quantified by NanoDrop ND-1000 (Thermo Scientific, USA). Based on the segregant ratio of 3:1, 750 ng of WT genomic DNA was mixed with 250 ng of mutant genomic DNA to prepare the total 1 µg  $F_1$  sample. Another 1 µg of mutant genomic DNA was prepared as the mu sample. Both  $F_1$  and mu electrophoresis was performed with a 1% agarose gel (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd, China) at 120 V for 30 min to check the DNA quality. SNP genotyping and data processing were carried out with an Illumina<sup>®</sup> MaizeSNP50 and GenomeStudio<sup>®</sup> Genotyping Module per the instructions of the manufacturer.

For *Mu* tag isolation, genomic DNA was prepared for mechanical shearing, adapter ligation and hybridization to a biotinylated oligonucleotide as described previously (Williams-Carrier et al., 2010).

#### Light microscopy, TEM and SEM

For light microscopy analysis, immature WT and *dek35-ref* seeds at 12 DAP, 15 DAP, 21 DAP and 24 DAP were collected from the same ear and were cut along the longitudinal axis for paraffin and resin section preparation and along horizontal axis for resin section preparation. The sections were fixed in a formalin–acetic

acid–alcohol mixture and were dehydrated in an ethanol gradient series of 50%, 60%, 70%, 85%, 95% and 100% ethanol. After replacement of acetone and infiltration with paraffin or resin, the sections were embedded and cut using RM2265 (Leica). The paraffin section was stained by fuchsin basic, and the resin section was stained by fuchsin basic (horizontal cut) and toluidine blue (longitudinal cut). The sections were observed using Leica DFC500 at Shanghai University.

For TEM analysis, immature WT and *dek35-ref* seeds at 21 DAP were collected from the same ear and were cut along the horizontal axis into small pieces and fixed in paraformaldehyde. The samples were further processed and stained by osmic acid and observed at Shanghai Normal University. For SEM analysis, mature WT and *dek35-ref* seeds were treated with vacuum drying after a horizontal axis cut and were observed using JSM-6700F (JEOL) at Shanghai University.

#### Protein quantification

Mature seeds of the WT and *dek35-ref* were soaked in water, and the endosperm was separated from the embryo and pericarp. Endosperm samples were critically dried to a constant weight, powdered in liquid nitrogen, and measured as described previously (Wang et al., 2011). Proteins were extracted from 50 mg of two pooled floury endosperm samples and measured using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Pierce) according to the instructions. Measurements of all samples were replicated three times.

#### Measurement of starch

Mature seeds of the WT and *dek35-ref* were soaked in water, and the endosperm was separated from the embryo and pericarp. Endosperm samples were dried to a constant weight and starch was extracted and measured using an amyloglucosidase/alpha-amylase method starch assay kit (Megazyme) according to the manufacturer's instructions and adapted as follows: 0.2 mL of aqueous ethanol (80% [v/v]) was added to a 100 mg sample to aid dispersion; immediately thereafter, 3 mL of thermostable alpha-amylase was added. The tube was incubated in a boiling water bath for 6 min, then placed in a bath at 50°C, to which 0.1 mL of amyloglucosidase was added. The tube was stirred with a vortex mixer and incubated at 50°C for 30min. Duplicate aliquots (0.1 mL) of the diluted solution were transferred to the bottom; 3.0 mL of GOPOD reagent was added, and the

tubes were incubated at 50°C for 20 min. The absorb ance for each sample and the D-Glc control at 510 nm was read against the reagent blank.

#### Measurement of oil

About 50 mature seeds of the WT and *dek35-ref* were dried at 45°C for 60 h, and then were grounded with a high speed disintegrator. About 0.2~0.3 g seed powder was added with a 4 mL mixture of methanol and acetyl chloride at a 10:1 volume ratio, 5 mL C19:0 internal standard, and then was incubated in a water bath at 80°C for 2 h. After cooling, 5 mL of 7% K<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> was added. The supernatant was injected into a Shimadzu GC-2014 gas chromatograph with a standard manipulating procedure.

#### Subcellular localization of DEK35

The DNA sequence of full-length DEK35 protein without the stop codon was cloned into pSAT6-EYFP-N1 (vector data are available at <u>http://www.arabidopsis.org/index.jsp</u>). The recombinant plasmid was extracted with ~1 µg total amount and then was introduced into onion epidermal cells and tobacco leaf epidermal cells through transient transformation using the Bio-Rad PDS-1000/HeTM biolistic particle delivery system. The fluorescence signals were detected using LSM710 (Occult International Ltd.). The mitochondria-mCherry marker was from a collection of multicolored organelle markers.

#### **RNA extraction and qRT-PCR**

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Tiangen), and DNA was removed by a treatment with RNase-Free DNase I (Takara). Using ReverTra Ace reverse transcriptase (Toyobo), RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the attached random primers. Amplification of mitochondrial transcripts was performed using primers as described previously (Liu et al., 2013). Quantitative RT-PCR of *dek35* were performed to three independent RNA samples sets with ubiquitin as the reference gene. Quantitative RT-PCR primers of mitochondrial introns were designed as described previously with some modifications (Table S3) (Haili et al., 2013). The primer designs and the examination of *Aox* genes relative quantifiable differences in gene expression were analyzed as described previously (Li et al., 2014). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green Real-Time PCR Master Mix (Toyobo) using a Mastercycler ep realplex 2 (Eppendorf) according to the standard protocol.

#### **Polyclonal antibodies**

For antibody production, the DNA sequence corresponding to the 488-765 amino acids of DEK35 was cloned into pET-28a expression vector (Amersham Biosciences). Protein expression was followed by established procedures. For production of polyclonal antibody against NAD7, the first 21 amino acids were synthesized. Peptide synthesis, protein purification and production of antibodies in rabbits were performed according to standard protocols of Shanghai ImmunoGen Biological Technology.

#### Immunoblot analysis

Proteins extracted from developing WT and *dek35* seeds were separated by SDS-PAGE. Separated protein samples were then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (0.45mm; Millipore). The membrane with the protein sample attached was incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. Using the Super Signal West Pico chemiluminescent substrate kit (Pierce), the signal was visualized according to the manufacturer's instructions. The polyclonal antibody against DEK35 and NAD7 were used at 1:500. The antibodies against COX2 (Agrisera) was used at 1:1,000. The antibodies against Cyt c (Agrisera) and alpha-tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich) were used at 1:5,000.

#### Isolation of mitochondria for BN-PAGE and DEK35 localization

For BN-PAGE, about 15 g immature seeds at 18 DAP were harvested and were grounded with a mortar and pestle in liquid nitrogen, adding 20ml extraction buffer (100 mM tricine, 300 mM sucrose, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 1 mM EDTA-K, 0.1% BSA, 5 mM DTT, pH7.4) and 60  $\mu$ L plant protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). The samples were centrifuged twice at 2,600 g for 15 min after filtration through a Miracloth membrane (Calbiochem); the supernatant was then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 25 min to pellet the crude mitochondria. The pellet was resuspended in wash buffer (100 mM tricine, 300 mM sucrose, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 1 mM EDTA-K, 0.1% BSA, pH7.4) and was loaded on sucrose density gradients of 1.5 mL, 2.5 mL,

2.5 mL, 2 mL and 2 mL containing, respectively, 1.8 M, 1.45 M, 1.2 M, 0.9 M and 0.6 M of sucrose diluted in wash buffer. After 90 min of centrifugation at 24,000 rpm at 4 $^{\circ}$ , the mitochondria were collected from the 1.2 M/1.45 M interface and diluted 4 times in wash buffer. The enriched mitochondria were collected after 20 min of centrifugation at 12,000 rpm at 4 $^{\circ}$ . For DEK35 loca lization, mitochondria were extracted from immature seeds at 18 DAP as described previously (Uyttewaal et al., 2008).

#### BN-PAGE and NADH dehydrogenase activity examination

The enriched mitochondria were resuspended in 50  $\mu$ L B25G20 solution (25 mM Bis-Tris, 20% glycerin, pH7.0), adding 20% DDM to a final concentration of 1% DDM, and were gently mixed on ice for 1 h. After 15 min of centrifugation at 12,000 rpm at 4°C, the supernatant was collected and was a dded to the loading buffer for blue native polyacrylamide electrophoresis. The concentration of the separation gel was from 5% to 13.5%. At first electrophoresis was run at 50 V, and 25 V was added every 20 min to the final 150 V until the loading dye migrated to the edge of the gel. The gel was stained by coomassie brilliant blue. Measurement of NADH dehydrogenase activity was performed as described previously (Meyer et al., 2009).

### **RNA-seq analysis**

Total RNA was extracted from the developing WT and *dek35-ref* seeds at 18 DAP. Library construction was performed according to Illumina standard instructions. Reads were aligned to the maize B73 genome using TopHat2 (Langmead et al., 2009). Data were normalized as fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped, as the sensitivity of RNA-seq depends on the transcript length. RNA-seq data are available from the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus (<u>http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo</u>) under the series entry GSE79583.
