# The quest for epigenetic regulation underlying unisexual fower development in *Cucumis melo*

David Latrasse1 , Natalia Y. Rodriguez‑Granados1†, Alaguraj Veluchamy2†, Kiruthiga Gayathri Mariappan2 , Claudia Bevilacqua3 , Nicolas Crapart3 , Celine Camps1 , Vivien Sommard<sup>1</sup> , Cécile Raynaud1 , Catherine Dogimont4 , Adnane Boualem1 , Moussa Benhamed1,2 and Abdelhafd Bendahmane1\*

## **Abstract**

**Background:** Melon (*Cucumis melo*) is an important vegetable crop from the *Cucurbitaceae* family and a reference model specie for sex determination, fruit ripening and vascular fuxes studies. Nevertheless, the nature and role of its epigenome in gene expression regulation and more specifcally in sex determination remains largely unknown.

**Results:** We have investigated genome wide H3K27me3 and H3K9ac histone modifcations and gene expression dynamics, in fve melon organs. H3K9ac and H3K27me3 were mainly distributed along gene-rich regions and con‑ strained to gene bodies. H3K9ac was preferentially located at the TSS, whereas H3K27me3 distributed uniformly from TSS to TES. As observed in other species, H3K9ac and H3K27me3 correlated with high and low gene expression levels, respectively. Comparative analyses of unisexual fowers pointed out sex-specifc epigenetic states of TFs involved in ethylene response and fower development. Chip-qPCR analysis of laser dissected carpel and stamina primordia, revealed sex-specifc histone modifcation of MADS-box genes. Using sex transition mutants, we demonstrated that the female promoting gene, *CmACS11*, represses the expression of the male promoting gene *CmWIP1* via deposition of H3K27me3.

**Conclusions:** Our fndings reveal the organ-specifc landscapes of H3K9ac and H3K27me3 in melon. Our results also provide evidence that the sex determination genes recruit histone modifers to orchestrate unisexual fower develop‑ ment in monoecious species.

Besides melon, the *Cucurbitaceae* family comprises several important vegetable crops, such as cucumber (*C. sativus*), watermelon (*Citrullus lanatus*), squash and pumpkin (*Cucurbita* spp.), and many neglected cultivated species that are major food crops in many developing countries. At the chromatin level, DNA conformation can be modifed by the addition or removal of chemical groups at specifc N-terminal residues of histones [[8,](#page-14-4) [9\]](#page-14-5). Some of these modifcations are in charge of genome connectivity and heterochromatin stability, while others regulate gene expression or repression along the euchromatic regions. Te distribution and abundance of these marks, at the gene and genome-wide scales, defne an epigenetic landscape that directs cell diferentiation and identity [[10–](#page-14-6)[13](#page-15-0)]. In the plant kingdom, genome-wide analyses of epigenetic landscapes have been performed in several species including *Arabidopsis* [[14–](#page-15-1)[17\]](#page-15-2), maize [[18](#page-15-3)[–20](#page-15-4)], rice [\[21](#page-15-5)[–23](#page-15-6)], common bean [[24\]](#page-15-7) and barley [\[25](#page-15-8)].

Histone methylation and acetylation are epigenetics marks that are usually associated with various biological processes ranging from transcriptional regulation to epigenetic silencing via heterochromatin assembly. While histone acetylation is commonly associated with transcription activation, histone methylation can either promote or inhibit gene transcription [[5,](#page-14-7) [11](#page-15-9), [18](#page-15-3), [26](#page-15-10)[–29](#page-15-11)]. Histone H3 lysine 9 acetylation (H3K9ac) is an epigenetic mark commonly found in active promoters [\[5](#page-14-7), [20](#page-15-4), [30,](#page-15-12) [31](#page-15-13)]. Genome-wide studies in *Arabidopsis* have found around 5200 non-Transposable Element (TE)-related genes that are targeted and regulated by H3K9ac [[31](#page-15-13)]. In contrast, H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) is commonly associated with gene repression, thereby contributing to the mitotic heritability of PRC2 (Polycomb repressive complex)-mediated silencing of numerous developmental and homeotic genes [\[29](#page-15-11), [32](#page-15-14), [33\]](#page-15-15). In the plant kingdom, the study of these epigenetic marks has elucidated the epigenetic component regulating plant growth and adaptation to their harsh environment [[34–](#page-15-16)[44](#page-15-17)].

Te majority of angiosperms are hermaphrodite producing exclusively bisexual fowers. Sex determination is a developmental evolutionary process that leads to unisexual fowers. Monoecious species, such as melon, exhibit male and female fowers on the same plant. Dioecious species have separate male and female individuals [[45,](#page-15-18) [46](#page-15-19)].

In melon, foral primordia are initially bisexual with sex determination occurring by the selective developmental arrest of either the stamen or the carpel primordia, resulting in unisexual fowers [[47](#page-15-20), [48\]](#page-15-21). Tis sexual organ arrest is genetically governed by the interplay of alleles of the *andromonoecious* (*M*), *androecious* gene (*A*) and *gynoecious* (*G*) genes [[47](#page-15-20), [49](#page-15-22)[–51](#page-15-23)]. Te cloning and characterization of *M*, *G* and *A* genes have shown that the *gynoecious* (*G*) gene encodes a zinc fnger transcription factor, *CmWIP1* [\[47](#page-15-20)], the andromonoecious (*M*) gene and the androecious gene (*A*) encode ethylene biosynthesis enzymes: CmACS-7 [\[49](#page-15-22)] and CmACS11 [[51](#page-15-23)], respectively. Genetic analysis revealed a mechanistic model in which expression of the carpel inhibitor, *CmWIP1*, is dependent on non-expression of *CmACS11* and expression of the stamina inhibitor, *CmACS*-*7*, is dependent on non-expression of *CmWIP1*. In monoecious plants, male fowers result from non-expression of *CmACS11* that permits *CmWIP1* expression. Female fowers develop on branches expressing *CmACS11*, which represses the expression of *CmWIP1*, and thus, releasing the expression of *CmACS7* inhibiting stamina development [[51–](#page-15-23)[53\]](#page-15-24).

Now that the identity of the androecy, monoecy and gynoecy sex genes are revealed and genetic pathway controlling sex determination in *Cucurbitaceae* discovered, among the next challenges is to decipher how the sex determination signals are perceived and how the information is translated to cause organ-specifc abortion at the fower and the plant level. Several studies have reported a wide-range of epigenetic processes and environmental cues that determine sex in animals [[54](#page-15-25)[–57](#page-15-26)]. In plants, it is well known that sex ratio and determination can be infuenced by diferent environmental factors such as day length and light intensity [\[58\]](#page-15-27), water restriction [[59\]](#page-16-0) and some plant hormones such as ethylene [\[49](#page-15-22)[–51,](#page-15-23) [60](#page-16-1)[–63\]](#page-16-2), auxin [\[64](#page-16-3)] and gibberellins [[65](#page-16-4)[–67](#page-16-5)]. Tis multifactorial regulation of sex determination is still poorly characterized from the epigenetic point of view, where limited literature have reported the association for instance of small RNA (sRNAs) transcription and DNA hypomethylation with sex determination [\[54](#page-15-25), [68–](#page-16-6)[71](#page-16-7)].

In melon, the epigenetic control of sex determination remains poorly characterized and is mainly attributed to a transposon-induced DNA methylation that leads to gynoecy [[47\]](#page-15-20). To gain new information on the epigenetic control of sex determination, we have examined the genome-wide landscape of H3K27me3 and H3K9ac histone modifcations and gene expression dynamics, in fve melon organs, focusing our analysis on unisexual fowers. Te combination of epigenomic and gene ontology analysis pointed out sex-specifc epigenetic states of TFs involved in ethylene response and sexual organs development. Furthermore, using sex transition mutants, we demonstrated that the female promoting gene represses the expression of the male promoting genes via deposition of H3K27me3.

#### **Results**

### **Genome‑wide landscape of H3K9ac and H3K27me3 in melon**

Genome-wide studies in *Arabidopsis* [[14–](#page-15-1)[17\]](#page-15-2), and other plant species such as maize [\[19,](#page-15-28) [20](#page-15-4)], rice [\[21](#page-15-5)[–23\]](#page-15-6), and barley [\[25](#page-15-8)], have evidenced the important role of H3K9ac and H3K27me3 in gene activation and repression, respectively. Te roles of these histone modifcations in melon development remain unknown and represent a limit to fully understand how thousands of bioprocesses are regulated. To determine the genomic landscape and the organ specifcity of those marks, we performed ChIPseq analyses using H3K27me3 and H3K9ac antibodies on diferent organs, namely fruits, leaves, roots, female and male fowers. Two replicates per tissue were processed and present a correlation coefcient of at least 0.99 (Additional fle [1:](#page-13-0) Figures S1 and S2). A minimum of 14 millions of mapped reads were obtained in the replicates implemented (Additional fle [2:](#page-14-8) Table I). Te MACS2 algorithm, which is designed to detect typical histone peaks [[72\]](#page-16-8), was used to determine loci that are signifcantly enriched with H3K9ac or with H3K27me3 (Additional fle [1:](#page-13-0) Figures S3 and S4). We identifed 18,424 H3K9ac marked genes in leaves, 18,024 in roots, 13,655 in fruits, 17,660 in male fowers and 16,348 in female fowers. On the other hand, we identifed 6486 H3K27me3 marked genes in leaves, 7741 in fruits, 6823 in roots, 6055 in male and 6054 in female fowers (Fig. [1a](#page-2-0); Additional fle [3](#page-14-9): Table II).

Next we analyzed the distribution of those two marks at the chromosome and gene levels. For the sake of simplicity, we will describe mainly results obtained in leaves, and results obtained in the other organs are shown in the additional fle. Te chromosomal distributions of annotated genes, H3K9ac and H3K27me3 and in each organ on the 12 chromosomes, are illustrated in Additional fle [1](#page-13-0): Figure S3. At the chromosomal scale we observed an enrichment of both marks in gene-rich regions, which are localized at chromosome arms, compare to centromeric and pericentromeric regions, where the majority of transposable and retro transposable elements are located [[73\]](#page-16-9), consistent with the role of these histone marks in the control of gene expression (Fig. [1b](#page-2-0); Additional fle [1](#page-13-0): Figures S5 and S6). In order to detail the H3K27me3 and H3K9ac distributions at the gene level, the peaks obtained in each tissue for both modifcations were analyzed and averaged within a 4000-bp region, fanking the TSS and TES of all the annotated genes. We found that the peak length of H3K9ac ranged from 200 bp to 700 bp (Additional fle [1](#page-13-0): Figure S7), located preferentially at the TSS regions, which correspond to the frst nucleosomes of the genes (Fig. [1](#page-2-0)c; Additional fle [1](#page-13-0): Figure S8). In contrast, H3K27me3 peaks presented an averaged length that ranged from 2500 to 2700 bp, which covered the entire gene body (Fig. [1](#page-2-0)c; Additional fle [1:](#page-13-0) Figure S7). Tose patterns were observed in all the organs (Additional fle [1](#page-13-0): Figures S7 and S8) and were consistent with previous studies on diferent plant species, highlighting conserved aspects of the epigenetic system in the plant kingdom. Integration of H3K9ac and H3K27me3 datasets showed a clear anti-correlation between those two marks (Fig. [1d](#page-2-0)). Altogether, these results showed that in melon, as in other plant species, H3K27me3 and H3K9ac are not enriched in intergenic regions but distributed along the gene body, supporting the role of these two marks in gene regulation in an exclusive manner.

### **Relationship between gene expression and histone modifcations in melon**

To connect H3K9ac and H3K27me3 histone marks with gene expression, we generated and integrated RNAseq data from the fve diferent organs. Firstly, we confrm that H3K9ac and H3K27me3 in *Cucumis melo* are, respectively, associated with gene expression and gene repression (Fig. [2](#page-6-0)a). Secondly, in order to determine whether the level of acetylation or methylation could be correlated with gene expression, we divided all the protein coding genes into four quantiles based on their expression levels and plotted their H3K9ac or H3K27me3 profle (Fig. [2b](#page-6-0)). We observed that H3K9ac occupancy increases with expression level, while H3K27me3 showed the opposite pattern, where it displayed a high enrichment in the lowest-expressed genes but it remained unresponsive to intermediary and high expression levels. Tis results suggest that in *Cucumis melo* the more a gene is marked by H3K9ac and H3K27me3, the more it will be expressed and repressed, respectively (Fig. [2](#page-6-0)b).

To confrm the relationship between gene expression and organ-specifc landscape of H3K9ac and H3K27me3, we identifed genes specifcally expressed in each organ (Fig. [2c](#page-6-0); Additional fle [5:](#page-14-12) Table IV) and we compared them with organ-specifc H3K9ac marked genes. Interestingly, in the case of leaves, we found that a signifcant part of genes specifcally hyperacetylated (43%) and hypomethylated (32%) are specifcally expressed in this organ (Fig. [2d](#page-6-0), e). Gene ontology analyses of these genes showed a signifcant enrichment in photosynthesis and response to light. In the case of roots, fowers and fruits we observed the same tendency, suggesting that both H3K9ac and H3K27me3 controlled organ-specifc gene expression (Additional fle [1](#page-13-0): Figure S13).

#### **Female and male fower‑specifc epigenome landscapes**

Beyond elucidating general aspects of the melon epigenome, the analyses we performed so far evidenced how H3K27me3 and H3K9ac organ-specifc distributions can be crucial to study gene expression regulation and organ identity. Tus, we directed epigenomic and transcriptomic analyses toward the elucidation of female and male fower-specifc epigenome landscapes and their role in sex determination. To this end, we integrated RNA-seq and, H3K9ac and H3K27me3 ChIP-seq data of male and female fowers in order to identify genes that are epigenetically regulated in a sex-specifc manner (Fig. [3](#page-6-1); Additional fle [6:](#page-14-13) Table V). We observed that 22% of genes that are hypoacetylated in female fowers compared to male fower are downregulated and only 3% of the set of hypoacetylated genes are upregulated (Fig. [3a](#page-6-1)). Regarding hyperacetylated genes, we also found that a signifcant proportion of them (28%) are upregulated in female fowers compared to male fower and only 5% are dow-regulated. Furthermore, gene ontology analysis of female-specifc epigenetically regulated genes showed enrichment in biological processes such as carpel development, response to ethylene and response to jasmonic acid. For male-specifc epigenetically regulated genes, we observed enrichment in biological processes such as, stamen development and lipid metabolic process (Fig. [3a](#page-6-1)). Te same analyses were performed for hypo- and hypermethylated genes, obtaining similar results and confrming that a signifcant portion of sex-specifc genes is epigenetically controlled (Fig. [3b](#page-6-1)).

After analyzing the biological processes involving the sex-specifc epigenetically regulated genes, we classifed these genes based on their molecular function. We found enrichment for transporter activity, catalytic activity, oxidoreductase activity and interestingly, for transcription factor (TF) activity (Fig. [3](#page-6-1)c; Additional fle [1](#page-13-0): Figures S14–S18; Additional fle [7:](#page-14-14) Table VI). Since transcription factors are the best described and commonly associated components of gene expression regulation, we further focused on them and grouped them according to their TF families (Fig. [3](#page-6-1)d). We found that the ethylene-responsive factor (ERF) family displayed the highest number of gene members, consistent with previous studies that reported the role of ethylene and ethylene transduction pathways in sex determination in the cucurbitaceae family (22). In addition, we found other TF families such as Zinc Finger (17 genes), basic Helix-Loop-Helix (15 genes), MYB (13 genes) and NAC families (13) (Fig. [3d](#page-6-1)). Interestingly, we also detected 11 genes belonging to the MADS-box TF family. Several members of this family are conserved master regulators of fower ontogenesis [\[76](#page-16-12)[–78](#page-16-13)]. When we assessed the identity of these 11 genes, we observed that all of them are potential homologs of diferent *Arabidopsis* genes that are part of the ABC model of fower development (Additional fle [7:](#page-14-14) Table VI).

### **Role of** *CmACS11* **and** *CmWIP1* **genes in sex‑specifc epigenome acquisition**

We previously showed that sex determination in melon relies on the interplay between alleles of three sex determination genes, *M*, *G* and *A* [[47,](#page-15-20) [49–](#page-15-22)[51\]](#page-15-23). In monoecious melon, most of the fowers are male and female fowers develop at the youngest nodes of the growing vines (Fig. [5a](#page-10-0)). Male fowers result from the expression of the male promoting gene, *CmWIP1*. Female fowers develop on branches expressing *CmACS11*, which represses the expression of *CmWIP1*. Androecious plants result from a loss-of-function of *CmACS11* leading to expression of *CmWIP1* in all fowers on a plant. Gynoecious plants are obtained by inactivation of *CmWIP1* function [\[47,](#page-15-20) [51](#page-15-23)] (Fig. [5a](#page-10-0)).

To bring new insight on the role of these sex-determining genes in establishing epigenetic signatures important for sex determination in melon, we investigated the H3K27me3 genome-wide landscape in male and female fowers of wild-type monoecious melon and two androecious and gynoecious isogenic lines, carrying a loss-of-function mutation in *CmACS11* and *CmWIP*-*1,* respectively.

To determine the proportion of genes whose diferential epigenetic state between male and female fowers can depend on *CmACS11* and *CmWIP*-*1*, we compared male and female fowers from the wild type with fowers occupying the same position on the inforescence from androecious and gynoecious sex transition mutants, respectively (Fig. [5b](#page-10-0); Additional fle [1:](#page-13-0) Figures S20– S22). Te gene lists generated by these comparisons are illustrated in a Venn diagram, where the overlapping genes correspond to *CmWIP1*—(Additional fle [1:](#page-13-0) Figure S20B) and *CmACS11*-related (Additional fle [1:](#page-13-0) Figure S20C) H3K27me3 changes linked to sex identity. We observed that 54% (1200/2197) of sex-specifc regulated gene was under the control of the *G* locus and that 60% (1324/2197) was under the control of the *A* locus (Fig. [5](#page-10-0)b). Altogether, these data suggested that *CmACS11*

and *CmWIP*-*1* are two master regulator controlling sexspecifc epigenome acquisitions.

Te androecious gene *CmACS11* plays central role in sex determination as it controls the expression the gynoecious gene *CmWIP1* [[51\]](#page-15-23). Knowing from this study that *CmWIP1* is epigenetically regulated through histone methylation (Additional fle [1](#page-13-0): Figure S23), we predicted that *CmACS11* represses the expression of the *CmWIP1* by modifying its methylation status. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed the H3K27me3 status of *CmWIP1* in fowers that do or do not express *CmACS11* and in fowers impaired in *CmACS11* function. We performed an H3K27me3 ChIP followed by qPCR using 9 diferent primers covering the entire *CmWIP1 gene*. As predicted, we observed that *CmWIP1* is hypermethylated in female buds that express *CmACS11* but not in male buds that do not express *CmACS11*. Te hypermethylation of *CmWIP1* is also lost in *CmACS11* loss of function mutant (Fig. [5c](#page-10-0)). Te hypomethylation correlates with the expression *CmWIP1* and the hypermethylation with the non-expression of *CmWIP1*. Based on this, we concluded that the *A* gene represses the expression of *CmWIP1* via deposition of H3K27me3.

### **Discussion**

Deposition of chromatin modifcations is instrumental for the concerted expression or repression of thousands of genes, thereby allowing the establishment of organ or tissue specifc transcriptomes [[10](#page-14-6)[–13](#page-15-0)]. H3K9ac and H3K27me3 are among the main chromatin marks involved in the control of gene expression [[5,](#page-14-7) [20,](#page-15-4) [30](#page-15-12)[–33](#page-15-15)]. In this study, we generated genome-wide and organ-specifc maps of H3K27me3 and H3K9ac in melon, thereby generating a repertoire for the elucidation of mechanisms controlling the expression of the melon genome. Tis work will provide new information for the analysis of melon development or several agricultural traits, that have until now been mainly studied via classical genetic approaches. Te distributions of H3K9ac and H3K27me3 in the melon genome displayed common chromosomal and genic distributions when compared to other plant species such as *Arabidopsis* [[14–](#page-15-1)[17](#page-15-2)], maize [\[19](#page-15-28), [20](#page-15-4)] and rice [\[21–](#page-15-5)[23\]](#page-15-6): H3K27me3 and H3K9ac were mainly enriched at the chromosome arms, resembling the distribution of genes, but were confned to the gene body and not spread along intergenic regions. Within the gene body, H3K27me3 distributes uniformly from the TSS to the TES, whereas H3K9ac is mainly enriched at the TSS.

As described in other plant species, we observed that H3K9ac and H3K27me3 marks display opposite correlation with gene expression. Interestingly, we found a direct proportionality between H3K9ac and gene expression levels, thereby suggesting the crucial role of H3K9ac in

In contrast, H3K27me3 did not display an evident proportionality with gene expression, being unresponsive to intermediate gene expression levels as it has been observed for barley [25] and maize [19]; however, it was clearly enriched at the lowest-expressed genes, suggesting that this histone mark is involved in the full repression of genes rather in the fine-tuning of their expression.

Beyond their role in gene expression regulation, we observed that both H3K9ac and H3K27me3 are important in the control of organ identity and development, thus mediating the formation of diferent cell types and functions from a single genome. A considerable portion of H3K9ac and H3K27me3 target genes was expressed in an organ-specifc manner, the diferential and unique deposition of these two histone marks in each organ, creates an epigenetic landscape on genes that is crucial for its metabolic needs and physiology.

In the case of fowers, female-specifc H3K9 hyperacetylated and H3K27 hypo-trimethylated genes are involved in carpel and fower development as well as in several other bioprocesses previously reported to play a role in fower feminization in cucurbits. Such is the case for the hormone ethylene, whose synthesis and perception have important roles in development of female unisexual fowers. Te expression of *CmACS7* and *CmACS11,* two enzymes that are part of the ethylene biosynthesis pathway, and *CsETR1*, one of the reported ethylene receptors, have been already placed in the regulatory network promoting female fower development in cucurbits [[47,](#page-15-20) [49](#page-15-22)[–51](#page-15-23), [80–](#page-16-15)[83](#page-16-16)]. On the other hand, other genes related to jasmonic acid response were specifcally modifed with active marks in the female fower. Consistent with this, jasmonic acid (JA) was also shown to be associated with sex determination in maize [\[84](#page-16-17)]. Specifcally, downregulated genes (H3K9 hypo-acetylated and H3K27 hyper-trimethylated) in the female fower pointed out bioprocesses such as lipid metabolism, which has been previously reported to play a role in pollen-tube growth and pollen-stigma interactions [[85\]](#page-16-18).

When we assessed the molecular function of sex-specifc epigenetically regulated genes, we found enrichment for transcription factor, DNA and nucleotide binding activities. We, therefore, categorized these TFs according to their corresponding protein families and found that the majority of them belong to the ethylene-responsive factors [\[86](#page-16-19)[–88](#page-16-20)]. Tese results suggest that histone modifcations regulate ethylene transduction pathway in melon as it has previously reported in Arabidopsis [\[89\]](#page-16-21).

Interestingly, we also found genes belonging to the MADS-box TF family, which are well recognized for their important role in fower whorl identity and is in concordance with previous reports regarding the regulation of *AP1, AG* and *SEP3* by the histone demethylase REF6 that removes H3K27me3 [[90,](#page-16-22) [91\]](#page-16-23), and polycomb-dependent regulation of *AG* [\[92,](#page-16-24) [93](#page-16-25)].

Te elucidation of epigenetic control of the class C genes for the development of unisexual fowers demands the assessment of the whorl-specifc epigenetic state of carpel- and stamen-related identity of MADS-box genes. Tissue heterogeneity has been considered a persistent constraint for the identifcation of epigenetic events occurring in a specifc cell population thus, masking the natural complexity of gene expression regulation. So far in the plant kingdom, few technical improvements to this limitation have been reported, especially due to the complexity of performing ChIP on small samples. In this study, we provide the frst protocol to overcome such limitation that can be transferred to any plant species and demonstrate that epigenetic changes that are undetectable with standard methods using whole organs can be identifed by using purifed cell populations. By this method, we also demonstrated that stamen—(i.e., *PI* and *AP3*) and carpel-related (i.e., *STK*) identity genes present a contrasting H3K27me3 and H3K9ac landscapes. Tis suggests that the sex determination genes recruit histone modifers to target organ identity genes in the sexual whorls of the fower, leading to unisexual fower development.

In the cucurbit sex determination pathway, the gynoecious gene, *CmWIP1*, plays a central role in unisexual fower development. From this analysis, we showed that CmACS11 represses *CmWIP1* expression by inducing H3K27me3 at this locus (Fig. [5](#page-10-0)d). Tis data suggest that the local production of ethylene via CmACS11 in companion cells is able to activate histone methyltransferase to inhibit *CmWIP1* expression in a defned spatio-temporal manner.

Detailed knowledge about spatio-temporal gene expression and epigenetic regulation dynamics is pivotal for a comprehensive understanding of development of unisexual fowers. In species recalcitrant to plant transformation, such as melon, this has been hampered mainly by difculties in isolating sufcient amounts of tissues from distinct organ primordia for chromatin analysis. In this context, using laser capture micro-dissection, we succeeded in generating high-quality chromatin, from dissected carpel and stamina primordia, from unisexual fowers, and discovered that the sex determination genes recruit histone modifers to orchestrate unisexual fower development in monoecious species. Genome-wide analyses of chromatin modifcations in the dissected reproductive organs from unisexual and hermaphrodite fowers and from key histone modifer mutants will likely to bring new insight on the epigenetic control of unisexual fower development.

## **Methods**

## **Plant material and growth conditions**

Monoecious melon (*Cucumis melo*) cultivar charentais (*Cucumis melo* L. subsp. *melo* var *cantalupensis*) were used for H3K9ac and H3K27me3 genome-wide descriptive analyses. For each replicate, 7-day-old seedlings were transferred to individual pots and incubated in a growth chamber for 30 days (long day conditions, temperature: 27 °C (day) and 21 °C (night), relative humidity: 60%). Young male and female fowers (3 mm length; Developmental stage 8 [\[52\]](#page-15-29)) were collected, organized according to their location in the plant (i.e., main stem or branches) and conserved at −80 °C for epigenomic and transcriptomic analyses.

For the determination of *a*- and *g*-related epigenetic changes, we used two families of EMS-treated *C. melo* lines carrying missense mutations in *CmWIP1* and in *CmACS11*, both previously reported to lead to gynoecy [[47\]](#page-15-20) and androecy [[51\]](#page-15-23), respectively. Germination, growth and fower collecting conditions were the same to the previously described for the monoecious WT plants.

#### **Gene expression analysis**

Total RNA were extracted from leaves, root, fruit, male and female fowers using the Nucleospin RNA kit (Macherey–Nagel), according to the manufacturer's instructions. For RT-QPCR analyses, frst-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using Improm-II reverse transcriptase (A3802, Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions. 1/25th of the synthesized cDNA was mixed with 100 nM of each primer and LightCycler® 480 Sybr Green I master mix (Roche Applied Science) for quantitative PCR analysis. Products were amplifed and fuorescent signals acquired with a LightCycler® 480 detection system. Te specifcity of amplifcation products was determined by melting curves. ACT2 was used as internal control for signals normalization. Exor4 relative quantifcation software (Roche Applied Science) automatically calculated relative expression level of the selected genes with algorithms based on ΔΔCt method. Data were from duplicates of at least two biological replicates. Te sequences of primers can be found in Additional fle [8:](#page-14-15) Table VII.

For RNA-seq analysis, libraries were synthetized using NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA library Preparation Kit (NEB) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Two biological replicates were run for each tissue. Raw reads from RNA-Seq were frst adaptor trimmed and quality fltered using Trimmomatic [\[94](#page-16-26)]. Filtered reads were mapped to the *Cucumis melo* genome v3.5.1 using TopHat v2.0.9 [[95](#page-16-27)]. Transcript quantifcation was derived using Cufinks v2.2.0 [\[96](#page-16-28)]. CufDif is used for diferential expression analysis (*p* value 0.05; statistical correction: Benjamini Hochberg; FDR: 0.05). A cutof of 0.5 fold upor down-regulation has been chosen to defne signifcant diferential expression. CummeRbund v2.0.0 was used for visualization of diferential analysis [[97](#page-16-29)]. Functional annotation using GO terms was performed using the Plant MetGenMAP tool [\[75\]](#page-16-11). All *p* values are shown in Additional fle [9](#page-14-11): Table VIII.

#### **Chromatin immunoprecipitation experiments**

ChIP assays were performed using anti-H3K9ac (Millipore, ref. 07-352) or anti-H3K27me3 (Millipore, ref. 07-449) antibodies, using a procedure adapted from Veluchamy et al. [[17\]](#page-15-2). Briefy, after plant material fxation in 1% (v/v) formaldehyde, tissues were homogenized, nuclei isolated and lysed. Cross-linked chromatin was sonicated using a water bath Bioruptor UCD-200 (Diagenode, Liège, Belgium) (30 s on/30 s of pulses, at high intensity for 60 min). Protein/DNA complexes were immunoprecipitated with antibodies, overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking, and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C with 50 μL of Dynabeads Protein A (Invitrogen, Ref. 100-02D). Te beads were washed for 2 × 5 min in ChIP Wash Bufer 1 (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 20mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 2 mM EDTA pH 8, 150 mM NaCl), 2 × 5 min in ChIP Wash Bufer 2 (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 2 mM EDTA pH 8, 500 mM NaCl), 2 × 5 min in ChIP Wash Bufer 3 (0.25 M LiCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8,1 mM EDTA pH 8) and twice in TE (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 1 mM EDTA pH 8). ChIPed DNA was eluted by two 15-min incubations at 65 °C with 250 μL Elution Bufer (1% SDS, 0.1 M NaHCO3). Chromatin was reverse-crosslinked by adding 20 μL of NaCl 5 M and incubated over-night at 65 °C. Reverse-cross-linked DNA was submitted to RNase and proteinase K digestion and extracted with phenol–chloroform. DNA was ethanol precipitated in the presence of 20 μg of glycogen and resuspended in 50 μL of nucleasefree water (Ambion) in a DNA low-bind tube.

For ChIP-qPCR experiments, fold enrichment of targets in ChIPed DNA relative to input was calculated from an average of three replicate qPCRs. Te sequences of primers can be found in Additional fle [8](#page-14-15): Table VII. Positions of the amplifed regions on the diferent loci are indicated in Fig. [5](#page-10-0).

For ChIP-seq assays, 10 ng of IP or input DNA was used for ChIP-Seq library construction using NEB-Next Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) according to manufacturer's recommendations. For all libraries, twelve cycles of PCR were used. Te quality of the libraries was assessed with Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent), and the libraries were subjected to high-throughput sequencing by Illumina Sequencing technology.

#### **Computational analysis of ChIP‑seq**

Preprocessing of sequencing reads for quality was performed using FASTQC [\(http://www.bioinformatics.](http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) [babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/](http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/)). Filtering and trimming of reads was done using Trimmomatic with the following parameters: Minimum length of 36 bp; mean Phred quality score greater than 30; leading and trailing bases removal with base quality below 3; sliding window of 4:15. Using Bowtie, the remaining high-quality reads were mapped onto the *Cucumis melo* genome v3.5.1 with maximum mismatch of 1 bp. Unique mapping of reads was adopted. To determine the target regions of H3K9Ac ChIP-Seq, the model-based analysis of ChIP-Seq (MACS2) was adopted (Number of duplicate reads at a location: 1; Bandwidth: 300; mfold of 5:30; q-value cutof: 0.05) [[72\]](#page-16-8). For peak detection in H3K27me3 modifed regions, we used SICER with the following criteria: Window size: 200, Gap size: 600 [[98](#page-16-30)]. Alignment and tag-density were inspected with IGB. HOMER was used to associate peaks to nearby genes [\[99](#page-16-31)]. To cluster the H3K9Ac and H3K27me3 peaks, linear normalization and clustering of tag density with Density Array method (window size 50 bp; 2 kb fanking region of genes) was performed using SeqMINER [\[100](#page-16-32)]. Annotation of corresponding genes was done using melonomics resource (<https://melonomics.net>). Average profle of coverage along the genic region (between transcriptional start sites (TSS) and TES) along with the 2 kb fanking region was plotted using NGSplot in binning mode [[101](#page-16-33)]. To identify diferentially enriched histone modifed sites in leaves, root, fruit and fowers, we used DifReps with the following settings: Window size 1 kb; step size: 100 bp; *p* value: 0.0001; Statistical testing method: Chi-square method) [[74\]](#page-16-10).

### **Chromatin immunoprecipitation of Laser capture microdissected primordia**

Male and female fowers of 3 mm were collected on monoecious melon plants, fxed in 1% (v/v) formaldehyde for 15 min under vacuum, and the crosslink reaction was stopped by addition of glycine (130 mM fnal) for 5 min under vacuum. Next, the samples were centrifuged 10 min at 3500*g* and the pellet was resuspended in 130 µl of nuclei lysis bufer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH8; 10 mM EDTA). Chromatin was sonicated using Covaris (M220 Focused-ultrasonicator; DC 5%; PIP 75Watts; CPB 200; 20 min) in 130 µl microtubes (Covaris AFA tubes) and centrifuged 10 min at 16,000*g*. Supernatant was then diluted ten times with ChIP dilution bufer (1,1% Triton; 1,2 mM EDTA; 16,7 mM Tris–HCl pH8; 167 mM NaCl) before immunoprecipitation with H3K9ac or H3K27me3 antibodies overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking, and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C with 50 μL of Dynabeads Protein A (Invitrogen, Ref. 100-02D). After several washes, immunoprecipitated DNA was then recovered using proteinase K digestion, reverse crosslink and phenol–chloroform extraction as previously described. Fold enrichment of targets in ChIPed DNA relative to input was calculated from an average of three replicate qPCRs. Te sequences of primers can be found in Additional fle [8](#page-14-15): Table VII. Positions of the amplifed regions on the diferent loci are indicated in Fig. [4](#page-8-0).
