Running title: Alternative polyadenylation mediates abiotic responses

Key words: FIP1, Alternative polyadenylation, stem cell maintenance, root development, stress responses, Arabidopsis

#### **Abstract**

Root development and its response to environmental changes is crucial for whole plant adaptation. These responses include changes in transcript levels. Here we show that the alternative polyadenylation (APA) of mRNA is important for root development and responses. Mutations in *FIP1*, a component of polyadenylation machinery, affects plant development, cell division and elongation and response to different abiotic stresses. Salt treatment increases the amount of poly(A) site usage within the coding region and 5´-UTRs, and the lack of FIP1 activity reduces the poly(A) site usage within these non-canonical sites. Gene ontology analyses of transcripts displaying APA in response to salt show an enrichment in ABA signaling, and response to stresses such as salt or cadmium genes, among others. Root growth assays show that *fip1-2* is more tolerant to salt but hypersensitive to ABA or Cd. Our data indicate that FIP1-mediated alternative polyadenylation is important for plant development and stress responses.

## **Introduction**

Plant root growth is sustained by a continuous balance between cell division and differentiation in the root tip (Sablowski and Carnier-Dornelas, 2014, Ramirez-Parra *et al.*, 2017). The root system can be elaborated by promoting roots growth and/or by increasing the number of lateral roots (LR), which are lateral organs with postembryonic development (Perianez-Rodriguez *et al.*, 2014). Lateral root organogenesis requires the coordinate action of several regulatory pathways (Moriwaki *et al.*, 2011) that involve differential gene transcription (Moreno-Risueno *et al.*, 2010, Peret *et al.*, 2012, Lewis *et al.*, 2013, Manzano *et al.*, 2014), protein phosphorylation (Zhang *et al.*, 2013) and protein degradation (Jurado *et al.*, 2010).

Although gene transcription is probably the most important mechanism to regulate transcript accumulation, in the last years, additional alternative posttranscriptional regulatory mechanisms, such as alternative splicing or polyadenylation, are proving to be important in the generation of new transcript isoforms and therefore in new gene function (Chen and Manley, 2009, Di Giammartino *et al.*, 2011). Differential selection of polyadenylation sites (alternative polyadenylation, APA), is used to generate new transcripts, their stability or capability of protein translation (Di Giammartino *et al.*, 2011, Lutz and Moreira, 2011, Akman and Erson-Bensan, 2014). Genome-wide studies in yeast, animals and plants have shown that a large proportion of genes have multiple poly(A) sites (Nagalakshmi *et al.*, 2008, Fu *et al.*, 2016, De Lorenzo *et al.*, 2017). Although the largest number of polyadenylation events occurs at the 3´-UTR, APA may also occur at different regions inside loci (5′-UTRs, introns, or exons), which are known as non-canonical sites.

Polyadenylation is a highly regulated process that involves the activity of a large protein complex (Tian and Manley, 2016). One of the subunits of this complex is FIP1, which provides a bridge between poly(A) polymerase (PAP) and other subunits of the cleavage and polyadenylation factors (Helmling *et al.*, 2001, Mandel *et al.*, 2008). Arabidopsis FIP1 has the capacity to stimulate PAP activity and binds RNA (Forbes *et al.*, 2006) and is thought to be involved in poly(A) site selection.

In plants, several genomic studies to identify and characterize different poly(A) sites and differential usage have been carried out (Shen *et al.*, 2011, Wu *et al.*, 2011, Guo *et al.*, 2016, De Lorenzo *et al.*, 2017). It was noted that genes possessing polyadenylation sites within protein-coding regions or introns are significantly associated with stress responses (Wu *et al.*, 2011). In Arabidopsis, hypoxia leads to an increased usage of poly(A) sites within coding regions and 5´-UTR, affecting their stability and translation (De Lorenzo *et al.*, 2017). In addition, APA also seems to be important to maintain correct hormonal signaling, as in the case of auxin (Hong *et al.*, 2018). Recently, a T-DNA insertional mutant in the *FIP1* gene has been reported. Similar to mutations in *CPSF-30L*, another component of the polyadenylation machinery, this *fip1* allele had a reduced induction of nitrate-responsive genes upon nitrate treatment (Li *et al.*, 2017, Wang *et al.*, 2018). In addition, *fip1* seedlings show a reduction in nitrate content due to a deregulation of nitrate uptake genes and different content of amino acids (Wang *et al.*, 2018).

In this work we have identified a viable EMS allele for the *FIP1* gene (*fip1-2*). This mutant shows several developmental defects associated with problems in cell division and elongation. The *fip1-2* mutant shows defects in stem cell niche maintenance and reduced *SCARECROW* (*SCR*) and *WUSCHEL-related homeobOX 5* (*WOX5)* expression. A transcriptomic analysis showed that loss of *FIP1* function leads to higher level of transcripts involved in ABA, salt or water deficiency responses among others. Genomic poly(A) site

usage studies showed that an excess of salt increases poly(A) site usage within non-canonical regions (coding and 5´-UTR), but a mutation in *FIP1* reduces this non-canonical usage. Gene ontology analyses of transcripts regulated by differential poly(A) site usage show an enrichment of genes involved in ABA signaling and in responses to abiotic stresses such as salt or cadmium (Cd), among others. Root growth assays show that *fip1-2* roots are ABAand Cd-hypersensitive but more tolerant to salt treatment. Taken together, our data indicate that *FIP1* is needed for the correct polyadenylation of transcripts and for the generation of "non-canonical" mRNA isoforms during stress responses.

#### **Results**

#### **Cloning of the** *fip1-2* **locus**

A genetic screen to identify plants with an altered root system was carried out using an EMSmutagenized SKP2Bp::GUS marker population (Manzano *et al.*, 2012, Manzano *et al.*, 2017). Mutants displaying root development defects or *SKP2B* (S-phase kinase-associated protein 2) altered expression were identified. Among them, the *fip1-2* mutant showed fewer emerged lateral roots than the control and delocalized *SKP2B* expression (Figure 1a, Figure S1a-d). By genetic mapping, the *fip1-2* mutation was located on chromosome 5 (Method S1). Further, by whole genome sequencing of fip1-2 and control genomes, we identified nine SNPs that were located in genomic loci. T-DNA mutants for each of these 9 loci were identified and phenotyped. Only the SALK\_099558 T-DNA line that tagged the At5g58040 locus showed a similar, but weaker, phenotype than *fip1-2*. The At5g58040 locus encodes the mRNA polyadenylation factor *FIP1* (Forbes *et al.*, 2005, Addepalli and Hunt, 2007). To corroborate their allelic nature, a complementation test was conducted, crossing *fip1-2* and the SALK\_099558 line. The F1 seedlings developed as few lateral roots as the *fip1-2* mutant

(Figure S1b-c). Moreover, overexpression of the At5g58040 genomic region (containing ATG, exons and introns and terminator) in *fip1-2* completely restored the LR formation defects and SKP2B::GUS expression pattern (Figure S1d-e). Taken together, these analyses confirmed that the *fip1-2* mutation affects FIP1 activity. The SNP identified in *fip1-2* changed the guanine in position 23490391 (chromosome 5) to an adenine. This coordinate corresponds to the first base in the second intron, suggesting that the *fip1-2* mutation affects *FIP1* RNA splicing (Figure S2a). To demonstrate this possibility, *FIP1* cDNA was amplified and sequenced using RNA extracted from control and *fip1-2* seedlings. As shown in Figure S2b, *FIP1* mRNA splicing was altered by the *fip1-2* mutation, generating a messenger RNA that retains intron 2 and originates a premature STOP codon (Figure S2c).

#### **Phenotypic characterization of** *fip1-2* **allele**

The *fip1-2* mutant showed a significant reduction in the number of emerged lateral roots and a moderate reduction in primary root growth (Figure 1a,b; Figure S1a). Confocal image analyses showed that *fip1-2* root meristems were longer than control meristems (SKP2B::GUS), but the cells at the elongation zone were significantly shorter in the *fip1-2* roots than in the control roots (Figure 1d). Root stem cells are organized around the quiescent center (QC), a group of cells with a low division rate involved in the maintenance of stem cell niche. Confocal image analyses of *fip1-2* meristems showed a disorganized quiescent center and stem cell niche, displaying extra cell divisions (Figure 1e). To further investigate the defects in the stem cell niche, we analyzed the levels of *SCR* and *WOX5,* a downstream *SCR*-regulated gene, since they are both needed for proper quiescent center (QC) maintenance and stem cell niche organization (Sarkar *et al.*, 2007). We found that expression

of both genes was significantly reduced in *fip1-2* mutant compared with the control (Figure 1f-i)*.*

To investigate the reduced number of lateral roots (LRs) in *fip1-2,* we analyzed lateral root primordia (LRP) at different stages. We found that LR reduction was mainly due to a lower number of lateral root primordia (LRP) (Figure S1a) and the accumulation of LRP in stage IV-V (Figure 2a). Close observation at the LRP showed that in *fip1-2* LRP in stage V contained flatter cells with disorganized cell division planes (Figure 2b, c). We also found that the *fip1-2* mutation affected shoot development, as the mutant showed a smaller height and a significant reduction (more than 3-fold) in leaf area (Figure 2d-e) as well as altered flowering time. Under long day (LD) conditions, *fip1-2* plants flowered slightly earlier than the control (16 days versus 20) and with fewer leaves (8 days versus 10) (Figure 2f). This premature flowering phenotype was more marked under a short day (SD) photoperiod, where control plants flowered after 48 days with 39 leaves, whereas *fip1-2* plants flowered in about 35 days and produced 21 leaves (Figure 2f). These differences were not found in the recued *fip1-2* plants (*fip1-2*/*FIP1*-ox) (Figure S1f). We also found that *fip1-2* presented defects in ovule development, showing smaller and abnormal shapes (Figure 2g). However, a germination test determined that these smaller ovules, which gave rise to smaller seeds, were completely viable and germinated like the control seeds (Figure S1g).

#### *FIP1* **is expressed in dividing cells**

In order to understand the role of *FIP1* and explain the defects observed in *fip1-2* mutant, we analyzed its spatio-temporal expression pattern. GUS staining analyses of pFIP1::GUS seedlings, which harbors a transcriptional fusion, showed that *FIP1* was expressed in root and shoot meristems, dividing cells in developing leaves and in stipules (Figure 2 h). In addition,

in young and growing leaves, *FIP1* was expressed in the leaf margin and hydathodes, and to less extend in vascular tissue (Figure 2i). *FIP1* was expressed in young developing flowers, mainly in stamens (Figure 2j) and in developing ovules (Figure 2k). In roots, FIP1 was expressed in QC, columella cells, vascular initials and in lateral root primordia at different stages (Figure 2l-n). As auxin plays an important role in lateral root formation, we checked whether *FIP1* expression was altered by this hormone. A short-term auxin treatment induced the expression of *FIP1* in the whole root (Figure 2o). Promoter sequence analyses with PlantCare (Lescot *et al.*, 2002)identified an auxin response element at –717 base pairs upstream of ATG (Figure S2d).

#### **The** *fip1-2* **mutation alters gene expression**

The *fip1-2* mutant shows a pleiotropic phenotype. To understand the effect of this mutation at the molecular level, we carried out a comparative transcriptomic analysis. RNA extracted from 12 day old control and *fip1-2* was subjected to deep sequencing (see Methods S1). We determined that the *fip1-2* mutation increased transcript level of 1088 loci (Table S1b) while decreasing 837 loci (Table S1d) (>2 or <-2-fold; FDR<0.05). Among these deregulated genes, we found a significant enrichment of genes involved in protein phosphorylation, amino acid biosynthesis, responses to stresses, including salt, water deprivation, wounding or hydrogen peroxide, and also to hormone signaling (Table S1c-e). In addition, a large proportion of genes encoding ion transporters were also deregulated (Table S1f). Using the eFP expression browser (Toufighi *et al.*, 2005), we found that a significant number of genes deregulated in *fip1-2* were also deregulated by Fe-deficiency and by phosphate (Pi) starvation in roots (Dinneny *et al.*, 2008, Lin *et al.*, 2011)(Table S2). We found that *fip1-2* showed upregulation of key phosphate starvation response genes, such as *IPS1*, *SPX1* or phosphate

transporters (Tables S1b-S2f). Based on these findings, we decided to analyze the effect of Pi starvation in *fip1-2*. First, we found that *FIP1* expression was slightly increased in the root tip (Figure S3a). As expected, Pi starvation reduced root growth in control seedlings (Figure S3a,b). However, this reduction was significantly smaller in *fip1-2*. We also quantified the level of free inorganic Pi, finding that *fip1-2* accumulated about 20% more free Pi in both the root and shoot than control seedlings (Figure S3c).

#### **Poly(A) site selection is altered in** *fip1-2*

Poly(A) site selection or alternative polyadenylation (APA) plays an important role in regulating mRNA production and therefore in shaping the final transcriptome and proteome (Lutz and Moreira, 2011, de Klerk and t Hoen, 2015). As *FIP1* encodes a core polyadenylation complex protein (Helmling *et al.*, 2001, Forbes *et al.*, 2005, Forbes *et al.*, 2006, Mandel *et al.*, 2008, Wang *et al.*, 2018), we decided to analyze poly(A) usage in the mutant and control roots by sequencing libraries which query the mRNA-poly(A) tail junction as previously described (Wu *et al.*, 2011, Thomas *et al.*, 2012, De Lorenzo *et al.*, 2017). The sequencing data were subsequently analyzed using two general approaches. In one, poly(A) site choice in annotated 3'-UTRs was assessed on a gene-by-gene basis, and differences between the mutant and control quantified. The output for this analysis, which rationale for this approach is described in detail in (Thomas *et al.*, 2012), was the absolute value of differences in fractional usage for each site that lies within an annotated 3'-UTR. Analyses of the aggregate results for different comparisons revealed large-scale changes in poly(A) site choice (Figure 3a). In order to compare and validate our data, we compared the poly(A) usage obtained with the *oxt6* mutant (Thomas et al., 2012), which alters poly(A) site choice in thousands of genes, and its control (control1) with our data (*fip1-2* and control-2).

The box named "*oxt6-oxt6"* corresponded to results comparing different biological replicates of the *oxt6* mutant and provide a measure of the variability inherent in the assay as well as in poly(A) site usage in a mutant affected in poly(A) selection. A second box (*oxt6*/control-1) corresponded to the results of the previous comparison of the control and *oxt6* mutant (Thomas *et al.*, 2002), and is indicative of the case where poly(A) site choice differs widely between the two samples. A third box (control-2/control-1) compared the control libraries generated in Thomas *et al.*, 2012 and the control libraries from the current study. This comparison showed an intermediate value between that of *oxt6* replicates and the control-1/*oxt6* analysis. This provides a measure of the variability between studies and library preparation methods. The *oxt6*/control-2 comparison shows a much greater difference than the control-2/control-1 comparison, indicating that the poly(A) site usage in control plants from the current study is different from that seen in the *oxt6* mutant. Interestingly, poly(A) site usage in the *fip1-2* mutant differs greatly from usage in both control samples, and also in the *oxt6* mutant (*fip1-2*/control-2, *fip1-2*/control-1 and *fip1-2*/oxt6; Figure 3a). These results suggests that the *fip1-2* mutation produces a large alteration in poly(A) site usage, and, at least partially, one that is not recapitulated in *oxt6* mutant.

To better understand the extent to which the *fip1-2* mutation alters the mRNA landscape, the average mRNA lengths for more than 11,000 genes was compared in the control and *fip1-2*  mutant. For each gene, an average mRNA length was calculated based on the lengths of different mRNA isoforms, weighting these lengths according to their fractional contribution towards the entire set of mRNAs derived from a given gene, and summing these contributions. As can be seen in Figure 3b, mRNA lengths did not significantly differ in the mutant. This result suggests that, while alteration of poly(A) site usage is widespread in the mutant (Figure 3a), the effects are not manifested in large changes in overall mRNA length.

Examples of poly(A) site changes are shown in the browser tracks in Figure 3c. These tracks illustrate the principal effects of the *fip1-2* mutation inferred from data in Figures 3a,b. We found low-level usage of poly(A) sites that were not seen in the control (as well as occasional instances of sites seen only in the control), along with sites used in both backgrounds and specific poly(A) usage in the *fip1-2* mutant or in the control. To further explore this, the relative nucleotide compositions of the regions surrounding these three groups of poly(A) sites in the mutant, in the control and the common in both backgrounds were examined. As shown in Figure 3d, "common", poly(A) sites seen in both backgrounds have a distinctive nucleotide composition that was similar to those noted before for plant poly(A) sites (Thomas *et al.*, 2012). The same distribution was seen in sites that are used predominantly in the control (control-specific; Figure 3d). In contrast, *fip1-2*-specific sites lacked the characteristic A-rich region upstream from the poly(A) site itself.

#### *FIP1* **functions in ABA and salt responses**

Our transcriptomic data indicate that responses to ABA and salt, two factors that influence root growth and development (De Smet *et al.*, 2003, Zolla *et al.*, 2010, Luo *et al.*, 2014), are altered in *fip1-2*. Expression analyses revealed that both ABA and salt treatments slightly changed *FIP1* expression in the root tip, increasing *FIP1* expression in vascular cells (Figure 4a; Figure S3g). We found that when seedlings were treated with a low concentration of ABA (1 µM) *fip1-2* root growth was significantly inhibited (more than 30%), without influencing root growth in control seedlings (Figure 4b). A similar trend, although weaker, was observed for the *oxt6* mutant (Figure 4b).

We also analyzed the effect of salt on *fip1-2* root growth. As shown in Figure 4c, *fip1-2* root growth was less inhibited than the control by 75 or 100 mM NaCl. Salt stress reduced root meristem size in control and *fip1-2* (Figure 4d), but the reduction was significantly smaller in the *fip1-2* mutant (Figure 4d), correlating with its larger root in this condition. Similar to the root´s responses to ABA, we also found that *oxt6* was slightly resistant to salt-mediated root growth inhibition than control seedlings, but only at 100 mM (Figure 4c). Salt excess induces changes in the cell wall composition and increases suberin deposition (Dinneny *et al.*, 2008, Barberon *et al.*, 2016, Byrt *et al.*, 2018). Fluorol Yellow staining showed that *fip1-2* roots accumulated higher levels of suberin than control roots (Figure 4e), suggesting that this deposition might contribute to the higher salt tolerance in the mutant.

#### **FIP1 functions in poly(A) site selection in response to salt**

Previous reports in yeast and Arabidopsis showed that the abundance of mRNAs with poly(A) sites in non-canonical locations (outside of 3'-UTRs) increased upon exposure to various stresses (Yoon and Brem, 2010, De Lorenzo *et al.*, 2017). As *FIP1* seems to be needed for the plant's response to salt stress (Figure 4c), we decided to analyze whether the response to salt is regulated by APA events as well as the role of *FIP1* in this process. Accordingly, the usage of non-canonical poly(A) sites was studied in roots of plants grown in media with or without salt. In control roots, salt treatment promoted poly(A) usage in the 5´- UTR and CDS regions (Figure 5a). This result is consistent with prior observations showing that the use of these sites was increased in plants subjected to hypoxia (De Lorenzo *et al.*, 2017). In contrast, in the *fip1-2* mutant, there was essentially no change in poly(A) usage located within 5'-UTRs or CDS (Figure 5b). The contrast between control salt-treated and *fip1-2* salt-treated was more apparent when comparing poly(A) usage of different classes of

sites (Figure 5c). Sites located in 5'-UTRs and the CDS were utilized at a lower level in the *fip1-2* plants relative to the control when treated with salt. Usage of the different classes of sites was not appreciably different between *fip1-2* and control plants grown under control conditions (Figure 5d). These results indicate that a major effect of the *fip1-2* mutation is to reduce the usage of non-canonical poly(A) sites upon salt stress.

Next, we analyzed poly(A) usage in individual genes using the DEXseq package (Anders *et al.*, 2012). In control plants, salt treatment generated APA in 652 genes (p-value < 0.01) expressed in roots. Gene Ontology analysis indicates that this set of genes is enriched for those associated with different developmental processes as well as with genes associated with responses to salt stress and ABA, other categories included "translation" (containing ribosomal proteins) and responses to cadmium (Table S3a).

Many fewer genes (208) were impacted by APA in salt-treated *fip1-2* plants (Table S3b), indicating that FIP1 likely participates in the APA in response to salt. This result is consistent with the absence of salt-induced usage of non-canonical poly(A) sites in the mutant (Figure 5b). As with the control, genes associated with salt stress, exposure to cadmium, and translation were over-represented in this set of genes (Table S3b). Interestingly, the sets of genes affected by salt-induced APA in the control and *fip1-2* mutant had few specific genes in common (Table S3c).

In addition, we wanted to analyze whether the transcriptional and APA regulation in response to salt treatment were more similar than expected by chance. We found a lower than expected overlap between root salt-response genes (Dinneny *et al.*, 2008) and the set of transcripts showing differential APA in salt-treated roots (Table S4b,c), indicating that both regulatory systems, transcriptional regulation and APA, act independently in response to salt.

Alternative polyadenylation might affect protein translation efficiency by different mechanisms (Yamashita and Takeuchi, 2017). We found that a significant number of genes exhibiting salt-induced APA in control and the *fip1-2* mutant were involved in ribosome biogenesis and categories related to translation (Table S3a). As an example, the Eukaryotic Release Factors, which determine protein synthesis termination via stop codon recognition, displayed a differential APA in more proximal regions of the 3´UTR in *fip1-2* and/or in response to salt (Figure 6a).

Based on these results, we decided to analyze the effect of the *fip1-2* mutation and salt treatment on protein translation. Five-day old control and *fip1-2* seedlings were treated with 0 or 150 mM NaCl for 16 hours and then labeled with 35S-L-methionine/35S-L-cysteine for 60 minutes. We found that the incorporation of radiolabeled amino acids into nascent proteins was higher in *fip1-2* than control, especially in roots, suggesting that the *fip1-2* mutation enhances protein synthesis. On the other hand, salt treatment significantly reduced radiolabeled amino acid incorporation into *de novo* synthesized proteins in both *fip1-2* and control plants, although this reduction was lower in *fip1-2* (Figure 6b).

#### **The** *fip1-2* **mutation increases cadmium root inhibition**

We found that salt treatment and *fip1-2* mutation affected poly(A) site usage in a significant number of genes associated with the response to cadmium (Table S3a,b). Seedlings treated with cadmium (Cd), a non-essential toxic heavy metal, inhibits root growth (Yuan and Huang, 2016), affects gene expression (Li *et al.*, 2010) and reduces root growth, likely by affecting *SCARECROW (SCR)* expression (Bruno *et al.*, 2017) and auxin signaling (Yuan and Huang, 2016). First, we analyzed whether Cd affects *FIP1* expression. As shown in Figure 7a, Cd treatment slightly increased *FIP1* expression in the vasculature close to the root tip that was corroborated by qRT-PCR (Figure S3g). This Cd treatment reduced root growth in both control and *fip1-2* seedlings, but the reduction was significantly bigger in the latter (Figure 7b,c). Similar to *fip1-2*, *oxt6* mutant was also more sensitivity to Cd (Figure 7c). When we analyzed the expression of the SKP2B::GUS, a cell cycle repressor marker (Manzano *et al.*, 2012), we found that low amounts of Cd significantly increased its expression in the RAM of *fip1-2,* but had no effect in control seedlings (Figure 7d). This is consistent with the function of *SKP2B* as a cell cycle inhibitor in the root meristem (Manzano *et al.*, 2012) and the root growth arrest in *fip1-2* in response to Cd.

As mentioned above, Cd affects auxin signaling, which seems to involve changes in poly(A) site usage (Hong *et al.*, 2018). We found that a significant number of genes involved in auxin signaling were deregulated in *fip1-2* (Table S1e), and some of them displayed differential poly(A) usage (Table S1h). To test the relevance of this findings, we analyzed the effect of auxin treatment on root growth. The *fip1-2* mutant showed a significant bigger root growth inhibition than control seedlings (Figure 7e). Furthermore, we also found that the auxin response marker DR5::GFP was less accumulated in the *fip1-2* root tip (Figure 7f), suggesting that auxin response and/or homeostasis is altered in *fip1-2* mutant.

## **Discussion**

#### **FIP1 is important for root development and protein translation**

In this work we have identified a viable mutation of the *FIP1* gene, which encodes a subunit of the plant polyadenylation complex (Forbes *et al.*, 2006, Zhang *et al.*, 2008). The *fip1-2*

mutation affects many aspects of plant development. At the seedling stage, the most striking defect is seen in roots, as *fip1-2* develops very few emerged lateral roots. Roots are essential organs for plant growth, development and adaptation to adverse environments. Root system architecture is genetically and environmentally defined (Wachsman *et al.*, 2015). During its formation, different genes are regulated in both a cell type-specific and time-dependent manner (Brady *et al.*, 2007). In addition to transcriptional regulation, alternative splicing of transcripts and generation of small interfering and non-coding RNAs increase the number of functional genes that participate in root system architecture (Li *et al.*, 2016, Kiegle *et al.*, 2018), thereby increasing its complexity.

In the last years, it has been proven that in plants differential poly(A) usage and/or polyadenylation sites selection is an important regulatory mechanism (Shen *et al.*, 2011, Wu *et al.*, 2011, Sherstnev *et al.*, 2012, Wu *et al.*, 2014, De Lorenzo *et al.*, 2017, Deng and Cao, 2017, Li *et al.*, 2017). The majority of plant loci possess several poly(A) sites with at least one located at the end of the defined 3'-UTR (Shen *et al.*, 2011, Wu *et al.*, 2011, Wu *et al.*, 2014, Fu *et al.*, 2016). However, by adding alternative poly(A)-sites in other locations than the 3´UTR, in introns, in exons or even in the 5´-UTR, new transcripts that encode different proteins or regulatory RNAs can be generated. In addition, the new transcripts generated by APA might include or exclude information encoded in certain sequences such as miRNA targets, translation enhancers or RNA stability elements, leading to altered gene expression that may affect to the final phenotype (Blazie *et al.*, 2017). The selection of poly(A)-sites is directed by a protein complex that contains several subunits (Shi and Manley, 2015, Tian and Manley, 2016) that are evolutionary conserved in eukaryotes, suggesting the essential role of polyadenylation of mRNA (Hunt *et al.*, 2008). One of these proteins is FIP1, which acts as a bridge between poly(A) polymerase (PAP) and other subunits of the cleavage and polyadenylation factors (Helmling *et al.*, 2001). In mammals, Fip1 is needed to promote stem

cell self-renewal by mediating the selection of alternative polyadenylation sites (Lackford *et al.*, 2014). Genome-wide analyses of poly(A) site usage shows that Fip1 favors the usage of proximal poly(A)-sites, generating mRNAs with shorter 3′-UTRs.

Recently, a T-DNA allele for *FIP1* (*fip1-1*) has been reported in Arabidopsis (Wang *et al.*, 2018). Although no visible phenotype for this *fip1* allele was presented, it shows several physiological and molecular alterations related mainly with nitrate uptake and assimilation (Wang *et al.*, 2018). In *fip1-1*, the nitrate sensor *NRT1.1* presents alternative polyadenylation in the 3´-UTR, forming a sorter transcript (Wang *et al.*, 2018). Our data also supported a differential poly(A) usage at the 3´UTR of NRT1.1 in *fip1-2* (Table S3e). Remarkably, *fip1-2* accumulates higher phosphate levels and a significant number of genes deregulated in this mutant corresponds to phosphate starvation responses genes. These data, together with the role of *FIP1* in nitrate assimilation, suggest that *FIP1* plays an important role in nutrient uptake and usage.

It is noteworthy that the phenotype of *fip1-2* is similar to that of a mutation in another gene encoding a polyadenylation complex subunit, *CPSF30.* In the null mutant for *CPSF30, oxt6,*  lateral root formation is diminished (Liu *et al.*, 2015). The same nitrate-response phenotype observed in the *fip1-2* mutant (Wang *et al.*, 2018) was found in the *cpsf30-2* mutant (Li *et al.*, 2017). Here, we report that *oxt6*, although to lesser extent than *fip1-2*, also has altered responses to ABA, Cd and salt. FIP1 and CPSF30 enga*g*e in a distinctive set of proteinprotein interactions (Forbes *et al.*, 2006, Addepalli and Hunt, 2007) and form a possible link between two of the large conceptual protein-protein interaction hubs that define the plant polyadenylation complex (Hunt *et al.*, 2008). It is thus tempting to speculate that the phenotypic similarities seen *fip1* and *oxt6* mutants may reflect the actions of these two proteins as part of a common complex. However, the functioning of these two proteins may be more nuanced; for example, *oxt6* seems to mainly influence the progression of lateral root primordia from stages I- to-III, while in the case of *fip1-2*, there is a higher accumulation of LRP in stages IV and V.

Interestingly, *fip1-1* shows higher rate of nitrate assimilation and amino acid (AA) content (Wang *et al.*, 2018). It is possible that the higher nitrate assimilation in *fip1* mutants leads to a higher biosynthesis of amino acids. Pools of free amino acids are significantly smaller than the corresponding pools of protein-bound amino acids and a balance between free AA and proteins is needed for proper development and responses (Hildebrandt *et al.*, 2015). Thus, it is possible that an elevated AA content might stimulate protein synthesis, phenotype observed in *fip1-2*, to store them. On the other hand, it is also possible that this increase in protein translation in *fip1-2* is due to APA modification of a large number of transcripts involved in ribosome biogenesis (ribosomal proteins, RP) and translation regulators. As APA might produce new isoforms of RP and generate "different type of ribosomal protein complexes" that may contribute to the regulation of translation (Browning and Bailey-Serres, 2015, Merchante *et al.*, 2017). Interestingly, different RP paralogs are regulated by environmental clues, including phosphate or iron deficiency (Wang *et al.*, 2013) and mutation in RP leads to developmental defects such as reduced shoot and root growth or cell proliferation (Byrne, 2009). Conversely, APA in these transcripts might eliminate the control of protein translation, leading to continuous protein formation. Based on our transcriptomic data, it is unlikely that the increase in protein synthesis is due to an increase of gene transcription as *fip1-2* shows a larger number of repressed than activated genes. Nevertheless, further analyses have to be done to address the molecular mechanism that increase protein synthesis in *fip1-2*.

Roots have the ability to maintain growth almost indefinitely during their life by producing new cells from stem cell populations in root tips. Stem cells in the root apical meristem as well as from pericycle founder cells are the source of the majority of cells in the root system

(Scheres, 2005). As in animals, plant stem cells are self-renewing and have the potential to form different organs and tissues, and even regenerate an entire organism (Greb and Lohmann, 2016). In the root meristem, *WOX5*, which is regulated by the *SHR-SCR* module, is a root stem cell niche organizer needed to regulate the columella stem cell (CSC) identity (Stahl *et al.*, 2009). At the quiescent centre (QC), *wox5* mutants possess cells with an abnormal shape and which are enlarged in comparison to the control. In addition, columella stem cells adjacent to the QC are larger and precociously differentiated (Sarkar *et al.*, 2007). In addition, loss of function of *WOX5* leads to extra cell divisions of the QC (Forzani *et al.*, 2014). Here we show that the *fip1-2* mutant presents a disorganized QC with larger adjacent columella stem cells and extra QC divisions. This phenotype correlates with lower *WOX5* and *SCR* expression, suggesting that poly(A) selection/usage is important to maintain the stem cell niche and QC in the root meristem. It is worth mentioning that human Fip1 is needed for stem cell maintenance and renewal (Lackford *et al.*, 2014). Thus it is tempting to speculate that FIP1-mediated alternative polyadenylation is an evolutionary-conserved mechanism for stem cell niche maintenance.

## *The role of FIP1 in abiotic stress-associated alternative polyadenylation*

FIP1 is a core subunit of the eukaryotic polyadenylation complex and coordinates RNA processing and polyadenylation via interactions with the poly(A) polymerase as well as other subunits of the complex (Helmling *et al.*, 2001, Kaufmann *et al.*, 2004, Forbes *et al.*, 2006, Addepalli and Hunt, 2007, Zhu *et al.*, 2018). In mammals, depletion of Fip1 leads to lower overall polyadenylation efficiency and greater usage of distal (over proximal) poly(A) sites in 3'-UTRs (Lackford *et al.*, 2014, Li *et al.*, 2015). The results presented in this work provide further insights into the function of *FIP1* in polyadenylation in plants.

The principal effect of the *fip1-2* mutation is a wide-ranging, albeit low-level, usage of sites that are situated near "normal" poly(A) sites, but removed enough so that their upstream regions no longer possess the characteristic A-rich "Near Upstream Element" (Loke *et al.*, 2005) that is a hallmark of plant polyadenylation signals (Figure 3d). While wide-ranging (Figure 3a), the consequences are modest, in that the overall remodeling of mRNAs is minimal (Figure 3b, c). The mechanisms that underlie these pervasive yet unobtrusive changes are not clear. The effects of the *fip1-2* mutation on overall *FIP1* expression are dramatic, as almost all of the transcripts in the mutant possess the included intron (Figure S2b). However, it may be possible that a minimal amount of properly-processed *FIP1* mRNA might suffice for efficient and accurate 3' end formation, with the exception of the low-level alternative poly(A) site usage indicated in Figure 3. However, it should be noted that, in plants, genes that code for FIP1 encode two type of transcripts (Hunt *et al.*, 2012). The shorter of these variants retains only the first exon (Figure S2a), and the encoded polypeptide possesses the capability of interacting with many other subunits of the plant polyadenylation complex (Forbes *et al.*, 2006, Addepalli and Hunt, 2007, Hunt *et al.*, 2008). Importantly, this includes the plant orthologues of CFIm25 (Forbes *et al.*, 2006, Hunt *et al.*, 2008), that mediates interactions between the upstream UGUA in mammalian poly(A) signals with FIP1 and the rest of the complex (Zhu *et al.*, 2018). It may be that most of the functions of FIP1 can be provided by the N-terminal part of the protein, and that the subtle (but wide-ranging) effects of the *fip1-2* mutation reflect a more complicated interplay between different FIP1 isoforms and the rest of the complex. Further research is needed to sort out these possibilities.

The subtle effects of the *fip1-2* mutation on poly(A) site choice belie the more dramatic phenotypes seen in the mutant, such as the diminished capacity to develop lateral roots, leaf growth, the tolerance to salt and the increased sensitivity to cadmium and ABA. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses have identified a large number of genes/proteins

involved in root development and the adaptation of plants to salt stress or ABA responses (Brady *et al.*, 2007, Dinneny *et al.*, 2008, Brady *et al.*, 2011, Li *et al.*, 2013, Guo *et al.*, 2014, Ding *et al.*, 2016, Kumar *et al.*, 2016). It is possible that, among the numerous changes in poly(A) site choice that are seen in the *fip1-2* mutant, some of them affect to important regulatory genes, what might have a large impact on plant developmental and response. However, our results (Figure 5) suggest an interesting alternative. In plants and yeast, a global effect of some abiotic stresses is a shift of transcription from the synthesis of "productive" to "non-productive" mRNA isoforms (Yoon and Brem, 2010, De Lorenzo *et al.*, 2017) via alternative polyadenylation. The present study indicates that *FIP1* is important for the salt-induced production of non-canonical mRNA isoforms (Figure 5). This result, along with the increased tolerance of the *fip1-2* mutant to salt, raises the possibility that the redirecting of transcriptional output to non-canonical isoforms, together with other regulatory mechanisms, is one of the elements of the responses of plants to abiotic stresses. Importantly, the increased production of non-canonical mRNAs would seem to be at the expense of growth, as the *fip1-2* mutant, which is smaller than control seedlings, reduces production of non-canonical mRNAs but improved tolerance to salt. The connections between these have yet to be explored, but one tantalizing possibility deserves to be mentioned. This possibility holds that non-canonical mRNAs might act as long-non-coding RNA. For example, *IPS1* (Martin *et al.*, 2000), a long-non-coding RNA induced by phosphate starvation in Arabidopsis contains a complementary sequence to the phosphate starvation-induced miRNA miR399. Thus, induction of *IPS1* blocks the activity of miR-399 (Franco-Zorrilla *et al.*, 2007). Recently, it has been reported that dehydration stress promotes the extension of 3'- UTR of mRNAs. Although they have characteristics of long noncoding RNAs, they rather act as antisense RNA to regulate gene transcription (Sun *et al.*, 2017). It could be possible that non-canonical mRNA isoforms might, in some cases, act as miRNA repressors or other sorts of RNA regulators. Thus, the balance between RNA-mediated regulation and APA may be an important factor in the responses of plants to abiotic stresses. Similar mechanisms may also be responsible for the other phenotypes associated with the *fip1-2* mutant.

To summarize, we have identified an allele that affects *FIP1* function. We present evidence that root responses to salt excess implies differential poly(A) site selection and usage and FIP1-mediated alternative polyadenylation. In addition, we also show that FIP1 function is important for plant development and responses to ABA and abiotic stress including salt and cadmium. Our results suggest that the functioning of FIP1 in 3' end processing and polyadenylation is more complex than previously thought, and that this protein plays an active role in the remodeling of the transcriptome to regulate the structure and coding potential of transcripts.

#### **Material and Methods**

#### **Plant material**

*SKP2Bp*:GUS (Manzano *et al.*, 2012) seedlings were mutagenized according to the protocol described in ftp://ftp.arabidopsis.org/Protocols/compleat\_guide/ 6\_EMS\_mutagenesis.pdf. F2 populations were screened for root system anomalies and differential GUS staining pattern. In those experiments where *fip1-2* is compared to a control, the control corresponds to SKP2B::GUS seedlings. WOX5::GFP (Sarkar *et al.*, 2007), DR5::GUS (Ulmasov *et al.*, 1997), SKP2B::GUS (Manzano *et al.*, 2012) and SALK\_099558C (NASC stock center) were in Col-0 ecotype. To generate the transcriptional fusion of GUS reporter gene to a promoter region of *FIP1* (pFIP1::GUS), a 2500 bp upstream from the ATG was cloned into the

Gateway vector pDONOR221 and later transferred to pGBW3. Arabidopsis seedlings were transformed as described in (Clough and Bent, 1998) and selected with hygromicin.

All seedlings were sown under sterile conditions on vertically oriented 12 cm square plates containing half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS1/2) 0.05% MES, 1% sucrose and 1% plant-agar (Duchefa Biochemie B.V.). For experiments without phosphate, the medium was similar but no phosphate was added.

#### **Root growth assays, GUS staining and microscopic analysis**

Primary root length was determined as described previously by (Lucas *et al.*, 2011). All data are the mean value of at least 50 plants, and these experiments were repeated twice, obtaining similar values in each experiment. Total number and stages of LRP were counted according to methods used previously (Malamy and Benfey, 1997).

Root meristem size was calculated based on the number of meristematic cortex cells and/or the distance from the quiescent center (QC) to the last meristematic cell using confocal images taken with a Leica Z8 microscopy as described by Silva-Navas *et al.* (2016).

Histochemical GUS staining was performed as described in (del Pozo *et al.*, 2006). Pictures were taken using a Leica stereomicroscope MZ9.5 with a DCF280 camera or a Leica MD2000 microscope with a DCF300 camera

To analyze the effect of ABA on root growth inhibition, 5 day old seedlings were transferred to fresh medium containing 0 (control) or 1 µM of ABA and root growth was measured after 3 days from the transference point to the root tip using the Imagen J software.

To quantify root growth inhibition by salt, 5 day old seedlings were transferred to fresh medium containing 0, 75, 100 or 125 mM of NaCl for 4 extra days. Root growth was measured and represented as percentage of growth in medium without salt.

For cadmium treatments, 5 day old seedlings were transferred to fresh medium containing 0, 50 or 75 µM of Cd (CdCl2) for the indicated time. Afterwards, root growth was measured and GUS staining performed.

#### **Poly(A) Tag library preparation, sequencing and analyses**

 Arabidopsis seedlings were grown 5 days in MS1/2 and then transferred to fresh medium containing 0 or 125 mM of NaCl to induce the salt stress for additional 4 days. Total RNA was isolated from Arabidopsis roots after treatment using the Trizol reagent and RNeasy columns (Qiagen). Quantity and quality measurements were taken using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Poly(A) tags (PATs) were generated with 1 μg of total total RNA. Libraries preparation sequencing and analyses were done as described in De Lorenzo et al. (2017) and as described in methods S1.

Gene ontology analyses were done using the Genecodis3 bioinformatic tool (Tabas-Madrid *et al.*, 2012).

#### **Metabolic Labeling**

Arabidopsis control or *fip1-2* seedlings were grown in MS1/2 plates during 5 days and then approximately 40-50 seedlings were transferred to fresh medium containing 0 or 150 mM of NaCl for 16 hours. Both samples were metabolically labeled with 50µCi/ml 35Smethionine/35S-cysteine (EasyTag™ EXPRESS35S Protein Labeling Mix, Perkin Elmer). The

radiolabeled amino acid mixture was added to a whatman paper that covered the root system for 60 min. Afterwards, roots and shoot were manually separated and immediately frozen. Protein were extracted with PBS-triton buffer and disrupted with glass beads for 10 seconds. After 10 minutes of incubation on ice, protein fraction was separated of plant insoluble material by centrifugation (10 min at 13.000 rpm). The resulting protein extracts were normalized according to protein concentration and 10 µg of each lysate was subjected to SDS-PAGE and gel was stained with Coomassie-blue. The stained gel was dried and exposed using the BAS-SR imaging plates to visualize the new synthesized proteins.

#### **Statistical Analyses**

The data were statically analyzed by one-way ANOVA using the on-line tool (http://statistica.mooo.com/OneWay\_Anova\_with\_TukeyHSD). Univariate analyses were performed with Tukey's post hoc test. When indicated, T-test (two-tails) was used to assess differences between two samples.
