# <sup>43</sup>**Abstract**

44 Root architecture varies widely between species, and even between ecotypes of the 45 same species, despite the strong conservation of the coding portion of their genomes. By 46 contrast, non-coding RNAs evolve rapidly between ecotypes and may control their 47 differential responses to the environment, since several long non-coding RNAs 48 (lncRNAs) are known to quantitatively regulate gene expression. Roots from Columbia 49 (Col) and Landsberg *erecta* (L*er*) ecotypes respond differently to phosphate starvation. 50 Here, we compared transcriptomes (mRNAs, lncRNAs, and small RNAs) of root tips from 51 these two ecotypes during early phosphate starvation. We identified thousands of 52 lncRNAs that were largely conserved at the DNA level in these ecotypes. In contrast to 53 coding genes, many lncRNAs were specifically transcribed in one ecotype and/or 54 differentially expressed between ecotypes independent of phosphate availability. We 55 further characterized these ecotype-related lncRNAs and studied their link with siRNAs. 56 Our analysis identified 675 lncRNAs differentially expressed between the two ecotypes, 57 including antisense RNAs targeting key regulators of root-growth responses. Mis-58 regulation of several intergenic lncRNAs showed that at least two ecotype-related 59 lncRNAs regulate primary root growth in Col. RNA-seq analysis following the 60 deregulation of the lncRNA *NPC48* revealed a potential link with root growth and 61 transport functions. This exploration of the non-coding transcriptome identified 62 ecotype-specific lncRNAs-mediated regulation in root apexes. The non-coding genome 63 may harbor further mechanisms involved in ecotype adaptation of roots to different soil 64 environments.

<sup>66</sup>**Introduction** 

67 Over the last decade, genome-wide transcriptomics has revealed that a large 68 intergenic part of eukaryotic genomes is transcribed. These transcripts, globally known 69 as non-coding RNAs (Ariel et al., 2015) may regulate genome expression at 70 transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and epigenetic levels, and are generally classified 71 as small (21–24 nt), long (> 200 nt, < 100 kb), and circular (circRNAs) non-coding RNAs. 72 Plant small RNAs (sRNAs) are produced by processing longer non-coding transcripts 73 that generally contain a hairpin structure or lead to double-stranded RNA formation. 74 Plant sRNAs include miRNAs, endogenous siRNAs (generally 21–22 nt long), and the 75 most abundant heterochromatin siRNA (hc-siRNAs with a length of 24 nt (Borges and 76 Martienssen, 2015). On the other hand, long non–coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a 77 heterogeneous group of RNA molecules with a coding capacity shorter than 50 amino 78 acids (Chekanova, 2015). LncRNA transcripts are generally polyadenylated (polyA) and 79 can be intergenic (lincRNAs), intronic, or natural antisense (NATs) with respect to 80 protein-coding genes (Ariel et al., 2015). When compared to mRNAs, lncRNAs are 81 expressed at low levels in a tissue-specific manner or in response to environmental 82 stresses (Liu et al., 2012) and are more frequently accumulated in the nucleus (Derrien 83 et al., 2012) where they can regulate nuclear organization or function (Ariel et al., 2015).

84 LncRNAs utilize both *cis* and *trans* modalities of action to regulate gene 85 expression through interactions with ribonucleoproteins, and can form scaffolds and/or 86 sequester proteins or RNA molecules as decoys or sponges. However, molecular 87 functions have only been identified for a few lncRNAs in plants. As lncRNA genes lack 88 regions with high primary sequence constraints (Derrien et al., 2012), it is difficult to 89 use sequence conservation to identify potential functions. Even though the sequences of 90 lncRNAs are not particularly conserved between plant species, they may show relative 91 positional conservation in genomes (Mohammadin et al., 2015).

92 Finally, lncRNAs could be simply transcriptional by-products; in this framework 93 the sole act of their transcription rather than their sequence *per se* would be the source 94 of the regulatory activity (Kopp and Mendell, 2018).

95 Resequencing approaches in model species have allowed the determination of 96 whole-genome variations and evolution, from which it has been possible to provide the 97 characterization of pan-genomes composed of "core" genomes (present in all 98 accessions) and "dispensable" genomes (specific to two or more accessions or even 99 unique sequences specific to only one accession). Core genes are frequently highly 100 expressed whereas dispensable genes are variably expressed, and generally in a tissue-101 specific manner (Contreras-Moreira et al., 2017). The dispensable genomes may play 102 important roles in the capacity of individual organisms to cope with environmental 103 conditions (Vernikos et al., 2015). Indeed, identification of natural variations in large 104 worldwide populations (accessions) of *Arabidopsis* showed an average of one SNP per 105 10 bp more frequently located in intergenic regions than in coding mRNAs (The 1001 106 Genomes Consortium, 2016). This has also been observed recently in rice for which only 107 3.5% of SNPs and 2.5% of small InDels were located in coding regions (Zhao et al., 108 2018). This latter observation would explain why lncRNAs differ even between closely 109 related plant species (Nelson et al., 2017). In plants, three mechanisms have been 110 proposed for the origin of lncRNAs: evolution from TE sequences, pseudogenization of 111 protein-coding gene sequences, or duplication of preexisting lncRNAs (Kapusta and 112 Feschotte, 2014)

113 The inorganic phosphate (Pi) accumulated in the upper soil layer is perceived by 114 plants at the root apex (Svistoonoff et al., 2007). The Col and L*er* accessions display 115 different primary root growth and architecture in response to Pi starvation (Reymond et 116 al., 2006). The identification of LPR1 (LOW PHOSPHATE ROOT1), a major QTL, has been 117 done in RIL lines obtained by the crosses of accessions presenting this opposite root 118 response to low Pi and it has been linked to a differential expression of *LPR1* in root 119 apexes (Reymond et al., 2006; Svistoonoff et al., 2007). When the primary root tip of a 120 Col seedling encounters a low Pi medium, cell elongation in the transition zone rapidly 121 decreases and cell proliferation in the root apical meristem (RAM) progressively ceases 122 as callose deposition occurs in RAM plasmodesmata (Müller et al., 2015; Abel, 2017; 123 Gutiérrez-Alanís et al., 2018). Root growth inhibition in low Pi depends on iron (Fe) 124 availability in soil or media (Svistoonoff et al., 2007; Ward et al., 2008) as Fe 125 concentrations clearly increase in Col plants during phosphate starvation (Misson et al., 126 2005; Hirsch et al., 2006; Baxter et al., 2008). Indeed, inhibition of cell elongation and 127 the RAM arrest are Fe-dependent (Svistoonoff et al., 2007; Ward et al., 2008; Müller et 128 al., 2015; Abel, 2017; Balzergue et al., 2017; Mora-Macías et al., 2017; Gutiérrez-Alanís et 129 al., 2018). Interestingly, in low-Pi conditions, Fe accumulates in the elongation zone but 130 not in the RAM, and more generally, in Col plants, Fe is redistributed among tissues 131 (Mora-Macías et al., 2017; Gutiérrez-Alanís et al., 2018). By contrast, in L*er* seedlings 132 that are subject to low Pi, elongation and proliferation of root cells in the root apex

133 continue, thereby sustaining root growth (Reymond et al., 2006). The corresponding 134 regulatory system controlling root inhibition involves LPR1, PDR2 (PHOSPHATE 135 DEFICIENCY RESPONSE 2), STOP1 (SENSITIVE TO PROTON RHIZOTOXICITY 1), and 136 ALMT1 (ALUMINIUM-ACTIVATED MALATE TRANSPORTER1). The LPR1-PDR2 and 137 STOP1-ALMT1 modules allow Fe accumulation in roots under low Pi (Ticconi et al., 138 2009; Abel, 2017; Balzergue et al., 2017; Gutiérrez-Alanís et al., 2018). From these 139 results, one concludes that interactions between Pi and Fe determine the differential 140 growth response of Col and L*er* ecotypes. In the present work, we identified and 141 characterized the non-coding transcriptomes of Col and L*er* root apexes during early 142 phosphate starvation responses. Thousands of *Arabidopsis* lncRNAs, notably in the L*er* 143 accession, were identified with only a minor fraction being linked to small RNA 144 production. Several "ecotype-specific" or "-enriched" variants were highly conserved at 145 the DNA level and showed expression variation correlated with changes in the 146 expression of key regulators of phosphate-starvation response. Functional analysis of 147 five lncRNAs in Col revealed two further regulators of primary root growth, allowing us 148 to hypothesize that lncRNA expression patterns contribute to the modulation of 149 environmental responses in different ecotypes.

# <sup>151</sup>**Results**

### 152 *Columbia and Landsberg root tip transcriptome assemblies*

153 We characterized the root-tip transcriptome of Columbia (Col) and Landsberg 154 *erecta* (L*er*) ecotypes, which present contrasting root phenotypes in response to Pi 155 deficiency (Reymond et al., 2006). We performed comparative whole-genome 156 transcriptomic analyses using paired-end sequencing of three biological replicates of 157 root tips during a short kinetics (0, 1, and 2 h) of low 10µM phosphate treatment (Table 158 S1). To avoid possible differences related to the *erecta* mutation present in L*er* ecotype, we used here the Coler105 159 mutant. For each ecotype, the reads were independently 160 mapped to their reference genome (Fig. S1A): TAIR10 for Col (Lamesch et al., 2012) and 161 L*er* v7 for L*er* (Gan et al., 2011) that shared the same TAIR10 annotation (unlike the L*er* 162 v8 Zapata et al. (2016)). We predicted previously unannotated transcripts by comparing 163 our data to TAIR10. The homology of these predicted transcripts in Col and L*er* was 164 determined by mapping them onto the other genome. We retained as transcripts only 165 RNA molecules of at least 200 nt. When these previously uncharacterized transcripts 166 overlapped with pre-existing annotations, fusions were generated. Transcripts 167 identified by this pipeline (Supplemental Fig. S1A) were compared with those from 168 different *Arabidopsis* databases: Araport 11 (Cheng et al., 2017), RepTas (Liu et al., 169 2012), CANTATAdb (Szcześniak et al., 2016), miRBase v21 (Kozomara and Griffiths-170 Jones, 2014), and with two previous studies concerning lncRNAs (Amor et al., 2009; Li et 171 al., 2016). Finally, we used the COME software to determine the potential coding 172 capacity of identified transcripts (Hu et al., 2017). On the basis of both database 173 information and COME predictions we classified the corresponding genes as coding or 174 non-coding.

175 In total, we identified 5313 and 6408 previously uncharacterized putative genes 176 respectively in Col and L*er* ecotypes (Fig. 1A; Supplemental Table S2; File S1 and S2). In 177 root apexes, these identified genes were predominantly non-coding RNAs: 76% for Col 178 and 77% for L*er* of total previously uncharacterized genes (Fig. 1A; Supplemental Fig. 179 S1B and C). As expected, non-coding genes were globally less expressed than coding 180 genes (Supplemental Fig. S2A and B). Genes specifically detected in one ecotype belong 181 much more to the non-coding class (>40% of the total non-coding genes) than to the 182 coding one (<8% of the total coding genes; Fig. 1B and C), notably for intergenic lncRNA 183 genes (52% of the total lncRNAs) than for NATs (34% of the total NATs; Fig. 1D and E). 184 Overall, the expression of non-coding genes is more ecotype-specific than for coding 185 genes.

186 We detected a greater number of previously uncharacterized genes in the L*er* 187 ecotype (Fig. 1A and C). Such differences do not result from library sequencing 188 saturation. Indeed, in the last 2% of sequencing reads, less than 10 additional of these 189 genes were detected (Supplemental Fig. S2C). Thus, sequencing was deep enough to 190 detect expressed genes and the difference of gene detection between Col and L*er* does 191 not result from a sequencing bias.

192 We next sought whether these previously uncharacterized detected genes 193 expressed in L*er* (coding or non-coding) could correspond to specific parts of L*er* 194 genome missed or rearranged in Col genome. Out of these 7357 genes, only 41 and 53 195 genes in Col and L*er*, respectively, coincided with missing DNA sequences in the other 196 ecotype (Fig. 2A) showing that the DNA sequence of the different previously 197 uncharacterized genes is largely conserved apart from a few SNPs. Thus, the ecotype 198 differences in transcript accumulation came from a shift in transcription that could be 199 due to the deregulation of gene regulators, the accumulation of small sequence 200 differences in promoters, or the consequence of specific differences in epigenetic status 201 in the lncRNA-producing region due to TE insertions or other rearrangements possibly 202 at large distance from the differentially expressed loci.

### 203 *Evolutionary analysis of lncRNA genes expressed in root tips*

204 Mechanisms modifying root architecture result from local signaling that occurs at 205 the root tip (Svistoonoff et al., 2007; Thibaud et al., 2010; Müller et al., 2015; Balzergue 206 et al., 2017). We therefore characterized the *Arabidopsis* genes expressed in the root 207 apex, taking advantage of the extensive sequence information in *Arabidopsis* accessions 208 (The 1001 Genomes Consortium, 2016). According to current annotations, genes were 209 considered as non-NAT (no gene on the other strand) or NAT (presence of a gene on the 210 other strand). For *Arabidopsis* species, we calculated the rate of SNP accumulated in the 211 different types of genes among all accessions (Fig. 2B). As expected, TEs accumulated 212 many more SNPs than coding genes, whereas non-NAT lncRNAs and structural RNA 213 genes showed an intermediate level of SNPs between TEs and coding genes. By contrast, 214 the amount of SNPs was generally similar for NAT lncRNA and coding genes, as can be 215 justified by the fact that the coding regions are under strong selection pressure.

216 To investigate sequence evolution at a larger scale, we used the PhastCons score 217 that represents an inter-species level of nucleotide conservation (normalized between 0 218 and 1) according to the alignment of 20 Angiosperm genomes (Fig. 2C, (Hupalo and 219 Kern, 2013)). As expected, structural RNA genes were strongly conserved (median score 220 of 1), whereas transposable elements were not (median score of 0). Coding genes 221 presented a score between these two extremes (around 0.5). Interestingly, non-NAT 222 lncRNA genes showed an intermediate score between coding and transposable genes 223 (median score around 0.3) whereas NAT lncRNA genes had again the same degree of 224 conservation as coding genes. These observations further suggest that NAT lncRNA 225 genes are strongly constrained whereas intergenic non-coding genes allow more 226 variability even though they are more constrained than TEs.

### 227 *Few lncRNA transcripts co-localize with small RNA-generating loci*

228 In animals and plants, some lncRNA loci co-localize with regions producing small 229 RNA molecules (Matzke and Mosher, 2014). Therefore, we asked whether the lncRNAs 230 loci identified could generate sRNAs. Using similar samples previously prepared for the 231 lncRNA studies, we prepared small RNA libraries for each ecotype and sequenced sRNAs 232 to obtain a full description of the sRNAome mapped on each ecotype genome. Only a 233 minority of the lncRNAs accumulate sRNAs, but of those, most contained sequences 234 capable of generating non-phased RNA molecules of 21/22 nt or 24 nt (Supplemental 235 Fig. S3A and B) and only a small fraction of lncRNAs overlapped with phasiRNAs or were 236 miRNA precursors.

237 We then analyzed the potential link between siRNAs and lncRNAs in each 238 ecotype. The majority of lncRNAs did not lead to accumulation of siRNAs (6452 genes 239 out of 7850 detected). This is also true for the lncRNAs specifically detected only in one 240 ecotype since many of them did not generate any siRNA in either ecotype (2688 genes 241 out of 3110 ecotype-specific genes; Fig. 3A, long in Col and ND in L*er* or *vice versa*). Thus, 242 the differential detection of lncRNA between ecotypes could not be linked to a change in 243 the processing of siRNA from the encoding lncRNA loci.

244 We then wondered whether different sRNA processing from lncRNAs could occur 245 between the two ecotypes. We first looked at lncRNA that accumulated sRNA only in one 246 accession: (i) lncRNAs that could generate siRNAs in only one ecotype while detected as 247 lncRNAs in both ecotypes (long in L*er* and long and small in Col and *vice versa*, 248 respectively 113 and 240 genes) and (ii) loci that produced only siRNAs in one ecotype 249 yet detected as lncRNAs only in the other one (long in L*er* and small in Col or *vice versa*, 250 respectively 57 and 47 genes). It is known that 21/22-nt siRNAs act on gene transcripts 251 whereas 24-nt siRNAs mediate chromatin modifications (Matzke and Mosher, 2014). 252 Thus, a difference in the size of accumulated siRNAs for a given gene in one ecotype 253 could indicate a modification of post-transcriptional (21/22 nt) or epigenetic (24 nt) 254 regulations in the other ecotype. Among the lncRNA genes accumulating siRNA, a large 255 portion accumulated the same size in both ecotypes (591 for 21/22-nt siRNA and 391 256 for 24-nt siRNA) or produced siRNA in just one ecotype (367 for 21/22-nt and 316 for 257 24-nt, Fig. 3B). Only 29 lncRNA genes accumulated a different size of siRNAs between 258 the two ecotypes among the 1694 lncRNA genes accumulating siRNAs. Therefore, no 259 *major* change of reciprocal posttranscriptional or transcriptional regulation of lncRNA 260 by sRNAs could be established between ecotypes.

261 Finally, we investigated the specificity of detection of sRNAs between the two 262 ecotypes. First, we studied lncRNA genes predicted to produce phased 21/22-nt siRNAs. 263 Among the 7 predicted lncRNAs, only 2 were specific to L*er* (Supplemental Fig. S3C). 264 Second, searching for miRNA loci, we found that 23 and 12 miRNAs of the 191 detected 265 miRNAs were specifically detected respectively in Col and L*er* (Supplemental Fig. S3D), a 266 proportion related to the variation detected for protein-coding genes. Third, we 267 analyzed the proportion of specific expression for the vast majority of 21/22-nt and 24- 268 nt siRNAs located in coding or non-coding genes. Altogether, the L*er* ecotype produces a 269 larger number of 21/22-nt siRNAs specifically linked to this ecotype (Fig 3 C and E) 270 whereas Col is more enriched for ecotype-specific 24-nt siRNAs (Fig. 3D and F), 271 potentially suggesting that, in these loci, links with differential post-transcriptional and 272 epigenetic regulations among ecotypes occurred.

273 Overall, the major difference in the non-coding transcriptome of the two ecotypes 274 was linked to lncRNAs and not associated to small RNAs, even though in certain cases 275 small RNAs may be involved in ecotype-specific regulations.

### 276 *Differential accumulation of transcripts between ecotypes in early response* 277 *to phosphate deficiency*

278 The root growth arrest of Col ecotype occurs in the first hours of low-phosphate 279 sensing by the root tip (Balzergue et al., 2017), whereas root growth continues in L*er*. To 280 determine the effects of short kinetics of phosphate deficiency, we examined gene 281 expression patterns in the two ecotypes in response to this stress. Principal component 282 analysis (PCA) showed a data dispersion that allowed a clear distinction between effects 283 of the ecotype (first axis, Supplemental Fig. S4A) and of the kinetics (second axis, 284 Supplemental Fig. S4A). Thus, we used a multifactor analysis that takes into account the 285 ecotype, the kinetics, and their interaction to investigate differential gene expression 286 independent of being classified as coding or non-coding genes, as they had comparable 287 dispersion in our experiments. For each comparison, we confirmed the distribution of p-288 values as a criterion of statistical robustness (Rigaill et al., 2018). After processing the

289 differential analyses, we interpreted the results by separating the genes as "coding" or 290 "non-coding" using the categories defined previously.

291 For coding genes, we observed 3315 genes differentially expressed between the 292 two ecotypes over the kinetics with 2504 differentially expressed between at least two 293 points of the kinetics over the 2 ecotypes (Fig. 4A; Supplemental Fig. S4B Table S3). The 294 number of differentially expressed coding genes between ecotypes or along the stress 295 kinetics was similar. However, the response to phosphate starvation of only 55 genes 296 was significantly impacted by the ecotype ("interaction" of both factors, Fig. 4A). 297 Respectively 1566 and 1749 coding genes were up-regulated in Col and in L*er* along the 298 kinetics (Fig. S4B). Interestingly, a clear bias of expression between ecotypes could be 299 observed for non-coding genes (Fig. 4B). Indeed, 675 (666 + 6 + 2 +1) non-coding genes 300 were differentially expressed between the two ecotypes whereas only 70 (61 + 6 + 2) 301 were differentially expressed along at least one point of the kinetics. Comparable biases 302 were observed for both classes of non-coding genes, lincRNAs and NATs (Fig. 4C and D). 303 Globally, 146 lincRNAs and 236 NATs were significantly up-regulated in Col compared to 304 L*er* and 106 lincRNAs and 187 NATs in L*er* compared to Col (Supplemental Fig. S4C).

305 We used reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis of independent replicates of Col, Coler105 306 , and L*er* to confirm the differential expression of 307 14 intergenic lncRNA genes (7 in Col and 7 in L*er*) previously identified in the RNA-seq 308 analysis. We were thereby able to confirm the differential expression of 12 lncRNAs (Fig. 5 A and B). Globally Col and Coler105 309 showed similar expression levels despite minor 310 differences. To investigate any dominant expression effect from one ecotype, we 311 investigated the level of expression of these lncRNAs in the F1 offspring of Col and L*er*

312 crosses. Among the 12 differentially expressed genes, an intermediate level of 313 expression was always detected (8 were statistically significant; Fig. 5A and B). This 314 suggests independent regulation of lncRNAs between the parental genomes and 315 discards major dominant "trans" regulatory effects of lncRNA expression between 316 genomes.

317 One interesting possibility is that specific lncRNAs may be expressed in the Col 318 and L*er* genomes in relation with known regulators of the phosphate-starvation 319 response. As a first such case, we were able to identify two specific *Ler* antisense 320 lncRNAs to the phosphate transporter *AT5G43370/PHT1.2* gene (Mudge et al., 2002), 321 which is expressed at a higher level in L*er* compared to Col (Supplemental Fig. S5A). The 322 increase of phosphate transporter expression in L*er* might impart an increased Pi 323 uptake. As a second case, we found that a Col-expressed NAT RNA is complementary to 324 *SPX4*, a critical regulator of phosphate responses (Duan et al., 2008); in our analysis, it 325 shows reduced expression in Col compared to L*er* (Supplemental Fig S5B). In other 326 cases, we observed that two consecutive coding transcripts showing differential levels of 327 expression among ecotypes flank an intergenic lncRNA with an ecotype-specific 328 expression pattern (Supplemental Fig. S5C), suggesting that various cis-effects may be 329 involved in these differential ecotype-linked expression patterns.

### 330 *Differential accumulations of small RNAs between ecotypes*

331 The differential accumulations of small RNAs of 21/22 nt and 24 nt were also 332 examined in each ecotype and during the Pi starvation response. PCA of these 333 sequencing data again clearly separated small RNA abundance between ecotypes but not 334 at the level of the kinetics response (Supplemental Table S3 and Fig S6A and B). We 335 identified 416 coding and 211 non-coding genes that accumulated 21/22-nt siRNAs 336 differentially between ecotypes with generally more siRNAs in L*er* (298 coding genes, 83 337 lincRNAs and 40 NATs) than in Col (118 coding genes, 49 lincRNAs and 39 NATs; Table 338 S3; Supplemental Fig. S6D). A greater number of genes differentially accumulated 24-nt 339 siRNAs between ecotypes showing up-regulated siRNAs in L*er* (758 coding genes, 189 340 lincRNAs and 67 NATs) compared to Col (391 coding genes, 104 lincRNAs and 69 NATs; 341 Supplemental Fig. S6E).

342 Concerning miRNA, as for other small RNAs, the PCA analysis showed only 343 differences between ecotypes (Fig. S6C). Indeed, 38 miRNAs were differentially 344 expressed between the two ecotypes (15 and 23 for Col and Ler respectively, Fig. S6F, 345 Table S3). Interestingly, the families of miR399 and miR397 were specifically 346 accumulated in the L*er* ecotype. These miRNAs target the *PHOSPHATE 2* (*PHO2*) and 347 *NITROGEN LIMITATION ADAPTATION* (*NLA*) transcripts, the encoded proteins of which 348 are known to act together to allow the degradation of the phosphate transporter PHT1;4 349 (Park et al., 2014). In L*er*, the higher amount of miR399 and miR397 might be expected 350 to lead to a lower level of PHO2 and NLA and therefore a higher level of PHT1;4 protein; 351 up-regulation could increase Pi uptake if there were no counter-acting post translational 352 regulations affecting Pi transporters (Bayle et al., 2011; Nussaume et al., 2011; Ayadi et 353 al., 2015). However, we observed no difference in the accumulation of transcripts 354 between ecotypes of those targets, namely *PHO2* and *NLA*, nor of *PHT1;4* (as also 355 previously reported (Shin et al., 2004; Ayadi et al., 2015)). This result suggests that the 356 promoter activity of *PHO2* and *NLA* may compensate for the increased accumulation of 357 these miRNAs in *Ler*.

#### 358 *Mis-regulation of lncRNA expression affects primary root growth in Col*

359 The different patterns of lncRNAs expression between ecotypes may induce 360 regulations of root-growth responses. We selected 5 lncRNA genes: *NPC15*, *NPC34*, 361 *NPC43*, *NPC48*, and *NPC72* showing differential expression among ecotypes in order to 362 study the impact of their expression on Col primary root growth. In RNA-seq data, three 363 of these lncRNA genes were more highly expressed in Col (*NPC15*, *NPC43*, and *NPC72*) 364 and two in L*er* (*NPC34* and *NPC48*) (Fig. 6A). The potential dominant expression 365 patterns of these lncRNA genes were evaluated in two F1s of reciprocal crosses Col x 366 L*er*. Expression analysis by RT-qPCR confirmed the RNA-seq results for *NPC15*, *NPC34*, 367 and *NPC72* genes (Fig. 6B), whereas for *NPC48*, the differential expression was only detected in the Coler105 368 mutant, suggesting that the *erecta* mutation affects the 369 expression of this gene. No differential expression could be detected for the *NPC43* gene. 370 This latter result might be due to the concomitant accumulation of antisense transcripts 371 (*NPC504*) in this locus. The differential expression of lncRNA genes between ecotypes 372 could be linked to genetic changes at their loci. No significant modifications (except a 373 few SNPs) were detected between Col and L*er* for the *NPC34*, *NPC43*, and *NPC48* loci 374 (Fig. S7). By contrast, the *NPC15* locus contains an insertion of 2417 nt in L*er* v8 and 375 *NPC72* is completely missing in L*er* v7 and v8 genomes (Fig. S7 A to E). Therefore, 376 genome modifications at the locus level could explain the specific expression pattern of 377 *NPC72* and *NPC15* genes in L*er*.

378 To support the potential actions of lncRNAs at a phenotypic level, we used over-379 expressing (35S promoter) and T-DNA insertion lines for those same five genes (*NPC15*, 380 *NPC34*, *NPC43*, *NPC48*, and *NPC72*) in Col to monitor the effects on root growth in 381 control and low-phosphate conditions. In control conditions, only *NPC48* and *NPC72* 382 overexpression lines led to a significant root growth reduction when compared to Col 383 (Fig. 7 A and B, Fig S8A to E). The T-DNA insertions have been mapped in the 5′ region of 384 the *NPC48* and *NPC72* loci. In these lines, the lncRNAs were overexpressed (Fig. 7C). 385 Furthermore the *npc48* T-DNA line strongly supported the phenotype observed with the 386 *35S:NPC48* lines. Repeating the analysis in low-phosphate conditions known to inhibit 387 root growth in Col but not in L*er*, we observed minor differences in root length across 388 the different lines. *A priori*, the ratio of root growth in control and low-phosphate 389 conditions should highlight potential differences in phosphate sensitivity of transgenic 390 lines. This ratio was significantly increased for *NPC48* and *NPC72* lines compared to Col 391 (Fig S8F).

392 *NPC48* and *NPC72* de-regulated lines presented a significant decrease of root 393 length in control, but not in low-phosphate conditions. Perhaps, even in control 394 conditions, the mutants might be acting as partially limited in Pi. Hence, we asked 395 whether these phenotypes could be linked to a root growth arrest due to *oversensitive* 396 perception of phosphate starvation under control conditions or an alteration of Pi 397 systemic sensing (which would affect Pi uptake). This does not seem to be the case as i) 398 known phosphate-starvation markers were not deregulated in roots under control 399 conditions and ii) these markers were induced in these lines to the same extent as in Col 400 (Fig. S9A and B). Then, we investigated the local Pi signaling response, exploiting the 401 genes *LOW PHOSPHATE ROOT 1* and *2* (*LPRs*) and the transcription factor *STOP1* that are 402 known to be locally involved in primary root growth arrest under low Pi (Svistoonoff et 403 al., 2007; Ticconi et al., 2009; Müller et al., 2015; Balzergue et al., 2017; Mora-Macías et 404 al., 2017). Expression analysis of *NPC48* and *NPC72* genes in *lpr1/lpr2* and *stop1* mutant 405 lines revealed no significant variation of these lncRNA expression patterns (Fig. 7C). 406 Reciprocally, no significant expression variation was detected for the LPR1/LPR2 407 pathway in *NPC48* or *NPC72* lines (*LPR1*, *LPR2*, *STOP1*, *ALMT1*, and *MATE* genes; Fig. S9C 408 to G).

409 To gain further insight into the function of *NPC48*, we performed an RNA-seq 410 analysis of Col ecotype overexpressing *NPC48* (*35S:NPC48-1*) or not under control 411 conditions (where the phenotype was observed) in order to assess the impact of *NPC48* 412 de-regulation on the genome-wide transcriptome (Fig. 8). Among the 158 differentially 413 expressed genes, 140 were coding genes and the majority was up-regulated in 414 correlation with increased *NPC48* expression (Fig. 8 A and B). On the other hand, the 415 great majority of non-coding genes were downregulated in *35S:NPC48-1*, including two 416 lincRNAs and 15 NATs but none of their antisense coding genes. Since *NPC48* was up-417 regulated in the L*er* ecotype, we asked whether, among the de-regulated genes, several 418 could be linked to ecotype- or phosphate kinetics-related variations (Fig. 8C and 8D). 419 However no such direct link could be made as the large majority of de-regulated genes 420 are not linked to any of these categories. To further confirm the de-regulation of specific 421 targets, we also tested another *35S:NPC48* line showing an intermediate level of 422 expression compared to *35S:NPC48-1* (Fig S10). Several genes could not be confirmed in 423 this second line, notably the strongly down-regulated gene in *35S:NPC48-1* encoding the 424 iron-regulated gene AT3G01260 (Rodríguez-Celma et al., 2013). Among the genes 425 differentially expressed, we could identify several genes linked to nutrient transport and 426 root hair growth such as *ABCB3* (Shibata et al., 2018), *CPL1* (Zhang et al., 2016), and 427 *JAL22* (Diet et al., 2006). *ABCB3* and *CPL1* were repressed by the overexpression of 428 *NPC48* in contrast to *JAL22* (Fig. S10). There were also several genes encoding known

429 growth regulators of primary root growth that may be potentially linked to the *NPC48* 430 overexpression phenotype (Table S8), such as *RGF7*, *BIG*, *RPK2*, and *CASP5* that were up-431 regulated. Differentially expressed genes are significantly enriched in genes regulated during iron starvation (25 genes out of 138, p=1.62 × 10-3 432 , (Rodríguez-Celma et al., 433 2013)). Hence, we tested, in the two different *NPC48* overexpressing lines, whether the 434 overexpression of *NPC48* might lead to iron-related phenotypes such as: i) modification 435 of the expression of genes related to iron responses (Fig S11A and B) and/or ii) 436 significant root growth phenotype in response to changes in iron concentration of the 437 growth medium (Fig. S11C and D) or iii) modification of iron accumulation in root tips 438 (Fig. S11E and F). However, no clear link could be made with any alteration of Fe-related 439 responses dependent on *NPC48* mis-regulation.

440 Altogether, the identification of ecotype-related lncRNAs allowed us to 441 characterize further regulators of primary root growth. However, the over-expression of 442 *NPC48* did not affect the Col transcriptome in a manner that could be linked to L*er* 443 expression patterns. Nevertheless, several genes related to transport, growth regulation, 444 and root-hair function are deregulated by mis-regulation of this ecotype-specific 445 lncRNA.

# <sup>447</sup>**Discussion**

448 Until recently, transcriptome studies were mainly focused on protein-coding gene 449 transcripts and ignored lncRNAs. Variation in the nucleotide sequences or expression 450 patterns of the non-coding genome can have less pleiotropic effects than changes in the 451 protein sequence of critical regulators. However, it is now commonly accepted that 452 lncRNAs can play central roles in development and response to environmental 453 conditions by their expression in a particular cell-type (Ariel et al., 2015). In the present 454 study, by using strand-specific RNA-seq analysis in root tips, we identified thousands of 455 previously uncharacterized lncRNAs (lincRNAs and NATs or antisense RNAs) expressed 456 at low levels from two *Arabidopsis* accessions. We focused on root growth as it is a 457 complex trait that is responsive to the soil environment (Petricka et al., 2012) and 458 impacts a large number of loci spread across the genome. Interestingly, in our study, we 459 observed many lncRNA genes differentially or specifically expressed in Col or L*er*, which 460 was in contrast to protein-coding genes. It had already been shown in other systems that 461 intraspecies variation is strongly linked to the non-coding part of the genome. For 462 example, approximately 45% of disease–associated human SNPs mapped to non-coding 463 regions of the genome (Ning et al., 2017). In chicken, domestication traits governing 464 body morphology or behavior are under selection and often associated with lncRNA 465 genes (Wang et al., 2017). Similarly, in plants, the comparison of SNPs associated with 466 fruit phenotypes in two tomato cultivars also corresponded to non-coding genomic 467 regions (Scarano et al., 2017). The SNPs could act directly at the level of lncRNA 468 expression or affect the expression of lncRNA-neighboring genes (Kopp and Mendell, 469 2018). LncRNAs are thus elements to be considered in genetic association studies.

470 Surprisingly, very few ecotype-specific lncRNAs coincided with the deletion of 471 specific DNA sequences in one particular ecotype (Fig 3). Hence, we propose that the 472 lncRNA differential expression of a relatively similar DNA molecule results from shifts in 473 transcription rate or stability of lncRNAs that could be connected to SNP or InDels 474 polymorphisms in promoters and/or rearrangements distant from lncRNA loci in the 475 two ecotypes (e.g. transposon insertions). As lncRNAs can repress or activate the 476 transcription of other genes, the expression polymorphisms observed between the two 477 ecotypes could also result in a cascade of *cis*-local or *trans*-distal action on target genes 478 (Ariel et al., 2015; Marchese et al., 2017). It is noteworthy that the majority of ecotype-479 specific lncRNAs identified did not co-localize with siRNAs and thus could not reflect 480 only putative gene silencing differences between ecotypes (either transcriptional or 481 post-transcriptional processes, (Matzke and Mosher, 2014)). This points to the lncRNA 482 itself or its transcription *per se* to be linked to the quantitative regulation of target gene 483 expression (Marchese et al., 2017).

484 We were able to confirm, by RT-qPCR, the expression levels of 12 lincRNAs 485 among the 14 chosen for validation, supporting the expression variation identified by 486 RNA-seq (Fig 5). Allele-specific expression is known to affect productivity in plants 487 (Springer and Stupar, 2007). Moreover, in *Arabidopsis*, heterosis has been reported for 488 different traits such as flowering time (Seymour et al., 2016) or phosphate acquisition 489 (Narang and Altmann, 2001). As lncRNAs are able to modify chromatin and thus alter 490 gene expression, we added in our expression analysis the study of the F1 resulting from 491 reciprocal crosses between Col and L*er*. In the F1 hybrid, the 12 confirmed differentially 492 expressed lncRNA genes chosen for validation exhibited globally an additive expression 493 pattern when compared to their parents. This is consistent with results obtained in 494 maize F1 hybrids, where additivity is frequently observed for lncRNAs (Li et al., 2014).

495 The analysis of pan-genome (restricted to coding genes) of 19 *Arabidopsis* 496 ecotypes showed that at least 70 accessory genes could be identified in each ecotype 497 (Contreras-Moreira et al., 2017). In response to stress, accessory genes can explain at 498 least part of the phenotypic difference of behavior observed among ecotypes (Gan et al., 499 2011). Since we find that lncRNAs have a lower selection pressure (Fig 2), our results 500 give further evidence that they might play a similar role as accessory coding genes in 501 response to environmental changes.

502 Root apexes play an important role in sensing external stimuli. We examined the 503 gene expression profile soon after stress application (1 and 2 h) in two ecotypes that 504 present differences in response to phosphate starvation. In the two ecotypes, a similar 505 number of coding genes were differentially expressed during the stress kinetics or 506 between ecotypes. By contrast, a clear bias of specific expression of lncRNAs and siRNAs 507 was identified. However, among the coding genes that are differentially expressed along 508 the phosphate kinetics, one quarter are also differentially expressed between the two 509 ecotypes. For the non-coding genes, these represent only one eighth (Fig 4). This 510 suggests that the main part of the ecotype-specific phosphate response comes from the 511 coding portion of the genome. However, despite not responding to early stress 512 application, lncRNAs may still influence the plant response to stress by priming the 513 chromatin conformation for a fast response of the coding part of the genome.

514 The general adaptation of root architecture in response to low Pi consists of an 515 arrest of primary root growth after the perception of Pi limitation. In *Arabidopsis*, 516 studies concerning plant phosphate homeostasis during Pi deficiency characterized the 517 *IPS1*/*AT4* lncRNA controlling the distribution of Pi from root to shoot. It acts as a target 518 mimic for miR399, which regulates *PHO2* mRNAs (Lin et al., 2008). Moreover, Yuan et al. 519 (2016) identified lncRNAs differentially expressed in roots and shoots of plants grown 520 in the presence or absence of Pi for 10 days. Those authors suggested that a co-521 expression between lncRNAs and adjacent coding genes may be linked to a *cis*-522 regulation by lncRNAs of target genes involved in Pi-starvation processes. Interestingly, 523 in fission yeast (*Schizosaccharomyces pombe*), two of the three genes of the phosphate 524 regulon are repressed in Pi rich medium by the transcription of lncRNA genes (Shah et 525 al., 2014) that are present in the 5′ region (*cis*-regulation). The molecular mechanisms 526 that govern root growth modification by Pi have been mostly elucidated in Col plants. 527 For the local impact of Pi (restricted to root architecture), Pi deficiency is sensed by the 528 root tips and a primary root growth inhibition is induced by both the reduction of cell 529 elongation (*STOP1* and *LPR1/LPR2* pathways) and the progressive arrest of meristem 530 division (*LPR1*/*LPR2* pathway), notably linked to the presence of iron in the medium. 531 Expression analysis in response to Pi deficiency in our mutant lines *NPC48* and *NPC72* 532 were not able to link these two lncRNAs to phosphate starvation root growth arrest 533 mediated by *LPR1*/*LPR2* and *STOP1* pathways.

534 The overexpression of *NPC48* leads to the reduction of the main root growth. The 535 transcriptome analysis of the strongest deregulated line (*35S:NPC48-1*) shows an 536 enrichment in genes that are deregulated during iron starvation (Rodríguez-Celma et al., 537 2013). However, no visible relation to iron homeostasis could be confirmed in the lines 538 over-expressing *NPC48*. Apart from the link to iron starvation, no enrichment of any 539 pathway could be demonstrated. Few identified deregulated genes could be confirmed 540 in the second line, possibly because of the lower level of expression of *NPC48* in this line 541 or because of the specificity of the T-DNA insertions of both lines. Notably, the iron-542 regulated gene *AT3G01260* was only de-regulated in the *35S:NPC48-1* line. Few potential 543 regulators of root growth or genes related to root hair growth and nutrient transport are 544 modified. Nevertheless, we confirmed these latter genes in the second overexpressing 545 line, sustaining their link with *NPC48* overexpression. The over-expression of *NPC48* 546 may decrease the absorption of essential nutrients leading to a restriction of root 547 growth by global nutrition deficiency. This lncRNA is a quantitative regulator of primary 548 root growth, but its over-expression did not show any major alteration in the 549 transcriptome. However, it did modify the expression of several genes dealing with root 550 growth or nutrient assimilation and this is likely linked to its quantitative phenotype. 551 Only a few core regulators of root growth rate have been identified up to now (Satbhai 552 et al., 2015; Motte et al., 2019). Clearly, one can expect more subtle regulators to exist 553 and *NPC48* might be one of them.

554 In *Arabidopsis*, by using grafts between ecotypes presenting a high frequency of 555 SNPs, Thieme et al. (2015) showed that about 2,000 mRNAs, among which 9,300 contain 556 SNPs, could move in plants that were subjected to Pi deficiency for two weeks. These 557 mRNAs were transported from root-to-shoot or shoot-to-root. The authors suggested 558 that these mobile mRNAs might function widely as specific signaling molecules 559 coordinating growth, cell differentiation, and stress adaptation of distant plant organs. 560 As the lncRNAs described here have 3′ polyA tails and are probably 5′ capped, it is 561 tempting to assume that at least some of them can be transported through the xylem 562 and/or the phloem and may contribute to systemic signaling responses.

563 Globally, the in-depth exploration of the non-coding transcriptome of two 564 ecotypes presented in the present work identified thousands of previously 565 uncharacterized lncRNAs with ecotype-specific expression. Statistical analysis among 566 ecotypes identified several co-regulations between coding and non-coding genes 567 (including small RNAs). These co-regulations are likely linked to the evolution of 568 different regulatory mechanisms among ecotypes grown in diverse soil environments, 569 and our detailed study of specific cases has provided two ecotype-related lncRNAs that 570 are potentially involved in regulating primary root growth.

# <sup>571</sup>**Materials and Methods**

#### 572 *Plant growth.*

573 Seeds were surface-sterilized and sown on a horizontal line in plates vertically 574 disposed in a growing chamber (16-h photoperiod; intensity 90 µE; 21°C). The growth 575 medium was previously described in Balzergue et al. (2017). The -Pi and +Pi agar 576 medium contained 10 and 500 μM Pi, respectively.

577 For the root apex isolation, seeds were sown on 1-cm bands of nylon membrane 578 (Nitex 100 μm). After one week on +Pi agar medium, the membranes were transferred 579 on -Pi agar medium. Plants were sampled at time points 0, 1, and 2 h after transfer. Each 580 biological replicate is a pool of more than 100 root apexes cut at 0.5 cm from the root tip.

#### 581 *Arabidopsis lines*

582 The *stop1* (SALK\_114108, NASC reference N666684), *lpr1*;*lpr2* (Svistoonoff et al., 583 2007), *npc15 T-DNA 1* (SALK\_027817, NASC reference N527817), *npc15 T-DNA 2*  584 (SALK\_090867, NASC reference N590867), *npc43 T-DNA* (SALK\_007967, NASC reference 585 N507967), *npc48 T-DNA* (SAIL\_1165\_H01, NASC reference N843057), and *npc72 T-DNA* 586 (SAIL\_571\_C12, NASC reference N824316) lines are in the Col-0 (Col) background. Over-587 expressing lines *35S:NPC43*, *35S:NPC48-1*, *35S:NPC48-2*, and *35S:NPC48-3* were retrieved 588 from Amor et al. (2009) and are in the Col-0 background. *npc34 T-DNA* (FLAG\_223D08 or FLAG\_228A07) is in the WS background. Coler105 589 is in the Columbia background (Col-590 0) with the null allele erecta-105.

#### 591 *Library construction and sequencing.*

592 For each time point (0, 1, and 2 h), total RNA of three biological replicates of Coler105 593 and L*er* pool of root apexes were extracted following the RNeasy micro kit 594 (Qiagen) protocol. One microgram of total RNA of each sample was used for mRNA 595 library preparation using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA library preparation kit 596 according to the manufacturer's instructions. Libraries were sequenced on a HiSeq™ 597 2000 Sequencing System (Illumina) using 100-nt paired-end reads.

598 For *35S:NPC48-1* RNA-seq analysis, total RNA of three biological replicates of 599 whole roots from Col and *35S:NPC48-1* were extracted using a *Quick*-RNA Miniprep kit 600 (Zymo Research). One microgram of total RNA of each sample was used for mRNA 601 library preparation using an Illumina Truseq Stranded mRNA library preparation kit 602 according to the manufacturer's instructions. Libraries were sequenced on NextSeqTM 603 500 Sequencing System (Illumina) using 75-nt single-ends reads.

604 Small RNAs of root apexes were extracted using *mir*Vana™ miRNA Isolation Kit 605 (Ambion). Small RNA libraries were constructed using Ion Total RNA-Seq Kit v2 (Ion 606 Torrent, Life Technologies) according to manufacturer's instructions. Libraries were 607 then sequenced using IonProton and the adapters.

### 608 *Previously uncharacterized transcript identification*

609 According to their ecotype of origin, transcript reads were aligned to the TAIR10 610 (Lamesch et al., 2012) or Ler v7 (Gan et al., 2011) genome. The previously 611 uncharacterized transcripts were predicted in each ecotype independently and then 612 cross-positioned on the other genome to analyze homology. Using the information

613 available on Col genome from databases, the different transcripts were classified as 614 coding and non-coding: Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) involved lincRNAs (long 615 intergenic non-coding RNAs) and NAT (natural antisense lncRNAs) See detailed protocol 616 in Supplemental Materials and Methods.

617 *Small RNA analysis* 

618 The cleaned small RNA reads were aligned on TAIR10 or Ler v7 genome. For 619 Araport11 annotations and previously uncharacterized genes predicted here, the 620 accumulation of small RNA was analyzed to classify them as miRNA, phased siRNA 621 cluster, or siRNA cluster and determine the main siRNA size. See detailed protocol in 622 Supplemental Materials and Methods.

### 623 *Expression analysis*

624 For each annotation, mRNA reads were counted to estimate the level of 625 expression of each gene. These counts were used for differential gene expression 626 analysis. Using siRNA accumulation on each annotation, differential accumulation of 627 21/22-nt and 24-nt sRNAs on coding and non-coding genes was computed 628 independently. Bonferroni correction of the p-value was used for each analysis. 629 Differential expressed genes or siRNA accumulations were defined as having an adjusted 630 *p*-value inferior to 0.01. See detailed protocol in Supplemental Materials and Methods.

#### 631 *Measurement of the primary root length*

632 The plates pictures were taken with a flat scanner and root lengths were 633 measured using RootNav software (Pound et al., 2013).

#### 634 *Reverse Transcription Quantitative PCR*

635 Total RNA was extracted from whole roots using the Quick-RNA MiniPrep™ kit 636 (Zymo Research, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Reverse 637 transcription was performed on 500 ng total RNA using the Maxima Reverse 638 Transcriptase (Thermo Scientific™). Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) 639 was performed on a 480 LightCycler thermocycler (Roche) using the manufacturer's 640 instructions with Light cycler 480 SYBR Green I Master (Roche) and with primers listed 641 in Supplemental Table S4. We used PP2A subunit PD (AT1G13320) as a reference gene 642 for normalization.

### 643 *Statistics and reproducibility of experiments*

644 For each measure (root length, qPCR expression level), the least-squares means 645 were computed. This approach allows correcting for inter-repetition variation. Data are 646 presented as least-squares means ± SEM. The statistical significance tests are included in 647 the legend of each figure. See detailed protocol in Supplemental Materials and Methods.
