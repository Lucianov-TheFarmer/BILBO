#### ABSTRACT

Protein ubiquitination is critical for various biological processes in eukaryotes. A ubiquitin (Ub) chain can be linked through one of the seven lysine (K) residues or the N-terminus methionine of the Ub, and the Ub-conjugating enzymes called E2s play a critical role in determining the linkage specificity of Ub chains. Further, while K48-linked polyubiquitin chain is important for protein degradation, much less is known about the functions of other types of polyubiquitin chains in plants. We showed previously that UBC22 is unique in its ability to catalyze K11-dependent Ub dimer formation in vitro and ubc22 knockout mutants had defects in megasporogenesis. In this study, further analyses of the Arabidopsis ubc22 mutants revealed four subtypes of plants based on the phenotypic changes in vegetative growth. These four subtypes appeared consistently in the plants of three independent ubc22 mutants. Transcriptomic analysis showed that transcript levels of genes related to several pathways were altered differently in different subtypes of mutant plants. In one subtype, the mutant plants had increased expression of genes related to plant defenses and showed enhanced resistance to a necrotrophic plant pathogen. These results suggest multiple functions of UBC22 during plant development and stress response.

## 1. Introduction

Protein ubiquitination represents one fundamental post-translational modification in all eukaryotes in the attachment of the small protein ubiquitin (Ub) to a substrate protein through an isopeptide bond [[1](#page-8-0)]. This process requires the coordinated efforts of three different types of enzymes: E1 for Ub activation, E2 for Ub conjugation, and E3 for Ub-protein ligation) to attach one Ub unit (referred to as monoubiquitination) or an Ub chain (referred to as polyubiquitination) to a substrate protein. It is likely that protein ubiquitination plays important functions in all aspects of plant development and adaption to environmental changes based on the very large number of genes devoted to different aspects of protein ubiquitination. For instance, in Arabidopsis about 5% of genome encodes genes with a function in protein ubiquitination and there are over 1,000 genes for putative E3s and components of E3 complexes [[2](#page-8-1)].

In polyubiquitination, the poly Ub chain can be linked through either one of the seven lysine (K) residues (at positions of 6, 11, 27, 29, 33, 48 and 63) as well as the N-terminus methionine of Ub, which greatly increases the complexity of the poly-Ub chains and thus their functional implications. In Arabidopsis, diverse forms of polyubiquitin chains have been found, except for the K27-linked chain, with K48 linked Ub chain being the most abundant followed by K63- and K11 linked chains [\[3](#page-8-2)–5]. The K48-linked polyubiquitination is well known for its function in protein degradation in both animals and plants [\[6\]](#page-8-3). It has been shown that the attachment of a polyubiquitin chain of four Ub molecules or more linked via K48 is required for degradation by the 26S proteasome [\[7\]](#page-8-4).

In general, the linkage specificity of the polyubiquitin chains is determined by the E2 enzyme [\[1\]](#page-8-0). The Arabidopsis genome is predicted to encode about 37 E2s which are grouped into 14 subfamilies [[8](#page-8-5),[9](#page-9-0)]. Although in vitro results have shown that most of the E2s possess the classical E2 activity in forming a thioester-linkage between Ub and an E2 or could catalyze Ub chain formation [\[9,](#page-9-0)[11](#page-9-2)], current understanding is very limited regarding the E2s for the formation of different Ub chains, the functions of specific Ub chains and further the target substrates in plants.

The Ub linkage specificity for most of E2s is not clearly understood but is presumably through K48 of a Ub for the degradation of substrate proteins. Of the 37 E2s in Arabidopsis, some have been characterized at least initially for the biological functions. UBC18 belongs to a subfamily VII which consists of four members (UBC15, UBC16, UBC17, and UBC18), was reported to regulate abiotic stress response by promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of ERF1 (ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR1) [[12\]](#page-9-3). UBC21, the sole member of subfamily IX, which was identified initially as PEX4 through screening for mutants defective in peroximal processes [[13\]](#page-9-4). It is suggested to function in both monoubiquitination and K48-linked polyubiquitination of the peroxisome matrix protein receptor, PEX5 [[14\]](#page-9-5). UBC24/PHO2 (PHOSPHATE OVERACCUMULATOR 2), belonging to the four-member subfamily XI, functions in organic phosphate (Pi) signaling. More recent studies have shown that UBC24/PHO2 together with an E3 named NLA (NITROGEN LIMITATION ADAPTATION) ubiquitinate a downstream target, the Pi transporter PT2, leading to its degradation [[15,](#page-9-6)[16](#page-9-7)].

The K63-linked ubiquitination has been implicated in several processes in plants [\[17](#page-9-8)–21]. The K63-linked polyubiquitination in Arabidopsis is believed to be mediated by two E2s in the subfamily XV, UBC35 and UBC36, which are closely related to Ubc13 proteins of nonplant species [\[22](#page-9-9)]. Accordingly, these two E2s are also referred to as UBC13A and UBC13B. Earlier UBC13A (UBC35) was shown to catalyze K63-linked ubiquitination in vitro, similar to the UBC13-type E2s in yeast and mammals [\[19](#page-9-10)]. Further, the ubc35 ubc36 double mutant as well as the single mutants showed much reduced levels of K63-linked polyubiquitinated proteins compared to the wild type (WT) plants, providing further support for the biochemical function of UBC35/36 in catalyzing K63-linked ubiquitination in plants [\[23](#page-9-11)]. Results from studying the T-DNA insertion mutants show that the two E2s have important functions in several plant development and cellular processes, including root and root hair development, DNA damage repair, response to iron deficiency, and endocytic pathway [\[17](#page-9-8)[,18](#page-9-12),[20\]](#page-9-13).

Due to the sessile lifestyle, plants need to rely heavily on the immune responses to survive from various pathogen attacks. The K48- and K63-linked ubiquitination plays crucial roles in plant immunity by regulating the stability or activity of many central players in plant immune responses [\[24](#page-9-14)]. The plant hormone salicylic acid (SA) accumulates in response to the biotrophic pathogens and induces transcriptional reprograming through the transcriptional coactivator NPR1 (NON-EXPRESSOR OF PATHOGENESIS-RELATED GENE 1). NPR1 can be ubiquitinated in the nucleus by an E3 ligase complex, the Cullin-RING Ligase (CRL3), leading to NPR1 degradation by the 26S proteasome [[25\]](#page-9-15). Another hormone jasmonic acid (JA) regulates plant immune response to the necrotrophic pathogens through controlling the activity of transcription factors such as MYC2. In the absence of JA, the JAZ (JASMONATE ZIM-DOMAIN) proteins repress MYC2 transcriptional activity. Upon JA reception, JAZs can be polyubiquitinated by the Skp1/Cullin/F-box complex (SCFCOI1) complex resulting in JAZ degradation [\[26](#page-9-16)]. The ubiquitination of NPR1 and JAZs in these hormonal signaling pathways is presumed to be through K48-linked polyubiquitination. In addition, emerging evidence also indicates a role for K63-linked polyubiquitination in plant immunity. The tomato Fni3, a UBC13 homologue, has been shown to interact with and regulate the function of the protein kinase Fen, which is important for the immune response in tomato [[27\]](#page-9-17). A recent study in Arabidopsis showed that ubc13 mutant plants displayed an SA-dependent lesion mimic phenotype and this phenotype partially depended on SNC1, which functions in the SA-dependent defense response [\[28](#page-9-18)].

Little is known about the functions of other forms of polyubiquitination in plants. Arabidopsis UBC22 is the sole member of the subfamily X. Its sequence similarity to human UBE2S with the ability to catalyze K11-linked ubiquitination and the experimental evidence that UBC22 was able to catalyze the K11-specific ubiquitin dimer formation in vitro indicate that UBC22 is an E2 involved in K11-linked ubiquitination in plants [\[29](#page-9-19)]. Thus, UBC22 provides a good opportunity to study the functions of K11-linked ubiquitination in plants. Previous research showed that the Arabidopsis ubc22 T-DNA insertion mutants had a high frequency of ovule abortion, due to the degeneration of functional megaspore (FM) and abnormalities during megagametogenesis, indicating that UBC22 plays a critical role in female gametophyte development [[29\]](#page-9-19). Further, it was observed that UBC22 was ubiquitously expressed in other tissues, suggesting that UBC22 may have a role in these tissues [\[29](#page-9-19)]. In this study, we further studies ubc22 mutants during vegetative development and identified four different subtypes of mutant plants. Transcriptomic analysis revealed distinct expression patterns among the subtypes. Additionally, plant pathogen resistance phenotype was observed in one subtype of plants, suggesting that K11-linked polyubiquitination may play a role in plant immune response.

#### 2. Materials and methods

#### 2.1. Plant materials and growth

Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype "Columbia'' and its mutant lines were grown in a growth room or chamber (20 °C constant, 16/8 h day/night photoperiod with a fluence rate of 90 ± 10 μmoles/m<sup>2</sup> /min). Arabidopsis UBC22 T-DNA insertion line (ubc22-1) SALK\_011800 and (ubc22-2) GK\_642C08 were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resources Center (ABRC) (<http://abrc.osu.edu>) and Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Centre [\(http://arabidopsis.info](http://arabidopsis.info)).

### 2.2. Isolation of RNA, cDNA synthesis and qPCR

Leaf tissues were collected from 3-week old plants and ground in liquid nitrogen. Each total RNA sample was isolated from about 100 mg tissue powder using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and RNA concentration was determined with a NanoVue Plus Spectrophotometer (GE Healthcare) following the manufacturer's instructions. The synthesis of the first-strand cDNA was performed with the ThermoScript RT-PCR kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Typically, for each sample, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed in a 10 μL cDNA synthesis reaction. The cDNA mixture was diluted 3 times with sterile H2O, and 1 μL was used in a 20 μL PCR reaction. qPCRs were performed using Green-2-Go 2X qPCR Mastermix (BioBasic) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad), following the manufacturer's instructions. Each sample had at least three biological replicates. Data were analyzed with the CFX software (Bio-Rad). Student t-test was performed to determine the significant difference of each gene analyzed between a subtype and the WT. The qPCR primers used are listed in Table S1.

## 2.3. Construct preparation

UBC22-CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats)/Cas9 construct was prepared using the vector system as described [[30\]](#page-9-20). Two target sequences 5′- CTTGACGAATCCCCTCCAGA-3′ and 5′-TGTTATCAAGCAGCTTGCCA-3′ were selected within the first exon of the UBC22 open-reading frame, and each region as a sgRNA (sequence-specific single guide RNA) fragment was cloned into an intermediate vector pYLsgRNA to form a sgRNA expression cassette. Two sgRNA expression cassettes were released from the intermediate vectors by enzyme digestion and ligated into one pYLCRISPR/Cas9 binary vector [\[30](#page-9-20)]. The construct was used to transform WT Arabidopsis plants.

### 2.4. RNA-seq preparation and analysis

Purified total RNA was precipitated and resuspended in RNase-free water to a final concentration of 100 ng/μL. Fifteen cDNA libraries were constructed using the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit v2 (Illumina) using RNAs from Type I, Type II, Type III, Type IV ubc22 mutant and also WT plants, with three biological replicates for each type. Pairedend sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq2500 (Illumina), generating 101-nucleotide reads, at the National Research Council, ACRD-Saskatoon, Canada. Sequencing adapters were removed, and low-quality reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic with default settings [[31\]](#page-9-21). The filtered reads were mapped to TAIR10 genome sequence using STAR software program [\[32](#page-9-22)]. Transcripts were identified using StringTie [[33\]](#page-9-23) and followed by Cuffmerge tool [[34\]](#page-9-24). HTseq-count [\[35](#page-9-25)] was employed to count the reads spreading at the exonic regions of each gene. Differential expression analysis was performed with DEseq2 between mutants and WT [\[36](#page-9-26)]. Normalized counts from DESeq2 were expressed as gene expression levels. Genes with less than 10 reads across all samples were excluded to eliminate the extremely low expressed transcripts. Functional annotations of genes were obtained from the Bio-Array Resource for Arabidopsis Functional Genomics database (BAR) (<http://bar.utoronto.ca/>) using the AGI numbers. Hierarchical clustering was performed to identify the expression patterns of differentially expressed genes. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was analyzed using ClusterProfiler [\[37](#page-9-27)]. The RNA-seq data have been deposited into the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus database under the accession number GSE138777.

#### 2.5. Pathogenicity test against Botrytis cinerea

The strain of Botrytis cinerea was grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plate at 22 °C for 7 days. Conidial spores were collected from plates and suspended in sterilized distilled water to 5 × 10<sup>5</sup> /mL conidial spores. Leaves (the third pair) were detached from 4 to 6-week old Arabidopsis plants and maintained on wet filter papers in large Petri dishes. For infection, 5 μL of the spore suspension was applied on each of the leaves. The Petri dishes containing inoculated leaves were wrapped with parafilm and placed on the lab bench at room temperature (about 22 °C). To evaluate B. cinerea infection, the diameters of the necrotic lesions were measured after 3 days of the inoculation. Student t-test was performed to determine the significant difference of the lesion diameter between a certain subtype and WT. To visualize B. cinerea hyphae growth in Arabidopsis leaves, 1 cm<sup>2</sup> of infected leaf tissue was fixed in buffer (methanol : chloroform : acetic acid/60 : 30 : 10, v/v), stained with trypan blue dye solution (0.4 % (w/v) trypan blue) in a microcentrifuge tube, and then observed under a microscope. For the intact plant inoculation, 5 μL of the spore suspension was applied on leaves of 4-week old plants. The inoculated plants were incubated in the dark for at least 24 h and photographed after 3 days of the inoculation.

### 3. Results

## 3.1. Characterization of four subtypes of ubc22 mutants

In our previous study, characterization of two individual T-DNA insertion mutant lines ubc22-1 and ubc22-2 revealed that inactivation of UBC22 affects female gametophyte development [[29\]](#page-9-19). In terms of vegetative plant development such as leaf size and morphology, although the majority of ubc22 mutant plants appeared similar to WT plants, we observed that a certain proportion of the ubc22 mutant plants were very different from WT plants ([Fig. 1A](#page-3-0)). Based on leaf size, shape and color, ubc22-1 mutant plants could be generally categorized into four different subtypes: (1) Type I: This subtype represented the majority (∼70 %, [Table 1\)](#page-4-0) of ubc22 mutant plants. These plants had much shorter silique length which is due to defects in female gametogenesis as demonstrated from our previous study [\[29](#page-9-19)]. However, leaf morphology was similar to the WT plants. The first two leaves appeared longer than the leaves of the other three subtypes and the length/width ratio was around 1.2 ([Fig. 1A](#page-3-0) and 1B). (2) Type II (about 16 %): This type of plants had smaller but rounder leaves, and the length/width ratio of the first two leaves was about 1.0 [\(Fig. 1](#page-3-0)A and 1B). The petioles were shorter, and thus leaves appeared more compact compared to WT plants. The flowering time was dramatically delayed, and seed setting was highly reduced ([Fig. 1C](#page-3-0)). (3) Type III (about 7 %): Leaves of this type of plants appeared smaller and narrower with a length/width ratio about 1.7 ([Fig. 1A](#page-3-0) and 1B). Leaves appeared paler and started to senesce earlier compared to WT plants [\(Fig. 1A](#page-3-0)). Very few seeds could be collected from this type of plants ([Fig. 1C](#page-3-0)). (4) Type IV (about 7 %): Mutant plants in this category were much smaller. The leaves were long, narrow and wrinkled, normally associated with obvious serrations ([Fig. 1A](#page-3-0)). The length/width ratio of this type was about 2.0 [\(Fig. 1B](#page-3-0)). Early senescence was also observed in old leaves. Moreover, the plants were almost completely sterile. These four subtypes could be easily recognized based on their leaf morphology and color at the early seedling stage (as early as 2 weeks): Type I plants being similar to the WT plants (the length/width ratio for the first two leaves similar to that of WT around 1.2), Type II for round leaves (the length/width ratio around 1.0), Type III for long pale leaves (the length/width ratio around 1.7), and Type IV for small and narrow serrated leaves (the length/width ratio around 2.0). These four subtypes of plants with similar percentages were also observed in a different T-DNA insertion mutant line ubc22-2 [\(Table](#page-4-0) 1). Furthermore, the four subtypes of plants reappeared in the progenies of Type I and Type II mutant plants. Few seeds could be collected from Type III and Type IV plants due to sterility making it difficult to study the progeny populations, thus we mainly investigated the progenies of Type I plants.

We observed that the female gametophyte phenotypes of the ubc22 mutant could be mostly rescued by the expression of the ProUBC22::HA-UBC22 construct [[29\]](#page-9-19). Thus, we determined whether the subtype phenotypes would also be rescued. The complementation line had been characterized previously and its silique length was similar to that of the WT [\[29](#page-9-19)]. About 96 % of plants of the complementation line belonged to Type I category and they appeared similar to the WT in term of leaf morphology (Figure S1). The percentages of Type II, III and IV plants were highly reduced to 1.8 %, 1.2 %, and 1.0 %, compared to 16.0 %, 11.5 % and 6 % in ubc22-1 mutants, respectively [\(Table 1](#page-4-0)). Thus, the expression of ProUBC22::HA-UBC22 in the ubc22-1 mutant rescued mostly the phenotypes of different subtypes, similar to the rescue of the female gametophyte phenotypes. To further confirm that the occurrences of four subtypes in mutant plants are due to UBC22 inactivation, we created yet another ubc22 mutant using CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats)/Cas9 system [\[30](#page-9-20)]. This mutant (W76-1) had a 28-base deletion (from the 51 st to the 78th nucleotide downstream of the "ATG" start codon) in the first exon of the UBC22 open-reading frame causing a frame shift in UBC22 ([Fig. 2A](#page-4-1)). In this mutant line, majority of the mutant plants have short siliques and the four subtypes of mutant plants were also observed as in the two T-DNA insertion mutant lines, ubc22-1 and ubc22-2 [\(Fig. 2B](#page-4-1)). Collectively, these data indicate that the appearance of the four different subtypes of mutant plants is not due to the variation in plant growth or inactivation of an off-target gene, but to the inactivation of UBC22.

## 3.3. Enhanced defense responses and resistance in ubc22 Type II mutant plants against a necrotrophic pathogen

Interestingly, the expression of a set of PDF (PLANT DEFENSIN) genes was drastically increased in Type II mutant plants ([Table 2](#page-6-0)). For instance, PDF1.3 and PDF 1.2, two plant defense-encoding genes, were up-regulated by 42.3 and 21.0 folds. These drastic increases in the two PDF genes were only observed in Type II mutant plants, but not in other subtypes of mutant plants, which led us to investigate whether the other plant defense genes might also be up-regulated in Type II mutant plants. To have a better understanding, DEGs from the category of Type II mutant plants were applied with a more stringent cut-off p-value less than 0.01. Thirty-four defense-related genes were up-regulated and the

<span id="page-4-1"></span>average fold change was 5.0 ([Table 2](#page-6-0)). In addition to PDF genes, other defense-related genes such as AT4G16260, LAL5 (AT3G23550), and DIN11 (AT3G49620) were also up-regulated by about 7.7, 6.8, and 5.5 folds, respectively. The change in the expression of PDF1.2 was confirmed by qPCR [\(Fig. 4](#page-7-0)A). Together, these results clearly demonstrate the up-regulation of defense-related genes in Type II mutant plants.

The increases in the expression of many defense-related genes raised a question whether the Type II mutant plants had an increased resistance to pathogens. The plant microbial pathogens can be divided into three major groups according to their life styles: necrotrophic, biotrophic, and hemi-biotrophic pathogens. Necrotrophic pathogens invade plant tissue and kill it rapidly, and then live on the dead tissues. In contrast, biotrophic pathogens exist and propagate in living cells and derive nutrient from the cells. The hemi-biotrophic pathogens have combined features of the necrotrophic and biotrophic pathogens [\[38](#page-9-28)]. It is widely known that plant hormones, especially salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA) and ethylene (ET), play major roles in plant-pathogen interactions [[39,](#page-9-29)[40](#page-9-30)]. The SA response pathway is typically effective against microbial biotrophic pathogens, with the expression of a robust marker gene PR-1, whereas JA and ET pathways have a synergistic effect in the resistance to necrotrophic pathogens [[39,](#page-9-29)[40\]](#page-9-30). The activation of a marker gene PDF1.2 expression is controlled by both JA and ET signaling [\[41](#page-9-31)]. The up-regulation of JA or/and ET pathways, as indicated by the expression of a number of plant defense genes, including PDF1.2, is important for the resistance to the necrotrophic pathogens. Botrytis cinerea, also known as grey mold, is considered as a typical necrotrophic pathogen and can infect more than 200 crop species worldwide. This pathogen has been widely used for studying plantpathogen interactions in Arabidopsis [\[42](#page-9-32)].

Thus, we determined whether Type II mutant plants had an enhanced resistance to necrotrophic pathogens such as B. cinerea. Following the inoculation of the pathogen, the detached leaves from Type II mutant plants developed smaller lesions compared to larger lesions on the WT leaves [\(Fig. 5A](#page-8-6) and 5B). The increased resistance of Type II mutant plants to B. cinerea was also observed following the inoculation of the pathogen on intact plants (Figure S2). Trypan blue staining showed much less growth of fungal hyphae on leaves of Type II mutant plants than on the leaves of Type I and WT plants ([Fig. 5](#page-8-6)C). These results together show that Type II mutant plants had an enhanced resistance to B. cinerea compared to WT and Type I plants.

Third, when seeds from Type I or Type II mutant plants were planted, the four subtypes reappeared similarly. Fourth, distinct changes in gene expression were observed in the four subtypes of plants compared to the WT. These results indicate that the four subtypes of plants are not due to a difference in seed quality or growth conditions, but they are accompanied by specific changes in gene expression. Furthermore, it is unlikely that the occurrence of four subtypes is due to differential expression of UBC22 in different subtypes since the four subtypes were observed in the CRISPR/Cas9 mutant line in which a 28 nucleotide deletion resulted in a frame shift, which is not expected to differentially affect the gene expression level among the four subtypes. In addition, the RNA-seq data of ubc22-1 showed a similar reduction of UBC22 expression among the four subtypes (Figure S3).

In gene functional studies, it is common that the inactivation of one single gene may have pleiotropic effects on different aspects of plant growth and development, but all the mutant plants usually have similar types of changes although they may differ in the severity of the phenotypes. For the ubc22 mutant plants, although most of the plants appeared normal morphologically to the WT plants, some of them appeared very different. The phenomenon of having several distinct subtypes of plants from one single gene inactivation is not often studied.

The numbers of E1, E2 and E3 proteins in eukaryotic species display a pyramid structure, for instance, with 2 E1s, 37 E2s and over 1000 E3s in Arabidopsis [[10\]](#page-9-1). Understandably, an E2 may work with different E3s targeting different substrates. Thus, E2s in generally would have a broader spectrum of functions in different pathways than E3s, although some E3s could also interact with more than one E2 or target different substrates [\[43\]](#page-9-33). Indeed, RNA-seq analysis of different subtypes of the ubc22 mutant plants indicated a large number of DEGs in one subtype, suggesting that the expression of certain genes is uniquely altered in different subtypes. It can be envisioned that UBC22 as a sole member of the E2 subfamily X, along with cognate E3s, could target different substrate proteins involved in different pathways and cellular processes. Ubiquitination of these proteins could affect their cellular levels or functional properties such as interactions with other proteins. Deregulation of these substrate proteins due to UBC22 inactivation could result in different phenotypic effects.

Multiple phenotypic effects are often observed with important players in cell signaling. The observation that ubc22 mutant plants have four different subtypes each with different phenotypic features is intriguing. One possible explanation is that the de-regulation of certain proteins as a result of UBC22 inactivation could affect the stability of more than one signaling pathways or the balance among different pathways. There may be changes occurring in most of the mutant plants. The genes encoding the transcription factor MYC2 and its target VSP2 were found up-regulated in both Type I and Type II of mutant plants [\(Fig. 4B](#page-7-0)), indicating that a common pathway was affected in these two subtypes which constitute the majority of ubc22 mutant plants. On the other hand, in certain mutant plants, the changes to one particular pathway might become more prominent, leading to distinct phenotypes in the subtype. For example, in Type II mutant plants, the pathways involved in pathogen responses were more prominently affected.

## 4.2. UBC22 is most likely involved in regulating the JA-signalling pathway

Plant hormones, especially SA, JA, and ET, play critical roles in plant-pathogen interactions. The SA response pathway plays a major role against biotrophic pathogens. An important regulator in the SA signaling pathway is NPR1, which upon activation by SA acts as a transcriptional coactivator for a large set of defense-related genes [\[44](#page-9-34)]. PR-1 is one of the well-characterized PR genes, which is often used as a

The expression of the marker gene PDF1.2 is controlled by both JA and ET signaling [\[45](#page-9-53)]. In Type II mutant plants, the expression level of PR-1 was similar to WT, suggesting that the SA pathway was not affected. On the other hand, the transcript levels of PDFs were much higher in Type II mutant plants and enhanced resistance to B. cinerea was observed. These results indicate that JA and/or ET pathways were activated in Type II mutant plants.

In Arabidopsis, the JA signaling pathway consists of two major branches: the ERF branch and MYC branch. The ERF branch is regulated by members of the AP2/ERF (APETALA2/ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR) family of transcription factors, such as ERF1 and ORA59 (OCTADECANOID-RESPONSIVE ARABIDOPSIS 59) [\[41](#page-9-31)]. The expression of JA-responsive marker gene PDF1.2 is dependent on ERF1 or/and ORA59. Activation of the ERF branch requires either JA or ET. The MYC branch is controlled by MYC-family transcription factors such as MYC2. MYC2 acts as a positive regulator of JA-responsive genes, such as VSP2, whereas it negatively controls the expression level of ERF-regulated PDF1.2 [[46\]](#page-9-54). In general, the ERF branch of the JA pathway is associated with enhanced resistance to necrotrophic pathogens, whereas the MYC branch of the JA pathway is associated with the wound response and defense against insect herbivores [\[47](#page-9-55)]. In Type II mutant plants, the upregulation of PDF1.2 and VSP2, as well as the transcription factor ORA59 and MYC2 ([Fig. 4B](#page-7-0)), indicated that both the ERF branch and MYC2 branch of JA signaling were activated. The increased resistance to B. cinerea observed in Type II mutant plants is consistent with an activated ERF branch. Moreover, ET signaling may also be involved in the up-regulation of PDF1.2 since it can greatly affect JA response synergistically on the expression of ERF1 and ORA9 in the ERF branch of JA signaling pathway [\[41](#page-9-31)].

The occurrence of four different subtypes of ubc22 mutant plants and response of the ubc22 mutant to the pathogen B. cinerea strongly suggest that UBC22 plays multiple roles in plant vegetative development. Since K11-linked Ub chain is found as the third most abundant chain type in Arabidopsis [\[3](#page-8-2)[,4\]](#page-8-7) and UBC22 is the only known E2 in plants that possesses the ability to catalyze K11-linked ubiquitination, its functions in different pathways or processes can be attributed to its role in regulating multiple substrate proteins. To better understand how UBC22 functions in different pathways and cellular processes, it will be important to identify the interacting E3s as well as the substrates.
