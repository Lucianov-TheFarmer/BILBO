# **Global gene regulation in tomato plant (***Solanum lycopersicum***) responding to vector (***Bactericera cockerelli***) feeding and pathogen ('***Candidatus* **Liberibacter solanacearum') infection**

**Ordom Brian Huot[1](http://orcid.org/0000-0003-0836-6560) · Julien Gad Levy2 · Cecilia Tamborindeguy[1](http://orcid.org/0000-0002-0933-5785)**

Received: 25 February 2017 / Accepted: 28 March 2018 © Springer Science+Business Media B.V., part of Springer Nature 2018

#### **Abstract**

### *Key message* **Different responses are elicited in tomato plants by** *Bactericera cockerelli* **harboring or not the pathogen '***Candidatus* **Liberibacter solanacearum'.**

**Abstract** '*Candidatus* Liberibacter solanacearum' (Lso) has emerged as a major pathogen of crops worldwide. This bacterial pathogen is transmitted by *Bactericera cockerelli*, the tomato psyllid, to solanaceous crops. In this study, the transcriptome profiles of tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*) exposed to *B. cockerelli* infestation and Lso infection were evaluated at 1, 2 and 4 weeks following colonization and/or infection. The plant transcriptional responses to Lso-negative *B. cockerelli* were different than plant responses to Lso-positive *B. cockerelli*. The comparative transcriptome analyses of plant responses to Lso-negative *B. cockerelli* revealed the up-regulation of genes associated with plant defenses regardless of the time-point. In contrast, the general responses to Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* and Lso-infection were temporally different. Infected plants down-regulated defense genes at week one while delayed the up-regulation of the defense genes until weeks two and four, time points in which early signs of disease development were also detected in the transcriptional response. For example, infected plants regulated carbohydrate metabolism genes which could be linked to the disruption of sugar distribution usually associated with Lso infection. Also, infected plants down-regulated photosynthesis-related genes potentially resulting in plant chlorosis, another symptom associated with Lso infection. Overall, this study highlights that tomato plants induce different sets of genes in response to different stages of *B. cockerelli* infestation and Lso infection. This is the first transcriptome study of tomato responses to *B. cockerelli* and Lso, a first step in the direction of finding plant defense genes to enhance plant resistance.

### **Introduction**

Within the last two decades, bacteria in the genus *Liberibacter* have emerged worldwide as serious pathogens of citrus, solanaceous and apiaceous plants (Haapalainen [2014\)](#page-14-0). In the United States, *Liberibacter* species cause millions of dollars in agricultural losses annually. '*Candidatus* Liberibacter asiaticus' caused an estimated loss of \$4.5 billion from 2006 to 2011 in Florida, while '*Candidatus* Liberibacter solanacearum' (Lso) infecting potatoes caused an estimated loss of more than \$25 million between 2003 and 2005 in Texas (CNAS [2006;](#page-14-1) Hodges and Spreen [2012](#page-14-2); Munyaneza [2012](#page-14-3)).

Lso is a phloem-limited, Gram-negative, bacterial pathogen (Munyaneza [2012\)](#page-14-3). In the Americas and New Zealand, this pathogen is transmitted by the tomato psyllid

*Bactericera cockerelli*, also known as potato psyllid (Munyaneza et al. [2007\)](#page-14-4), and infects solanaceous plants including tomato, pepper, eggplant, tamarillo, and cape gooseberry (Liefting et al. [2008,](#page-14-5) [2009;](#page-14-6) Lin et al. [2009\)](#page-14-7).

Previous studies on the citrus-'*Candidatus* Liberibacter americanus' interaction identified changes in the plant primary and secondary metabolism, in particular in the carbohydrate metabolism (Mafra et al. [2013\)](#page-14-8). More recently, the citrus transcriptome in response to the vector *Diaphorina citri* and '*Candidatus* Liberibacter asiaticus' was analyzed comparing plant response to early and late infection. The early infection was characterized by the down-regulation of defense genes, while changes in the expression of genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism, hormone signaling and plant defense were identified later during the infection (Zhong et al. [2016](#page-15-0)). While citrus response to *Liberibacter* infection has been extensively studied, much less is known concerning the transcriptomic responses of solanaceous plants to Lso infection. In *Solanum tuberosum*, levels of phenolic compounds and activities of peroxidase and polyphenol oxidase increased following Lso infection (Wallis et al. [2012](#page-15-1)). In tomato (*S. lycopersicum*), Lso infection resulted in the up-regulation of allene oxide synthase and proteinase inhibitor 2 which are genes associated with jasmonate defenses and resulted in the up-regulation of phenylalanine lyase 5 and pathogenesis-related 1, genes associated with salicylate defenses (Casteel et al. [2012\)](#page-13-0). Yet little is known about the global plant transcriptomic response to Lso infection. Studying plant responses to the vector and the pathogen can reveal crucial aspects of plant defense and disease development. In particular, understanding plant innate immune responses to vector colonization and pathogen infection could lead to novel strategies for the management of *Liberibacter* diseases.

In this study, Illumina HiSeq RNA sequencing was used to evaluate the tomato transcriptional responses to *B. cockerelli* and Lso over time. Bioinformatic and empirical approaches were used to monitor the changes in gene expression of plants colonized by Lso-negative and by Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* over the course of 4 weeks. The expression of genes associated with plant defenses and metabolic responses was verified by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Protein assays were performed to link changes in enzyme activity to the up-regulation of defense genes. This study represents the first global gene expression analysis on tomato–*Liberibacter* interaction. Overall, the findings of this study contribute to the understanding of tomato responses to *B. cockerelli* and Lso at the molecular level.

# **Materials and methods**

## **Insect source**

Lso-negative and Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* colonies of Northwestern haplotype (Yao et al. [2016](#page-15-2)) were maintained on tomato plants in separate rooms under the 16:8 light to dark cycle at room temperature (24±2 °C). *Bactericera cockerelli* colonies were tested regularly for Lso infection via diagnostic PCR as previously published (Nachappa et al. [2011](#page-14-9)). On average, 80–100% of the insects tested positive in the Lso-positive colony. Both Lso haplotypes, Lso A and Lso B (Wen et al. [2009\)](#page-15-3), infect the Lso-positive colony. None of the insects tested positive in the Lso-negative colony.

## **Plant material**

Tomato plants, *S. lycopersicum* cv. Moneymaker (Thompson & Morgan), were grown from seed in Sun Gro® Metro-Mix 900 soil and transplanted into 10 cm square pots 4 weeks later. Plants were watered and fertilized weekly according to the label rate of the Miracle-Gro® Water Soluble Tomato Plant Food (18-18-21 NPK). All experiments were conducted at room temperature (24±2 °C) and with a 16:8 light to dark cycle.

## **Experimental design and sample collection**

Experiments were designed to evaluate tomato transcriptional responses to *B. cockerelli* herbivory and Lso infection at three time points: 1 week (T1), 2 weeks (T2), and 4 weeks (T3). There were three *B. cockerelli* treatments within each time point: plants exposed to Lso-negative *B. cockerelli* (P), plants exposed to Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* (L), and control plants (no *B. cockerelli*, C). Six-week-old plants of similar size were individually caged and randomly assigned to one of the three insect treatments (C, P, and L) within one of three the time points: T1, T2, or T3. Each plant in the *B. cockerelli* treatments (L and P) was exposed to 5 1-day-old-adult females and 5 1-day-old-adult males from either the Lso-negative or from the Lso-positive colonies. Control plants were caged in a way similar to infested plants, but without *B. cockerelli*. Seven days after their release, the *B. cockerelli* adults were collected and the progenies were allowed to develop on the plants.

Leaf tissue was collected only once from each plant, therefore independent plants were used for each time point. Leaf tissues were collected from a total of 27 plants independently. Tissues from different plants were not mixed. Plant tissue from the top-most fully-expanded leaf was collected and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. After the leaf sample was collected, each plant was kept until the end of the experiment in order to collect plant tissue for DNA extraction to verify Lso presence or absence. Lso in plants can be detected 4 weeks after *B. cockerelli* infestation (Levy et al. [2011](#page-14-10)). Time point T1 was considered the infestation stage when *B. cockerelli* were mating, laying eggs, and feeding on the host plant. Time point T2 was considered the colonization stage when the emerged nymphs fed on the host plant. Lastly, time point T3 was considered the repopulation stage when new adults emerged while the remaining nymphs were developing. For the plants in cages with Lso-positive *B. cockerelli*, each time point represented a different stage in Lso infection.

#### **Verification of Lso infection in plants**

DNA from 0.1 g of plant tissue was extracted and used for diagnostic PCR to verify the presence or absence of Lso in all the plants as previously published (Levy et al. [2011](#page-14-10)). Primers targeting Lso 16S ribosomal RNA gene were used for Lso detection (Nachappa et al. [2011](#page-14-9)) and primers targeting tomato elongation factor 1 (Nachappa et al. [2014\)](#page-14-11) were used as internal control. Fifty nanograms of nucleic acid from each plant were used as template in the diagnostic PCR.

#### **RNA extraction, library preparation, sequencing and analyses**

The flash-frozen tissue samples were ground to fine powder. Total RNA extraction was performed using the Plant RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA samples were treated with RNase-Free DNase (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) to eliminate DNA contamination. Any remaining DNA contamination was removed using TURBO DNA-free™ Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). RNAs for the 27 samples were submitted to the AgriLife Genomic and Bioinformatic Center. Libraries were made following the TruSeq RNA sample preparation protocol. Sequencing of libraries was performed using Illumina SE 135 bp using the HiSeq 2500 platform. Three libraries failed with this approach and were later re-sequenced using Illumina PE 125 bp, however, one L sample at T1 yielded a low quality library and was not used in this analysis. Three biological replicates per treatment per time point were sequenced using three lanes with the exception of L treatment at T1. Sequence cluster identification, quality prefiltering, base calling and uncertainty assessment were done in real time using Illumina's HCS 2.2.38 and RTA 1.18.61 software with default parameter settings. Sequencer basecall files were formatted into fastq files using the conversion software bcl2fastq 1.8.4. The sequence raw reads were cleaned using cutadapt 1.0 to remove barcode tags and adaptors. Individual samples were processed with FastQC, and the QC reports were checked for sequence quality. Quality trimming of the reads analyses were conducted by AgriLife Genomics and Bioinformatic Services according to the parameter on [http://www.txgen.tamu.edu/bioin](http://www.txgen.tamu.edu/bioinformatics/) [formatics/](http://www.txgen.tamu.edu/bioinformatics/).

#### **Bioinformatic data analysis and gene annotation**

The processed sequences were uploaded to the CyVerse Discovery Environment web interface ([http://www.cyver](http://www.cyverse.org) [se.org\)](http://www.cyverse.org) (Goff et al. [2011](#page-14-12)). The bioinformatic analyses were performed using the Tuxedo Suite. Reads were mapped to the *S. lycopersicum* genome, Ensembl 14 (Sato et al. [2012\)](#page-15-4) by TopHat 2.2.1 software with Bowtie 2. The aligned reads were assembled to transcripts using Cufflinks 2.2.1, and all transcripts were combined by Cuffmerge 2.1.1 with reference *S. lycopersicum* gene model annotation. Then, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using Cuffdiff2 (Trapnell et al. [2013\)](#page-15-5). Libraries were normalized by hits to known transcripts. The identification of DEGs was performed with the default false discovery rate (FDR=0.05) using Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction for calculating adjusted *P* values (Trapnell et al. [2012\)](#page-15-6). The parameters used for each software are presented in Supplementary Document 1.

Transcriptomes were compared among treatments. Each treatment consisted of three biological replicates except one for which a low quality library was obtained. Thus, the L treatment at T1 has only two replicates. Transcriptomes of plants from C and P treatments were compared to evaluate plant response to herbivory. Transcriptomes of plants from C and L treatments were compared to evaluate plant response to herbivory and infection. Transcriptomes of plants from P and L treatments were compared to evaluate the difference in plant responses to Lso-negative and Lso-positive *B. cockerelli*. Each comparison was done with respect to each time point (T1, T2, and T3). In total, nine comparisons were performed.

DEGs in each of the nine comparative transcriptomes were researched and annotated using EnsemblPlants, Uni-Prot Knowledgebase (Bateman et al. [2015\)](#page-13-1), the tomato genome database at <http://www.solgenomics.net> (Fernandez-Pozo et al. [2015](#page-14-13)) and Phytozome. GO term enrichment analyses of up-regulated and down-regulated DEGs were performed using PhytoMine (Goodstein et al. [2012\)](#page-14-14) with Holm–Bonferroni correction method to identify significantly enriched GO terms. In many cases the functions assigned to the DEGs are putative and based on sequence similarity.

#### **Comparing transcriptome results to qRT‑PCR results**

An aliquot of 500 ng of the total RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed using Thermo Fisher Scientific Verso™ cDNA Kit. The cDNA was diluted 5 times before it was used for analysis by qRT-PCR. Each reaction consisted of 1 µL cDNA, 5 µL SensiFAST SYBR Hi-ROX mix (Bioline), 0.4 µL of 400 nM of each primer, and 3.2 µL of molecular grade water. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed in an Applied Biosystem 7300 Real-Time PCR System with the following parameters: 2 min at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 5 s at 95 °C and 30 s at 60 °C. The melting curve for each reaction was generated to test amplicon specificity at the last step. All qRT-PCR reactions were performed in three technical and three biological replicates. The relative expression of each gene was analyzed using the ΔΔCT method (Riedel et al. [2014](#page-14-15)) using glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GADPH) as reference gene. The primers (Supplementary Table 1) were designed using the transcript sequence and Primer3 (Rozen and Skaletsky [2000](#page-15-7)). The transcript sequences can be found in the tomato genome database (Fernandez-Pozo et al. [2015\)](#page-14-13). The amplification efficiency for each primer set was calculated using LinReg-PCR (Ruijter et al. [2009\)](#page-15-8). Normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data that met the normality assumption was compared using *t* tests while data that did not meet the normality assumption was tested using Mann–Whitney *U* test to compare relative gene expression. Gene expression data from qRT-PCR and from the transcriptome analyses were compared and Pearson product-moment correlation test was performed to evaluate the similarity in gene expression obtained using the RNA-seq (fold change from Cuffdiff) and qRT-PCR (ΔΔCT) methods.

### **Protein assays**

Leaves from P, L, or C plants at T1, T2, and T3 were collected, weighed and flash frozen. Peroxidase (POD) and polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity assays were conducted according to the previously published protocol (Thaler et al. [1996](#page-15-9)). Briefly, 100 mg of frozen plant tissue were homogenized in 1 mL of cold 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer, containing 7% polyvinyl-polypyrrolidone. Then, 0.4 mL of 10% triton X-100 were added and the solution was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min. The resultant supernatant was used for the protein assays. For PPO assays, 10 µL of protein extract were added to 60 µL of caffeic acid solution (2.92 mM in 0.1 M phosphate buffer). For POD assays, 10 µL of protein extract were added to 90 µL of guaiacol solution (2.92 mM guaiacol in phosphate buffer with 30% hydrogen peroxide). Changes in absorbance were measured in a microplate reader (Tecan Infiniti Pro 200) at 470 nm in a kinetic loop for 10 min using three technical replicates. The activity of the enzymes is expressed in delta OD/min/ mg of protein. Protein quantification was performed according to the Bradford assay method (Bradford [1976](#page-13-2)) using the Coomassie protein assay reagent (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA). Each treatment (e.g. C, P, or L) consists of three biological replicates (e.g. plants). Each biological replicate for the enzyme assay is an average of three technical replicates (e.g. assays).

## **Results**

## **Lso infection in plants**

Diagnostic PCR tests performed at the end of the experiment (4 weeks post-infestation) indicated that all plants from treatments C and P were negative for Lso, and all plants from the treatment L were positive for Lso infection.

## **Transcriptome analyses**

A total of 27 purified RNA samples (i.e., three biological replicates per treatment per time point) were submitted for sequencing. From the 26 successfully sequenced libraries, a total of 558.9 million reads were obtained. On average, 80.98% of the reads mapped to the tomato genome (Supplementary Table 2). RNA-seq data can be found on NCBI GEO database (accession number: GSE95710).

## **Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs)**

For each time point, three transcriptome comparisons were performed: control plants versus plants exposed to Lso-negative *B. cockerelli* (C vs. P), control plants versus plants exposed to Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* (C vs. L) and plants exposed to Lso-negative versus plants exposed to Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* (P vs. L). Depending on the comparison, there were between 24 and 341 DEGs (Fig. [1](#page-4-0)). In the C versus P comparison, over 65% of the DEGs were up-regulated at T1, T2, and T3 in response to *B. cockerelli* herbivory (Fig. [1\)](#page-4-0). Similarly, larger percentages of the DEGs were up-regulated than down-regulated in the comparative transcriptomes of P versus L and C versus L at T2 and T3 (Figs. [1,](#page-4-0) [2,](#page-4-1) [3,](#page-5-0) [4](#page-5-1)).

The results of all the comparative transcriptomic analyses revealed that changes in plant gene expression were unique for each treatment and time point (Fig. [5](#page-6-0)). Only one gene, Solyc03g026280 (CBF1), was differentially expressed in all three comparisons at T1. No DEGs were shared among the three comparisons at T2 and T3. #### **C versus L comparative transcriptome**

The putative functions of the DEGs from the C versus L comparison were identified (Supplementary Table 4). In the C versus L comparisons, the number of DEGs increased at each time point (Fig. [1](#page-4-0)). At T1, the C versus L comparative transcriptome had the lowest number of DEGs out of the nine comparisons. GO terms associated with photosynthesis were enriched among the down-regulated genes but no GO term was enriched among the up-regulated genes (Fig. [1](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Table 7). Plants exposed to Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* also down-regulated plant defense genes such as arginase 2, asparagine synthetase 1, metallocarboxypeptidase inhibitor, and threonine dehydratase (Supplementary Table 4).

At T2, 70.8% of the DEGs were up-regulated (Fig. [1](#page-4-0)). Among those DEGs, four GO terms associated with peptidase inhibitory activity were significantly enriched (Fig. [1](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Table 7). These four enriched GO terms were associated with proteinase inhibitor genes which are involved in plant defense. Other up-regulated plant defense genes were cytochrome P450s (CYP76A2-like and Isoflavone 3′-hydroxylase-like), asparagine synthetase, glycosyltransferase, POD, PPO, lipoxygenase, NAC domain transcription factor and pathogenesis-related proteins STH-2 and 5 (Supplementary Table S4). Among the down-regulated genes, there were two enriched GO terms associated with plant photosynthesis. Down-regulation of photosynthesis genes such as chlorophyll a/b-binding proteins can be associated with disease development and result in leaf chlorosis.

At T3, the C versus L comparative transcriptome had the greatest number of DEGs compared to the other comparative transcriptomes. In this comparison, there were ten times more DEGs than at T1 and over twice as many as in the P versus L comparative transcriptome at T3. Twelve GO terms were significantly enriched among the up-regulated genes and five GO terms were significantly enriched among the down-regulated genes (Fig. [1](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Table 7). The enriched GO terms among the up-regulated genes were primarily associated with WRKY transcription factor, POD, xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase and cytochrome P450 genes (CYP71A1-like, CYP81D11-like, CYP81F3-like, and CYP736A12-like) which can be involved in plant defenses. Other up-regulated defense genes were cell-wall invertase, pathogenesis related proteins P2, P4, STH-2, and 10, glycosyltransferase, ß-1,3-glucanases, calcium-transporting ATPase, Avr9/Cf-9 rapidly elicited protein 65, ASC1, and acidic 26 kDa endochitinase. Despite the up-regulation of defense-related genes, other genes potentially associated with disease development were also up-regulated, such as the sugars will eventually be exported transporters (SWEET) and hexose transporter. The enriched GO terms among the down-regulated genes were associated with 13 chlorophyll a/b binding protein genes (Supplementary Table 7). Other down-regulated genes were threonine dehydratase, auxin responsive proteins IAA1 and IAA7, phospholipase A2-alpha, and glutamine synthetase.

#### **P versus L comparative transcriptome**

The putative function of the DEGs from the P versus L comparison were identified (Supplementary Table 5). At T1, there were 139 DEGs in the P versus L comparison, 132 of them were down-regulated on plants exposed to Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* compared to plants exposed to Lso-negative *B. cockerelli*. Two MF GO terms associated with twelve transcription factors were enriched among the down-regulated genes (Fig. [1](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Table 8). Coincidentally, eight of the ten down-regulated transcription factors in the P versus L comparative transcriptome were up-regulated in C versus P comparative transcriptome at T1 (Table [1](#page-7-0)). Other down-regulated genes included at least 26 known defense genes (Supplementary Table S5) including BON1-associated protein 2, MAPK kinase 4, matrix metalloproteinase, metallocarboxypeptidase inhibitor, POD, and Kunitz trypsin inhibitor. Interestingly, only three known defense genes were up-regulated in the P versus L comparative transcriptomes; these genes were glycosyltransferase and expansin, which play a role in restructuring plant cell walls, and Pathogenesis-related protein P2.

At T2, there were 127 DEGs. Compared to T1, there was a shift in gene regulation since at T2, 61% of the DEGs were up-regulated and 38% were down-regulated (Fig. [1](#page-4-0)). Five GO terms associated with plant response to biotic stimulus and plant defense were significantly enriched among the up-regulated genes and two GO terms associated with photosynthesis were enriched among the down-regulated genes (Fig. [1](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Table 8). The up-regulated genes playing a role in plant defenses were cytochrome P450 (CYP736A12, CYP736A12-like, and CYP76A2-like), ß-1,3-glucanase, glycosyltransferase, pathogenesis related proteins 5, 10, P2, P4, and STH-2, POD, PPO, Kunitztype trypsin inhibitor alpha chain-like, lipoxygenase, NAC domain transcription factor, and cell wall remodeling genes (Supplementary Table S5). The down-regulated genes were mainly involved in photosynthesis. The down-regulation of chloroplast photosystem and chlorophyll a/b binding genes is usually associated with leaf chlorosis and disease development.

At T3, there were 97 up-regulated and 41 down-regulated genes. No significant GO term was enriched among the up-regulated genes. Two GO terms associated with photosynthesis were enriched among the down-regulated genes (Fig. [1](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Table 8). At both T2 and T3, GO terms associated with photosynthesis were over-represented among the down-regulated genes in the P versus L comparative transcriptomes. #### **Transcriptome and qRT‑PCR results comparison**

The transcriptional profiles of five genes were analyzed by qRT-PCR (Table [2](#page-9-0)). The selected genes were associated with either plant defenses or disease development. The bioinformatic and the qRT-PCR methods revealed similar expression profiles for 13 out of 16 comparisons. The relative gene expression obtained by RNA-seq and qRT-PCR methods were also compared quantitatively. The Pearson product-moment correlation test showed a significant correlation between the result from the RNA-seq and the qRT-PCR methods (r=0.67, n=16, *P*=0.004).

#### **Transcriptome and protein assay results comparison**

Peroxidases and PPOs play crucial roles in plant induced defenses against insects and pathogens. Since POD genes were up-regulated in four comparative transcriptomes at T2 and T3, POD activities in leaves were compared among treatments. Differences in POD activity were measured at T2 [ANOVA, *F*(2,5) = 10.3, *P* = 0.0169] and at T3 [ANOVA, *F*(2,6) = 5.69, *P* = 0.0411, Fig. [6](#page-10-0)a]. At both time points, higher POD activity was measured in L plants compared to C or P plants. These results are in agreement with the expression profile of three POD genes (Solyc02g092580, Solyc03g006700.2, and Solyc06g050440.2; Fig. [6b](#page-10-0)). Similarly, PPO activity in plants at T2 was tested since the expression of a PPO gene (Solyc02g078650.2) was up-regulated in L plants compared to C or P plants at T2 (Fig. [7](#page-10-1)a). Higher PPO protein activity was detected in L plants compared to P plants at T2 [*t*(6)=2.75, *P*=0.0479, Fig. [7b](#page-10-1)]. The PPO activity was also higher in L plants than C plants but statistical analysis was not possible because only two samples from C plants were available.

The emergence and spread of diseases caused by Lso pose several concerns regarding the future of food security, yet management methods including biological and chemical control are not always effective (Munyaneza [2012](#page-14-3)). In spite of the increasing number of publications on this topic, little is known regarding gene expression changes in host plants exposed to Lso-positive vectors, or about the differences in plant responses against Lso-negative and Lso-positive *B. cockerelli*. This study is the first investigation that has characterized global tomato transcriptional responses to Lso-positive and Lso-negative *B. cockerelli*. Plant responses to the vector and/or the pathogen were evaluated in a temporal manner to determine how the progression of *B. cockerelli* infestation and Lso infection affected plant gene expression over time. Gene functional annotation performed in this study was based the gene sequence similarity. The functions of all the significantly differentially expressed genes found in this study have not been experimentally validated. The predicted functions of these genes were based on the following databases: EnsemblPlants, UniProt Knowledgebase (Bateman et al. [2015\)](#page-13-1), the tomato genome database (Fernandez-Pozo et al. [2015\)](#page-14-13) and Phytozome. Literature review was also performed to verify the predicted function of these putative genes.

In response to Lso-negative *B. cockerelli* (C vs. P), tomato plants responded predominantly by up-regulating the expression of defense genes. Interestingly, different genes were induced in response to *B. cockerelli* adults at T1, nymphs at T2, and nymphs and adults at T3. This is not surprising because *B. cockerelli* nymphs are less mobile than adults and tend to feed on the same plant during development. Therefore, it is possible that each lifestage uses a different strategy to feed on plants (Walling [2008](#page-15-10)). Overall, fewer DEGs were identified in response to nymphs (at T2) than to adults (at T1 and T3). At T1, WRKY and ERF transcription factors were induced in response to adults. WRKY transcription factors enhance plant immunity, which could be modulated by the phytohormones jasmonic acid (JA) and salicylic acid (SA) (Chen et al. [2013](#page-13-3); Pandey and Somssich [2009](#page-14-16)). Similarly, ERF genes affect the ethylene (ET) pathway and enhance the plant defense response against insects (Chen et al. [2013;](#page-13-3) Grennan [2008;](#page-14-17) Gu et al. [2000\)](#page-14-18). JA, SA, and ET play important roles in plant defenses and immunity as they regulate multiple defense genes (Karban and Baldwin [1997;](#page-14-19) Walling [2000\)](#page-15-11). Coincidentally, the genes Avr9/ Cf-9 rapidly elicited protein 65 and protein SRC2 homolog were up-regulated in plants in response to *B. cockerelli* at T1. (HR) (Liu et al. [2015](#page-14-20); Rowland et al. [2005](#page-15-12)). Modulated by nitric oxide and reactive oxygen species, HR regulates plant defenses upstream of the SA pathway (Walling [2000](#page-15-11)). Interestingly, other genes affecting HR were upregulated by nymphs (at T2) including POD genes. At T3, several HR associated genes were down-regulated including non-specific serine/threonine protein kinase and cathepsin B-like cysteine proteinase 3. *Bactericera cockerelli* also affected the plant's primary metabolism. In particular, genes involved in photosynthesis were down-regulated. For instance, the down-regulation of the genes encoding the photosystem II D2 and photosystem Q(B) proteins could result in leaf yellowing. Chlorosis is the main symptom of psyllid yellows, a condition associated with *B. cockerelli* feeding (Sengoda et al. [2010\)](#page-15-13). Moreover, the regulation of photosynthesis genes could affect source-sink carbon partitioning (Roitsch [1999](#page-15-14)). Indeed, genes associated with carbohydrate metabolism such as xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolases were up-regulated in response to *B. cockerelli* at T1 and T3. These genes are involved not only in polysaccharide metabolism but also are involved in pathways associated with ET (Munoz-Bertomeu et al. [2013](#page-14-21)) and JA-ET-dependent defenses (Tian et al. [2014](#page-15-15); Walling [2000](#page-15-11)).

Plant responses to Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* (C vs. L) were markedly different. While similar numbers of DEGs were identified at each time point in the C versus P comparisons, a greater number of DEGs were identified in the C versus L transcriptomes as time passed. However, only two replicates of the L treatment at T1 were analyzed, which might influence the number of DEGs identified for that time point. Second, over 65% of the DEGs were upregulated independently of the time point in the C versus P comparisons but approximately 25, 70 and 55% were up-regulated at T1, T2 and T3, respectively in the C versus L comparisons. These differences could result from the concomitant infection of the plants with Lso. Interestingly, photosynthesis-related genes were down-regulated independently of the time point. This is not surprising since chlorosis is a common symptom of *Liberibacter* infection in all crops. Plant defense genes, however, were regulated in a temporal dynamic manner. While down-regulated at T1, plant defense genes were up-regulated at T2 and T3. The down-regulation of defense genes at an early stage of *B. cockerelli* colonization and Lso infection could be detrimental to plants since quick defense responses could deter *B. cockerelli* and restrict pathogen infection to minimize damage. Although plant defense genes such as cytochrome P450, pathogenesis-related proteins, POD, WRKY, and cell-wall invertase were up-regulated at T2 and T3 (Hamberger and Bak [2013](#page-14-22); Pandey and Somssich [2009](#page-14-16); Roitsch et al. [2003](#page-15-16)), genes associated with plant disease were also up-regulated suggesting that disease development was in its early stage; examples include the sugar transporter SWEET, hexose transporter, and NAC domain transcription factors (Chen [2014;](#page-13-4) Chen et al. [2010](#page-13-5); Kocal et al. [2008](#page-14-23); Sherson et al. [2003\)](#page-15-17). A key difference in plant response to Lso-positive and Lso-negative *B. cockerelli* was the abrupt change in the expression of plant defense and carbohydrate-related genes. The up-regulation of carbohydrate-related genes in response to pathogen infection could affect sink-source carbohydrate regulation and favor pathogen infection and disease development.

Since differences in the expression profile of DEGs were found in the C versus P and C versus L comparative transcriptomes, plant responses to Lso-negative and Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* were compared. Interestingly, more DEGs were identified in P versus L comparison than in C versus P or C versus L comparisons at T1 and T2. At T1, 95% of the genes were down-regulated in response to Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* compared to that of Lso-negative *B. cockerelli*. Therefore, it is possible that, upon infection, Lso downregulated plant genes or modulated the ability of plants to respond to attacks. This modulation of plant responses by Lso might also positively or negatively affect the vector. It is difficult to differentiate Lso direct and indirect effects on *B. cockerelli* fitness, however *B. cockerelli* nymphs develop faster on potato and silverleaf nightshade if associated with Lso (Thinakaran et al. [2015\)](#page-15-18).

The P versus L comparative transcriptome also showed that plants exposed to Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* had lower expression of photosynthesis-associated genes than plants exposed to Lso-negative *B. cockerelli* at T2 and T3. Several studies showed that *Liberibacter*-infected plants downregulate photosynthetic activities as the disease develops (Nwugo et al. [2013a](#page-14-24), [b;](#page-14-25) Xu et al. [2015\)](#page-15-19). Other studies also showed that infected plants down-regulate photosynthetic activities as a consequence of disease development (Berger et al. [2007](#page-13-6); Huang [2001\)](#page-14-26). The reduction of photosynthesis and changes in the activity of invertases are common plant responses to pathogen attack (Berger et al. [2007](#page-13-6); Bonfig et al. [2006](#page-13-7); Kocal et al. [2008](#page-14-23); Tauzin and Giardina [2014](#page-15-20)). Results of the P versus L and C versus L comparative transcriptomes are in agreement and photosynthesis genes were down-regulated in Lso-positive plants.

Also, plants that were exposed to Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* overexpressed cell wall invertase and pathogen-related protein genes at T2 and T3. An extensive literature review of cell-wall-bound invertases reported that both cell wall invertase and pathogenesis-related protein genes were upregulated in plants in response to pathogen infection (Roitsch et al. [2003](#page-15-16)) similar to the results of this study. Due to the similarity in their overexpression in response to pathogen infection, researchers questioned whether the cell wall invertase genes have a similar role as the pathogen-related protein genes in plant defenses despite their primary role in sugar metabolism (Roitsch et al. [2003\)](#page-15-16). Cell wall invertase can limit sucrose export and inhibit photosynthesis in tomato (Kocal et al. [2008](#page-14-23)) and in other plants (Koch [1996](#page-14-27)). The association between the cell wall invertases and sugar transporters in plants was reviewed in details (Sherson et al. [2003\)](#page-15-17). Interestingly, a similarity in the up-regulation of a cell wall invertase gene and SWEET, a sugar transporter gene, was also found in our study; both were overexpressed in response to the Lso infection. The connection between SWEET and cell wall invertase genes is not yet known. However, the up-regulation of the SWEET gene is associated with plant response to pathogen. Particularly, SWEET proteins and/or genes, which are involved in the phloem-loading process, can be manipulated by pathogens to provide them with nutrients (Chen [2014](#page-13-4)) and probably allow them access to the plant's sucrose pools (Roitsch et al. [2003\)](#page-15-16). Similar to the C versus L comparative transcriptome, the P versus L comparative transcriptome revealed that a SWEET gene was up-regulated in response to Lso. In *S. tuberosum*, symptoms associated with Lso infection include irregular sugar distribution in the roots (Rashed et al. [2013\)](#page-14-28), leaves (Wallis et al. [2015\)](#page-15-21) and stems (Alvarado et al. [2012\)](#page-13-8). In citrus, huanglongbing, a disease caused by '*Candidatus* Liberibacter asiaticus', disrupts the carbohydrate flow resulting in accumulation of starch in source tissue (Rosales and Burns [2011](#page-15-22); Xu et al. [2015\)](#page-15-19). Thus, disease symptoms on tomato plants could be a result of Lso manipulating one or more genes involved in photosynthesis, and/or sugar metabolism and transport. Vector-borne pathogens can manipulate their vectors and the host plant resulting in increased transmission (Blanc and Michalakis [2016\)](#page-13-9). Pathogens can change the host by manipulating the plant metabolism and defenses. The differences in gene expression in response to Lso-positive and Lso-negative *B. cockerelli* identified at each time point could be linked to the reported changes in *B. cockerelli* behavior following Lso infection (Davis et al. [2012](#page-14-29); Mas et al. [2014](#page-14-30)).

In the present study, a positive correlation was obtained between the transcriptome and the qRT-PCR analyses. However, for some genes, differential expression was identified in the comparative transcriptome analyses but not on the qRT-PCR analyses (*P*>0.05). This could be related to differences in sensitivity between the two methods. Further validation of the bioinformatics results was performed by assaying POD and PPO enzyme activities since changes in the expression of genes encoding those proteins were identified among the treatments. The results of the enzymatic assays were in accordance with the transcriptomic results. The upregulation of POD and PPO genes and the increased enzyme activities in plants exposed to Lso-positive *B. cockerelli* suggest that plant defenses were up-regulated in response to the insect vector and/or the pathogen.

Overall, the results from this study revealed similarities between the tomato and citrus transcriptional responses to *Liberibacter* pathogens. Examples include up-regulation of ERF and WRKY transcription factor genes, induction of defense genes such as lipoxygenase, pathogenesis-related proteins, invertase, heat shock proteins, and MAPK (Fan et al. [2011](#page-14-31); Levy et al. [2017](#page-14-32); Mafra et al. [2013;](#page-14-8) Zhong et al. [2015](#page-15-23), [2016\)](#page-15-0). Interestingly, the up-regulation of WRKY and other defense genes within their network was detected during the later stage of infection. The WRKY genes and other defense genes were initially down-regulated during the early stage of Lso infection. Results from these studies will contribute to understand plant responses to *B. cockerelli* and Lso. This could be a first step towards finding plant defense genes that could be used to enhance plant resistance against the vector and pathogen. Although the result shown in this study may provide an insight into plant global gene regulation responding an insect vector and a bacterial pathogen based on gene sequence similarity, more studies are needed in the area of plant functional genomic.

In conclusion, tomato regulated sets of specific genes in response to Lso-positive and Lso-negative *B. cockerelli*, and in response to different life-stages. Interestingly, the changes in gene expression supported the observed symptoms associated with *B. cockerelli* herbivory and/or Lso infection including chlorosis (down-regulation of photosynthesisassociated genes), disruption in sugar transport and carbon partitioning (regulation of sugar transporters and genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism), and stunting (regulation of phytohormone-related genes and of NAC domain transcription factor genes). Based on the results presented here, plants perceived *B. cockerelli* herbivory and Lso infection and responded by activating defenses. A summary of plant responses involved in defense and disease symptom development across the nine comparative transcriptomes is shown in Fig. [8](#page-13-10). However, those defenses did not stop insect development or Lso infection. Therefore, we speculate that a complex molecular dialog was established among *S. lycopersicum, B. cockerelli*, and Lso, characteristic of the evolutionary arms-race between plants and their attackers.
