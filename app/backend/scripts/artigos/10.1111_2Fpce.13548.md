# **Sorghum tiller bud growth is repressed by contact with the overlying leaf.**

#### ABSTRACT

Basal branching in grasses, or tillering, is an important trait determining both form and function of crops. While similarities exist between eudicot and grass branching programs, one notable difference is that the tiller buds of grasses are covered by the subtending leaf, whereas eudicot buds are typically unconstrained. The current study shows that contact with the leaf sheath represses sorghum bud growth by providing a mechanical signal that cues the bud to refrain from rapid growth. Leaf removal resulted in massive reprogramming of the bud transcriptome that included signatures of epigenetic modifications, and also implicated several hormones in the response. Bud abscisic acid (ABA) transiently increased, then decreased following leaf removal relative to controls, and ABA was necessary to repress bud growth in the presence of the leaf. Jasmonic acid (JA) levels and signaling increased in buds following leaf removal. Remarkably, application of JA to buds *in situ* promoted growth. The repression of bud growth by leaf contact shares characteristics of thigmomorphogenic responses in other systems, including the involvement of JA, though the JA effect is opposite. The repression of bud growth by leaf contact may represent a mechanism to time tillering to an appropriate developmental stage of the plant..

#### Summary Statement

Sorghum tiller buds are repressed by mechanical signals generated by contact with the overlying leaf sheaths that alter bud-local signaling by the phytohormones abscisic acid and jasmonic acid.

Key words: Abscisic acid, bud, jasmonic acid, leaf sheath, mechanical signal, *Sorghum bicolor*, thigmomorphogenesis, tillering.

#### INTRODUCTION

Shoot branching is an important trait that contributes to fitness in natural environments, and to end use and yield in agriculture. Branches that arise from the base of monocots are known as tillers. In sorghum, tillering is a trait targeted by breeders when developing varieties for specific end-uses. Prolific tillering is typically desired in sorghum varieties selected for forage, low tillering is targeted for grain types, and a complete absence of tillers is the ideal phenotype for sorghum used as a feedstock for sucrose extraction.

Tillering (or branching) occurs in a series of steps, beginning with the establishment of an axillary meristem in the leaf axil, followed by the elaboration of the meristem into an axillary bud. Buds may remain dormant or semi dormant, may grow immediately to form a tiller (branch), or may be activated from the dormant or semi-dormant state in response to a variety of factors, including intrinsic developmental programs and environmental signals (Rameau et al. 2015). Thus branching is a plastic trait, and the expression of this plasticity gives rise to the variety of shoot forms observed in diverse species and accessions grown in diverse environments.

Tiller development is analogous to branch development in eudicots and shares many similarities. For instance, axillary buds in both programs are suppressed by a low ratio of red light to far-red light (R:FR) (Kebrom et al. 2006; Finlayson et al. 2010), are promoted by photosynthate (Kebrom & Mullet 2015; Mason et al. 2014), are promoted by cytokinins (Du et al. 2017; Muller et al. 2015), are inhibited by strigolactones (Janssen et al. 2014), are inhibited by auxin (Zhang et al. 2009; Stirnberg et al. 1999) and share homologous TCP domain family regulators that suppress outgrowth (Doebley et al. 1997; Aguilar- Martínez et al. 2007).

While many of the factors regulating axillary bud development appear to be conserved between eudicots and monocots, the differences that exist in the general morphology of these two groups of plants may impact specific bud developmental programs. For instance, in most eudicots the axillary bud is directly exposed to the environment, whereas in monocots the leaf sheath typically shields the bud during at least part of the life of the plant. Thus, eudicot buds might be expected to show increased acclimation to light and variation in humidity compared

to monocots, while monocot buds might be expected to exhibit specific responses to life in the sheltered environment provided by the overlying leaf sheath.

Given the intimate contact between the grass leaf sheath and axillary bud, it was anticipated that the overlying structure would exert some control over the fate of the bud. Leaves are understood to promote tillering/branching via the provision of photosynthate for early bud growth and establishment of the new axis (Kebrom & Mullet 2015). However, the leaf sheath could also act as a barrier to growth of the tiller bud, by physically impeding bud swelling or extension, or by a less direct mechanism of action that could involve signals produced by mechanical forces. In the present study it was hypothesized that tiller bud growth is suppressed by contact with the subtending leaf and that relief of this contact signals the bud to begin or accelerate growth.

### MATERIALS AND METHODS

#### **Plant material, growth conditions and bud measurements**

Sorghum cultivar 100M was used for the majority of experiments. Other cultivars were used as indicated. Seeds were sown singly in 50 mL conical centrifuge tubes modified with a drain hole and filled with LC1 soilless medium supplemented with 7.5g/L 20-20-20 Osmocote fertilizer. Plants were grown in a growth chamber providing 575 M m-2 s -1 of PAR at a R:FR of 13.30 from t5 fluorescent lamps with a photoperiod of 16/8 light dark and 32/22<sup>o</sup>C day night temperatures.

Axillary bud lengths were measured under a dissecting microscope using a digital caliper, 48 h after the initiation of treatments or as specified. Shoot lengths were determined from the root-shoot junction to the tip of the longest leaf.

## **Experimental treatments**

Defoliation was conducted by removing the lamina and approximately 25% of the upper part of the sheath. Leaf removal was conducted by peeling the entire coleoptile and first leaf from the culm to expose the axillary bud. The dissection was easily accomplished with minimal

handling and without touching the bud. For some experiments sheath-bud contact was relaxed by carefully incising the first leaf sheath with a sharp scalpel in an inverted "V" over axillary bud. Similar incisions made approximately 2 mm acropetal to the position of the underlying bud served as wounding controls. Leaf sheath-bud force was also defeated by wedging the first leaf sheath from the culm with a wooden toothpick, which was then removed 24 h later. To investigate the effect of photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), a piece of clear plastic tubing was placed around the culm without contacting the exposed bud. The tubing was surmounted by a clear plastic cap, and this treatment represented the control. Aluminum tape was applied over the clear tubing and cap to restrict exposure to light. Contact force was applied using a short (3-4 mm) piece of surgical rubber tubing (1.6 mm internal diameter, 0.8 mm wall thickness) that was expanded and carefully placed over the base of the culm to contact the axillary bud following contraction of the tubing.

#### **Estimation of bud-leaf and bud-compress contact force**

The force required to deform the leaf sheath or the compress tubing near the bud was estimated using a custom built apparatus (see Supporting Information Fig. S1). The frame and vertically translocating stage were derived from a dissecting microscope. A block mounted to a beam affixed to the stage served to support a miniature microexpander. The microexpander was constructed as a lever bent at the fulcrum to pivot on an immobile support, with the insertable portion 1 mm in width, 3 mm long and 0.5 mm thick. One end of the microexpander lever was attached via a wire linkage to a weight that preloaded a mini balance under the stage, while the other end was inserted 2 mm into a 3 mm long basal stem section between the first leaf sheath and culm. The stage was moved upwards until the microexpander lever attached to the weight came into contact with the microexpander support, which resulted in 0.25 mm of deformation between the leaf sheath and culm or compress tubing. The force required to accomplish this deformation was recorded from the mini balance with a remotely-operated digital camera. The beam, block and microexpander were custom fabricated from aluminum.

#### **Phytohormone and inhibitor treatments**

BAP, JA, and abamineSG were applied near the axillary bud of plants with intact leaves using a small syringe fitted with a long, blunt, small diameter needle. The needle was coated with a silicone-based lubricant and inserted between the first leaf sheath and the culm until the tip was near the base of the leaf. 40 nmoles of BAP were applied in 4 L of 0.02N NaOH with 0.05% Silwet. JA was applied as 2 nmoles of the active (+) isomer in 4 L of 60% ethanol with 0.05% Silwet. abamineSG was applied as 10 nmoles in 4 L of 60% ethanol with 0.05% Silwet. ABA was applied directly to axillary buds of plants with the first leaf removed. 100 pmoles of the active (-) isomer of ABA was applied in 1 L of lanolin with 25% ethanol, 0.25% DMSO and 0.07% Silwet every 24 h for a total of 2 applications. The JA-Ile biosynthesis inhibitor Jarin-1 (Analyticon Discovery GmbH, Potsdam, Germany) was also applied directly to axillary buds of plants with the first leaf removed. 1 nmole was applied in 1 L of lanolin with 25% ethanol, 1.0% DMSO and 0.07% Silwet every 24 h for a total of 2 applications. Control solutions without hormones/inhibitors were applied in the same manner to provide mock treatments.

#### **Quantification of ABA and JA**

For hormone analyses, buds were collected at various times after removing the first leaf (or immediately after removing the leaf at each time point for controls) weighed and frozen in liquid N2. For each treatment/time point 4 biological replicates of 15 buds were collected. A 5 mm basal culm/stem section adjacent to the bud was also collected, weighed and frozen at the same time. For each treatment/time point 4 biological replicates of 6 stem sections were collected. The total time needed to collect tissue samples was less than 5 min per replicate. Treatment and sampling were staggered to ensure equivalent timing for each replicate. Tissues were spiked with labeled standards and extracted as described previously (Yao & Finlayson 2015). Hormones were separated using a Waters HSS C18 column (1.8 M, 2.1 x 100 mm) on a Waters Aquity UPLC system using a gradient of 85% 10 mM formic acid in water: 15% acetonitrile to 10% 10 mM formic acid in water: 90% acetonitrile at 0.35 mL min-1 , over 9 min. Hormones in the UPLC eluent were quantified using a Waters TQD detector with an ESI probe and mass filters operated in positive MRM mode. Source parameters were: desolvation N<sup>2</sup> at 900 mL min-1 , desolvation temp 450<sup>o</sup>C, cone flow 90 mL min-1 N2, 3.1 kV capillary voltage, 27 V cone voltage, Ar collision gas at 0.15 mL min-1 . For ABA quantification, collision energy was set at 11 V and precursor and product ions 263.10, 153.20 and 269.10, 159.20 m/z were monitored for the endogenous hormone and internal standard, respectively. For JA quantification, collision energy was set at 16 V and precursor

and product ions 209.00, 59.00 and 211.00, 61.00 m/z were monitored for the endogenous hormone and internal standard, respectively.

#### **General statistics**

Statistical comparisons of bud lengths and hormone abundances were made by two-tailed ttests, or ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD (implemented in R), as appropriate. Log transformation was applied prior to ANOVA in cases where variance scaled with the mean.

#### **Transcriptome analysis**

For transcriptome profiling, four replicates of 15 buds were harvested as described above for hormone analyses. RNA was extracted using Trizol, the extracted RNA was digested with DNAseI and purified using an RNAeasy kit. The three highest quality replicates of each sample (by Bioanalyzer) were used for further analysis. Libraries were constructed with the Illumina TruSeq kit and 125 bp single-end reads were obtained from an Illumina 2500 sequencer. Quality was verified by FastQC. Reads were mapped to the Phytozome 3.1 sorghum genome sequence using CLC Genomics Workbench 8.5.1 giving an average of 28 M unique exon reads per sample. A multi-group comparison was created and expression differences were assessed using the software's Extraction of Differential Gene Expression (EDGE) algorithm. Data were filtered by retaining those genes with an FDR less than 0.05 in any of the three time point comparisons of control versus leaf removed samples, and then further filtered by retaining those genes with an untransformed absolute fold change of 2.48 or greater. Unique exon reads were normalized by scaling to the median and then used to conduct Weighted Gene Coexpression Network Analysis (WGCNA) as described in Langfelder & Horvath (2008). The network modules (clusters) generated by the WGCNA were used for GO analysis using the AgriGO tool (http://bioinfo.cau.edu.cn/agriGO/). GO analysis was conducted both with the stringent GO terms provided by Phytozome, and with an expanded set that also included terms associated with homologous Arabidopsis genes. Hormone and touch responsive gene expression patterns were used to estimate the corresponding signaling trends, using sorghum homologs of Arabidopsis genes described as responsive to touch (Lee et al. 2005) or ABA and JA (Goda et al. 2008). Reads per kilobase per million mapped reads were normalized to the average of the control 1 h values and reported as average log2 changes. Values of zero were substituted with the lowest non zero

value for each individual gene. Statistical comparisons of signaling levels (average relative stimulus-associated gene expression) were made by ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD (implemented in R). Transcriptome data is deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus with accession number GSE127822.

## RESULTS

## **Tiller bud growth is promoted by removal of the overlying leaf**

Sorghum cultivar 100M was used for all experiments, unless noted otherwise. This grain cultivar exhibits a relatively high tillering phenotype and its bud development has been characterized in several previous studies (Kebrom et al. 2006; Kebrom et al. 2010; Kebrom & Mullet 2015; Kebrom & Mullet 2016). Removal of the first leaf including the sheath at 7 days after sowing resulted in accelerated axillary bud elongation after an intervening span of 48 h (Fig. 1a, b). The 48 h growth increment of buds following leaf removal was up to 6 times that of buds in intact plants. The effect of leaf removal was evaluated in a selection of sorghum lines that showed variation in tillering, including BTx623 (low tillering grain type), DDYSM (high tillering grain type), Greenleaf (high tillering forage type) and Keller (low tillering sweet type). Leaf removal promoted bud development in all of the lines, with the increment in elongation roughly proportional to initial bud size (Fig. 1c).

Removal of the overlying leaf exposed the bud to more direct light, which could conceivably alter bud growth. The effect of photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) on bud growth was tested by removing the leaf and then shading the bud with a section of clear plastic tubing that encircled the stem, which in turn was covered with an opaque screen that included a cap (Fig. 2b). Shading the bud from direct illumination did not inhibit bud growth (Fig. 2a). An identical covering system without the opaque screen was used with plants subjected to leaf removal to control for the effects of the plastic cover that are unrelated to light. Bud growth was equivalent with both coverings.

The effect of the leaf sheath on transmitted light parameters was explored by scanning a leaf sheath with a spectroradiometer modified with a slit aperture over the sensor. The sheath transmitted about 40% of the incident light, and reduced the R:FR from 13.30 to 3.86. While reduced phytochrome activity inhibits bud growth, phytochrome inactivation will not occur

until the R:FR falls well below 1, suggesting that sheath effects on the R:FR do not contribute to the growth response.

To determine what part of the leaf was necessary for repression of bud growth, the effect of removing the entire leaf including the sheath versus removing the lamina and the upper portion of the sheath was examined. Removal of the lamina and upper sheath had no effect on bud growth (Fig. 2c). Bud growth was strongly promoted when the portion of sheath in contact with the bud was removed, demonstrating that the sheath normally suppresses bud growth.

#### **Bud growth is suppressed by mechanical signals generated by bud-leaf sheath contact**

The leaf sheath could potentially inhibit bud growth by mechanical effects associated with contact between the sheath and the bud, or by the action of chemical signals associated with the leaf. The potential for a leaf-derived repressive chemical signal was investigated by leaving the leaf on the plant, but relieving bud-sheath force by incising a small inverted "V" in the sheath where it contacts the bud, or making the same incision in the leaf just acropetal to the bud as a wounding control (Fig. 3c). In both cases the leaf appeared to continue functioning normally. The buds grew out through the leaf to form tillers when the incision relieved the contact between the leaf and bud, but not when the incision was made just a few mm away (Fig. 3a). The leaf was also wedged from the culm with a toothpick to relieve or reduce contact with the bud while retaining the leaf (Fig. 3d). The leaf again appeared to function normally, but the bud grew much faster compared to the non-wedged plants (Fig. 3b). The results suggested that the inhibitory signal was mechanical rather than chemical in nature.

To test if bud growth was actually physically constrained by the overlying leaf, the synthetic cytokinin 6-benzylaminopurine (BAP) was applied near the buds of plants with intact leaves to determine if the bud was capable of overcoming the physical obstacle when stimulated to grow. BAP readily promoted bud growth versus the mock control (Fig. 3e), demonstrating that although the presence of the leaf sheath inhibited bud growth, the inhibition was not due to an insurmountable barrier to growth, but rather appears to represent a signal for the bud to remain repressed.

#### **Bud growth is suppressed following leaf removal by restoring contact force with the bud**

The mechanical nature of the repressive signal was further supported by removing the entire first leaf and then restoring physical contact with the bud using a short, small diameter section of elastic tubing applied as a compress encircling the shoot base and bud (Fig. 4b). Reestablishing bud contact force inhibited bud growth in the absence of the leaf, and removing the compress permitted growth (Fig. 4a). It was also theoretically possible that the force of the tubing repressed bud growth by providing an insurmountable barrier to growth. However, bud growth occurred when BAP was applied to buds fitted with the compress, demonstrating that the inhibition was not due to inability of the bud to grow, but rather due to a repressive signal generated by contact with the tubing (Fig. 4c).

The force exerted by the leaf and compress was estimated by measuring the force required to deform the leaf or tubing near the bud in excised shoot sections. The leaf required greater force for deformation than the compress tubing (Fig. 4d). In fact, the leaf deformation force is likely an underestimation of the actual force since a substantial portion of the leaf was removed in order to prepare shoot sections for the measurements.

# **RNA-Seq of the bud reveals massive transcriptome reprogramming in response to leaf removal**

To investigate the molecular events and potential pathways associated with the promotion of axillary bud growth by leaf removal, a transcriptome profiling experiment using RNA-Seq was conducted on buds at different times after leaf removal. While leaf removal does not exactly mirror the natural developmental process relieving contact force between the bud and leaf sheath, it does provide a reliable, facile, robust and synchronous signal for the initiation of bud growth that is very suitable for probing the bud transcriptome response. A large portion of the bud genome (13958 genes, Supporting Information File S1) showed significant differential expression (FDR<0.05) in response to leaf removal at 1, 3 or 6 h. Expression fold changes ranged from 444 fold repressed (3 h) to 5540 fold induced (1 h). A subset (8623 genes showing the greatest variation in expression, Supporting Information File S1) of the total differentially expressed gene set was explored using Weighted Gene Coexpression Network Analysis (WGCNA) as a tool to reveal potentially important pathways (Langfelder & Horvath 2008). WGCNA produced 15 modules, or clusters, (Fig. 5a) containing varying

numbers of coexpressed genes (Fig. 5b, Supporting Information File S1), and each module was subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. Eleven of the 15 modules provided support for GO enrichment. The magenta and blue modules were enriched in terms related to DNA replication, mitosis, the cell cycle and microtubule/cytoskeleton function indicating that the cells in the bud may be preparing for, or undergoing, division in response to leaf removal. Several of the modules (cyan, magenta, blue, brown) also included terms related to chromatin modification and epigenetic regulation of gene expression, suggesting that global reprogramming of transcriptional processes were occurring, an hypothesis supported by the large number of differentially expressed genes discovered. Hormonal GO terms were overrepresented in the salmon (response to cytokinin stimulus) and turquoise (response to SA, JA, ABA stimuli and ethylene mediated signaling pathway) modules.

#### **ABA and JA regulate bud growth**

Hormonal GO enrichment discovered by the WGCNA prompted a more detailed examination of the potential roles of ABA and JA. Average global ABA- and JA-inducible gene expression was investigated by focusing on sorghum homologs of Arabidopsis genes documented as induced by the specific hormones (Goda et al. 2008). ABA signaling was elevated at 1 and 3 h after leaf removal, but declined to the control level by 6 h (Fig. 6a). Global JA signaling was elevated at 1 and 3 h after leaf removal and remained elevated at 6 h, although it had declined from the levels of the earlier time points (Fig. 7a). Buds and adjacent shoot tissue sections were collected after removal of the first leaf and ABA and JA were quantified over a 12 h time course. ABA in control buds showed a steady rise over the 12 h timespan of the experiment (Fig. 6b), a possible reflection of a diurnal/circadian rhythm. Compared to controls, ABA was elevated in buds of plants subjected to leaf removal at 3 h, but by 6 h bud ABA had declined, and was much lower than controls at 12 h. ABA levels in the adjacent stem increased slightly and remained elevated after leaf removal, demonstrating independent regulation of ABA homeostasis in bud and stem (Fig. 6c). Bud JA increased about 42 fold in response to leaf removal at 3 h, remained at a similar high level at 6 h and declined to 4.4 fold greater than control at 12 h (Fig. 7b). JA levels in the adjacent stem were unaffected by leaf removal, and like ABA, showed independent homeostasis in buds vs. stems (Fig. 7c).

Arabidopsis studies have shown that ABA suppresses bud growth (Reddy et al. 2013; Yao & Finlayson 2015; Gonzalez-Grandio et al. 2017). The current data also implied a regulatory role for ABA in sorghum tiller buds, which was tested. Bud growth was strongly suppressed when ABA was applied following leaf removal, and the ABA biosynthesis inhibitor abamineSG was effective at promoting bud growth when applied near the buds of intact plants (Fig. 8a). Thus, ABA appears to maintain the repressed state of the bud in response to contact between leaf sheath and bud. This hypothesis was supported by the eventual decrease in bud ABA and ABA signaling following leaf removal. The initial increase in bud ABA and signaling may be the result of a decline in humidity experienced by the bud as a result of leaf removal.

The increase in bud JA and JA signaling levels suggested that JA might also be involved in the bud's response to leaf removal. The role of JA was tested by applying it near the buds of intact plants with a syringe. JA is typically considered inhibitory to growth, and it did inhibit shoot elongation slightly (Supporting Information Fig. S2a), but it also promoted tiller bud growth (Fig. 8a). A positive role for JA in stimulating bud growth was confirmed by applying the JA-Ile biosynthesis inhibitor Jarin-1 (Meesters et al. 2014) to buds. Jarin-1 application to buds following leaf removal significantly reduced, but did not eliminate, the bud growth response (Fig. 8b). Jarin-1 had no effect on shoot elongation (Supporting Information Fig. S2b).

## **Thigmomorphogenesis-related gene expression is altered in buds in response to leaf removal**

Previous studies in a diverse species have discovered transcriptional changes responsive to mechanical stimulation (Lee et al. 2005; Ishihara et al. 2017; Pomiès et al. 2017). Sorghum homologs of Arabidopsis genes induced by touch (Lee et al. 2005) were identified in the set of bud genes differentially expressed in response to leaf removal. Touch-induced genes were significantly overrepresented in the turquoise module (Chi-Square = 72.23, P < 0.0001), and were used to determine average global signaling trends. Touch signaling increased in controls over the 6 h of the time course, possibly in response to incidental contact while harvesting other plants of the cohort during the experiment (Fig. 9). Leaf removal resulted in a sharp increase in touch signaling at 1 h, followed by a decline to 6 h, though signaling was still elevated compared to controls (Fig. 9).

#### DISCUSSION

#### **Leaf contact regulates tiller bud growth**

Sorghum tiller bud growth is repressed by contact with the overlying leaf sheath. This finding expands the current knowledge regarding the mechanisms associated with branching and is highly relevant to sorghum tillering. The data suggest that the relief of leaf contact force acts as a developmental cue to promote tillering at a critical developmental stage. As the sorghum seedling grows, culm circumference growth outpaces growth of the first leaf. Eventually, leaf sheath structural integrity is compromised, often by longitudinal splitting of the leaf over the bud. The splitting of the leaf sheath relieves contact force on the bud, which is then signaled to grow more rapidly. This process repeats at several superior nodes, which contributes to the production of early tillers. Later leaves usually retain structural integrity and tiller bud growth from these positions generally occurs after flowering, if at all, giving rise to aerial branches. It may be hypothesized that a sensory apparatus in the bud cells detects the contact force of the overlying leaf sheath, and in response inhibitory signals are generated that slow the growth of the bud. When the force is relieved, these inhibitory signals are repressed, promotive signals are induced, and bud growth is accelerated.

An earlier study in wheat explored the mechanics associated with tillering using rigid plastic tubes applied to the base of the plant to constrain stem expansion (Williams & Metcalf 1975). Tiller buds in the leaf axils of plants subjected to constraint were suppressed, and growth of the coleoptile bud, which is typically repressed, was promoted by relieving mechanical force by surgical incision. The authors concluded that physical constraint plays a role in regulating tiller development. Given the nature of the experimental constriction applied it is likely that the buds did indeed face an insurmountable obstacle to growth, however the current study shows that bud growth may be inhibited without a requirement for actual physical constraint.

Prior research on mechanical stimulation has produced limited evidence regarding how transcriptional outputs are regulated. The sorghum bud-leaf response offers a dissimilar system to compliment thigmomorphogenic studies in Arabidopsis and trees. The sorghum system is unique as bud growth is a positive response to mechanical perturbation, whereas those previously described are inhibitory (Chehab et al. 2009). It is possible that the bud-leaf system reflects the reverse of applying the force or strain typically employed in this type of

research (ie- relieving an existing force or strain imposed on the bud). It is also possible that leaf removal imposes a force on the bud cells that is not experienced in the presence of the leaf sheath. Further research is obviously needed to understand the similarities and differences of the early responses and the functions of downstream components.

#### **Bud responses to leaf contact force involve ABA and JA**

Bud growth was repressed by bud local ABA, as was documented in Arabidopsis (Reddy et al. 2013; Yao & Finlayson 2015; Gonzalez-Grandio et al. 2017; Holalu & Finlayson 2017). Thus, ABA repression of bud growth represents a mechanism that contributes to the generation of plant form over wide evolutionary distances, as had been suggested by many prior studies employing various species (Arney & Mitchell 1969; Tucker & Mansfield 1972; Tamas et al. 1979; Knox & Wareing 1984; Le Bris et al. 1999; Cline & Oh 2006, Arend et al. 2009). ABA levels initially increased following leaf removal, but later declined below levels in buds of intact plants. The initial increase in ABA was likely associated with water loss from the bud following leaf removal, discussed in more detail below. The early elevation of ABA might slow bud growth until levels declined sufficiently, or until other promotive signals overcame the ABA-induced inhibition.

Previous Arabidopsis bud transcriptome profiling identified JA GO terms associated with bud growth responses to the R:FR (González-Grandío et al. 2013; Reddy et al. 2013), suggesting JA may play a role in branching. In the present study, GO terms associated with JA were overrepresented in the expression data and bud JA levels and signaling increased robustly following leaf removal. JA has been associated with both wounding (Koo & Howe 2009), and mechanical stimulation in various species (Chehab et al. 2009). The growth of wild type Arabidopsis is suppressed by touching, but JA deficient mutants do not show this response (Chehab et al. 2012), which is consistent with the general growth inhibiting action of this hormone (Huang et al. 2017). However, JA promoted the growth of sorghum buds, a surprising finding given its more typical inhibitory effects. JA promotes stamen development, trichome formation, and tuber formation, providing some precedent for growth stimulatory action (Huang et al. 2017). JA also contributes to Arabidopsis lateral root development by stimulating IAA accumulation in the root (Sun et al. 2009). Root IAA levels are promoted by JA induction of ERF109 expression, a transcription factor that in turn promotes expression of the IAA biosynthetic genes *ASA1* and *YUCCA2* (Cai et al. 2014). In

the current study the sorghum homolog of *ERF109* localized to the turquoise module (associated with JA GO terms), and was robustly induced by leaf removal (418 fold at 1 h). It is possible that JA-induced *ERF109* expression could elevate bud IAA levels, increasing source strength and auxin export to the stem to accelerate bud growth.

#### **Leaf removal results in massive reprogramming of the bud transcriptome**

A large fraction of the genome showed differential expression in buds following leaf removal. This may not be unexpected since leaf removal radically transformed the bud's environment. Enrichments for GO terms related to thigmotropism and mechanical stimulation/response were not discovered. However, these terms are rare, and thus are unlikely to be detected. Directly interrogating the data for sorghum homologs of touch responsive Arabidopsis genes provided evidence for robust and rapid stimulation of this response, supporting the contention that leaf removal elicits a thigmostimulation response in the bud, which translates into growth promotion.

Wounding terms were overrepresented in the turquoise module even though buds did not show damage as a consequence of leaf removal. Conceivably, wounding of the adjacent stem could induce wound responsive genes in the bud, but it is also possible that some of these genes could be more closely associated with thigmostimulation rather than wounding *per se*. Defining the functions of specific genes in the bud growth response will be necessary to resolve this paradox.

The cyan, magenta, blue and brown modules included GO terms related to epigenetic regulation of gene expression. Eigenvalues in these modules tended to be repressed following leaf removal, possibly indicating that the maintenance of epigenetic markers suppressing gene expression is relaxed to permit expression to occur. Emerging evidence supports a role for chromatin modification in thigmomorphogenesis. VIP3, a component of the histone modifying PAF1 complex, modifies histone methylation at the touch responsive *TCH3* and *TCH4* genes, and is also necessary for typical responses to touch in Arabidopsis (Jensen et al. 2017). Additionally, SET DOMAIN GROUP 8, a protein involved in histone remodeling, is necessary for the differential expression of a subset of genes induced by mechanical stimulation in Arabidopsis shoots (Cazzonelli et al. 2014). A gene encoding a SET DOMAIN GROUP protein (Sobic.007G161600), was the most highly connected gene in the brown module, suggesting that it might serve a similar key function in the sorghum bud system. Given the large number of differentially expressed genes discovered, and the overrepresentation of terms related to epigenetic modification of gene expression in several modules, further exploration of the potential role of epigenetics seems warranted.

Several modules (pink, red, green, black) were enriched in terms associated with cell division and active cellular metabolism such as ribosome biogenesis, translation, nitrogen metabolism and amino acid metabolism. Eigenvalues of these modules tended to increase with time following leaf removal perhaps reflecting the eventual growth effect rather than rapid response pathways. The magenta and blue modules were enriched for mitosis-related GO terms, which would be expected in tissues signaled to accelerate growth.

#### **Leaf removal alters light and water responses in the bud**

In addition to altering mechanical forces, leaf removal also exposed the bud to more light. The bud responded to the increased light by rapidly inducing light responsive genes, many of which grouped in the turquoise module. Presumably these gene products tuned the bud's photosynthetic apparatus to the elevated PAR as it transitioned from sink to source. However, while the bud responded to the increased light, it was not necessary for the growth response since shading the bud did not prevent it.

Leaf removal also likely altered the humidity the buds experienced. Occasionally very small buds showed signs of desiccation following leaf removal. GO terms associated with water deprivation and ABA responses were overrepresented in the turquoise module suggesting an attempt to mitigate the negative effects of moisture loss. Since most buds went on to grow robustly after leaf removal these expression responses seemed to be successful.

### CONCLUSIONS

The discovery that contact between the bud and overlying leaf acts as a signal to repress sorghum tiller bud growth adds a new dimension to our understanding of the generation of shoot architecture. The loss of leaf sheath contact with the bud may provide a cue promoting bud growth at an appropriate developmental stage of the plant defined by the structural failure of the leaf sheath attachment to the expanding culm. Massive reprogramming of the

bud transcriptome following leaf removal was evident and suggested roles for epigenetic modifications and several phytohormones. In accordance with recently described ABA suppression of bud growth in Arabidopsis, sorghum tiller bud growth appears to be promoted by a relative decline in bud ABA accumulation as a consequence of the relief of sheath contact. Unexpectedly, JA accumulated to high levels specifically in the bud following leaf removal and stimulated bud growth. Increased JA accumulation links the promotion of bud growth by relief of leaf sheath contact to other thigmomorphogenic responses, though the effects of JA are opposite in these systems. The finding that a previously unexplored program exerts strong effects on sorghum tillering increases an expanding array of confirmed pathways, and provides a new point of comparison to examine commonalities and differences in the mechanisms associated with regulation of the important tillering trait.
