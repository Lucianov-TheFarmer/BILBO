# ABSTRACT

Although DNA methylation is known to play an important role in the silencing of transposable elements (TEs) and introduced transgenes, the mechanisms that generate DNA methylation-independent transcriptional silencing are poorly understood. Previous studies suggest that RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) is required for the silencing of the RD29A-LUC transgene in the Arabidopsis ros1 mutant background with defective DNA demethylase. Loss of function of ARGONAUTE 4 (AGO4) gene, which encodes a core RdDM component, partially released the silencing of RD29A-LUC in the ros1/ago4 double mutant plants. A forward genetic screen was performed to identify the mutants with elevated RD29A-LUC transgene expression in the ros1/ago4 mutant background. We identified a mutation in the homologous gene of PRP31, which encodes a conserved pre-mRNA splicing factor that regulates the formation of the U4/ U6.U5 snRNP complex in fungi and animals. We previously demonstrated that the splicing factors ZOP1 and STA1 contribute to transcriptional gene silencing. Here, we reveal that Arabidopsis PRP31 associates with ZOP1, STA1, and several other splicing-related proteins, suggesting that these splicing factors are both physically and functionally connected. We show that Arabidopsis PRP31 participates in transcriptional gene silencing. Moreover, we report that PRP31, STA1, and ZOP1 are required for development and stress response. Under cold stress, PRP31 is not only necessary for pre-mRNA splicing but also for regulation of cold-responsive gene expression. Our results suggest that the splicing machinery has multiple functions including pre-mRNA splicing, gene regulation, transcriptional gene silencing, and stress response.

Key words: PRP31, splicing factor, DNA methylation, transcriptional gene silencing, stress response

# INTRODUCTION

Epigenetic silencing is vital for proper gene regulation and genome stability. The formation of heterochromatin, as a consequence of repressive DNA methylation and histone modifications, contributes to the transcriptional silencing of both transposable elements (TEs) and undesirable transgenes ([Law](#page-13-0) [and Jacobsen, 2010\)](#page-13-0). In plants, an RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) pathway leads to *de novo* cytosine methylation of DNA regions that are homologous to 24-nt siRNAs (small interfering RNAs) ([Wassenegger et al., 1994; Matzke and Mosher,](#page-14-0) [2014\)](#page-14-0). It is believed that two plant-specific DNA-dependent RNA polymerases, Pol IV and Pol V, are responsible for producing the noncoding RNAs required for establishing DNA methylation at RdDM target loci [\(Haag et al., 2012; He et al.,](#page-13-0) [2014](#page-13-0)). Pol IV is thought to produce single-stranded RNAs (ssRNAs), which are converted into double-stranded RNAs (dsRNAs) by the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase RDR2 and then cleaved into 24-nt siRNAs by the Dicer-like protein DCL3 ([Xie et al., 2004; Matzke et al., 2007\)](#page-14-0). Both Pol V and Pol II have been reported to produce scaffold RNAs for silencing adjacent siRNA-targeted loci ([Wierzbicki et al., 2008;](#page-14-0) [Zheng et al., 2009](#page-14-0)).

In the nucleus, AGO4 localizes to the Cajal body, which is characterized by the U2 snRNAbinding protein U2B ([Li et al., 2006\)](#page-13-0). AGO4 can interact with the largest subunit of Pol V, NRPE1, and the SPT5-like protein KTF1 via their conserved GW/WG domains [\(Bies-Etheve et al.,](#page-13-0) [2009; He et al., 2009b\)](#page-13-0). Pol V-produced scaffold RNAs are thought to interact with 24-nt siRNAs and facilitate the recruitment of the AGO4-siRNA complex to RdDM target loci [\(Wierzbicki et al., 2009](#page-14-0)). Eventually, the DNA methyltransferase DRM2, which associates with the AGO4-siRNA complex, is recruited to catalyze *de novo* DNA methylation at the RdDM target loci ([Zhong et al., 2014](#page-15-0)).

In eukaryotic cells, pre-mRNA splicing is required for the production of mature mRNAs as well as for gene expression and regulation [\(Nilsen and Graveley, 2010; Will and Luhrmann,](#page-14-0) [2011\)](#page-14-0). Four small ribonucleoprotein particles (snRNPs) U1, U2, U4/U6, and U5 snRNP combined with numerous other snRNP-associated proteins form a spliceosome complex to catalyze pre-mRNA splicing ([Jurica and Moore, 2003\)](#page-13-0). The assembly of the spliceosome is precise and ordered. First, U1 snRNP and U2AF recognize and bind to the 5<sup>0</sup> splice site and 3<sup>0</sup> splice site, respectively, to form the splicing E complex; then U2 snRNP binds to the branchpoint within the introns and forms the A complex. Subsequently, the B complex is formed by the recruitment of U4/U6. U5 tri-snRNP into the A complex [\(Makarova et al., 2001\)](#page-14-0). Before the replacement of U1 snRNP by U6 snRNP and the release of U1 and U4 snRNP, the B complex undergoes conformational rearrangement to form a catalytically active complex, the C complex. Finally, the adjacent exons are connected, and the intron lariats are released and degraded [\(Sanford et al.,](#page-14-0) [2005\)](#page-14-0).

Many splicing factors are involved in pre-mRNA splicing and are required for producing mature mRNAs [\(Will and Luhrmann,](#page-14-0) [2011\)](#page-14-0). Recent research has demonstrated that some plant splicing factors play an important role in transcriptional gene silencing ([Ausin et al., 2012; Dou et al., 2013; Huang et al.,](#page-13-0) [2013; Zhang et al., 2013\)](#page-13-0). The splicing factor SR45 affects Pol IV-dependent siRNA accumulation and contributes to the establishment and maintenance of DNA methylation at RdDM target loci, suggesting that SR45 functions at an early step of siRNA biogenesis in the RdDM pathway [\(Ausin et al., 2012](#page-13-0)). Previous studies show that the *RD29A-LUC* transgene is silenced in the mutant of the DNA demethylase ROS1 ([Gong](#page-13-0) [et al., 2002; Agius et al., 2006\)](#page-13-0). By screening for suppressors of *ros1*, we identified three splicing factors: ZOP1 (Zinc finger and OCRE domain containing Protein1), STA1 (Stabilized1, PRP6-like splicing factor), and RDM16 (Pre-mRNA-splicing factor 3) [\(Dou et al., 2013; Huang et al., 2013; Zhang et al.,](#page-13-0) [2013\)](#page-13-0). The three splicing factors are required for the silencing of *RD29A-LUC* and endogenous transposable elements. Interestingly, the three splicing factors affect transcriptional gene silencing via different mechanisms ([Dou et al., 2013;](#page-13-0) [Huang et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2013\)](#page-13-0). In the *zop1* mutant, Pol IV-dependent siRNA accumulation and DNA methylation are reduced but Pol V-dependent scaffold noncoding RNAs are not affected [\(Zhang et al., 2013\)](#page-15-0). However, the *sta1* mutation not only affects siRNA accumulation and DNA methylation but also suppresses Pol V-dependent noncoding RNA accumulation. STA1 colocalizes with AGO4 in the Cajal body, suggesting a connection between STA1 and the RdDM pathway ([Dou et al., 2013](#page-13-0)). In the *rdm16-1* mutant, DNA methylation but not siRNA accumulation is reduced at RdDM target loci [\(Huang et al., 2013\)](#page-13-0). However, the details of how these splicing factors participate in transcriptional gene silencing remain unclear.

The genome-wide alternative splicing profiles of plants change in response to many environmental stresses, and particularly to cold stress ([Iida et al., 2004](#page-13-0)). Various plant hormones and environmental stresses, including salt and cold, dramatically change the expression and splicing patterns of many serine/ arginine-rich (SR) proteins, which act as important components and regulators of the pre-mRNA splicing machinery in eukaryotes [\(Palusa et al., 2007\)](#page-14-0). The stress-induced expression alterations of the pre-mRNA splicing machinery highlight the close connection between pre-mRNA splicing and stress response. *SAD1* (supersensitive to abscisic acid [ABA] and drought) encodes an Sm-like snRNP protein that functions in pre-mRNA splicing, mRNA export, ABA signaling, and homeostasis; the *sad1* mutant exhibits increased sensitivity to ABA and drought stress in seed germination [\(Xiong et al., 2001](#page-14-0)). The splicing factor mutants *sta1-1* and *rdm16-1* are also hypersensitive to ABA and salt stress in seed germination [\(Lee et al., 2006;](#page-13-0) [Huang et al., 2013\)](#page-13-0). Many splicing factor mutants were identified through the screening for cold regulatory genes. *STA1* is cold inducible and required for the splicing of the cold-response gene *COR15A* after cold treatment [\(Lee et al.,](#page-13-0) [2006](#page-13-0)); RCF1, a recently discovered DEAD (Asp-Glu-Ala-Asp) box RNA helicase, is essential for proper splicing of many cold regulatory genes under cold stress [\(Guan et al., 2013\)](#page-13-0). Mutations of the splicing factors RCF1, STA1, and BRR2b affect the expression of many cold-responsive genes and severely impede the growth of plant under chilling conditions ([Lee et al., 2006; Guan et al., 2013](#page-13-0)). These discoveries suggest that plants depend on proper pre-mRNA splicing to respond to environmental stress.

In this study, we performed a forward genetic screen and found that a splicing factor mutation, *prp31*, enhances the expression of the *RD29A-LUC* transgene in the *ago4/ros1-1* mutant background. PRP31 is a conserved splicing factor in eukaryotes, and the yeast PRP31 ortholog was reported to be a critical component of the U4/U6 snRNP complex ([Weidenhammer et al., 1996\)](#page-14-0). Our study indicates that PRP31 associates with many splicing-related proteins including STA1, ZOP1, and RDM16, suggesting that these splicing factors are not only physically but also functionally associated. Our results also demonstrate that PRP31 is involved in transcriptional silencing of some endogenous transposable elements, although this function of PRP31 is independent of the RdDM pathway. The function of STA1 and RDM16 in plant stress response was previously reported [\(Lee et al., 2006;](#page-13-0) [Huang et al., 2013\)](#page-13-0). ## RESULTS

### A Pre-mRNA Splicing Factor Mutant prp31 Was Identified by Screening for Enhancers of ago4

To study transcriptional gene silencing in *Arabidopsis,* we performed an enhancer screen using an ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS)-mutagenized population in the *ros1/ago4* background. The *ros1/ago4* mutant carries a cold-inducible *RD29A-LUC* reporter gene and a *35S-NPTII* transgene. The expression of the two transgenes is genetically stable in the C24 wild-type ([Ishitani et al., 1997\)](#page-13-0). Loss-of-function mutation in the DNA glycosylase ROS1 leads to hypermethylation at the promoters of *RD29A-LUC* and *35S*-*NPTII*, resulting in the silencing of *RD29A*-*LUC* and *35S*-*NPTII* in the *ros1* mutant [\(Gong et al.,](#page-13-0) [2002](#page-13-0)). In the suppressor screen of *ros1* for *LUC*-reactivated mutants, we identified the *ros1/ago4* mutant, which harbors a 6-bp deletion near the 5<sup>0</sup> splice site at the 18th intron of *AGO4* ([He et al., 2009a](#page-13-0)). The *ago4* mutation leads to reactivation of the silenced *RD29A-LUC* transgene through the hypomethylation of the transgenic *RD29A* promoter region. Moreover, the *ago4* mutation slightly influences the silencing of *35S-NPTII* transgene [\(He et al., 2009a\)](#page-13-0). Here, we identify and characterize a novel mutant *ros1/ago4/#156* in the *ros1/ago4* mutant background, which greatly increases the expression of *RD29A-LUC* after cold treatment (Figure 1A). The silencing of *35S-NPTII* in the *ros1/ago4/#156* mutant, however, is not affected relative to its status in the *ros1/ago4* mutant (Figure 1A).

From a population of 256 F2 plants displaying an enhanced *LUC* expression phenotype, we mapped the mutation to an 850-kb interval (21856752–22714691 bp) on chromosome 1 ([Figure 1B](#page-2-0)). The protein encoded by *At1G60170* is homologous to the yeast splicing factor Prp31 (accession number NP\_011605). Thus, we named the gene *PRP31* in *Arabidopsis*. We ordered three available T-DNA insertion mutants (*SALK\_133742*, *SALK\_103130*, and *SAIL\_134\_H08*) from ABRC (Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center) and found that none of them is a true *prp31* mutant. Moreover, a true *prp31* mutant (*emb1220-1*) was severely defective in embryonic development, as reported in the T-DNA insertion mutagenesis screen for defective seeds ([McElver et al., 2001\)](#page-14-0).

To validate that the *prp31* mutation was responsible for the phenotypes of the *ros1/ago4/#156* mutant, we carried out the complementation experiments by transforming the whole wildtype *PRP31* genomic sequence including its 1.69-kb promoter into the *ros1/ago4/#156* mutant. In the T2 generation, four transgenic lines substantially expressed the transgenic *Myc*tagged *PRP31*. In the four transgenic lines, the transgenic *LUC* expression was fully recovered to the level of *ros1/ago4* [\(Figure 1C](#page-2-0) and [Supplemental Figure 2](#page-13-0)). Furthermore, the developmental phenotypes, such as shorter petioles and smaller rosette leaves, were complemented as well ([Figure 1C](#page-2-0)). These data confirmed that the elevated *LUC* expression and the developmental phenotypes were caused by the nucleotide substitution in *PRP31.*

*PRP31* encodes a conserved pre-mRNA splicing factor of 485 amino acids with a predicted molecular mass of 52.6 kDa. Sequence analysis indicated that PRP31 has a Nop domain (IPR002687) and a PRP31 C-terminal domain (IPR019175) ([Figure 1](#page-2-0)B). As defined by the study on the human pre-mRNA splicing factor PRP31, the Nop domain, from amino acid 188 to 334, is mainly responsible for the RNA- and protein-binding selectivity ([Liu et al., 2007\)](#page-14-0), and the PRP31C-terminal domain is from amino acid 337 to 458 [\(Makarova et al., 2002](#page-14-0)). The *Arabidopsis* PRP31 displays 67.2% similarity with and 46.9% identity to the human PRP31 (accession number NP\_056444) ([Supplemental Figure 3\)](#page-13-0), which is indispensable in U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP formation and spliceosome assembly ([Schaffert](#page-14-0) [et al., 2004](#page-14-0)). *Arabidopsis* PRP31 also exhibits 55.1% similarity with and 32.1% identity to yeast Prp31 (accession number NP\_011605) ([Supplemental Figure 3](#page-13-0)). Moreover, sequences of the PRP31 orthologs from different plants are highly conserved ([Supplemental Figure 4\)](#page-13-0). Other than the pseudogene *AT3G60610*, there is no homologous gene of *PRP31* in the *Arabidopsis* genome.

#### PRP31 Physically Associates with ZOP1, STA1, and Other Splicing-Related Proteins

To characterize the complex in which PRP31 exists, we conducted affinity purification of the PRP31-Flag fusion protein from the transgenic T2 seedlings. The proteins purified with anti-Flag antibody-conjugated beads were eluted [\(Supplemental Figure 7](#page-13-0)) and subjected to mass spectrometry. As predicted, the resulting peptides were matched to a number of splicing-related proteins (Supplemental Table 1), including the previously characterized splicing factors STA1 and RDM16 [\(Dou et al., 2013; Huang](#page-13-0) [et al., 2013](#page-13-0)). Among the purified proteins, ATRSP35 is an SR protein that is directly related to pre-mRNA splicing ([Lopato](#page-14-0) [et al., 1996](#page-14-0)), and MEE5 is an Snu114-like splicing factor that directly interacts with PRP8 and BRR2a [\(Liu et al., 2009](#page-14-0)). Taken together, the affinity purification result suggest that PRP31 associates with STA1, RDM16, and several other splicingrelated proteins.

PRP31 was previously identified in the affinity purification of both STA1-Flag and ZOP1-Flag ([Dou et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2013](#page-13-0)), suggesting that PRP31 could interact with STA1 and ZOP1. To verify such interactions, we performed coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments in the F1 transgenic plants expressing both *Myc*-tagged *PRP31* and *Flag*-tagged *STA1* or *ZOP1*. The results confirmed that PRP31 interacts with STA1 and ZOP1 *in vivo* [\(Figure 3A](#page-5-0) and 3B). Moreover, the interaction between PRP31 and AGO4 was not detectable ([Figure 3C](#page-5-0)). Pre-mRNA splicing, facilitated by numerous splicing factors, occurs within the nucleus. To determine where PRP31 functions, we performed nuclear/cytoplasmic fraction and immunostaining experiments. Similar to STA1 and ZOP1 ([Lee et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2013](#page-13-0)), PRP31 was present only in the nuclear fraction ([Figure 3](#page-5-0)D), consistent with its role in pre-mRNA splicing. The nuclear localization pattern of PRP31 was visualized by immunostaining. The results showed that PRP31 mainly exists in condensed particles with partial dispersion. PRP31 almost completely colocalizes with the splicing factors ZOP1 and STA1 ([Figure 3](#page-5-0)E and 3F; [Supplemental Figure 8\)](#page-13-0). The U2-specific binding protein (U2B) is characterized as a hallmark of the Cajal body ([Boudonck et al., 1999\)](#page-13-0), which is a nuclear body required for the modification and assembly of spliceosomal U snRNPs ([Shaw and Brown, 2004; Morris, 2008\)](#page-14-0). Similar to STA1 [\(Dou](#page-13-0) [et al., 2013\)](#page-13-0), the signal of PRP31 partially overlaps with that of U2B in the majority of nuclei we observed [\(Figure 3](#page-5-0)G and [Supplemental Figure 8\)](#page-13-0). These coimmunoprecipitation and immunolocalization results imply that PRP31 functions together with STA1 and ZOP1 in spliceosome dynamics and pre-mRNA splicing.

#### PRP31 Contributes to Transcriptional Gene Silencing Independently of the RdDM Pathway

Given that AGO4 functions in the transcriptional silencing of genes and transposons by the RdDM pathway, we examined whether *prp31* affects the transcriptional silencing of RdDM targets. To avoid the complexity caused by *ros1,* we analyzed the RNA transcript levels of *AtGP1*, *solo LTR*, *SDC*, and *TUB4* in wild-type C24, *prp31*, *ago4*, and *ago4/prp31* mutants with or without cold treatment ([Figure 4A](#page-6-0) and [Supplemental Figure 9A](#page-13-0)). We observed that the *prp31* mutation itself weakly releases the transcriptional silencing of *AtGP1*, *solo LTR*, and *SDC*, while the *ago4* mutation strongly releases the transcriptional silencing of these targets. Under cold stress condition, *prp31* mutation mildly releases the transcriptional silencing of these targets as well [\(Supplemental Figure 9A\)](#page-13-0). Interestingly, we also found that the *ago4* and *prp31* mutations additively affect the silencing of these targets ([Figure 4](#page-6-0)A and [Supplemental](#page-13-0) [Figure 9A\)](#page-13-0). The additive effect between *prp31* and *ago4* on the silencing of RdDM targets suggests that *PRP31* participates in transcriptional silencing of these RdDM targets, at least partially, in an RdDM-independent manner.

#### PRP31 Is Required for Chilling Tolerance and Transcriptional Regulation and Pre-mRNA Splicing of Cold-Responsive Genes

The splicing factors STA1, RCF1, and BRR2b play a significant role in the cold stress response ([Lee et al., 2006; Guan et al.,](#page-13-0) [2013\)](#page-13-0). Considering the physical and functional connection between STA1, ZOP1, and PRP31 ([Figures 3](#page-5-0)A, 3B, 3E, 3F, and [5A](#page-7-0)), we examined the effect of *prp31*, *zop1*, and *sta1* mutations on the chilling stress response. The chilling tolerance was determined based on hypocotyl elongation in darkness at 4-C. The hypocotyl elongation of *prp31*, *zop1*, and *sta1* was similar to that of the wild-type at 23-C but was reduced relative to the wild-type at 4-C [\(Figure 8](#page-10-0)A). The result suggests that all three splicing factors (PRP31, ZOP1, and STA1) are required for plant tolerance to chilling stress.

To study the mechanism by which PRP31 affects the cold stress response, we examined the expression of many cold-responsive genes in the wild-type and *prp31* with or without cold treatment. Quantitative RT–PCR results revealed that the transcript levels of transgenic *RD29A-LUC* and endogenous *RD29A* increased in *prp31* relative to the wild-type after a 48-h cold treatment ([Figure 2C](#page-3-0)). The transgenic/endogenous *RD29A* promoters contain Dehydration-Responsive Element (DRE)/C-repeat (CRT) *cis*-elements, which specifically respond to cold stress ([Yamaguchi-Shinozaki and Shinozaki, 1994\)](#page-14-0). CRT-binding factors (CBFs) are the transcription factors that bind the *cis*element-containing promoters and activate downstream coldresponsive genes, including *RD29A* ([Stockinger et al., 1997; Liu](#page-14-0) [et al., 1998](#page-14-0)). We found that, relative to the wild-type, *prp31* accumulates higher levels of *CBF2*, *CBF3*, and their downstream targets *RD29A*, *COR47*, *KIN1*, *COR15A*, *COR15B*, and *COR6.6* when subjected to a 2-day cold stress [\(Figure 8](#page-10-0)B) ([Zhu, 2001; Chinnusamy et al., 2010](#page-15-0)). Moreover, the expression of the splicing factor genes *STA1* and *BRR2b* was elevated in the *prp31* mutant, suggesting that these splicing factors might function together in cold response [\(Figure 8B](#page-10-0)). Consistent with the RNA deep sequencing results ([Supplemental Table 2](#page-13-0)), the expression of the cold-responsive gene was unaffected in *prp31* under normal conditions, indicating that PRP31 specifically regulates the transcript levels of these cold-responsive genes under cold stress. Nevertheless, the *prp31* mutation did not affect the transcript levels of other cold regulatory genes including *HOS1*, *SIZ1*, and *CBF1* or the transcript levels of the splicing factor genes *ZOP1* and *PRP31* [\(Supplemental Figure 14\)](#page-13-0). HOS1 is a RING-finger protein that negatively regulates *CBF2* and *CBF3* ([Lee et al., 2001](#page-13-0)), and the small ubiquitin-related modifier ligase SIZ1 controls the expression of *CBF3* through salicylic acid accumulation [\(Miura and Ohta, 2010\)](#page-14-0). Our RNA sequencing results [\(Supplemental Table 2](#page-13-0)) and quantitate RT–PCR results ([Figure 8B](#page-10-0) and [Supplemental Figure 14\)](#page-13-0) suggest that PRP31 affects the transcript levels of the *CBFs* independently of HOS1 and SIZ1.

Given that the splicing of the cold-responsive gene *COR15A* is defective in *sta1-1* ([Lee et al., 2006\)](#page-13-0), we performed intronspecific quantitative RT–PCR to detect possible pre-mRNA splicing defects in *prp31*. Three up-regulated, cold-responsive genes (*RD29A*, *COR6.6*, and *COR15A*) were chosen for indepth investigation. In the cold-treated *prp31* mutant, the mRNA levels of *RD29A*, *COR6.6*, and *COR15A* increased roughly by 2-fold, while their nonspliced transcripts increased by 10-, 8-, and 5-fold, respectively [\(Figure 8](#page-10-0)C). These data demonstrate that PRP31 is required for the pre-mRNA splicing of these cold-responsive genes. However, the mis-spliced transcripts of cold-responsive genes constitute only a small fraction of the total transcripts. In *prp31*, the mature mRNA transcripts of *RD29A*, *COR6.6*, and *COR15A* were much more abundant than their mis-spliced pre-mRNA transcripts ([Figure 8C](#page-10-0)). We speculate that the accumulation of cold-responsive genes in the *prp31* mutant is a consequence of a compensatory mechanism. According to this speculation, the *prp31* mutation disrupts the plant's internal cold-response machinery and makes the plants more susceptible than the wild-type to cold stress. Accordingly, the plant must adopt alternative compensatory mechanisms, which include the up-regulation of the coldresponsive genes.

CBFs are AP2 domain-containing transcription factors that specifically bind to *DRE cis*-elements, which are characterized by the core sequence CCGAC [\(Baker](#page-13-0) [et al., 1994](#page-13-0)); CBFs activate the expression of downstream coldresponsive genes including *RD29A* and *COR15A* [\(Stockinger](#page-14-0) [et al., 1997; Liu et al., 1998\)](#page-14-0). These results suggest that PRP31 is required for plant response to cold stress. The splicing factors STA1 and BRR2b are also important for cold stress tolerance [\(Guan et al.,](#page-13-0) [2013](#page-13-0)). We have demonstrated that the expression of *STA1* and *BRR2b* is up-regulated by *prp31* [\(Figure 8B](#page-10-0)). The results suggest that PRP31 not only functionally associates with the splicing factors STA1 and BRR2b but also affects the expression of the *STA1* and *BRR2b* genes. The up-regulation of *STA1* and *BRR2b* in the *prp31* mutant represents a feedback mechanism when the pre-mRNA splicing function is affected by *prp31*.

Although PRP31 is a conserved splicing factor, only 15 genes show intron retention in the *prp31* mutant under normal growth conditions (Supplemental Table 2). However, we found that the levels of mis-spliced mRNAs caused by *prp31* were markedly elevated under cold stress ([Figure 8C](#page-10-0)). The results imply that PRP31 contributes to pre-mRNA splicing for cold-responsive genes predominantly under cold stress rather than under normal growth conditions. A recent study indicated that the pre-mRNA splicing of 204 genes is disrupted by the mutation of the splicing-related protein RCF1 under cold stress, whereas only six genes were mis-spliced under normal growth conditions ([Guan et al., 2013](#page-13-0)). These observations suggest that a subset of splicing factors preferentially function under cold stress to maintain plant viability. Further study is required to elucidate how these splicing factors coordinate cold stress responses.

#### MATERIALS AND METHODS

#### Plant Materials, Growth Conditions, Genetic Screening Strategy, and Cloning

The wild-type C24 and the *ros1* and *ros1/ago4* mutants carry a homozygous luciferase transgene driven by an *RD29A* promoter and an *NPTII* transgene driven by a *35S* promoter ([Ishitani et al., 1997; Gong et al.,](#page-13-0) [2002; He et al., 2009a\)](#page-13-0). Seeds were sown on MS agar medium and were kept at 23-C with a photoperiod of 16 h light and 8 h dark. An EMS-treated *ros1/ago4* population was generated. In the M2 generation, the mutant seedlings were screened by luminescence imaging after 4-C cold treatment for 2 days. We selected the mutants showing enhanced luminescence and confirmed their luminescence phenotypes in the M3 generation. #### **RNA Transcription Analysis and Small RNA Blot**

Ten-day-old seedlings on MS plates with or without 2-day cold treatment were harvested and ground into fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted with Trizol reagent (Invitrogen), precipitated with an equal volume of isopropanol, washed with 75% ethanol, and dissolved into RNase-free water. The libraries were generated and sequenced by HiSeq 2000 (Illumina). The RNA deep sequencing data sets were deposited in NCBI with the GEO accession code GSE63757. The transcriptome analysis methods were as described before (Zhang et al., 2013). Intron retention events were determined based on the RNA read coverage difference (coverage percentage  $\geq 70\%$ ,  $P \leq 0.01$ ).

For the northern blot assay, small RNA was isolated from 3-g quantities of 2-week-old seedlings on MS plates. An equal volume of PEG8000 solution (1 M NaCl, 20% PEG8000) was added to total RNA solutions to separate small RNAs. After centrifugation, the supernatant fraction, which contained small RNAs, was transferred and precipitated with an equal volume of isopropanol. The pellet containing small RNAs was washed with 80% ethanol and dissolved in 80 µl of RNase-free water. A 30-µg quantity of small RNA of each sample was loaded onto a 15% polyacrylamide gel for electrophoresis. The small RNA was electroblotted to nylon Hybond-N+ membranes (GE), and each membrane was crosslinked under UV light for 90 s. The primers used in this study are listed in Supplemental Table 3.

#### **DNA Methylation Assay**

DNA methylation status was investigated by chop–PCR, southern blotting, and bisulfite sequencing. For chop–PCR, 500 ng of genomic DNA was digested overnight with the methylation-sensitive restriction enzymes HaeIII and Alul. The digested DNA was used as a template to amplify the RdDM targets *AtSN1*, *IGN5*, *IGN23*, and *solo LTR*. In the southern blotting experiment, 18-µg quantities of genomic DNA were digested with methylation-sensitive restriction enzymes for 48 h, separated in 1% agarose gel at 30 V overnight, and processed by a routine southern blotting method as described previously (He et al., 2009a). For each sample, 2 µg of genomic DNA was used to amplify both endogenous and transgenic *RD29A* promoters. The PCR products were purified with the Wizard DNA clean-up system (Promega) and cloned into the *pMD18-T* vector (Takara). The DNA methylation level of each sample was calculated based on the sequences of >15 individual clones.
