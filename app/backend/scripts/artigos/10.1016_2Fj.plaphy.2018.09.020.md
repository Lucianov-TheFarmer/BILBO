# 45 Introduction

| 46 | Day by day, the ingredients from soybean are making new avenues in the global                   |
|----|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 47 | economy. Apart from human and animal consumption, soybean oil has emerged as a source           |
| 48 | of renewable fuel, and its advantages over the current food-based biofuels have been            |
| 49 | demonstrated (Hill et al., 2006). The agonist and antagonist activity of soy compounds play a   |
| 50 | critical role in cancer research (Fritz et al., 2013; Bratton et al., 2015). Fermented soybean  |
| 51 | products may help prevent or attenuate the progression of type 2 diabetes (Kwon et al., 2010).  |
| 52 | However, unfavourable field conditions may severely restrict soybean yield due to the           |
| 53 | pathogenic fungus Phakopsora pachyrhizi.                                                        |
| 54 | P. pachyrhizi is responsible for Asian soybean rust (ASR) disease. ASR is one of the            |
| 55 | top twelve most virulent plant pathogens as observed by plant pathologists (Dean et al.,        |
| 56 | 2012). It devastates 89 different plants on all the continents of the world (Tremblay et al.,   |
| 57 | 2013). The soybean producers mostly face 20 to 90% yield loss during environmental              |
| 58 | conditions conducive for P. pachyrhizi infection. The most severe damage is largely in world    |
| 59 | famous soybean producing areas including Asia, North America, and South America                 |
| 60 | (Akamatsu et al., 2013; Akamatsu et al., 2017). Fungicides have detrimental effects on the      |
| 61 | environment and are not economically viable, however, without fungicide treatment               |
| 62 | agronomic practices alone cannot limit the damage of this fungus (Ivancovich et al., 2007). A   |
| 63 | superior variety with great yield and long-lasting resistance is one of the most efficient ways |
| 64 | to control ASR in soybean and other crops (Vuong et al., 2016). The development of an           |
| 65 | ASR-resistant soybean cultivar is dependent on eight major resistance loci which confer         |
| 66 | resistance to Phakopsora pachyrhizi (Rpp) (Rpp1-Rpp7, and Rpp1-b) (Goellner et al., 2010;       |
| 67 | King et al., 2016; Kelly et al., 2015; Childs et al., 2018). These Rpp genes mediated           |
| 68 | resistance have been characterized as phenotypic, molecular, geographical, and temporal         |
| 69 | responses. The most challenging issue is that ASR shows a different virulence capacity in       |

different geographical and temporal situations. Some resistance responses were shown to be 70 less effective against recent *P. pachyrhizi* populations from South America. For example, 71 *Rpp1* (accession, PI 200492) appeared susceptible, whereas *Rpp1-b* (PI 587880A) was 72 resistant against recent ASR populations in this area (Akamatsu et al., 2017). 73 Plants display their immunity after a series of consecutive reactions of recognition, 74 signal transduction, and signalling pathways to downstream defence responses (e.g., the 75 production of antimicrobial compounds like phytoalexin) (Dodds and Rathjen, 2010). The 76 ability of a plant to defend itself is mostly dependent on the recognition of potential 77 pathogens (Montesano et al., 2003). The first layer of perception is related to the typical 78 molecular signatures of microbial elicitors called pathogen-associated molecular patterns 79 (PAMPs). Stimulation of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) leads to PAMP-triggered 80 immunity (PTI). The second layer of perception involves recognition of pathogen virulence 81 molecules called effectors by intracellular immune receptors (Dodds and Rathjen, 2010). The 82 immune receptors are often called the NOD-like receptor (NLR) type (Macho and Zipfel, 83 2014) or nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NB-LRR) proteins (Sarris et al., 2016). This 84 layer confirms the effector triggered immunity (ETI). Higher plants also have a multilevel net 85 of structural-like plant cell wall strengthening and chemical barriers, which mark the 86 synthesis of phytoalexins and pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins in their plant defence 87 mechanism (Berger et al., 2007). They evoke effective means of communication between 88 cells regarding adjustment to new environmental conditions via hormonal signalling 89 pathways, the phytoalexin biosynthesis pathway, and pathways related to the biosynthesis of 90 other phenolic compounds such as callose deposition. 91 Near-isogenic lines termed as NILs have an almost homogeneous genetic background 92

Most recently, many efforts have concentrated on revealing the mediated 97 defence mechanisms of *Rpp* genes. For example, research pertaining to the *Rpp2* resistant 98 genotype PI 230970 and the greatly susceptible genotype Embrapa-48 against the ASR 99 transcriptome was conducted by van de Mortel et al., 2007. In the *Rpp2* resistant genotype 100 they observed the expression profiles of 424 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were 101 increased their expression within the first 12 hai, which had mostly returned to the mock 102 inoculated levels at 24 hai only in resistance genotype. These DEGs were further increased in 103 expression again at 72 hai, whereas in the susceptible genotype, gene expression remained 104 unaffected until 96 hai. mRNA transcript profiling of variety, Ankur (PI 462312) carrying the 105 *Rpp3* gene was done by Schneider et al., 2011. They categorized the 54 over-represented GO 106 biological process into seven broad groups that were important in *Rpp3* gene mediated 107 defence mechanisms (Schneider et al., 2011). The microarray datasets compared the *Rpp2*, 108 *Rpp3*, and *Rpp4* lines with respect to transcription factors (Morales et al., 2013). All the 109 efforts were unable to clarify the mechanism of *Rpp* gene mediated resistance. In addition, 110 the defence responses against ASR in NILs containing Rpp genes have no individual or 111 comparative study. Our study priors to clarify the question that "is there any specific gene or 112 sets of genes or a specific pathway are involved in *Rpp* gene mediated resistance mechanism 113 in soybean NILs? This study represents the first elucidation of variation in *Rpp* gene 114 mediated resistance to *P. pachyrhizi* focusing on major genes of a phenylpropanoid pathway 115 of different Rpp carrying NILs. 116

#### 133 *Pathogen inoculation*

Fresh urediniospores of the P. pachyrhizi isolates T1-2 (Yamaoka et al., 2014) were 134 used. Before inoculating the soybean plants, microtubes containing frozen urediniospores 135 were moved from the -80°C freezer and thawed in a 39°C water bath for 1 min. Then, the 136 spores were suspended in sterile distilled water containing 0.005% (v/v) Tween 20 and 137 adjusted to a concentration of  $1 \times 10^6$  spores ml<sup>-1</sup> with a haemocytometer. Inoculation solution 138 was prepared as 100 µl of spore solution/leaflet and sprayed onto the abaxial surface of the 139 leaves. The inoculated plants were incubated in a dew chamber at 21°C overnight 140 (approximately 16 h) and subsequently placed into a growth chamber. The whole procedure 141 was conducted following the instructions at 142 https://www.jircas.go.jp/en/publication/manual\_gudeline/30 143

144

145 Tissue collection, total RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis

Fully expanded and inoculated leaves from V2 stage plants were sampled for RNA 146 extraction. Leaves were placed in a falcon tube wrapped with aluminium foil and dipped into 147 liquid nitrogen, and subsequently were ground with a mortar and pestle using Invitrogen<sup>TM</sup> 148 TRIzol<sup>R</sup> reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. Carlsbad, CA 92008 USA). Total RNA was 149 extracted for RT-qPCR from the ASR susceptible host plant BRS184 and four NILs. each of 150 which carried one of the ASR resistance genes (*Rpp1*, *Rpp2*, *Rpp3*, or *Rpp4*). Leaves were 151 collected at different durations after inoculation (0, 12, 24, 48, and 96 hai). Each sample was 152 pooled from 12 plants. As a mock control plants without ASR inoculation (0 hai) were 153 sampled. Two µg of total RNA of each sample was subsequently treated for gDNA 154 elimination with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa clontech), and the reverse-transcription reaction was 155 conducted using the Prime Script<sup>TM</sup> RT reagent kit for the SYBR® Green RT-qPCR assay. 156 For RNA-seq, the experiment was designed based on the *P. pachyrhizi* isolate T1-2, 157 the susceptible soybean genotype BRS184, and NIL for the *Rpp3* gene. The mock inoculation 158 (no *P. pachyrhizi* inoculation) and *P. pachyrhizi* inoculation was conducted and subsequently 159 RNA isolation was performed for both genotypes. Mock inoculation on BRS184 was termed 160 as "Susceptible Mock (SM)", ASR inoculated on BRS184 was termed as "Susceptible 161 Induced (SI)", Mock (water) inoculated on the soybean accession no PI 462312 (carrying the 162 *Rpp3* resistance gene) was termed as "Resistance Mock (RM)", and ASR inoculated on this 163 accession was termed as "Resistance Induced (RI)". 0 hai (SM and RM) and 24 hai (MI and 164 RI) soybean leaf samples were collected for RNA-seq. Transcriptomic data were generated 165 from all the combinations of SM, SI, RM, and RI which were SM vs SI, SM vs RM, SM vs 166 RI. SI vs RM. SI vs RI. and RM vs RI 167

169 Total RNA preparation, RNA-seq library preparation, and sequencing

170 Total RNA was prepared from leaf tissues using RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Oiagen, MD, USA). Leaves were completely crushed using the multi-beads shocker (YASUI KIKAI, 171 Japan). The concentration of total RNA was measured using a NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher 172 Scientific, MA, USA) and adjusted to 100 ng/ul by dilution. The quality was checked for 173 integrity before performing the RNA sequencing process using the Bioanalyzer (Agilent 174 Technologies, CA, USA). For library preparation, 500 ng of total RNA per sample was used. 175 The library construction was carried out as previously described (Nagano et al., 2015). 176 Sequencing was carried out on each library to generate 100 bp PE reads for transcript 177 sequencing on an Illumina High-Seq 4000 platform by a commercial service provider 178 (Macrogen, Kyoto, Japan). Clean reads were mapped to well-annotated 'Williams 82' 179 soybean reference genome (assembly Glyma.Wm82.a1.0, annotation v1.1) obtained from 180 phytozome website (Phytozome) using Tophat v2.0.13 (Trapnell et al., 2009). Raw count 181 data were obtained by Cuffdiff embedded in Cufflinks pipeline v2.1.1 (Trapnell et al 2010). 182 Differential expression analysis of two samples was performed using the DESeq R package 183 (Anders and Huber 2010) genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 by DESeq were determined 184 to be differentially expressed genes (DEGs). For samples without biological replicates, the 185 DEGseq R package (Wang et al., 2010) was used; q value < 0.005 and |log2 (fold change) |>1 186 were set as the thresholds for differential gene expression. A versatile, platform independent 187 and easy to use Java suite for large-scale gene expression analysis was developed by genesis 188 (Sturn et al. 2002). 189

#### 340 **Discussion**

In general, the whole genome of soybean has about 46,000 genes, however 70%-80%
are duplicates. Transcriptomics is good way to avoid functional duplication to select correct
genes or pathways for soybean development. Our transcriptomic efforts found that 4518

344 DEGs were induced against ASR. All the genes were annotated by different databases (Table S2). KEGG functional annotation suggested the large subunit ribosomal protein, IRAK4, and 345 phenylpropanoid biosynthesis related genes are greatly involved in ASR defence. Such 346 results are similar to other studies pertaining to the regulation of defence and stress tolerance 347 systems of soybean against ASR (van de Mortel et al., 2007; Schneider et al., 2011; Morales 348 et al., 2013; Pandey et al., 2011). Venn diagram sorting, K-means clustering, and GO 349 enrichment analysis specified some potential candidate genes for soybean defence to ASR. 350 A venn diagram (Fig. 2A) clearly presented the 3,759 DEGs under the SM, SI, RM, 351 and RI interactions. 455 DEGs were found in the SM vs SI and RM vs RI combinations. 352 These genes represent the common genes which are induced during the soybean and ASR 353 interaction. Most of the genes in this category were down-regulated. Fourteen genes were 354 selected using the threshold of two or greater fold up-regulation in RM vs RI than SM vs SI 355 (Table S3). These genes are mostly related to defence mechanisms of different plant species 356 to various pathogens. For instance, CHR was the top up-regulated gene in the RM vs RI 357 interaction, and exhibited three times greater expression than the *CHR* transcript observed in 358 the SM vs SI interaction. CHR induced the biosynthesis of 5-deoxyisoflavonoids that 359 suppress race-specific resistance against in *Phytophthora asojae* infection in sovbean 360 (Graham et al., 2007). The flavonoids pathway gene CHR also contributes to the biosynthesis 361 of other phytoalexins, which triggers hypersensitive cell death against different pathogens 362 (Graham et al., 2007; Chang et al., 2011). Seven genes were more than 2-fold down-regulated 363 in the RM vs RI than SM vs SI combinations (Table S3). These genes also participate in 364 phenylpropanoid pathway dependent Rpp gene mediated resistance mechanisms. 365 Specifically, *IRAK* was recruited by PRRs with ligand binding which participate in the plant 366 immune system (Couto and Zipfel, 2016). The 26S proteasome is responsible for the 367 repression of jasmonate-zim domain protein (JAZ) in the JA defence mechanism (Pauwels 368

369 and Goossens, 2011). However, the down-regulation of these genes may be the response of the plant to ASR attack. Cluster 2 of the K mean clustering (Table. S4) analysis also showed 370 that the MYC2 gene which is related to the JAZ suppressor was also downregulated. The venn 371 diagram redirects us to consider flavonoids and hormonal pathways for ASR defence in 372 soybean. 373 K mean clustering (Fig. 2B) were done among 138 genes from the common DEGs from SM 374 vs SI, SM vs RM, and RM vs RI. Six different clusters were identified which guide the gene 375 groups during the ASR interaction. The greatest numbers of genes are in cluster 5, and the 376 genes are associated with chloroplast machinery, BX, and some transcription factors. The 377 chloroplast works as a processing unit for the regulation of plant development, metabolism, 378 and responses to the environment via complex signalling pathways (Bobik and Burch-Smith, 379 2015). During ASR infection, the photosynthesis rate may be reduced and the chloroplast 380 redox balance/state may be involved in soybean defence network (Demmig-Adams et al., 381 2014; Pierella Karlusich et al., 2017). The down-regulation of cluster 5 and genes in RM vs 382 RI is expected in the ASR interaction. Cluster 3 which was the second largest with 38 genes 383 showed the opposite trend of clusters 2 and 5. The DEGs in this cluster are involved mainly 384 in primary and secondary metabolism pathways and have a vital role for pathogen defence in 385 plants (Berger et al., 2007). Cluster 2 genes are mostly related to JA mediated resistance 386 mechanisms. In JA response, the 26S-proteasome mediated polyubiquitination and jasmonate 387 signaling is closely related to plant defence network (Nagels Durand et al., 2016). Therefore, 388 down-regulation of these genes indicates that during ASR defence warfare the JA signalling 389

390 were inhibited. This facilitates the SA mediated defence. Generally, the SA and JA pathways

391 are the signalling hubs that activate against biotrophic and necrotrophic pathogens,

respectively, and work together antagonistically (Pieterse et al., 2012; Caarls et al., 2015).

393 Cluster 4 showed a similar pattern to cluster 3 where the SM vs SI and RM vs RI genes were

394 up-regulated, but the genes for SM vs RM were diverse with respect to the type of genes which were down-regulated, including the top than cluster 3. Most of the genes in this cluster 395 actively contribute to cell wall reinforcement, secondary metabolites, and pathogenesis 396 related genes (Table S9). Upon pathogen attack, these genes were active for host defence 397 (Mirella Aoun, 2017). Taken together, the results of the cluster genes indicate that the 398 resistant mechanism of *Rpp* soybean plants is dependent on perturbation of the basic plant 399 defence system. The major set of these genes is shown in a heat map (Fig. S5) as defence 400 response to fungus, response to chitin, transmembrane transport, JA stimulus, SA stimulus, 401 MAPK cascade, regulation of transcription by TF, signal transduction, flavonoid biosynthesis 402 process, flavonoid metabolic process, and SAR. 403

On the other hand, *ispG* is essential for the MEP pathway, 452 but it may be inactive without a functional *ispH* or other induced stress (Chang et al., 2013; 453 Li et al., 2017). The race specific defence of the *Rpp* lines is their prime limitation.
