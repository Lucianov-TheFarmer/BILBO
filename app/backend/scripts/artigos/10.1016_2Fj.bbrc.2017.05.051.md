# Identification and characterization of novel lncRNAs in Arabidopsis thaliana

#### abstract

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are important regulators of various biological processes, but few studies have identified lncRNAs in plants; genome-wide discovery of novel lncRNAs is thus required. We used deep strand-specific sequencing (ssRNA-seq) to obtain approximately 62 million reads from all developmental stages of Arabidopsis thaliana and identified 156 novel lncRNAs that we classified according to their localization. These novel identified lncRNAs showed low expression levels and sequence conservation. Bioinformatic analysis predicted potential target genes or cis-regulated genes of 91 antisense and 32 intergenic lncRNAs. Functional annotation of these potential targets and sequence motif analysis indicated that the lncRNAs participate in various biological processes underlying Arabidopsis growth and development. Seventeen of the lncRNAs were predicted targets of 22 miRNAs, and a network of interactions between ncRNAs and mRNAs was constructed. In addition, nine lncRNAs functioned as miRNA precursors. Finally, qRT-PCR revealed that novel lncRNAs have stage- and tissue-specific expression patterns in A. thaliana. Our study provides insight into the potential functions and regulatory interactions of novel Arabidopsis lncRNAs, and enhances our understanding of plant lncRNAs, which will facilitate functional research.

#### 1. Introduction

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) encode non-coding regulatory elements in eukaryotic genomes and lack protein-coding capacity; they can be further divided into small RNAs (sRNAs) and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) [\[1,2\].](#page-6-0) lncRNAs are more than 200 nt in length [\[1,2\]](#page-6-0). Most lncRNAs have the same structure as classical mRNAs, namely, a 50 cap and 30 polyadenylation. A few lncRNAs are non-polyadenylated at the 30 end [\[3,4\].](#page-6-0) In contrast to mRNAs, lncRNAs are expressed at low levels in a tissue-specific manner, and in general show poor conservation across different species [\[2,5\].](#page-6-0) Based on their genomic locations relative to their neighboring genes, lncRNAs can be classified into four types: sense, antisense, intronic, and intergenic [\[6\]](#page-6-0). Moreover, lncRNAs play important roles in a variety of biological processes in eukaryotes by regulating gene expression via diverse mechanisms, such as cis and trans, as well as at the transcriptional or post-transcriptional level [\[2\].](#page-6-0)

Arabidopsis thaliana has been used extensively in plant development studies because of the large amount of biological information obtained from abundant genetic and transgenic plant resources. Thousands of lncRNAs of A. thaliana have been detected [\[11,14,15\].](#page-6-0) Plant lncRNA databases (including NONCODE, PLncDB, CANTATAdb, GreeNC, and lncRNAdb) contain a large number of A. thaliana lncRNAs. In addition, we constructed a network of interactions between ncRNAs (miRNAs, lncRNA) and mRNAs. We analyzed the expression patterns of several lncRNAs and their targets in various organs and at different developmental stages.

#### 2. Materials and methods

#### 2.1. Plant growth and RNA extraction

The wild-type (WT) *A. thaliana* Columbia ecotype (Col-0) was used in this study. The seed stratification, planting, and growth conditions were described previously [16]. To cover the life cycle of *A. thaliana*, whole plants at seven growth stages [1.04 (S1), 1.14 (S2), 3.90 (S3), 5.10 (S4), 6.50 (S5), 8.00 (S6), and 9.70 (S7)] [17] (Supplementary Fig. S1) were collected and separated into roots, stems, leaves, flowers, and siliques according to a previously established system.

Total RNAs from whole plants at each growth stage were isolated and purified as described previously [18]. The seven total RNA samples were pooled and further processed with a RiboMinus kit (RiboMinus™ Plant Kit for RNA-Seq, Invitrogen, A10838-08) to deplete ribosomal RNAs (rRNA). The pooled RNA sample was assessed using a NanoDrop ND-1000 (A260/A280: 2.09) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (28S/18S: 1.7; RNA integrity number [RIN]: 8.4) before RNA-seq.

#### 2.2. Strand-specific RNA library construction and RNA-sequencing

Pooled RNA was used to construct a strand-specific RNA-Seq (ssRNA-seq) library according to the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Guide. This ssRNA-seq library was sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform at the Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI) to obtain paired-end (PE) reads. The sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under the accession number GSE98141.

#### 2.3. RNA transcript reconstruction and novel lncRNA detection

To rule out remaining rRNA reads, clean reads were mapped to the ribosomal RNA (rRNA) reference using SOAPaligner/SOAP2. Next, filtered reads were mapped to the *A. thaliana* genome (TAIR 10) using TopHat2 to assemble transcripts using Cufflinks. Expression levels of the assembled transcripts were calculated and normalized using reads per kilobases per million mapped reads (RPKM) by Cufflinks. To obtain new transcripts, those transcripts that overlapped with known mRNAs and lncRNAs (NONCODEv4) were filtered using Cuffcompare. Any new transcripts with low mapping coverage ( $\leq$ 50%), an optimum value of RPKM (<0.4), and length < 200 nucleotides were removed. The coding potential of the remaining transcripts was evaluated using CPC and HMMER 3.0 (against the Pfam database), and transcripts with CPC scores > 0 or  $E\text{-values}\,\geq\,1E^{-3}$  were discarded. For further elimination of all possible known lncRNAs, the identified lncRNAs that match known Arabidopsis lncRNA transcripts identified from public lncRNA databases (GREENC, CANTATAdb, LncRNAdb, PLncDB) were filtered out (BLASTN, coverage  $\geq$  55% and E-value < 1E^{-10}). The filtered IncRNA transcripts were considered candidate novel IncRNAs in A. thaliana. The references or websites of bioinformatic software and lncRNA databases are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

#### 2.4. Genomic characterization of novel lncRNAs

We categorized the genomic positions of novel lncRNAs into

intronic lncRNAs (entirely within the introns of known genes, 'i'), pseudogenes (alternative lncRNA isoforms of known genes, 'j'), generic exonic overlap with known transcripts ('o'), intergenic lncRNAs (lincRNA, 'u'), and antisense lncRNAs (exonic lncRNAs present on the opposite strand, 'x'), as defined by Cuffcompare. All novel lncRNA sequences identified in this study were aligned with IncRNAs of other plant species from the GREENC and CANTATAdb databases using a cut-off *E*-value  $< 1E^{-10}$  and sequence coverage >20%; these were defined as sequence-conversed lncRNAs. Repetitive element contents in the lncRNAs were analyzed using Repeat Masker. To examine their function as miRNA precursors (premiRNA), the lncRNAs were subjected to a BLAST search against the miRNAs in miRBase (release 21), and hits with a sequence coverage > 90% were selected. The secondary structures of miRNAs and lncRNAs were predicted and plotted using the Vienna RNA package RNAfold web. Potential targets of miRNAs were predicted by psRNATarget, with an expectation  $\leq 3$  and unpaired energy  $(UPE) \leq 25$ . The references or websites of bioinformatic software are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

#### 2.5. Functional analysis of IncRNAs

To assess antisense lncRNA–mRNA interactions, RNAplex was first used to screen all antisense lncRNA–mRNA duplexes that exhibited complementary base pairing. Secondly, intergenic lncRNAs located in the 'u' region were classified as upstream or downstream (2 kb) of the PC genes to identify their involvement in transcriptional regulation of those genes through overlap with *cis*-regulation elements. The lncRNA-targeted PC genes were aligned against the NCBI non-redundant protein (Nr) and NCBI non-redundant nucleotide sequence (Nt) databases using BLASTX alignment with an E-value  $\leq 10E^{-5}$ . In addition, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG Orthology (KO) annotations of the potential lncRNA-targeted genes.

Motifs in the predicted lncRNAs were identified using MEME software (widths = 4–12, allowing 0 or 1 motif per sequence, E-value < 0.05). The function of each identified motif was predicted by the Gene Ontology for Motifs (GOMO) annotation server. The references or websites of bioinformatic software are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

# 2.6. Reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) validation and quantitative real-time (qRT-PCR) analysis

For RT-PCR, cDNA was synthesized from total RNA [18]. PCR was performed on a Bio-Rad T100<sup>™</sup> Thermal Cycler PCR (Bio-Rad, USA). IncRNA primers were designed using the Primer 5.0 software (Supplementary Table S2), and the products were analyzed on 1.5% agarose gels. To evaluate genomic DNA contamination, a non-reverse-transcriptase control was included.

We conducted qRT-PCR experiments to analyze basic expression levels of a subset of candidate lncRNAs and their targets, as well as miRNAs, in *A. thaliana*. The different organs (roots, stems, leaves, flowers, siliques) in S5 as well as leaves at different developmental stages were selected for RNA isolation. qRT-PCR was performed using a method described previously [18]; the primers are shown in Supplementary Table S2. *Actin2* mRNA was used as an internal control.

### 3.3. Functional analysis

A comparative interaction analysis between the identified antisense lncRNAs and the corresponding sense mRNAs was performed. Among the 91 antisense lncRNAs, 71 showed >80% complementary base pairing with the sense mRNAs (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table S5). Further functional annotation of target mRNAs showed that many lncRNAs targeted genes encoding proteins involved in cell wall organization, plant hormone signal transduction, defense response, and reproductive transition, for example, cellulase 5, SAUR-like auxin-responsive protein, and NB-ARC domain-containing disease resistance protein. Moreover, GO and KEGG annotations for these lncRNA-targeted genes suggested important functions, including in the developmental process and responses to stimuli (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S5).

We further examined the novel lncRNAs with neighboring PC genes. In total, 32 of 156 novel intergenic lncRNAs had upstream or downstream PC neighbors within 20 kb (Supplementary Table S5).

Functional annotation of these lncRNA neighbors showed that some encoded photosystem II subunit T, REVEILLE 6 (*REV6*), thymidine kinase b (*TK1B*), cysteine-rich receptor-like protein kinase 35 (*CRK35*), and autophagy-related protein 8c, which are involved in regulating photosynthesis, the circadian rhythm, the response to cold, the defense response, and autophagy, respectively. Moreover, lncRNAs neighboring PC genes were annotated with important terms, including signaling and signal transducer activity (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S5).

We predicted and characterized the DNA motifs of novel lncRNAs to evaluate their potential biological functions. Six conserved sequence motifs were identified in the novel lncRNAs using MEME (E-value  $\leq$  0.05) (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, six motifs related to fruit development, floral meristem determinacy, flower development, circadian rhythm, and amino acid phosphorylation were significantly enriched (Fig. 3C).

#### 4. Discussion

Compared with traditional microarrays and mRNA sequencing, strand-specific sequencing facilitates the determination of antisense transcription activity and the identification of novel lncRNAs with strand information [19]. This approach has been applied to the characterization of a wide range of novel lncRNAs in multiple plant species, such as rice and potato [20,21]. Although previous investigations of genome-wide lncRNAs in A. thaliana used various approaches, few applied strand-specific sequencing [10,14,15,22]. In addition, few studies involved BLAST searches against public lncRNA databases to identify high-confidence novel lncRNAs in A. thaliana. No study has conducted genome-wide screening of novel IncRNAs across different developmental stages of A. thaliana using a strand-specific library and sufficient sequencing depth. In this study, we constructed a comprehensive strand-specific library and by deep RNA sequencing generated approximately 62 million PE reads, more than that generated from a single A. thaliana ssRNA library [10,15,22]. The large amount of ssRNA-seq data facilitated transcript assembly and identification of potential novel lncRNAs in A. thaliana. Indeed, we obtained thousands of novel transcripts, including 372 lncRNAs, from the ssRNA-seq data. Furthermore,

<span id="page-6-0"></span>strict BLAST searches against all public lncRNA databases and RT-PCR confirmation were performed to identify and validate the novel lncRNAs. Therefore, our results indicated that a sufficient sequencing depth and a strict screening process enable the detection of high-confidence novel lncRNA transcripts in A. thaliana.

lncRNA transcripts showed less conservation than PC genes in plants, such as maize and rice [3,20]. Similarly, in our study, few conserved novel lncRNAs were found in A. thaliana, suggestive of a low degree of evolutionary constraint or rapid evolution of plant lncRNAs.

Although lncRNAs are increasingly recognized as an important class of regulatory molecules involved in diverse biological processes, few works have performed functional analyses of plant lncRNAs [4,11]. The function of lncRNAs can be evaluated by senseantisense base pairing, identification of neighboring PC genes in cisregulation, and trans-regulation [2,4,19]. In the present study, many PC genes with important biological functions (e.g., organ development, photosynthesis, circadian rhythm) were found to be targeted by lncRNAs through complementary base pairing and/or a neighbor effect, suggesting a role for the identified lncRNAs in the regulation of these biological functions. Moreover, the lncRNAs and their potential targets exhibited tissue- and stage-specific expression patterns. For example, TCONS\_00011126 was highly expressed in siliques and predicted to target AT3G13540 (MYB5), which is involved in seed coat development [23]. In addition, analysis of sequence motifs revealed that some conserved motifs might be involved in growth and development.

In eukaryotes, lncRNAs can be processed into small RNAs, and are targeted and regulated by miRNAs at the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels [2,4,13], suggesting that lncRNAs play complex roles in the regulation of gene expression. Recent genomewide studies in plants have showed that lncRNAs can function as precursors for, or be targeted by, miRNAs. For example, in Populus tomentosa, three lncRNAs harbor complete precursors of PtcmiR1448, PtcmiR482a.2, Ptu-172, and Ptu-37 [24]. In A. thaliana, some lncRNAs function as targets or target mimics of miRNAs [2,13]. In our study, several novel lncRNAs that may be targeted by miRNAs and harbor miRNA precursors were identified. This suggests that novel lncRNAs might affect biological processes by interacting with miRNAs and thus may be used to identify novel miRNAs. Furthermore, construction of a comprehensive regulatory network implied that the regulatory interactions between noncoding RNAs and mRNAs are frequent events in A. thaliana. Considering the diverse and complex functions of these regulatory networks, further investigations of lncRNAs that interact with miRNAs and mRNAs would improve our understanding of the mechanism of regulation of gene expression by lncRNAs in plants.
