Short summary: In plants, LHP1 evolved a specific domain for interaction with a plant specific domain of the putative chromatin remodeler ATRX. Arabidopsis ATRX localizes to a specific subset of sites marked by H3K27me3. ATRX participates in deposition of the histone variant H3.3 and assists H3K27me3 deposition by PRC2 at a subset of loci including the flowering time regulator *FLC*.

#### ABSTRACT

Heterochromatin Protein 1 (HP1) is a major regulator of chromatin structure and function. In animals, the network of proteins interacting with HP1 is mainly associated with constitutive heterochromatin marked by H3K9me3. HP1 physically interacts with the putative orthologue of the SNF2 chromatin remodeler ATRX, which controls deposition of the histone variant H3.3 in mammals. In Arabidopsis thaliana, we show that the orthologue of ATRX participates in H3.3 deposition and characterize the function of conserved domains of plant ATRX. We show that the plant Like HP1 (LHP1) interacts with ATRX through domains that evolved specifically in land plants ancestors. Interaction between ATRX and LHP1 affects the expression of a limited subset of genes controlled by the POLYCOMB REPRESSIVE COMPLEX 2 (PRC2), including the flowering time regulator FLC. In the context of flowering time, ATRX function requires the novel LHP1-interacting domain and the ATPase of the ATRX SNF2 helicase. We conclude that distinct evolutionary pathways led to interaction between ATRX and HP1 in mammals or its counterpart LHP1 in plants, resulting in distinct modes of transcriptional regulation.

#### INTRODUCTION

In fission yeast and animals, HP1 plays a major role in maintaining silencing at heterochromatic regions (Bannister et al., 2001; Nakayama et al., 2001). Metazoan genomes encode several paralogs of HP1, which differ only by a limited number of amino acid residues and play additional and specific roles in and outside of heterochromatin (Canzio et al., 2014). HP1 comprises two conserved domains: The N-terminal chromodomain that recognizes H3K9me3 and the C-terminal chromoshadow domain that binds to conserved "PxVxL" or "LxVxL" motifs in interacting proteins (Eissenberg and Elgin, 2014; Lechner et al., 2005; Ryu et al., 2014). In fission yeast, HP1 recruits proteins involved in the maintenance of silencing by H3K9me3 in a positive feed-back loop (Grewal and Jia, 2007).

In land plants, a protein family LIKE HETEROCHROMATIN PROTEIN 1 (LHP1) has been identified based on the presence of the chromo and chromoshadow domains (Gaudin et al., 2001; Jackson et al., 2002). Between these two conserved domains LHP1 also comprises plant specific motifs, the motifs I and II (Berke and Snel, 2015; Hennig and Derkacheva, 2009). The evolutionary origin of these motifs and their function remain unclear. In contrast to animals and fungi, there is usually a single LHP1 coding gene in plant genomes (Berke and Snel, 2015). In *A. thaliana* LHP1 is present in euchromatin and binds to H3K27me3 deposited by Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) (Libault et al., 2005; Turck et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2007b). LHP1 also assists PRC2 docking to H3K27me3, creating a positive feed-back loop, which involves interaction between LHP1 and MULTICOPY

SUPPRESSOR OF IRA1 (MSI1), a subunit of PRC2 (Derkacheva et al., 2013) and interaction with RNAs (Berry et al., 2017).

Amongst proteins that interact with HP1 in animals (Eissenberg and Elgin, 2014; Lechner et al., 2005; Ryu et al., 2014), the only putative chromatin is ALPHA-THALASSEMIA MENTAL RETARDATION remodeler the SYNDROME, X-LINKED (ATRX). ATRX contains a putative SNF2-type ATPase/helicase and an ATRX-DNMT3-DNMT3L (ADD) domain that binds to H3K4me0/H3K9me3 (Eustermann et al., 2011; Flaus et al., 2006; Gibbons et al., 1995; Gibbons et al., 2008; Iwase et al., 2011) (Figure 1A). ATRX interacts with HP1 through the motif "LxVxL" and with several other proteins including the histone variant H3.3 chaperone DEATH DOMAIN ASSOCIATED PROTEIN (Daxx), which deposits H3.3 at pericentric heterochromatin and telomeres (Eustermann et al., 2011; Iwase et al., 2011; Kernohan et al., 2010; Nan et al., 2007; Ratnakumar et al., 2012; Voon and Wong, 2016).

A potential orthologue of ATRX has been identified in plants (Duc et al., 2017; Flaus et al., 2006). Although it has been recently reported that the link of ATRX to H3.3 dynamics is likely to be conserved in plants (Duc et al., 2017), interactors and functions of conserved domains of ATRX have not been identified and characterized. Here, we report that, despite absence of the HP1 interacting motif "LxVxL", plants evolved a novel conserved domain that mediates interaction between orthologues of ATRX with the plant-specific motif II of LHP1. We demonstrate that ATRX participates in the positive regulation of floral transition trough the LHP1/PRC2 dependent repression of *FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC*) and this regulation requires the novel LHP1interacting domain and the ATPase of the SNF2 helicase. Moreover,

consistent with recent finding, we show that plant ATRX participates in H3.3 deposition using a mass spectrometry based method to measure endogenous level of histone H3.3 and H3.1. We propose that ATRX assists LHP1 function through its impact on histone H3.3 variant deposition at a specific subset of targets of PRC2.

#### RESULTS

#### ATRX localizes to euchromatic region and interacts directly with LHP1

In A. thaliana, At1g08600 is the only gene that encodes a protein comprising the combination of an ADD and a SNF2 helicase domain that defines ATRX orthologues in animals (Figure 1A) (Duc et al., 2017; Flaus et al., 2006). In mammals, the sub-cellular localization of ATRX at pericentric heterochromatin and telomeres depends both on the ADD domain that binds to H3K4me0/H3K9me3 and the HP1-interacting motif "LxVxL" (Eustermann et al., 2011; Iwase et al., 2011; Kernohan et al., 2010; Nan et al., 2007; Ratnakumar et al., 2012; Voon and Wong, 2016). We first investigated the sub-cellular localization pattern of plant ATRX. In A. thaliana, ATRX is expressed constitutively at moderate levels during development (Supplemental Figure 1A, B and C). To monitor expression of the ATRX protein we used transgenic lines expressing the fluorescent fusion protein ATRX::mCherry under the control of ATRX regulatory region. In root tips, ATRX is expressed in all cell types and accumulates in the nucleus (Figure 1B). Interestingly, A. thaliana ATRX is uniformly distributed in the nucleus but

does not accumulate at condensed DNA as reported in mammalian cells (Figure 1B). Moreover, during mitosis, unlike mammalian ATRX that associates with chromatin during cell division (McDowell et al., 1999), *A. thaliana* ATRX does not remain associated with mitotic chromosomes and is released in the cytoplasm (Figure 1B), suggesting distinct modes of chromatin association of ATRX in plants and mammals.

These observations prompted us to test the functional conservation of the predicted ADD domain. In plants and mammals, the ADD domains of ATRX orthologues share six highly conserved "CxxC" motifs that are essential for its 3D structure (Figure 2A) (Eustermann et al., 2011; Iwase et al., 2011). Circular dichroism analyses showed that ADD domains from *A. thaliana* (*At*ADD) and *H. sapiens* (*Hs*ADD) share a similar secondary structure (Figure 2B). However, the alignment of ADD domains of potential ATRX orthologues from plants revealed a lack of conservation of the amino acid residues responsible for interaction between *Hs*ADD and H3K4me0/H3K9me3 (Figure 2A), suggesting that the ADD domain might have lost or changed its binding specificity in plants. Accordingly, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) did not detect preferential binding of *At*ADD to peptides carrying H3K4me0/H3K9me3 compared to *Hs*ADD (Figure 2C). These results indicate that the biochemical properties of the ADD domain of ATRX diverged between plants and mammals.

To further investigate the sub-cellular localization of ATRX in a higher resolution, we performed immunolocalization of fluorescent protein tagged ATRX or LHP1 on isolated nuclei. In plants, LHP1 binds to H3K27me3 at euchromatin and is excluded from constitutive heterochromatin marked by

H3K9me2 (Turck et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2007b). We thus defined ATRX sub-cellular localization using LHP1::GFP as a reference. We observed that ATRX::GFP was excluded from heterochromatin marked by H3K9me2 but instead localized to chromatin marked by H3K27me3, with a pattern alike LHP1::GFP (Figure 3A and B). Co-immunostaining of ATRX::mCherry and LHP1::GFP showed a partial overlap (Figure 3A and B) suggesting that these two proteins might interact. In support of this hypothesis, ATRX::mCherry co-immunoprecipitated with LHP1::GFP *in planta* (Figure 3C). Yeast Two-Hybrid assays confirmed this interaction (Figure 3D). Altogether these results led us to conclude that ATRX physically interacts with LHP1 in *A. thaliana*.

## Plants evolved specific regions enabling interaction between ATRX and LHP1

In mammals, the motif "LxVxL" located between the ADD domain and the SNF2 helicase domain of ATRX interacts with HP1 (Berube et al., 2000; Eustermann et al., 2011; Kourmouli et al., 2005; Le Douarin et al., 1996; Lechner et al., 2005; McDowell et al., 1999; Picketts et al., 1998). In plants, the motif "LxVxL" is not conserved (Figure 1A) suggesting that plant ATRX orthologues contain a distinct LHP1-interacting domain. To identify the LHP1 interacting domain in ATRX, we performed *in vitro* pull-down assays using the purified recombinant GST tagged ATRX N terminal portion (1-660 amino acid, harboring the ADD domain) or the C terminal portion (661-1479 amino acid, harboring the SNF2 Helicase domain) incubated with total proteins extracted from a transgenic plant line expressing LHP1::GFP. Our results revealed that LHP1::GFP interacts with the N-terminal portion of ATRX containing the ADD

domain (Figure 4A). Using Yeast Two-Hybrid assays, we excluded the possibility that AtADD interacts with LHP1 (Figure 4B). Instead, we identified a distinct region located at the N-terminal portion of ATRX that is necessary and sufficient for the interaction with LHP1 (Figure 4B). Molecular phylogenetic analyses revealed that this region contains a domain conserved amongst putative ATRX orthologues in plants (Figure 4C and Supplemental Figure 2A). Using circular dichroism, we showed that this domain is highly-structured with a high content of helices (Figure 4D). We named this domain "LHP1interacting ATRX N-terminal" (LAN). Deletion of the LAN domain led to a significant reduction of the colocalization between H3K27me3 and ATRX::mCherry, whereas no significant change was observed with deletion of ADD domain (Figure 5A). Accordingly, we could not detect any binding of AtADD to H3K27me3 peptides using ITC (see Supplemental Materials and Methods for details). We concluded that the localization of ATRX to euchromatin marked by H3K27me3 was not mediated by direct binding of AtADD domain to modified histone H3 unlike previous reports for HsADD (Eustermann et al., 2011; Iwase et al., 2011). These results suggest that the plant-specific LAN domain interacts with LHP1 and is important for spatial distribution of ATRX.

In plants, LHP1 contains unique motifs I and II (Figure 5B) that were suggested to mediate protein-protein interaction (Berke and Snel, 2015). We tested requirement of both motifs in the interaction with ATRX. In GST pull-down assays the deletion of the motif II but not motif I impaired the pull down of Flag-LAN (Figure 5C), indicating that the motif II of LHP1 is required for the interaction with the LAN domain of ATRX. We extended previous phylogenetic

analyses (Berke and Snel, 2015) and confirmed that the motif I is present only in eudicots whereas the motif II is conserved in all land plants (Supplemental Figure 2B) as shown for the domain LAN (Figure 4C and Supplemental Figure 2A). Altogether our results suggest that the plant-specific domain LAN and motif II evolved in land plants and mediates the interaction between ATRX and LHP1.

# The impact of ATRX on floral transition requires the LAN domain and the ATPase of SNF2 helicase

To assess the functionality of interaction between ATRX and LHP1 we searched for a phenotype in *atrx* mutants (Supplemental Figure 3A) (Duc et al., 2017). Both null mutant alleles (Supplemental Figure 3B) did not show major developmental alterations but the relationship between ATRX and LHP1, led us to investigate the flowering time of *atrx* mutants. We observed that both mutant alleles flowered later than WT (Figure 6A and Supplemental Figure 3C). In A. thaliana, floral transition is regulated by the MADS-box protein FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC), which forms protein complexes with other MADS-box proteins, FLOWERING LOCUS M (FLM) and SHORT VEGETATIVE PHASE (SVP) to repress the expression of the floral activator FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) (Gu et al., 2013; Helliwell et al., 2006; Li et al., 2008; Michaels and Amasino, 1999; Searle et al., 2006; Sheldon et al., 1999). In *atrx* mutants, the level of *FLC* transcripts was higher than WT (Figure 6B) but expression of FLM and SVP remained unaffected (Supplemental Figure 3D). Consistent with the increased expression of FLC, expression of FT was reduced in *atrx* mutants (Supplemental Figure 3E). As both null mutant alleles

exhibited the same phenotypic defects, we focused on one mutant allele for further analyses. Genetic complementation rescued the late flowering phenotype in *atrx-1* (Figure 6A and Supplemental Figure 3F) and restored *FLC* and *FT* expression to WT levels (Figure 6B and Supplemental Figure 3E), leading to conclude that ATRX is responsible for the late flowering phenotype in *atrx* mutants.

Using the ATRX-dependent late flowering as a phenotypic readout, we performed complementation assays of *atrx-1* mutant to investigate the functional importance of each conserved domain of ATRX including LAN, ADD and SNF2 helicase domains. ATRX Full length and ATRX $\Delta$ ADD with either FlagHA or mCherry tags fully rescued the late flowering phenotype of the *atrx-1* mutant (Figure 6C, Supplemental Figure 4B and C). By contrast, the deletion of the LAN domain prevented genetic complementation (Figure 6C, Supplemental Figure 4B and C) demonstrating that the LAN domain is essential while the ADD domain does not play a major role in ATRX function with respect to the flowering phenotype of *atrx* mutant.

The function of ATRX might be associated with chromatin remodeling, which could be provided by the potential helicase activity of the highly conserved ATRX SNF2 domain (Figure 4C and Supplemental Figure 4A). Although the helicase domain identifies ATRX as a member of SNF2 family chromatin remodeling factors (Flaus et al., 2006), its enzymatic properties and functions have not been established in animals and plants. To test the impact of the SNF2 domain on ATRX function, we expressed in *atrx-1* mutant *mCherry-ATRX* or *FlagHA-ATRX* carrying point mutations in either the conserved ATP-binding motif (K760N) or in the conserved ATP-hydrolysis motif (E876Q) of

the SNF2 domain (Flaus et al., 2006) (Figure 6C, Supplemental Figure 4A, D and E). The late flowering phenotype caused by *atrx-1* was not rescued by any of these mutants suggesting that the ATPase activity of the SNF2 domain is required for ATRX function.

# Loss of ATRX deregulates expression of a small set of genes marked by H3K27me3

Although ATRX binds to LHP1, defects observed in *atrx* knock-out mutants are very mild in contrast with the pleiotropic defects reported in *lhp1* mutants. LHP1 also targets FT (Kotake et al., 2003), the activator of floral transition, resulting an early flowering phenotype in *lhp1* mutants opposite to *atrx* mutants. This suggested that ATRX likely targets less sites than LHP1. To identify ATRX target genes, we attempted ChIP sequencing from plant lines expressing functional fluorescent proteins or FlagHA tagged ATRX. However, we did not obtain DNA enrichment compared to the rabbit IgG control even with an additional protein crosslinking reagent described previously for ATRX ChIP in animal cell lines (Law et al., 2010), suggesting a weak association between ATRX and chromatin in plants. As an alternative approach, we generated transcriptomes from ten day old seedlings of WT and *atrx-1* grown in two distinct environmental conditions (see Materials and Methods). To remove effects related to environment we selected differentially expressed genes (DEGs) only statistically significant in both growth conditions for further analyses. In such stringent statistic selection, the number of DEGs between WT and *atrx-1* was relatively small and their degree of deregulation was more modest (Figure 7A) than reported previously (Duc et al., 2017). In addition to

*FLC*, a significant number of DEGs in *atrx-1* overlapped with genes marked by H3K27me3 and targeted by LHP1 (Lafos et al., 2011; Veluchamy et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2007a) (Figure 7B and C). We confirmed directly that both enrichment of H3K27me3 (but not H3K4me3) and the PRC2 subunit CLF on *FLC* in *atrx-1* were decreased compared to WT (Supplemental Figure 5A-D) and the loss of H3K27me3 did not result from a change of expression of genes encoding PRC2 core components in atrx-1 mutant (Supplemental Figure 5E) These results are also supported by the positive genetic interaction between atrx-1, clf, and swn on flowering time and FLC expression (Supplemental Figure 5F and G). Thus, our results suggest that ATRX participates in the transcriptional control of a restricted number of genes targeted by PRC2 and LHP1 in somatic vegetative tissues. This modest impact likely relates to the absence of a strong vegetative phenotype in atrx mutants and the lack of detection of ATRX in the mass spectrometric analyses of protein pulled down by LHP1 (Dekarcheva., 2016). Yet it is possible that ATRX plays a more important role at reproductive stages of development as suggested by the phenotypes observed in the double mutants deprived of ATRX and the H3.3 chaperone HIRA (Duc et al., 2017).

#### ATRX is involved in deposition of the histone variant H3.3

In mammals, H3.3 is involved in chromatin remodeling and its deposition is mediated by ATRX at specific domains marked by HP1 (Eustermann et al., 2011; Iwase et al., 2011; Kernohan et al., 2010; Nan et al., 2007; Ratnakumar et al., 2012; Voon and Wong, 2016). To explore a potential link between ATRX function and H3.3 we compared the impact of their loss on transcription

using transcriptomes of *atrx-1* and *h3.3kd-3* deprived of H3.3 (Wollmann et al., 2017). We observed a significant overlap of genes upregulated and downregulated in both mutant backgrounds (Figure 8A and B). We used the reporter fusion protein HTR5::GFP (Wollmann et al., 2012) to test whether ATRX is involved in deposition of H3.3. In comparison to WT the atrx-1 mutant showed reduced enrichment of H3.3 but not of total H3 at the FLC locus (Figure 8C and D). In addition, we detected a global decrease of HTR5::GFP level in *atrx-1* mutant nuclei compared to WT (Supplemental Figure 6A and B) as reported recently (Duc et al., 2017). To further confirm this result based on transgenic reporter lines expressing HTR5::GFP in addition to five genes that encode endogenous H3.3 and to establish the impact of the loss of ATRX on the endogenous levels of H3.3, we developed a mass spectrometry based method allowing us to measure the composition of endogenous H3 variants. Peptides that differentiate efficiently H3.1 from H3.3 were used to determine the ratio of H3.3/H3.1. To validate our method, we measured that ratio of H3.3/H3.1 decreased in h3.3kd-3 (Wollmann et al., 2017) but increased in h3.1kd-1-1 (Jiang and Berger, 2017) compared with WT (Figure 8E). In addition, the loss of the H3.3 chaperone HIRA (hira-1) caused a decrease of the H3.3/H3.1 ration whereas the opposite effect was observed in loss of the function of the H3.1 chaperone CAF1 in the allele fas1-4 (Kirik et al., 2006) (Figure 8E). The ratio of endogenous H3.3/H3.1 decreased significantly in *atrx-1* indicating that ATRX participates in the deposition of H3.3 variant in A. thaliana (Figure 8E).

#### DISCUSSION

In mammals, the H3.3 chaperone Daxx associated with ATRX is responsible for incorporation of H3.3 at heterochromatin (Eustermann et al., 2011; Iwase et al., 2011; Kernohan et al., 2010; Nan et al., 2007; Ratnakumar et al., 2012; Voon and Wong, 2016). In plants, Daxx is not conserved, questioning the role of ATRX in H3 variant incorporation in plants. Based on the distinct phenotypes of *hira* and *atrx* knock-out mutants and the additive phenotypes observed in the double mutant atrx hira reported in previous studies (Duc et al., 2017), it is unlikely that ATRX directs the H3.3 chaperone HIRA. We report that ATRX participates in H3.3 incorporation. The mechanism likely involves ATPase dependent chromatin remodeling and we propose than ATRX does not act as a chaperone as proposed previously (Duc et al., 2017). Our study shows a clear distinction in the properties of HP1 and ATRX counterparts in mammals and plants. In contrast with mammals, plant LHP1 and ATRX target euchromatic regions marked by H3K27me3 instead of heterochromatic regions marked by H3K9 methylation (Libault et al., 2005; Turck et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2007b). Given the absence of ATRX at constitutive heterochromatin it is likely that the impact of the loss of ATRX on transposons and silenced ribosomal RNA loci reported (Duc et al., 2017), are indirect. Two major factors explain the distinct locations of ATRX in mammals and plants: 1), the ADD domain of *A. thaliana* does not recognize methylated H3K9 marks at heterochromatin; 2), A. thaliana ATRX interacts with LHP1 that binds to H3K27me3 instead of H3K9me3 (Exner et al., 2009; Libault et al., 2005; Turck et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2007b).

The distinct properties of ATRX amongst eukaryotes are explained in part by variations in the ADD domain. In contrast to mammals, the plant ADD domain does not recognize histone H3 modifications H3K9me3 and H3K27me3, although the secondary structure of the domain is maintained (Figure 2A-C). Furthermore, the deletion of ADD domain does not affect ATRX localization or genetic complementation of *atrx* mutant, suggesting that the ADD domain does not play a major role in ATRX function with respect to the flowering phenotype. It is interesting to note that the ADD domain of ATRX is well conserved amongst bryophytes and ferns but underwent major changes in seed plants (Figure 2A) suggesting that ancient function in early land plants was not maintained or diverged in seed plants.

The interaction between ATRX and HP1 or LHP1 evolved in parallel in animals and plants and is mediated by domains uniquely present in either group. In plant orthologues of ATRX we identified the novel conserved LAN domain, which is responsible for interaction with LHP1 and is required for ATRX function. As an exception to the unique association between the LAN domain and ATRX in streptophytes, we identified this domain in *Emiliania huxleyi*, a haptophyte alga. However, there is no evidence for the presence of LAN domain in *Emiliania huxleyi* is unlikely due to evolutionary conservation. More likely it originates from horizontal gene transfer from green algae to *Emiliania huxleyi* as shown in the case of chromomethylases (lyer et al., 2011). Thus, the LAN domain likely evolved in streptophyte algae and has been retained in land plants.

The interaction between ATRX and LHP1 ascribes a function to the motif II of LHP1 that is exclusively present in land plants. Land plants shared their last common ancestors with charophytes, a group of fresh water algae (Becker and Marin, 2009; Delwiche and Cooper, 2015; Ju et al., 2015; Van de Poel et al., 2016). ATRX is conserved in charophytes (Klebsormidium flaccidum) (Figure 4C) but there is no clear evidence of LHP1 coding genes in genomes and transcriptomes from charophytes (Hori et al., 2014; Van de Poel et al., 2016), suggesting that LHP1 and its interaction with ATRX likely evolved in the last common ancestor of land plants. This evolution parallels that of the polycomb group complexes PRC1 (Berke and Snel, 2015) and PRC2 (Pereman et al., 2016), supporting the tight functional link between LHP1, ATRX and PRC2 observed in plants. Remarkably, ATRX proteins evolved independently in vertebrates and plants by combination of an ADD and a SFN2 domain. In both taxa, ATRX interacts with HP1 or LHP1 but distinct domains are involved. Similar examples of functional convergence are found in the evolution of PRC1 function between animal and plants (Berke and Snel, 2015). Our study thus illustrates the versatility of evolution in the relationship between chromatin readers and remodelers in Eukaryotes.

#### MATERIALS AND METHODS

#### Plant materials and growth conditions

*A. thaliana* mutants of *atrx-3* (Salk\_024609), *atrx-1* (Salk\_025678), *clf* (*clf-29*), *swn* (*swn-3*) were isolated from the SALK collection (Alonso et al., 2003)

using primers indicated in the primer list at the end of this section. Ihp1 LHP1-GFP. clf GFP-CLF lines were described previously (Derkacheva et al., 2013; Exner et al., 2009; Schubert et al., 2006). The mutant alleles h3.3kd-3, h3.1kd-1, hira-1 and fas1-4 were described previously (Jiang and Berger, 2017; Kirik et al., 2006; Nie et al., 2014; Wollmann et al., 2017). Seeds were stratified at 4°C in the dark for three days. Plant's were grown in the green house under long day (LD) condition (21°C; 16h light/8h dark, with light intensity of 130 µMol s<sup>-1</sup> m<sup>-2</sup> µA in general or 30 µMol s<sup>-1</sup> m<sup>-2</sup> µA for RNAseq from experiment) on soil (PROFI SUBSTRAT, EINHEITS ERDE supplemented with perlite from GRAMOFLO and Agra-vermiculite from RHP) or on MS plates (Murashige and Skoog Medium including Gamborg B5 Vitamins 2.21 g/L, MES 0.5 g/L, Sucrose 10 g/L, Plant Agar 10 g/L). Flowering time was measured by counting the number of primary rosette and cauline leaves produced at the time of bolting (Jiang et al., 2008). Under these growth conditions we did not observe the sterility that was reported to be associated with atrx mutants in another study (Duc et al., 2017).

#### **RNA** analyses

10-day old seedlings including roots were ground in liquid nitrogen and total RNA was extracted using RNeasy plus mini kit (Qiagen). Reverse transcription was performed with RevertAid first strand synthesis kit (Thermo Scientific). Real-time quantitative PCR was performed to measure levels of transcripts on an ABI Prism 7900HT sequence detection system or Roche lightcycler using FastStart Essential DNA Green Master mix (Roche). Each

sample was quantified in triplicate and normalized using *TUB2* (*At5g62690*) as the endogenous control. Primers for *SVP*, *FT*, *TUB2*, *CLF*, *SWN*, *EMF2*, *VRN2*, *FIE*, *MSI1* were described previously (Aichinger et al., 2009; Li et al., 2008). Primers for *ATRX*, *FLC*, and *FLM* are listed below. PCR detection was performed using primers listed below.

Data of *ATRX* expression levels in different tissues from different developmental stages were extracted from the ATH1 microarray results (Schmid et al., 2005), and were visualized using genevestigator (Hruz et al., 2008). Levels of ATRX expression was confirmed by RT-qPCR in selected tissue types from different developmental stages. Two different housekeeping genes (*GAPC* and *COX6A*) were used as endogenous control.

RNA sequencing was performed using the same RNA extraction method as mentioned above. RNA libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England BioLabs). The samples were multiplexed and sequenced as 50 bp single reads in one flow cell on Illumina HiSeq2000. The gene counts were estimated using Kallisto v 0.42 (Pimentel et al., 2016) with the parameters -I 180 -s 20 -b 100 (fragment length=180 bp, fragment standard deviation =20 and bootstrap =100). The index used was created from the FASTA file with all DNA sequences from TAIR10. The statistical analysis was done in R with the package DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014). The package tximport was used to read in the Kallisto results (Soneson et al., 2015). Within the DESeq2 analysis the normalization and dispersion estimations were done using the default settings. To adjust for the effect of the different growth conditions, a growth location covariate was included in the model, i.e. the counts per gene were modeled as a function of

the genotype (*atrx-1* or WT) and the light intensity (normal light or low light). Genes with significant up or down regulation in *atrx-1* compared to WT were identified using a FDR cutoff of 0.1. The sequencing data generated in this study are available from NCBI's Gene Expression Omnibus and accessible through GEO Series accession number GSE101777 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE101777).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in *atrx-1* mutant compared to WT were identified using a FDR cutoff of 0.1 or 0.05. The gene *ATRX* (AT1G08600) itself was excluded from the DEGs. We selected 273 DEGs with FDR <0.1 or 158 DEGs with FDR<0.05 to compare with published data sets of H3K27me3 or LHP1 target genes (Table 1) (Lafos et al., 2011; Veluchamy et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2007a). Enrichment fold was calculated using percentage of DEGs in *atrx-1* overlap with H3K27me3 or LHP1 target genes (108 or 118, respectively for DEGs with FDR<0.1; 69 or 73, respectively for DEGs with FDR<0.05) divided by percentage of H3K27me3 or LHP1 target genes (8293 or 8882, respectively) from *A. thaliana* total expressed genes (26170) (see also Table S1 for gene lists). P value of enrichment fold was calculated using HYPGEOMDIST.

#### **Plasmid construction**

To construct the *ATRX-GFP*, *ATRX-mCherry*, *mCherry-ATRX* and *FlagHA-ATRX*, first, a 1602 bp 5' promoter plus 738 bp 5'-UTR of *ATRX* gene and *ATRX* CDS were PCR-amplified and cloned into entry vector pDONR P4-P1R, pDONR 221 or pDONR P2rP3 by BP reaction (Invitrogen). The

fragments were subsequently cloned into a pAlligatorG43-derived plasmid (Kawashima et al., 2014) carrying *GFP*, *mCherry* or *FlagHA* sequence via MultiSite Gateway technology (Invitrogen) to obtain the *pATRX:ATRX-GFP*, *pATRX:ATRX-mCherry*, *pATRX:mCherry-ATRX* and *pATRX:FlagHA-ATRX* fusion constructs. The LAN domain was initially identified by its sequence conservation from the phylogenetic analysis. Then, the N- and C-termini of the LAN domain were defined based on secondary structure predicted using CLC (Qiagen). Plasmids for recombinant protein expression of *Hs*ADD, *At*ADD, GST-ATRX N, GST-ATRX C, Flag-LAN, GST-LHP1 were cloned into entry vector pDONR 221 first and then into pETG-30A (Addgene) plasmid via MultiSite Gateway technology (Invitrogen). Precision cleavage sequences were inserted for removal of GST tag. The plasmids to encode *mCherry* or *FlagHA tagged ATRXAADD*, *ATRXALAN*, *ATRX K760N*, *ATRX E876Q* or *GST* tagged *LHP1*Δ*MI* and *LHP1*Δ*MII* were made using site directed mutagenesis protocol as described previously (Zheng et al., 2004).

#### Yeast two-hybrid assay

Coding sequences of tested genes were cloned into *pGADT7* and *pGBKT7* vectors (Clontech) to fuse with GAL4 AD and BD, respectively, and then transformed into yeast reporter strain AH109. Transformed yeast cells were grown on synthetic medium SD lacking leucine and tryptophan or 10 mM 3-Amino-1,2,4-Triazol (3AT) containing SD medium lacking leucine, tryptophan, histidine, and adenine.

The harvested cells were suspended in 50mM NaH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>/Na<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub>, 300mM NaCl, 0.1mM ZnCl<sub>2</sub>, pH 7.5. After cell lysis and centrifugation, the GST-fusion protein was purified on a GST affinity column, followed by on-column tag cleavage using PreScission Protease (GE Healthcare). *Hs*ADD or *At*ADD protein was then directly separated from the digestion mixture by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 75 column (GE Healthcare) in 50mM NaH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>/Na<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub>, 300mM NaCl, 0.1mM ZnCl<sub>2</sub>, pH 7.5. The purified *Hs*ADD or *At*ADD was then concentrated to ~10 mg/ml and was used freshly for CD and ITC without freezing. #### Pull-down assay

Plants expressing LHP1::GFP (a transgenic line expressing functional GFPtagged LHP1) (Kotake et al., 2003) were ground into fine powder, mixed with pull-down buffer (50 mM Tris-HCI pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 0.1 mM ZnSO<sub>4</sub>, 0.05% NP40, 1 mM DTT, 1X EDTA free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche)) and briefly sonicated using the Bioruptor sonication device (Diagenode). After spin down, the supernatant was mixed with truncated recombinant GST-fused ATRX proteins or GST at 4°C f or 6 h. Glutathioneagarose beads (GE Healthcare) were added and further incubated for 2h at 4°C. Beads were washed with pull-down buffer five t imes and boiled with 1X SDS loading buffer. Western blot detection was performed with anti-GFP antibody (Life-Technologies A11122).

*In vitro* GST pull-down assay was preformed using recombinant Flag-LAN protein mixed with equal amounts (2 µg) of recombinant GST (control), GST-LHP1, GST-LHP1 with deletion of motif I or motif II and incubated over night at 4°C in phosphate buffer (50 mM Na <sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub>/NaH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>, 300 mM NaCl, 0.01% Triton X-100, pH7.5). Glutathione-agarose beads (GE Healthcare) were added and further incubated for 2h at 4°C. Beads we re then washed with phosphate buffer 5 times and boiled with 1X SDS loading buffer. Western blot detection was performed using anti-Flag antibody (F3165 Sigma) for Flag-LAN and anti-GST antibody (Sc-138, Santa Cruz) for GST fusion proteins. Intensity of each Flag-LAN band was measured using FIJI software and then normalized to GST-LHP1 pull down.

#### **Co-Immunoprecipitation**

Ten day old seedlings were ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen, and total proteins were extracted with Co-IP buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 0.1 mM ZnSO<sub>4</sub>, 1% Triton X-100, 1X EDTA free proteinase inhibitor cocktail (Roche)). Protein extracts were incubated with anti-mCherry antibody (Abcam ab167453) for 6 h and subsequently incubated with protein A magnetic beads (Life-Technologies) for 2 h. After five times wash with Co-IP buffer, immunoprecipitated proteins were boiled in 1X SDS loading buffer and detected by western blot using 1/1000 diluted anti-mCherry antibody (Abcam ab167453) or anti-GFP antibody (Life-Technologies A11122).

#### **Circular Dichroism (CD)**

For *Hs*ADD and *At*ADD, CD spectra were obtained at a concentration of 0.1 mg/ml in 10 mM NaH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub> pH 7.5, using a J-815 CD spectropolarimeter. The obtained spectra are a result of ten accumulations measured over a range of 180-260 nm with normal sensitivity and a bandwidth of 1 nm, at 50 nm per minute, a data integration time (D.I.T.) of 0.5 seconds and a data pitch of 0.5 nm. For Flag-LAN, three different concentrations of the sample containing 0.1770 mg ml<sup>-1</sup>, 0.0832 mg ml<sup>-1</sup> and 0.0338 mg ml<sup>-1</sup> were prepared in 50 mM Na<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub>/NaH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>, 300 mM NaF, pH 7.5. The concentration of each sample was calculated as described previously (Anthis and Clore, 2013) using the molecular mass of Flag-LAN as 36054,33 Da and the extinction coefficient at 205 nm as 1143250 M<sup>-1</sup> cm<sup>-1</sup> with the cuvette path length of 0.05 cm. Three

repeats of the spectrum were collected for each sample to account for measurement noise. A Chirascan Plus CD Spectrophotometer by Applied Photophysics was used to generate CD spectra. Percentages of secondary structures (helix, sheet and turn) were estimated based on DichroWeb (http://dichroweb.cryst.bbk.ac.uk) using Contin-LL with reference set 3 (Kelly et al., 2005; Whitmore and Wallace, 2004; Whitmore and Wallace, 2008).

#### Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

The ITC experiments were conducted using an automated Microcal AutoITC200 instrument (GE Healthcare) in 50 mM Na<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub>/NaH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>, 300mM NaCl and 0.1 mM ZnCl<sub>2</sub> pH 7.5. Heats of dilution were subtracted from the titration binding data curve fitting and curve fitting was performed with Origin 6.0 (Microcal Inc., Northampton, MA) using a one-site model. Initial experiments were carried out with 1.0 mM of peptide that was titrated to 50  $\mu$ M protein. When no binding was observed, experiments were repeated with 150  $\mu$ M protein and 2.5 mM Peptide.

#### Immunostaining and confocal imaging

Immunofluorescence experiments on isolated nuclei were performed as previously described (Jacob et al., 2009), with the following modifications. Ten day old seedlings of transgenic plants or mutants were collected and fixed immediately in 4% paraformaldehyde (16% methanol-free) in TRIS buffer (10 mM TRIS pH 7.5, 10 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl) for 20 minutes at 4°C and

washed twice (10 minutes for each wash) in TRIS buffer. Nuclei were isolated by chopping using a razor blade in 400 µL LB01 lysis buffer (15 mM TRIS pH 7.5, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM spermine, 80 mM KCl, 20 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100) in a small Petri dish on ice to a fine suspension and filtered through a 40micron cell strainer. Nuclei were washed two times and concentrated by centrifugation at 500 g for 3 minutes in LB01 lysis buffer. Concentrated nuclei (2-5 µL) were then post-fixed on cover slips in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 minutes at room temperature, followed by three washes for 5 min each in PBS and incubated in blocking buffer (1% BSA in PBS) for 30 min at 37°C. Primary antibodies, anti-K3K27me3 (Millipore 07-449) and anti-GFP (Life-Technologies A11122) from rabbit, and mouse anti-H3K9me2 (Abcam ab1220), anti-GFP (Roche 11814460001) and anti-mCherry (Abcam ab167453) antibodies were diluted 1:200 in 1% BSA in PBS and incubated with nuclei at 4°C overnight. After three washes 10 min each in PBS at room temperature, nuclei were incubated with the respective secondary antibody, goat anti-mouse or anti-rabbit Alexa 488 or 555 (Life technologies), at 1:400 dilution for 30 min at 37°C. Following three PBS washes, nuclei were counterstained and mounted in Vectashield mounting media containing DAPI (Vector Laboratories H-1000).

Slides were imaged using confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM780) with 63X oil lens (1.4 N/A). Images were taken with 16-bit depth on Z-stack for each nucleus.

#### NanoLC-MS Analysis.

Nuclei were isolated from one gram of ten days old seedlings, grown on MS plates under conditions described above, according to procedure described in (Lorkovic et al., 2017) and histones were acid extracted (Shechter et al., 2007). In short, pelleted nuclei were suspended in 800  $\mu$ I H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub> and rotated at 4°C for one hour. After spinning for 10 min at max speed supernatant was mixed with 270  $\mu$ I of 100% TCA and kept on ice for 30 min. Proteins were recovered by centrifugation at 4°C for 10 min at max speed. Pellets was washed twice with ice cold acetone (with centrifugations after each wash as

above), air dried and suspended in 60  $\mu$ l of 1× loading buffer in 0.3× PBS. Proteins (10  $\mu$ l per sample) were resolved on 4-20% gradient gels (Serva) and stained with Coomassie blue. Histone H3 bands were excised, reduced, alkylated and in-gel trypsin digested and processed for MS.

The nano HPLC system used was a Dionex UltiMate 3000 HPLC RSLC nano system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Amsterdam, Netherlands) coupled to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany), equipped with a Proxeon nanospray source (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Odense, Denmark). Peptides were loaded onto a trap column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Amsterdam, Netherlands, PepMap C18, 5 mm x 300 µm ID, 5 µm particles, 100 Å pore size) at a flow rate of 25 µl/min using 0.1% TFA as mobile phase. After 10 min, the trap column was switched in line with the analytical column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Amsterdam, Netherlands, PepMap C18, 500 mm × 75 µm ID, 2 µm, 100 Å). Peptides were eluted using a flow rate of 230 nl/min, and a binary 2-hour gradient. The gradient starts with the mobile phases: 98% solution A (water/formic acid, 99.9/0.1, v/v) and 2% solution B (water/acetonitrile/formic acid, 19.92/80/0.08, v/v/v), increases to 35% of solution B over the next 120 min, followed by a gradient in 5 to 90% of solution B, stays there for 5 min and decreases in 5 min back to the gradient 98% of solution A and 2% of solution B for equilibration at 30°C.

The Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent mode, using a full scan (m/z range 380-1500, nominal resolution of 60,000, target value 1E6) followed by MS/MS scans of the 10 most abundant ions. MS/MS spectra were acquired using normalized collision energy of 27%,

isolation width of 1.4 m/z, resolution of 30.000 and the target value was set to 1E5. Precursor ions selected for fragmentation (exclude charge state 1, 7, 8, >8) were put on a dynamic exclusion list for 40 s. Additionally, the minimum AGC target was set to 5E3 and intensity threshold was calculated to be 4.8E4. The peptide match feature was set to preferred and the exclude isotopes feature was enabled. For peptide identification, the RAW-files were loaded into Proteome Discoverer (version 2.1.0.81, Thermo Scientific). All hereby created MS/MS spectra were searched using MSAmanda v2.1.5.8733 (Dorfer et al., 2014) against Arabidopsis thaliana histone H3 sequences (7 sequences; 951 residues). The following search parameters were used: Betamethylthiolation on cysteine was set as a fixed modification, oxidation on methionine, deamidation on asparagine and glutamine, acetylation on lysine, phosphorylation on serine, threonine and tyrosine, methylation and dimethylation on lysine and threonine, tri-methylation on lysine and ubiguitinylation on lysine were set as variable modifications. Monoisotopic masses were searched within unrestricted protein masses for tryptic enzymatic specificity. The peptide mass tolerance was set to ±5 ppm and the fragment mass tolerance to ±15 ppm. The maximal number of missed cleavages was set to 2. The result was filtered to 1% FDR on peptide level using Percolator algorithm integrated in Thermo Proteome Discoverer. The localization of the phosphorylation sites within the peptides was performed with the tool ptmRS, which is based on the tool phosphoRS (Taus et al., 2011). In-house-developed tool Peakjuggler was used for the peptide and protein quantification (Karl Mechtler group, IMP/IMBA, Doblmann et al., in preparation).

Peptides diagnostic for H3.1 and H3.3 covering position 27-40 (KSAPATGGVK and KSAPATGGVKKPHR for H3.1 and KSAPTTGGVK and KSAPTTGGVKKPHR for H3.3) were used for the analysis. First, we calculated total levels of H3.1 and H3.3 by measuring Peakjuggler areas of all peptides mapping to each variant. The ratio of H3.3/H3.1 was calculated and normalized to WT. Measurements for WT, atrx-1, and h3.3kd-3 were done in triplicates and for *hira-1* and *fas1-4* in duplicates.
