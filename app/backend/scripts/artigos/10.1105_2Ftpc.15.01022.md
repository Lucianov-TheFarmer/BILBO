# 43 **ABSTRACT**

44 Proteotoxic stress, which is generated by the accumulation of unfolded or aberrant proteins due 45 to environmental or cellular conditions, can be mitigated by several mechanisms, including 46 activation of the unfolded protein response and coordinated increases in protein chaperones 47 and activities that direct proteolysis, such as the 26S proteasome. Using RNA-seq analyses 48 combined with chemical inhibitors or mutants that induce proteotoxic stress by impairing 26S 49 proteasome capacity, we defined the transcriptional network that responds to this stress in 50 *Arabidopsis thaliana*. This network includes genes encoding core and assembly factors needed 51 to build the complete 26S particle, alternative proteasome capping factors, enzymes involved in 52 protein ubiquitylation/deubiquitylation and cellular detoxification, protein chaperones, autophagy 53 components, and various transcriptional regulators. Many loci in this proteasome-stress regulon 54 contain a consensus *cis* element upstream of the transcription start site, which was previously 55 identified as a binding site for the NAM/ATAF1/CUC2 (NAC)-78 transcription factor. Double 56 mutants disrupting *NAC78* and its closest relative *NAC53* are compromised in the activation of 57 this regulon, and notably are strongly hypersensitive to the proteasome inhibitors MG132 and 58 bortezomib. Given that NAC53 and NAC78 homo- and hetero-dimerize, we propose that they 59 work as a pair in activating the expression of numerous factors that help plants survive 60 proteotoxic stress, and thus play a central regulatory role in maintaining protein homeostasis.

### 62 **INTRODUCTION**

63 All cellular organisms require mechanisms to dampen the accumulation of aberrant proteins 64 caused by transcription/translation errors, misfolding, cleavage, chemical modification, and 65 environmental conditions such as heat that perturb tertiary/quaternary structures. If allowed to 66 hyper-accumulate, this detritus can negatively impact a host of intracellular activities through an 67 imbalance in protein homeostasis and excessive protein aggregation (Morimoto, 2008; Hipp et 68 al., 2014). The resulting proteotoxic stress has strong physiological consequences, including 69 genome instability, arrest of the cell cycle, inhibition of translation through a reduction in 70 ribosomes, lost membrane integrity, inhibition of metabolism, and accelerated senescence, as 71 well as amino acid starvation if protein recycling becomes severely compromised. In fact, the 72 accumulation of misfolded, aggregation prone proteins is the hallmark of a broad range of 73 human diseases (Cuanalo-Contreras et al., 2013).

74 To alleviate this stress, plants, fungi, and animals elicit a number of cytoprotective 75 responses, including expression of protein chaperones and activation of the unfolded protein 76 response designed to promote refolding and sequester protein aggregates, maintenance of 77 chromatin integrity through SUMOylation, and up-regulation of several proteolytic pathways that 78 remove these aberrant polypeptides before they become cytotoxic (Hetz, 2012; Howell, 2013; 79 Kim et al., 2013; Amm et al., 2014; Seifert et al., 2015). Excessive protein aggregation and the 80 accumulation of defective protein complexes (*e.g.,* ribosomes and proteasomes) and organelles 81 (*e.g.,* chloroplasts and mitochondria) often engage autophagy (Li and Vierstra, 2012; Khaminets 82 et al., 2015; Marshall et al., 2015). These damaged structures are encapsulated into 83 cytoplasmic vesicles and delivered to the vacuole/lysosome for breakdown, in many cases 84 using ubiquitylation as a signal.

85 Arguably, the most important protease up-regulated during proteotoxic stress is the 26S 86 proteasome (Amm et al., 2014; Hanssum et al., 2014; Livnat-Levanon et al., 2014). This 2.5- 87 MDa, ATP-dependent proteolytic machine works in tandem with ubiquitin (Ub) to direct the 88 selective breakdown of aberrant polypeptides and normal short-lived proteins in the cytoplasm 89 and nucleus. Targets are first covalently modified with multiple Ubs; the resulting ubiquitylated 90 species dock with the 26S proteasome, which degrades the modified protein concomitant with 91 release of the Ub moieties for reuse. The 26S proteasome consists of two subcomplexes: the 92 20S core protease (CP) and the 19S regulatory particle (RP) (Finley, 2009; Bhattacharyya et al., 93 2014). The CP consists of four stacked heteroheptameric rings of distinct α and β subunits in 94 an α1-7/β1-7/β1-7/α1-7 configuration, which houses in a central chamber the proteolytic active sites 95 provided by the PBA (β1), PBB (β2) and PBE (β5) subunits. The RP has 18 or more subunits; it 96 caps one or both ends of the CP barrel and contains receptors for ubiquitylated targets, and

97 activities that remove the Ub moieties and unfold and translocate the target polypeptides into 98 the CP lumen before breakdown. In addition, a host of accessory factors associate 99 substoichiometrically, including dedicated chaperones that promote the sequential assembly of 100 the CP and RP complexes and final construction of the 26S particle, alternative capping factors 101 (PA200 and CDC48), shuttle proteins that help deliver ubiquitylated substrates, and ubiquitin-102 protein ligases (or E3s) and deubiquitylating enzymes (Finley, 2009; Hanssum et al., 2014).

103 Given its role(s) in maintaining protein homeostasis, the levels of the 26S proteasome 104 are highly regulated to meet demand, which is achieved by the coordinated expression of all 105 core subunits and most, if not all, accessory factors. In yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*), this 106 regulon is driven by the C2H2-type zinc-finger transcription factor Rpn4, which binds to the 107 PACE (Proteasome Associated Control Element) *cis* element found upstream of most 108 proteasome subunit genes (Mannhaupt et al., 1999; Xie and Varshavsky, 2001; Shirozu et al., 109 2015). Rpn4 responds to proteotoxic stress by itself being a target of proteasomal breakdown. 110 When proteolytic demand is low, Rpn4 is rapidly degraded (t1/2 ~2.5 minutes), thus attenuating 111 expression of 26S proteasome genes (Xie and Varshavsky, 2001; Dohmen et al., 2007). 112 However, as 26S proteasome capacity is challenged by proteotoxic stress, Rpn4 is stabilized 113 thus allowing its levels to rise and up-regulate particle synthesis.

114 Although orthologs of Rpn4 are not obvious outside of yeasts, a similar 'proteasome 115 stress regulon' (PSR) exists in many other eukaryotes, including mammalian cells, *Drosophila* 116 *melanogaster* and *Arabidopsis thaliana* (Meiners et al., 2003; Yang et al., 2004; Lundgren et al., 117 2005; Kurepa et al., 2008; Book et al., 2009)**.** In mice and humans, the Nrf1 (Nuclear Factor 118 Erythroid-derived 2-Related Factor 1) transcription factor, unrelated to Rpn4, was recently 119 shown to be a central effector (Radhakrishnan et al., 2010; 2014; Sha and Goldberg, 2014). 120 Nrf1 is bound to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and is constitutively degraded by the ER-121 associated protein degradation (ERAD) pathway, and like *Δrpn4* yeast cells, *nrf1*-null cells are 122 more sensitive to proteasome inhibitors. Upon proteotoxic stress, Nrf1 is proteolytically 123 released from the ER and is now free to enter the nucleus and activate the PSR.

124 How the PSR is controlled by proteotoxic stress remains unclear in plants. One 125 candidate regulator in Arabidopsis is NAC78, a member of the NO APICAL 126 MERISTEM/ARABIDOPSIS TRANSCRIPTION ACTIVATION FACTOR-1/CUP-SHAPED 127 COTYLEDONS-2 (NAC) family of transcriptional regulators. NAC78 was first implicated by 128 overexpression studies showing that it positively regulates the expression of core 26S 129 proteasome subunit genes, and that its putative DNA-binding site is present within many, but 130 not all, associated promoters (Yabuta et al., 2011; Nguyen et al., 2013). Whereas *NAC78-OX* 131 plants are smaller than wild type, *nac78* mutant plants are larger, which is consistent with a

132 reported role for 26S proteasome levels in controlling cell size (Kurepa et al., 2009; Sonoda et 133 al., 2009).

134 To more broadly define the plant PSR, we combined RNA-seq analysis with both 135 chemical inhibitors and proteasome mutants that induce proteotoxic stress. Gene co-136 expression combined with protein interactome network analyses revealed a complex network of 137 PSR genes/proteins enriched in protein chaperones, autophagy components and detoxification 138 enzymes that presumably help plants cope with proteotoxic stress, in addition to those encoding 139 the 26S proteasome and its assembly/capping components. Network and promoter-interaction 140 studies then identified the NAC78 paralog NAC53 as a key effector that, together with NAC78, 141 likely functions as homo- and heterodimers. Importantly, seedlings simultaneously lacking 142 NAC53 and NAC78 poorly activate the PSR during proteotoxic stress, and their growth is 143 strongly hypersensitive to proteasome inhibitors. As such, we propose that these two NAC 144 proteins are central regulators of an extended PSR in Arabidopsis by providing sufficient 26S 145 proteasomes and other protein homeostatic factors to mitigate proteotoxic stress.

### 147 **RESULTS**

#### 148 **26S Proteasome Subunits Are Up-Regulated During Proteotoxic Stress**

149 To better understand how 26S proteasome gene expression is up-regulated by proteotoxic 150 stress, we examined the transcript levels for representative Arabidopsis subunits either upon 151 short (3 hr) or long term (24 hr) exposure of seedlings to the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Yang 152 et al., 2004), or in mutant backgrounds that compromise 26S proteasome assembly (*rpn10-1* 153 and *rpn12a-1*) and elicit seedling phenotypes consistent with impaired capacity (Smalle et al., 154 2002; 2003). The mutant alleles were fortuitously generated by exon-trap mutagenesis; they 155 are viable but have stunted growth and show pleiotropic defects in hormone signaling. The 156 *rpn12a-1* allele dampens expression of the full-length *RPN12a* transcript (Figure 2A), but is 157 relatively mild phenotypically (Smalle et al., 2002). The *rpn10-1* allele expresses a truncation of 158 RPN10, the main Ub receptor in the 26S complex, and has much stronger developmental 159 consequences (Smalle et al., 2003). The translated polypeptide includes the N-terminal von 160 Willebrand Factor-A domain that links RPN10 to the rest of the RP but is missing the C-terminal 161 region containing the three Ub-interacting motifs that bind Ub, the autophagy adaptor ATG8, 162 and cargo receptors bearing Ub-like domains, respectively (Farmer et al., 2010; Fatimababy et 163 al., 2010; Marshall et al., 2015).

164 Previous studies demonstrated that both long-term exposure to MG132 and the *rpn10-1* 165 mutation elevate the steady-state levels of Ub conjugates and increase the abundance of 166 several core subunits of the 26S proteasome (Smalle et al., 2003; Yang et al., 2004; Kurepa et

167 al., 2008). We extended these results here by immunoblotting crude extracts from MG132- 168 treated wild-type and untreated homozygous *rpn10-1* and *rpn12a-1* seedlings with antibodies 169 against Ub, the CP subunit PBA1(β1), the RP subunits RPN1, RPT2 and RPN12a, and the CP 170 regulator PA200 (known as Blm10 in yeast) (Figures 1A and 1B). In particular, accumulation of 171 the unprocessed form of PBA1 increased strongly upon MG132 treatment, consistent with the 172 need for active proteasomes to generate the mature, truncated β1 polypeptide (Finley, 2009; 173 Book et al., 2010). The *rpn12a-1* allele had only a marginal effect on the abundance of Ub 174 conjugates and the PBA1, RPN1 and RPT2 subunits but its action was obvious based on its 175 strong effect on PA200 levels (Figure 1A). Why the effects on CP/RP subunit levels were mild 176 for the *rpn12a-1* allele was unclear but it could reflect the relatively modest phenotype of the 177 mutant (Smalle et al., 2002), and/or the possibility that genetically compromised 26S 178 proteasomes generated by the *rpn12a-1* allele more effectively induce autophagic turnover of 179 the complex as opposed to the *rpn10-1* mutant, which blocks such turnover (Marshall et al., 180 2015).

181 When mRNA abundance was then examined by reverse-transcription quantitative PCR 182 (RT-qPCR) analysis of seedlings, levels of the CP subunit *PBA1* and the RP subunits *RPN5a*, 183 *RPN10* and *RPN12a* were found to rise rapidly upon short treatments with MG132 and were 184 constitutively up-regulated in the *rpn10-1* and *rpn12a-1* backgrounds (Figure 2A). (Throughout 185 this study, we used transcripts from the *ACTIN* (*ACT)-2* and/or *TYPE-2A SERINE/THREONINE*  186 *PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE* (*PP)-2A* genes as controls given their relative immunity to 187 proteotoxic stress (see Supplemental Figure 1)). As with previous studies (Gallois et al., 2009; 188 Lee et al., 2011), this increased transcript abundance could also be demonstrated with 189 transgenic plants expressing the β-glucuronidase (GUS) reporter under the control of various 190 26S proteasome gene promoters. Up-regulation upon MG132 treatment was visualized 191 colorimetrically by staining the seedlings with X-Gluc, and quantitatively by 4-methylumbelliferyl-192 β-D-glucuronide (MUG)-based fluorescence activity assays of crude seedling extracts (Figures 193 2B and 2C). Interestingly, comparisons of several gene pairs that encode individual 194 proteasome subunits revealed that often only one responds to MG132, implying that the many 195 pairs have subfunctionalized with one locus mainly responsible for increasing subunit mRNA 196 levels during proteotoxic stress (*e.g., RPN3a, RPT1a, RPT2a,* and *RPT4b* (Figures 2B and 2C)). 197

### 198 **RNA-seq Analysis of the Proteasome Stress-Induced Regulon (PSR)**

199 To more fully identify the suite of genes that are up-regulated when proteasome capacity is 200 compromised, we performed RNA-seq analysis with our cohort of wild-type seedlings treated 201 with MG132 (3 and 24 hr) and untreated *rpn10-1* and *rpn12a-1* seedlings. These transcriptome

202 studies identified a large collection of mRNAs whose abundance was significantly affected as 203 compared to untreated wild-type seedlings based on negative binomial normalization using 204 edgeR differential expression analysis (*p-*value <0.01, FDR <0.05; see Supplemental Datasets 205 1 and 2). By merging the datasets, we identified 119 genes (including *RPN10* and *RPN12a* 206 given their up-regulation in 2 of the 3 conditions) that were coordinately up-regulated under all 207 three conditions (3-hr treatment with MG132 and in the *rpn10-1* and *rpn12a-1* backgrounds), 208 which we designated as members of the PSR (Figure 3A; see Supplemental Dataset 3). RNA-209 seq analysis after a 24-hr exposure to MG132 identified an additional set of 865 up-regulated 210 genes; these loci were not included in the final PSR as their long-term induction might be more 211 indirectly related to the prolonged effects of the inhibitor and the downstream responses to 212 severe proteotoxic stress (see Supplemental Dataset 4).

213 Gene ontology analyses via DAVID (Database for Annotation, Visualization and 214 Integrated Discovery; https://david.ncifcrf.gov) of the 119 PSR up-regulated genes detected a 215 significant functional enrichment for the 26S proteasome, Ub-related events, and general stress 216 response processes, and identified several transcription factors that might transcriptionally 217 activate the PSR. This enrichment for 26S proteasome genes was especially strong in the more 218 robustly affected group with a ≥ 2-fold increase (44% of total; Figure 3B). Of the 53 genes 219 encoding core 26S proteasome subunits (Book et al., 2010; Russell et al., 2013), 48 (91%) were 220 significantly up-regulated (Figures 3B and 3C). We note that the rank order of the up-regulated 221 and down-regulated genes in the MG132 and *rpn10-1*/*rpn12a-1* datasets based on their 222 strength of change differed markedly (Figure 3B); this deviation could reflect two distinct 223 subtypes of proteotoxic stress, one elicited rapidly and strongly by the inhibitor and the other 224 being a more subtle chronic stress elicited by the mutations. We also detected 33 genes whose 225 expression was down-regulated by all three conditions (Figures 3A and 3B; see Supplemental 226 Dataset 3); there was no significant functional enrichment in this group, thus leaving their 227 collective action(s) unclear.

228 From analysis of genes known or predicted to be associated with the Arabidopsis 26S 229 proteasome (Finley, 2009; Book et al., 2010), a strikingly coordinated up-regulation was evident 230 for most loci. This was apparent for both core CP and RP subunits as well as acknowledged 231 accessory factors (PA200 and ECM29) and chaperones (HSM3, NAS6, PBAC1, PBAC2 and 232 UMP1a) that are presumably needed during proteotoxic stress to assist in particle 233 regulation/assembly (Figure 3C). The proteasome-binding protein, PROTEASOME 234 REGULATOR (PTRE)-1, recently identified as a positive regulator of auxin signaling through 235 activation of the 26S proteasome (Yang et al., 2016), was also up-regulated, as was the 236 possible plant-specific chaperone PIP1. The only exception for subunits encoded by a single

237 gene was the locus encoding the Ub receptor RPN13, whose expression was relatively immune 238 to MG132 and was unchanged by the mutations. In agreement with the RT-qPCR and *26S-*239 *promoter:GUS* reporter studies above, we found that multiple gene pairs encoding individual 240 subunits had at least one locus that was PSR regulated with the second locus sometimes non-241 responsive. Examples include the *PAA2*, *PBB2, RPT1a, RPT6b, RPN3a*, and *RPN9b* loci, 242 which were highly sensitive to proteotoxic stress, whereas their paralogs *PAA1*, *PBB1, RPT1b,*  243 *RPT6a, RPN3*, and *RPN9a* were comparatively silent (Figure 3C).

244 When the analysis was extended to non-proteasome transcripts, additional loci 245 potentially relevant to the PSR were detected (see Supplemental Dataset 3). Included were 246 mRNAs encoding members of the NAC, WRKY, and heat shock protein transcription factor 247 (HSF) families that often have prominent roles in various plant stress responses (Rushton et al., 248 2010; Nakashima et al., 2012; Scharf et al., 2012), other Ub/proteasome pathway components 249 including Ub, several Ub E3 ligases (AIP2, At1g71020, At4g27050 and At5g27920), 250 deubiquitylating enzymes (UBP6, UBP7 and UCH3), and extra-proteasomal Ub-binding proteins 251 (DSK2a and DDI1), and components of the autophagy system (see Supplemental Figure 2). 252 Notable autophagy factors included several members of the ATG8 family required for 253 autophagic vesicle dynamics and cargo delivery, and the autophagic receptor NBR1, which is 254 devoted to the recruitment of ubiquitylated cargo (Li and Vierstra, 2012; Zhou et al., 2013). 255 Strong transcriptional up-regulation of *NBR1,* especially by MG132, implied a role for autophagy 256 in removing Ub conjugates that become stabilized when proteasome capacity is limited.

### 258 **The MG132-Induced PSR Network**

259 To better visualize the MG132-induced PSR, we entered the 336 MG132-induced genes that 260 were significantly up-regulated ≥ 2 fold after a 3-hr treatment (see Supplemental Dataset 1) into 261 the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Protein (STRING) co-expression and 262 interactome database (Jensen et al., 2009). Upon visualization in Cytoscape v3.0 (Shannon et 263 al., 2003), an extensive collage of networks involving 275 output loci emerged that clustered into 264 three main groups (Figure 4A). As expected, one tight cluster included loci encoding 26S 265 proteasome subunits and accessory factors/proteasome chaperones. A second cluster 266 encompassed a large collection of general heat shock protein (HSP) chaperones (several 267 HSP70 isoforms, HSA32, HSP81, and HSP101, and their dedicated transcriptional regulator 268 HSFA8) that presumably direct protein refolding and/or minimize aggregation of insoluble 269 proteins awaiting proteasomal/autophagic turnover (Lin et al., 2014). A third more diffuse 270 cluster was enriched in the stress-related NAC, WRKY, and HSF transcription factors that might 271 help activate the PSR, and transferase activities (*e.g.,* glutathione S-transferases and O-

272 methyltransferases) that could mitigate proteotoxic stress (Livnat-Levanon et al., 2014). The 273 third cluster also contained several UDP-glycosyl transferases and the main mitochrondrial 274 alternative oxidase AOX1a, which were shown previously to be up-regulated upon mitochondrial 275 perturbation (De Clercq et al., 2013). Many of these loci were also seen in clusters generated 276 with the 24-hr MG132 treatment dataset, especially for the 26S proteasome and related factors 277 (see Supplemental Figure 3). Additions to the 24-hr MG132 network included a pronounced 278 autophagy cluster, and a large cluster enriched in protein kinases that could extend the PSR 279 signaling network given the roles of some in stress defense and developmental signal 280 transduction cascades (*e.g.,* MEK1, MAPKKK1, RLP24, HLECRK, CRCK1 and CRCK11).

281 Under the assumption that many of the 336 loci are transcriptionally regulated by a 282 common set of transcription factors, we searched their promoter regions for shared *cis* 283 regulatory DNA elements using the motif-based sequence analysis tool MEME (Multiple 284 Expectation Maximization for Motif Elicitation; Bailey et al., 2006). Highly enriched were the 285 Proteasome-Related *cis* Element (PRCE) with a TGGGC core sequence, which was previously 286 implicated by Nguyen et al. (2013) in the regulation of proteasome genes, and the mitochondrial 287 dysfunction motif (MDM) associated with oxidative stress responses (De Clercq et al., 2013) 288 (Figures 4B and 4C). Both motifs were prominent in the 26S proteasome gene cluster, with few 289 promoters (14 of 48) predicted to be devoid of either motif (Figure 4D). When placed within the 290 region upstream of the translation start site for genes encoding core 26S proteasome subunits, 291 a wide dispersion of the PRCE and MDM motifs was seen, with some having these elements 292 close to the transcription start site and others placed >1000 bp away (see Supplemental Figure 293 4). Whereas single MDM elements were common, we often detected multiple PRCE-related 294 sequences in tandem, sometimes on opposite DNA strands, suggesting that PRCE is acted 295 upon by DNA-binding proteins that work palindromically. We also detected other potentially 296 relevant *cis* elements associated with the PSR at lower frequencies (see Supplemental Figure 297 5). Included were the SORLIP2 and T-Box motifs that are involved in light-induced 298 developmental responses (Chan et al., 2001; Hudson and Quail, 2003).

### 300 **The NAC53 and NAC78 Transcription Factors Are Associated with the PSR.**

301 To help identify the transcriptional regulators that might coordinate the PSR, we screened a 302 yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) library of ~1,700 known or predicted DNA-binding proteins from 303 Arabidopsis by an automated luciferase (*LUC*)-based expression system (Gaudinier et al., 304 2011), using both the *PA200* or *RPN12a* promoter regions as bait (1000 and 253-bp fragments 305 upstream of the translation start codon, respectively)*.* The activity of both promoters is strongly 306 up-regulated by MG132 exposure, and accordingly they contain multiple segments related to

307 the consensus PRCE sequence. (It should be noted that the *RPN12a* promoter is predicted to 308 be short given that only 253 bp separates its translation start site from the coding region 309 immediately upstream.) Among the list of 15 interactors that activated both Y1H assays was 310 NAC53 (At3g10500, also known as NTL4; see Supplemental Table 1), a 555-residue NAC 311 protein that we also discovered as a locus significantly up-regulated by brief MG132 exposure 312 and positioned by Cytoscape as a central hub within the PSR detoxification cluster (Figure 4A, 313 see Supplemental Figure 2). Upon scanning the Arabidopsis genome for related proteins, we 314 identified NAC78 (At5g04410, also known as NTL11/RPX1), a 584-residue NAC protein that is 315 its closest relative (~72% amino acid sequence identity) among the ~109 NAC-type 316 transcriptional regulators (Olsen et al., 2005a; Nakashima et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2012). Binding 317 of NAC53 and NAC78 to both promoters was confirmed by directed Y1H assays using 500 bp of 318 the *PA200* promoter and 253 bp of the *RPN12a* promoter as baits.

319 Intriguingly, several previous studies implicated NAC53 and NAC78 in the PSR and/or in 320 binding PRCE/MDM-type sequences. NAC78 was first linked by over-expression screens that 321 searched for factors that regulate either plant size or seedling responses to light stress, and was 322 subsequently shown by transcriptome studies to impact proteasome gene expression (Morishita 323 et al., 2009; Yabuta et al., 2011; Nguyen et al., 2013). Nguyen et al. (2013) further connected 324 NAC78 to the PSR by reporting that it recognizes the same PRCE element identified here as 325 common within the regulon (Figure 3C). A further connection to the PSR was provided by DNA-326 binding studies showing that both NAC53 and NAC78 might also recognize MDM elements 327 found here to be common within PSR loci (De Clercq et al., 2013). Finally, NAC53 was 328 previously connected to heat stress, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and senescence, 329 all of which are related to proteotoxic stress, through cursory phenotypic analyses of *NAC53*  330 mutant and overexpression lines (Lee et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2014),

331 To provide an evolutionary connection between NAC53 and NAC78, we examined the 332 two loci phylogenetically in the context of the entire NAC family. Bayesian analyses clustered 333 NAC53 and NAC78 together when either the 109 full-length NAC proteins were compared or 334 when just the DNA-binding NAM domains were analyzed, indicating that this pair forms a unique 335 subclade within the NAC family (Figure 5A; see Supplemental Figures 6 and 7). In fact, strong 336 amino acid sequence identity was seen throughout the NAM domain (~93%), implying that they 337 bind the same DNA sequence (see Supplemental Figure 8). Close sequence relatives (~50% 338 sequence identity) were also detected in other plant species, including various monocots and 339 dicots and the seedless plants *Selaginella moelendorffii* and *Physcomitrella patens,* but not in 340 the alga *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii*, implying that the pair has a conserved function in land 341 plants. Relatives of both NAC53 and NAC78 could be found in *Arabidopsis lyrata* that clustered

342 apart from likely orthologs in other dicots, suggesting that NAC53 and NAC78 originated from a 343 duplication that occurred recently within the *Brassicaceae* lineage.

344 The C-terminal ends of NAC53 and NAC78 are predicted to contain a 24-amino-acid 345 membrane-spanning sequence that is also found within 11 other Arabidopsis NAC proteins, 346 which are collectively designated as NTLs (NACs with transmembrane-like region (Kim et al., 347 2007; see Supplemental Figure 6). Like the NAM domain, this putative membrane-spanning 348 region is highly conserved between NAC53 and NAC78 (75% sequence identity), even when 349 aligning their sequences next to that of their closest NTL relative NAC13 (30% full polypeptide 350 sequence identify, 65% NAM domain sequence identity; see Supplemental Figure 8). Even 351 within the NTL subfamily, NAC53/NAC78 stand apart phylogenetically, suggesting that they 352 have distinct function(s) (Figure 5A; see Supplemental Figures 6 and 7). The role(s) of the 353 membrane-spanning domain is not yet clear; for at least one Arabidopsis NTL, it has been 354 proposed to facilitate binding of the NAC protein to cytosolic membranes, with proteolytic 355 release then permitting nuclear import (Kim et al., 2006; Kim et al., 2007).

#### 357 **NAC53 and NAC78 Regulate PSR Gene Expression**

358 Given the aforementioned connections of NAC53/NAC78 to proteasome gene regulation and 359 their likely ability to bind PRCE-type motifs (Yabuta et al., 2011; De Clercq et al., 2013; Nguyen 360 et al., 2013; this report), we hypothesized that NAC53 and NAC78 work in concert as homo-361 and heterodimers to up-regulate the PSR. As a first validation, we tested whether NAC53 and 362 NAC78 interact, as is common for paralogs within the NAC family (Nakashima et al., 2012; De 363 Clercq et al., 2013). By yeast two-hybrid assays (Y2H), we detected binding of NAC53 and 364 NAC78 to themselves and to each other, using the *Gal4/LacZ* pDEST system, thus generating 365 colonies that grew in the absence of His and were resistant to 50 mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3- 366 AT) (Figure 5B). This assembly of homo- and heterodimers was then confirmed *in planta* by 367 bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) with the split YFP system transiently 368 expressed in *Nicotiana benthamiana* leaf epidermal cells. Only when the NAC53 or NAC78 369 coding regions were simultaneously expressed as fusions to either the nYFP or cYFP fragments 370 was fluorescence reconstituted (Figure 5C, see Supplemental Figures 9A and 9C). 371 Fluorescence was easily detected in the cytoplasm and nucleus, suggesting that the two 372 proteins interact in each compartment.

373 It had been reported that when a GFP fusion of full-length NAC78 is transiently 374 expressed in onion (*Allium cepa*) epidermal cells, it mainly localizes to the cytosol but becomes 375 concentrated in the nucleus when expressed without the membrane-spanning region (Morishita 376 et al., 2009). To examine whether proteotoxic stress could recapitulate this redistribution, we

377 attempted to generate transgenic plants that stably express GFP-NAC53 and GFP-NAC78 with 378 or without this transmembrane domain. Unfortunately no fluorescent signals were detected, in 379 agreement with a previous failure with another NTL, NAC13 (De Clercq et al., 2013). As an 380 alternative, we examined the BiFC signal from the NAC53/NAC78 pair in *N. benthamiana* cells 381 simultaneously treated with MG132. Unfortunately, under our conditions, MG132 treatment 382 neither impacted the strength nor the distribution of the BiFC signals (see Supplemental Figure 383 9B).

384 As the next step in connecting at least one of this NAC pair to the PSR, we examined 385 transgenic lines expressing NAC78 from the β-estradiol-inducible XVE promoter, which was 386 provided by the TRANSPLANTA collection (Coego et al., 2014). In our hands, *NAC78* mRNA 387 levels rose ~60 fold after an overnight β-estradiol exposure. As shown in Figure 6, 388 *pEST:NAC78* seedlings, but not wild-type seedlings, treated with β-estradiol also strongly 389 increased the mRNA abundance for several representative 26S proteasome subunits, as first 390 observed by Nguyen et al. (2013). Importantly, this up-regulation extended to other members of 391 the PSR outside of the core 26S particle, including genes encoding the proteasome accessory 392 factor PA200, the NAS6 assembly chaperone, the UPS component UFD1, and the HSP 393 transcriptional regulator HSF8A. Two other PSR genes encoding the proteasome accessory 394 factor PA200 and the glutathione S-transferase GSTU25 also displayed increases close to 395 significance, consistent with NAC78 more broadly controlling the PSR.

396 Assuming that the NAC53/NAC78 proteins work together, we generated double mutants 397 impacting the pair to observe any adverse effects on PSR regulation. Potentially useful alleles 398 were identified in the SALK collection of T-DNA insertion mutants that either disrupted the 399 coding region downstream from the N-terminal NAM domain (*nac53-1*), within the NAM domain 400 (*nac78-1*), just upstream of that for the C-terminal transmembrane sequence (*nac53-2*), or 401 toward the end of the coding region (*nac78-2*; Figure 7A). Genomic sequencing mapped the 402 insertion sites to 735 and 372 nucleotides downstream of the translational start site for the 403 *nac53-1* and *nac78-1* mutations, and 87 and 21 nucleotides upstream of the translational stop 404 site for the *nac53-2* and *nac78-2* mutations, respectively.

405 Transcript analyses by RT-PCR failed to detect the corresponding full-length mRNAs in 406 homozygous *nac53-1* and *nac78-1* seedlings, (Figure 7B). For *nac53-1* we also could not 407 detect transcripts downstream of the insertion site, whereas for *nac78-1* we could detect a 408 transcript of slightly greater length than expected. Collectively the RT-PCR data imply that 409 *nac53-1* and *nac78-1* represent null alleles. For homozygous *nac53-2* and *nac78-2* seedlings, 410 we detected low levels of near-full length transcripts originating upstream of the insertion sites; 411 these mRNAs included the NAM domain coding sequence. For the *nac78-2* mutant, only a

412 small portion of the proposed C-terminal membrane-spanning region domain was eliminated, 413 whereas, nearly the entire membrane-spanning region was missing for the *nac53-2* mutant (see 414 Supplemental Figure 8), suggesting that they represent weaker alleles under the assumption 415 that the full transmembrane domain is not essential (at least for NAC53).

416 When grown at 24**°**C under a normal 16-hr light/8-hr dark photoperiod on agar medium 417 or soil, all the single and double mutant combinations germinated well, generated phenotypically 418 normal rosettes, flowered at the same time, and were equally fertile as wild type, indicating that 419 NAC53 and NAC78 alone or in combination are not necessary for Arabidopsis growth, 420 development and fecundity under non-stressed conditions. At least under our growth conditions, 421 the mutant seedlings were not larger than wild type, as had been reported previously for a T-422 DNA insertion line disrupting *NAC78* by itself (Nguyen et al., 2013).

423 Importantly, RT-qPCR analyses of PSR-related genes revealed that NAC53 and NAC78 424 combine to activate the PSR. Whereas the *nac53-1* mutation had little effect on expression of 425 representative PSR genes after an overnight MG132 treatment, a mild but statistically significant 426 dampening (*p-*value <0.01) was seen for the *nac78-1* line (Figure 8A). Moreover, when the 427 double *nac53-1 nac78-1* mutant was tested, a substantially attenuated PSR response was 428 observed. Compromised loci included genes encoding subunits of the 26S particle (PBA1, 429 RPN5a, RPN10 and RPN12a), the accessory factors PA200 and CDC48, the NAS6 chaperone, 430 the UPS factor UFD1, the glutathione S-transferase GSTU25, and the heat shock transcription 431 factor HSFA8 (*p*-value <0.01). For some loci, the *nac53-1 nac78-1* combination almost 432 completely ablated the transcriptional up-regulation. A smaller but significant drop in PSR gene 433 expression was also seen for the *nac53-2 nac78-2* double mutant, consistent with the milder 434 effect of these mutations on the *NAC53/NAC78* mRNAs (Figure 8A).

435 The diminished PSR in turn reduced the steady-state levels of some of the 436 corresponding proteins. Whereas MG132 treatment increased the seedling abundance of the 437 26S accessory protein PA200, the RP subunit RPN12a and the unprocessed form of the CP 438 subunit PBA1 in wild-type seedlings, this increase was substantially weaker in the *nac53-1*  439 *nac78-1* background (Figure 8B). However, this drop did not appear to translate into a 440 substantial stabilization of Ub conjugates, as the pool of immunodetectable species was only 441 mildly increased in the *nac53-1 nac78-1* plants as compared to wild type (see Supplemental 442 Figure 10). Consistent with the reduced strength of the *nac53-2* and *nac78-2* alleles on PSR 443 gene expression, the double mutants had little impact on the RPN12a and PBA1 protein levels, 444 and possibly only a mild effect on PA200 (Figure 8B). As the polypeptides derived from the 445 *nac53-2 nac78-2* alleles should be missing all or part of the presumed transmembrane domain, 446 respectively, membrane association might not be essential for NAC53/NAC78 function.

### 448 **Plants Missing NAC53 and NAC78 are Hypersensitive to Proteotoxic Stress**

449 Phenotypic analysis of homozygous *nac53-1 nac78-1* plants in turn revealed that both 450 transcription factors together help Arabidopsis survive proteotoxic stress. Whereas wild-type 451 plants could tolerate long-term exposure to sublethal doses of MG132 beginning at germination, 452 growth of the double mutant plants was substantially arrested (Figures 9A and 9B). In fact, 453 development of *nac53-1 nac78-1* seeds stalled soon after germination, and for many, the 454 radicals failed to exit the seed coat after rupture. Quantification of the response also detected a 455 significant growth inhibition (*p*-value <0.01) for the *nac78-1* single mutant and for the weaker 456 *nac53-2 nac78-2* double mutant as compared to wild type (Figure 9B).

457 Given that the smaller plants observed for the *nac53-1 nac78-1* line upon MG132 458 exposure might have arisen from defective or delayed germination, we also germinated and 459 grew the seedlings for 6 d in the absence of MG132 and then placed them on inhibitor-460 containing medium and measured seedling fresh weight 6 d afterwards. Even in this situation, 461 growth of the *nac53-1 nac78-1* plants was significantly impaired as compared to wild type 462 (Figure 9C). As MG132 might impact other proteases besides those within the CP, we also 463 tested a second 26S proteasome inhibitor with potentially greater specificity and potency – 464 bortezomib (Kisselev et al., 2012). Here, growth of the *nac53-1 nac78-1* plants was severely 465 inhibited at submicromolar concentrations, with a mild but significant effect also seen for the 466 *nac53-2 nac78-2* plants (Figures 9D and 9E).

### 468 **NAC53 and NAC78 Work Together and with Other Members of the NAC Family.**

469 While our studies indicated that NAC53 and NAC78 work together, possibly as heterodimers 470 based on our Y2H assays, to activate the PSR, they could also have temporally distinct 471 functions within the response (*e.g.,* early versus late) given that *NAC53* but not *NAC78*  472 transcripts were increased rapidly by MG132 treatment. To test these possibilities, we 473 compared the time course for PSR activation by MG132 in the single mutant *nac53-1* and 474 *nac78-1* backgrounds by RT-qPCR analysis of representative PSR loci. As shown in 475 Supplemental Figure 11, the induction time courses were identical (but slightly diminished) for 476 genes encoding the CP and RP subunits, CDC48, the RP chaperone NAS6, and GSTU25, with 477 the double *nac53-1 nac78-1* mutant lacking a significant response for all loci tested but *GSTU25*. 478 Together, the data imply that NAC53 and NAC78 work within the same time frame.

479 The mild attenuation of the PSR for the *nac53-1 nac78-1* plants in response to MG132 480 as seen with *GSTU25* and other loci (*e.g., WRKY25*) (Figure 8A; see Supplemental Figure 11), 481 suggested that other transcription factors also contribute to the PSR. Particularly notable were 482 several other members of the NAC family that were transcriptionally up-regulated by the 483 inhibitor, including NAC1, 13, 32, 44, 50, 55, 81/ATAF2, 82, and 87 (see Supplemental Figure 2). 484 To test whether they also heterodimerize with NAC53/NAC78, we subjected each to Y2H 485 assays using NAC53 and NAC78 as prey. Interestingly, NAC13 and NAC81, but not the others, 486 generated positive Y2H signals, suggesting that they also contribute to PSR activation in 487 combination with NAC53/NAC78 (Figure 5D). Whereas NAC13 bound to both NAC53 and 488 NAC78, NAC81 only showed an interaction with NAC53 under these conditions. NAC13 is 489 especially intriguing given its ability to recognize MDM *cis* elements common within the PSR 490 and its membership in the transmembrane-containing NTL-NAC subfamily like NAC53/NAC78.

### 492 **DISCUSSION**

493 Given the importance of protein quality control to maintaining healthy cellular functions, and the 494 likelihood that plants routinely experience proteotoxic stress (*e.g.,* ERAD; Howell 2013), we 495 considered it likely that plants have strong protective responses. Examples include various 496 environmental insults such as heat, cold, salt, drought, excess light, photooxidative stress, and 497 ROS production that perturb protein folding, carbon and nitrogen starvation that limits synthesis 498 of new polypeptides, pathogen invasion which often coincides with the massive translation of 499 pathogen-associated polypeptides that might express improperly in the host, and exposure to 500 toxins made by the pathogen, such as the proteasome inhibitors epoxomicin and syringolin A 501 that block host protein turnover (Yang et al., 2004; Groll et al., 2008). Our RNA-seq studies on 502 Arabidopsis seedlings induced to experience proteotoxic stress through impairment of 26S 503 proteasome capacity (MG132 inhibition and the *rpn10-1* and *rpn12a-1* mutants) revealed an 504 intricate network of PSR genes/proteins that presumably represent parts of these protective 505 mechanisms. Many of the associated activities are also important for protein homeostasis in 506 yeast and animals, indicating that they reflect conserved protein homeostatic processes among 507 eukaryotes (Morimoto, 2008; Hipp et al., 2014).

508 A prominent PSR cluster is the 26S proteasome itself and its associated 509 assembly/regulatory factors that are needed to build a functional holoenzyme, demonstrating 510 that increasing 26S proteasome capacity is one crucial protective mechanism. In fact, given the 511 plethora of core subunits and assembly chaperones required to construct 26S proteasomes, a 512 remarkable coordination of expression has evolved to ensure faithful production of complete 513 particles. As shown by mutational studies affecting individual subunits, sub-stoichiometric 514 accumulation of only one subunit is sufficient to stall construction of the entire complex and 515 induce off-product accumulation of assembly intermediates (Smalle et al., 2002; 2003; Book et 516 al., 2009; Lee et al., 2011). The importance of 26S proteasomes to protein homeostasis is also

517 demonstrated by the fact that nearly all core subunits are encoded by at least one gene that is 518 strongly sensitive to proteotoxic stress. The presence of a non-responsive paralog likely reflects 519 sub-functionalization of the pair, which might be important for maintaining proteasome levels in 520 specific spatio-temporal contexts.

521 A second PSR cluster includes a collection of protein chaperones that help protein 522 folding and minimize protein aggregation, along with several HSF transcription factors that 523 promote their expression during stress. Their inclusion was anticipated given the central roles 524 of chaperones in protein quality control and maintaining protein homeostasis (Morimoto, 2008; 525 Hipp et al., 2014). A third PSR cluster is enriched in a collection of enzymes important for 526 cellular detoxification, such as glutathione-S-transferases, O-methyltransferases, and AOX1a, 527 the last of which is responsible for alternative respiration in mitochondria. Presumably, these 528 proteins help repair damaged proteins and reduce oxidative stress and ROS. Several 529 autophagy components are also members of the PSR, including ATG8 and the Ub receptor 530 NBR1, thus connecting autophagy to plant protein homeostasis. A role for NBR1 is in 531 agreement with studies on *nbr1* mutants; they accumulate substantial amounts of high 532 molecular mass Ub conjugates during heat stress, which possibly represent ubiquitylated 533 protein aggregates awaiting autophagic clearance (Zhou et al., 2013). Interestingly, additional 534 autophagy genes appeared in the network created upon long-term MG132 exposure (see 535 Supplemental Figure 3), suggesting that autophagy represents a last line of defense during 536 proteotoxic stress. It should also be emphasized that we discovered a number of genes within 537 the Arabidopsis PSR whose function(s) remains to be discovered. Once their purposes become 538 clear, it is likely that additional protein homeostatic mechanisms will be revealed.

539 Combined with previous studies (Yabuta et al., 2011; Nguyen et al., 2013), our network 540 and promoter interaction studies identified NAC53 and NAC78 as key regulators of the PSR. 541 Support here includes: (i) Y1H binding of NAC53 to the *PA200* and *RPN12a* promoters that 542 strongly respond to proteotoxic stress, (ii) the ability of NAC53 and NAC78 to homo- and hetero-543 dimerize, (iii) β-estradiol-induced transcription of an assortment of PSR genes in plants 544 harboring the *pEST:NAC78* transgene, and (iv) strong transcriptional attenuation of 545 representative PSR genes in plants missing both NAC53 and NAC78. The ability to dimerize is 546 consistent with other NACs that require dimerization to bind a pair of palindromically oriented 547 DNA elements (Olsen et al., 2005b). Connections of NAC53 and/or NAC78 to heat, intense 548 light, drought stress, ROS production, and senescence have also been reported based on the 549 analysis of mutants and overexpression lines (Morishita et al., 2009; Yabuta et al., 2011; Lee et 550 al., 2012; 2014). It is conceivable that these phenotypes are indirectly manifested by direct

551 participation of the pair in proteotoxic stress protection and regulation of 26S proteasome 552 synthesis.

553 As expected for plants with a dampened PSR, we found that *nac53 nac78* double 554 mutants are highly sensitive to proteasome inhibitors. Seedling growth was completely blocked 555 with 50 μM MG132 and 0.5 μM bortezomib for the null *nac53-1 nac78-1* plants, with partial 556 inhibition seen for plants harboring the weaker *nac53-2 nac78-2* alleles. A similarly modest 557 growth inhibition was seen for the *nac78-1* single mutant but not for the single *nac53-1* mutant, 558 suggesting that the NAC78 protein is more critical to PSR activation. Although the proteotoxic 559 stress conditions studied here activated expression of NAC53 more robustly than NAC78, we 560 consider it likely that they work simultaneously within the PSR given the similar temporal 561 induction seen for the *nac53-1* and *nac78-1* single mutants. Regardless of their functions, we 562 note that *nac53 nac78* double null mutants are phenotypically normal under non-stress 563 conditions and do not hyper-accumulate Ub conjugates upon MG132 treatment (see 564 Supplemental Figure 10). These observations imply that other transcription factors are 565 responsible for the basal synthesis of 26S proteasomes and other protein homeostasis 566 regulators, and that during proteotoxic stress, additional recycling pathways are engaged to 567 eliminate the excess Ub conjugates that accumulate if 26S proteasome capacity is insufficient 568 (*e.g.,* autophagy). As an aside, we note that Arabidopsis growth is more sensitive to bortezomib 569 than MG132 and thus might represent a better agent to block 26S proteasome activity *in planta*.

570 We consider it likely that NAC53 and NAC78 recognize the same *cis* element given their 571 strong amino acid sequence similarity within the NAM DNA-binding motif. Unfortunately, the 572 nature of the element is unclear. Prior studies identified the PRCE element with the consensus 573 core TGGGC sequence as the preferred NAC78 binding site (Yabuta et al., 2010; Nguyen et al., 574 2013), whereas more recent interaction studies with NAC53 and NAC78 reported that both 575 prefers MDM-type motifs (Lee et al., 2012; De Clercq et al., 2013). Understanding this 576 discrepancy will certainly require more in-depth binding studies using NAC53 and NAC78 alone 577 and in combination. Both *cis* motifs were common in many of the PSR loci and are present 578 upstream of most 26S proteasome genes. Given the close proximity of PRCE and MDM 579 sequences within proteasome subunit promoters, it is conceivable that the NAC53/NAC78 580 dimers bind both motifs simultaneously and/or work with other transcription factors that bind.

581 How NAC53 and NAC78 activate the PSR during proteotoxic stress is unclear. In yeast, 582 their functional counterpart Rpn4 participates in a simple negative feedback circuit whereby 583 Rpn4 becomes stabilized during proteotoxic stress as proteasome capacity becomes 584 overloaded. In mice and humans, its potential counterpart is Nrf1, a transmembrane-containing 585 basic leucine zipper transcription factor that resides on the ER membrane under non-stressed

586 conditions. Upon proteotoxic stress, the DNA-binding region of Nrf1 is proteolytically released 587 from the membrane-spanning segment, which then permits its nuclear import to drive 26S 588 proteasome gene expression. Given that NAC53 and NAC78, as part of the Arabidopsis NTL 589 subfamily, are also predicted to have a transmembrane domain, and that others within this 590 subfamily have been reported to use proteolytic release from cytoplasmic stores to regulate 591 their transcriptional activity (Kim et al., 2006; Kim et al., 2007), it is plausible that a shuttle 592 mechanism similar to that of Nrf1 exists. However, this proteolytic step and membrane release, 593 if they occur, might not be essential as the truncated nac53-2 protein described here (Figure 8A), 594 and an engineered truncation of NAC78 by Nguyen et al. (2013) did not appear to constitutively 595 activate the PSR even in the absence of MG132. A similar lack of effect of the C-terminal 596 truncation was seen for another NAC-NTL, NAC13, in its ability to activate transcription (De 597 Clercq et al., 2013), thus questioning the role(s) of the transmembrane domain in NTL-NAC 598 action.

599 Interestingly, the PSR is populated with transcripts for a number of other NAC 600 transcription factors besides NAC53, including NAC1, 13, 32, 44, 50, 55, 81/ATAF2, 82, and 87, 601 that are robustly up-regulated upon MG132 exposure, and/or in the *rpn10-1* and *rpn12a-1* 602 backgrounds. These factors could represent additional transcriptional regulators that assist in 603 the immediate activation of the PSR, or reflect part of a transcriptional cascade working 604 downstream that helps activate the full suite of PSR loci. In support of the former, Y2H 605 analyses of these PSR-associated NAC proteins identified NAC13 and NAC81 as 606 NAC53/NAC78 binding partners that could directly participate in PSR activation. NAC13 is 607 particularly intriguing as it, like NAC53 and NAC78, is a member of the NTL subclade, and has 608 been implicated in the transcriptional response to mitochondrial dysfunction and binding to MDM 609 sequences (De Clercq et al., 2013). Notably, a number of the gene targets of the mitochondrial 610 dysfunction response are shared with the PSR (*e.g.,* encoding UDP-glycosyl transferases and 611 AOX1a), which combined with the prevalence of MDM sequences in PSR genes, suggests that 612 the two stress responses share a number of protective functions (*e.g.,* oxidative stress defense). 613 A similar overlap between proteotoxic stress and mitochondrial dysfunction has also been 614 observed in mammals (Livnat-Levanon et al., 2014). Taking these results together, an attractive 615 hypothesis is that the PSR loci containing both PRCE and MDM elements are activated by 616 heterodimers bearing NAC13 in combination with either NAC53 or NAC78.

617 We also note that a subpopulation of PSR genes are likely immune or only weakly 618 responsive to NAC53 and NAC78 regulation (*e.g., WRKY25*), indicating that other transcription 619 factors are responsible for their activation under proteotoxic stress. One or more of these 620 factors might be found within the PSR (*e.g.,* WRKY6, 25, 33 and 45; see Supplemental Figure

621 2), or present in the list of DNA-binding proteins identified as common in our Y1H screen with 622 the *PA200* and *RPN12a* promoters (see Supplemental Table 1 and Supplemental Figure 5). 623 Consequently, it is possible that the PSR is controlled by an additional suite of transcriptional 624 regulators beyond NAC53/NAC78 that generate a multi-layered system to fully activate the 625 regulon during the various iterations of proteotoxic stress.

# **Figure 1. Both the Proteasome Inhibitor MG132 and Proteasome Mutants Increase the Accumulation of Proteasome Components and Ub Conjugates in Arabidopsis.**

Wild-type seedlings treated with 100 µM MG132 or untreated *rpn10-1* and *rpn12a-1* seedlings were grown for 5 d. Total extracts were probed by immunoblotting with the indicated antibodies, using anti-histone H3 antibodies to verify near equal protein loading.

**(A)** Levels of individual subunits of the proteasome. The open and closed arrowheads identify the unprocessed and processed forms of the β1 subunit PBA1.

**(B)** Levels of Ub conjugates. Closed arrowheads locate free Ub and poly-Ub chains assembled with varying numbers of Ub monomers. Bracket locates high molecular mass Ub conjugates.

# **Figure 2. Expression of Arabidopsis Proteasome Genes is Up-Regulated in Response to Proteasome Stress.**

**(A)** Expression of 26S proteasome subunit genes following proteasome inhibition with MG132 or in *rpn10-1* or *rpn12a-1* mutant backgrounds that compromise assembly. Total RNA from 5-dold seedlings, either untreated or treated for 3 and 24 hr with 100 µM MG132, was subjected to qRT-PCR. The expression values were calculated using the *ACT2* transcript as a reference and normalized to those from untreated wild-type (WT) seedlings. Each bar represents the average of at least three biological replicates (± SD).

**(B)** Effects of MG132 on the expression of *proteasome* promoter:*GUS* transgenes. Transgenic WT seedlings expressing the fusions were grown for 3 d, treated for 1 d with 100 µM MG132, and then incubated overnight with the X-Gluc substrate.

**(C)** Quantitative measure of *proteasome promoter:GUS* expression following MG132 treatment. Ten-d-old seedlings were incubated overnight with or without 100 µM MG132 and homogenized, and the GUS activity in the resulting cell extracts was assayed using the MUG substrate. Each bar represents the analysis of at least 30 independent T1 lines, each assayed in triplicate (± SD). The data in panels B and C for the *RPT2a* and *RPT2b promoter-GUS* fusions were reported previously and are included here for comparison (Lee et al., 2011).

### **Figure 3. Characterization of the Proteasome-Stress Regulon (PSR) in Arabidopsis.**

**(A)** Venn diagrams of transcripts that were differentially expressed significantly (p-value < 0.01, FDR < 0.05) under three proteasome-stress conditions: wild-type (WT) after a 3-hr treatment with 100 µM MG132, and the *rpn10-1* and *rpn12a-1* genetic backgrounds. The shared 119 upregulated (including *RPN10* and *RPN12a*) and 33 down-regulated genes determined by edgeR analysis comprise the PSR.

**(B)** A heat map of the PSR for the three treatments ranked by the fold-change in expression obtained with the MG132-treated seedlings. The brackets indicate genes with more or less than a two-fold increase (log2(fold-change) = 1) in expression as compared to WT. Categories of enriched gene classes are indicated (TFs = transcription factors). See Supplemental Dataset 3 online for the full list.

**(C)** Expression change heat maps of 26S proteasome subunit and assembly factor genes after proteasome stress as compared to untreated WT. EST values obtained from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR v10) are listed.

# **Figure 4. Description of the MG132 Up-regulated Gene Interaction and Co-expression Network.**

**(A)** An interaction and co-expression map of Arabidopsis proteins encoded by MG132 upregulated genes**.** The connections reflect known protein/protein interactions and co-expression data collected from the STRING database for the available 275 of the total 336 significantly upregulated genes (≥ 2-fold up as compared to untreated wild type) after a 3-hr exposure to 100 µM MG132. Classifications of major functional groups in the network are highlighted. Members of the NAC transcription factor family are in red. Portions of the map enclosed by the dashed lines are statistically enriched for genes with the denoted functional categories (DAVID, *p*-value <0.01). If available, specific gene names were used instead of the TAIR locus identifier. See Supplemental Dataset 3 for the full list.

**(B)** Members of the network that contain the consensus PRCE or MDM *cis* motifs within their promoter regions (motif-containing gene nodes are in red).

**(C)** Sequence descriptions of the PRCE and MDM sequences as determined by MEME.

**(D)** Interactome maps focusing on the statistically significant central hubs present in the proteasome, chaperone, and 'detoxification' clusters as determined by MCODE analysis, and colored based on the presence of PRCE and/or MDM sequences.

### **Figure 5. NAC53 and NAC78 Are Closely Related and Physically Interact.**

**(A)** A Bayesian phylogenetic tree of all 109 NAC proteins in *Arabidopsis* rooted to a *Physcomitrella patens* NAC protein (gi\_168025227). Nodes highlighted in red indicate the NTL subclass with a predicted transmembrane-spanning motif. The same tree with the included names for each protein is in Supplementary Figure 6 and a text file of the alignment used is presented as Supplemental Data Set 5.

**(B)** Y2H assays showing that NAC53 and NAC78 homo- and hetero-dimerize. The full-length proteins were expressed as N-terminal fusions with either the GAL4 activating (AD) or binding (BD) domains. Shown are cells grown on selective medium lacking Leu and Trp, or lacking Leu, Trp and His, and containing 50 mM 3-AT.

**(C)** Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) analysis showing that NAC53 and NAC78 homo- and hetero-dimerize *in planta* and partially localize to the nucleus. *N. benthamiana* leaf epidermal cells were co-infiltrated with plasmids expressing the N- and Cterminal fragments of YFP (nYFP and cYFP, respectively) fused to the N-terminus of NAC53 or NAC78. Shown are reconstituted BiFC signals, as detected by confocal fluorescence microscopy of leaf epidermal cells 36 hr after infiltration, along with DAPI staining of nuclei (white arrowheads) and a bright field (BF) image of the cells. Scale bar = 10 µm.

**(D)** Y2H assays testing interactions between NAC53 and NAC78 and other NAC proteins within the PSR. The assays were conducted as in panel (B) with the selective medium lacking Leu, Trp and His, and containing 25 mM 3-AT.
