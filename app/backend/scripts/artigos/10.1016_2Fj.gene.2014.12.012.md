#### ABSTRACT

Boron (B) is an essential micronutrient for optimum plant growth. However, above certain threshold B is toxic and causes yield loss in agricultural lands. While a number of studies were conducted to understand B tolerance mechanism, a transcriptome-wide approach for B tolerant barley is performed here for the first time. A highthroughput RNA-Seq (cDNA) sequencing technology (Illumina) was used with barley (Hordeum vulgare), yielding 208 million clean reads. In total, 256,874 unigenes were generated and assigned to known peptide databases: Gene Ontology (GO) (99,043), Swiss-Prot (38,266), Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) (26,250), and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (36,860), as determined by BLASTx search. According to the digital gene expression (DGE) analyses, 16% and 17% of the transcripts were found to be differentially regulated in root and leaf tissues, respectively. Most of them were involved in cell wall, stress response, membrane, protein kinase and transporter mechanisms. Some of the genes detected as highly expressed in root tissue are phospholipases, predicted divalent heavy-metal cation transporters, formin-like proteins and calmodulin/ Ca<sup>2+</sup>-binding proteins. In addition, chitin-binding lectin precursor, ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase, and serine/threonine-protein kinase AFC2 genes were indicated to be highly regulated in leaf tissue upon excess B treatment. Some pathways, such as the Ca<sup>2+</sup>-calmodulin system, are activated in response to B toxicity. The differential regulation of 10 transcripts was confirmed by qRT-PCR, revealing the tissue-specific responses against B toxicity and their putative function in B-tolerance mechanisms.

## 1. Introduction

Boron (B) is an essential micronutrient for higher plants. It is thought to be involved in different cellular mechanisms related to nucleic acids, carbohydrates and proteins, cell wall structure, integrity and metabolism of phenolic compounds (Marschner, 1995; Goldbach, 1997). For instance, B cross-links with RG-II pectin polysaccharide maintains the cell-wall integrity (O'Neill et al., 2001). On the other hand, high B concentration in plants causes chlorosis/necrosis, with drastic reductions in production and yield (Nable et al., 1997). Apart from the passive diffusion and non-selective translocation of B, an active transport mechanism was first suggested in Arabidopsis. The AtBOR1 (GenBank accession At2g47160) was discovered as the first gene related to B transport ([Takano et al., 2002](#page-10-0)) which exports boric acid from root stellar cells for xylem loading [\(Miwa et al., 2007\)](#page-9-0). Afterwards, several B importers and exporters were identified and functionally characterized under both B deficiency and excess in several organisms: in human NaBC1 (GenBank accession SLC4A11) serves as an electrogenic Na+-coupled borate transporter ([Frommer and](#page-9-0) [von Wiren, 2002; Park et al., 2004\)](#page-9-0); OsBor1 (GenBank accession Os12g37840) with its three paralogs (OsBOR2–4) transports B under deficient conditions in rice for xylem loading [\(Nakagawa et al., 2007](#page-9-0)); Atr1 (GenBank accession YML116W) and Bor1p (GenBank accession EDP05760) were discovered in Saccharomyces cerevisiae as B exporter/ transporters ([Kaya et al., 2009](#page-9-0)); Bot1 (GenBank accession EF660435), BOR1 ortholog, provides B toxicity tolerance in barley (Hordeum vulgare) landrace cv Sahara [\(Sutton et al., 2007\)](#page-10-0). Recently, VvBOR1 (GenBank accession AEZ56957), a BOR1 ortholog, was identified as a B-efflux transporter in grapevine, preferentially expressing in flowers and thus accumulating sufficient B for its reproductive system [\(Pérez-](#page-10-0)[Castro et al., 2012](#page-10-0)). Also, under B-limited conditions, NIP5;1 (GenBank accession At4g10380) and NIP6;1 (GenBank accession At1g80760) were found to be up-regulated and functioned as boric-acid channels in Arabidopsis [\(Takano et al., 2006; Tanaka and Fujiwara, 2008](#page-10-0)). Finally, a multifunctional aquaporin gene HvNIP2;1 was proposed to play a critical role upon B-tolerance ([Schnurbusch et al., 2010\)](#page-10-0).

In the case of cereals, B toxicity limits grain yield, especially in wheat and barley. Both show greater sensitivity to excess B compared to other plants ([Gupta et al., 1985](#page-9-0)). Barley is the first cultivated plant in the world and is the most produced crop in Turkey, which is ranked as the world's third largest producer with 3.5 million ha of cultivation area [\(FAO, 2005](#page-9-0)). In Turkey, 90% of barley is used as animal food and 5% is used as raw material in food, malt and beer industries. The highly boron-tolerant landrace cv Sahara 3771 of barley was identified as a potential source of germplasm for variety improvement ([Nable, 1988;](#page-9-0) [Roessner et al., 2006; Sutton et al., 2007](#page-9-0)). Restriction-fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) linkage-map analyses have been conducted for mapping the chromosome regions responsible for B-toxicity tolerance. Four quantitative-trait loci (QTL) were detected (2H, 3H, 4H and 6H) in barley Sahara cultivar, and they are associated with B uptake, rootlength response, dry-matter production and leaf-symptom expression [\(Jefferies et al., 1999\)](#page-9-0). [Hayes and Reid \(2004\)](#page-9-0) showed that B-tolerant barley Sahara roots contained lower B concentrations. Interestingly, the B-hyper-accumulator plant Gypsophila sphaerocephala showed 60 times lower concentration of B in roots than in leaves [\(Babaoglu et al.,](#page-9-0) [2004](#page-9-0)). Some efflux-type proteins were proposed to maintain low-B concentrations in roots of tolerant species and thus Bot1 gene was identified as responsible for B-toxicity tolerance [\(Sutton et al., 2007](#page-10-0)). Also, metabolomic ([Roessner et al., 2006\)](#page-10-0) and proteomic [\(Patterson et al.,](#page-9-0) [2007; Atik et al., 2011\)](#page-9-0) studies have been conducted on B-tolerant cultivars of barley. More recently, the genes encoding for some transcription factors (TF) have been proposed to be involved in the regulation of B-homeostasis in plants. They include AtWRKY6, AtMYB13, and AtMYB68 in Arabidopsis [\(Nozawa et al., 2006; Kasajima et al., 2010](#page-9-0)) and NAC (NAM, ATAF, and CUC)-like TF gene (GenBank accession Os04g0477300) in rice [\(Ochiai et al., 2011\)](#page-9-0), which are associated with B regulation mechanisms. Finally, [Tombuloglu et al. \(2013\)](#page-10-0) identified 51 R2R3-type myeloblastosis (MYB)-like TF and discussed their possible roles upon B-toxic conditions in barley (cv Sahara). Apart from these studies, [Ozhuner et al. \(2013\)](#page-9-0) proposed that some microRNAs (miRNA), particularly miRNA408, might be involved in B-toxicity tolerance. While some significant genes and proteins involved in B homeostasis are known, the overall picture corresponding to Btolerance-responsible transcriptome remains unknown.

In the present study, in order to understand the gene expression at transcriptome level, we performed a second-generation sequencing strategy to profile the barley (cv Sahara) transcripts using Illumina RNA-deep sequencing platform. Besides the full transcriptome expression analyses, nucleotide and peptide sequence information were obtained. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional classification methods were used to annotate the transcripts. In addition, the relative gene expressions were compared between the control (50 μM of B) and B-exposed (1000 μM) root and leaf samples, separately. Digital gene expressions of ten genes were confirmed with quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Eventually, we identified dozens of differentially expressed genes (DEG), related to high-B exposure in both root and leaf tissues. These genes could possibly be involved in B-toxicity tolerance mechanisms. Thus, the data reported here represents a comprehensive genetic resource for further studies in barley and other species.

#### 2. Materials and methods

#### 2.1. Plant materials and B-treatment

Seeds of B-tolerant barley (H. vulgare L. cv. Sahara) were kindly provided by Dr. Tim Sutton from Adelaide University (Australia). The seeds were germinated at room temperature. Four-day-old seedlings were transferred to hydroponic culture and grown in plant growth chamber (22 °C, with 60–70% humidity and 16-hour photoperiod). Seedlings were incubated for 10 days in hydroponics culture [5 mM NH4NO3, 5 mM KNO3, 2 mM Ca(NO3)2·4H2O, 2 mM MgSO4·7H2O, 0.1 mM KH2PO4, 0.05 mM NaFe(III)-EDTA, 50 μM H3BO3, 5 μM MnCl2·4H2O, 10 μM ZnSO4·7H2O, 0.5 μM CuSO4·5H2O and 0.1 μM Na2MoO3 at pH 5.0] ([Sutton et al., 2007\)](#page-10-0). Boron treatment was performed according to a previous study ([Hassan et al., 2010](#page-9-0)), and plants were exposed to either physiologically appropriate (50 μM) or high (1000 μM) B which is toxic level for plants ([Miwa et al., 2007; Sutton et al., 2007](#page-9-0)). To understand the transcriptome-response of B-tolerant barley, first we approached the analysis on roots and then leaves. Also, it is known that roots can limit B uptake by using activate transport mechanisms. To extract possible mechanisms involved in roots and leaves, we carried out transcriptome analysis in organs other than whole plant. After 24 h of B exposure, leaf and root of individual plants in three replicates were harvested and stored at −80 °C.

#### 2.2. Total RNA isolation

Total RNA was separately isolated from leaf and root tissues using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Extracted RNA was dissolved in RNase-free water and stored at −80 °C. RNA quality was gel verified and examined for protein (A260/A280 nm ratio), reagent and carbohydrate contaminations (A260/A230 nm ratio) (NanoDrop, ThermoScientific). A total of 1 μg of RNA sample was treated with 1 U/μl of DNase I (MBI Fermentas, Vilnius, Lithuania) in a total volume of 10 μl and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Reactions were stopped by adding 1 μl of 25 mM Na2-EDTA followed by heating at 65 °C.

#### 2.3. Library sequencing, data processing and statistical analyses

Libraries were prepared using an Illumina Gene Expression Sample Preparation Kit (San Diego, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Beads with Oligo(dT) were used to isolate poly(A) mRNA after total RNA was collected from plant tissues. Ambion Fragmentation buffer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was added for interrupting mRNA into short fragments. Taking these short fragments as templates, random hexamer–primers were used to synthesize the first-strand cDNA. The second-strand cDNA was synthesized using buffer, dNTP, RNase H and DNA polymerase I (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Short fragments were purified with a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit and resolved with elution buffer (EB) for end reparation and addition of poly(A) (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). After that, short fragments were connected with sequencing adapters. After agarose gel electrophoresis, suitable fragments were selected for the PCR amplification as templates. Finally, the libraries were sequenced using Illumina HiSeq 2000 at Beijing Genomics Institute (China).

Barley whole genome sequence published in October 2012 ([IBSC,](#page-10-0) [2012](#page-10-0)), was undertaken by the International Barley Genome Sequencing Consortium (IBSC). Since this study was conducted before the barley genome data release, we used the de novo assembly for the cDNA reads against the reference genome. First, by using the Illumina Pipeline, raw sequence reads were filtered and all low-quality, empty reads and singletons were removed. De novo transcriptome assembly was carried out with the Trinity short-read assembling program ([Grabherr et al.,](#page-9-0) [2011\)](#page-9-0). Trinity first combined the reads with certain overlap length to form longer fragments, which are called contigs. Then, the read were mapped back against the contigs; with paired-end reads they were able to detect contigs from the same transcript, as well as the distances between these contigs. Finally, Trinity connected the contigs, and generated sequences that could not be extended on either end. Such sequences are defined as unigenes of barley. They were used as a reference sequence to align and identify the sequencing reads. Statistical comparison and differentially-expressed genes between four libraries were analyzed according to the method described by [Audic and](#page-9-0) [Claverie \(1997\).](#page-9-0) We used false discovery rate (FDR) to test for the possible corrections of p-values with FDR ≤0.001 and ratios larger than 1. For the classification of transcripts by putative function, we have utilized the GO classification scheme ([Ashburner et al., 2000\)](#page-9-0). To find putative biological functions of the gene sequences, the KEGG database was used [\(Kanehisa et al., 2008\)](#page-9-0).

#### 2.4. qRT-PCR analyses

To confirm the transcriptome results, 10 highly differentiallyexpressed genes were selected for qRT-PCR analyses. Primer design and qRT-PCR protocols were applied with minor changes, according to the method stated by [Tombuloglu et al. \(2012\)](#page-10-0). The specific primers were listed in Supporting information Table S9. The results were normalized by using 18S rRNA barley gene (GenBank accession AY552749).

#### 3. Results

## 3.1. Illumina sequencing and sequence assemblies

Using Illumina paired-end sequencing technology, a total of 242,171,498 raw sequencing reads were generated from four distinct libraries (50L, 50R, 1000L, and 1000R). In our study, 256,874 unigenes were obtained according to statistics of assembly quality. Upon quality check and data cleaning, approximately 208 million high-quality reads were obtained with 94.61% to 97,15% bases showing quality more than 20 (NQ20) (Online Resource Suppl. Table 1). Totally, 493,245 contigs were assembled with an average length of 336 bases (b) (Online Resource Suppl. Table 2). Then, a total of 256,874 unigenes were obtained (Online Resource Suppl. Table 3). The total raw reads and clean reads were very similar between the four libraries. The length of unigenes ranged from 200 b to 16,071 b and 200 b to 18,825 b in root and leaf tissues respectively, with an average length of 640 b (Online Resource Suppl. Table 3). The depth of sequencing varied from 0.02 to 2,804,835,618-fold with a mean depth of 360,043-fold. Using the reads per kb per million reads (RPKM) approach, the expression of each unigene was predicted [\(Fig. 1](#page-3-0)A). According to the deep sequencing results, the total number of expressed genes obtained from the two distinct libraries was determined as 63,449 and 40,373 for root and leaf samples, respectively. In the year of 2012, barley genome sequence was announced and the number of genes in the barley genome was stated as 79,379 ([IBSC, 2012\)](#page-10-0) which is similar to that of our results. The RNA sequences have been submitted to the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database and BioProject (PRJNA188758), BioSample (SUB337351) and SRX (SRX347871) IDs were taken.

## 3.2. Functional annotation of unigenes by BLASTx against peptide databases

Sequence similarity searches were performed for validation and annotation of assembled unigenes. The non-redundant (nr) peptide databases of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), Swiss-Prot, COG (Clusters of Orthologous Groups) and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) were searched for all unigenes. According to the BLASTx results, 67,129 (77%) unigenes showed a significant homology with 57,642 nr peptides and matched 39,431 unique peptide accessions. The Swiss-Prot database homology search found 38,266 unigenes (43.9%) matched with 33,376 accessions. The distribution of species for the best match results of each sequence and homology of Swiss-Prot rates are shown in [Fig. 1B](#page-3-0) and C. The sequences matched with the species were determined as following: 33.4% H. vulgare, 27% Oryza sativa, 21% Triticum aestivum, 6% Zea mays and 5.5% Phyllostachys edulis [\(Fig. 1](#page-3-0)B). The homology of distribution analysis was found to be comparable, with 17% of the sequences having a similarity higher than 60% [\(Fig. 1C](#page-3-0)).

## 3.3. Functional classification of unigenes by COG, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment

A total of 26,250 assembled unigenes were aligned and assigned to the appropriate COG clusters, being grouped into 24 functional categories [\(Fig. 1](#page-3-0)D, Online Resource Suppl. Table 4). The five most extended categories were determined as 'general function' (12.3%), 'translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis' (10.6%), 'unknown function' (9.4%), 'transcription' (9.2%), and 'replication, recombination and repair' (7.1%). The abundances of glycine-rich cell wall structural proteins, protein chain release-factor B, cell-wall proteins, transcription factors, and histone acetyltransferase genes were found to be high.

According to the GO classification system, the predicted genes and gene products were functionally grouped ([Conesa et al., 2005](#page-9-0)). Based on the sequence homology analyses, 99,043 sequences were categorized into 45 functional groups [\(Fig. 1E](#page-3-0)). The functions of the assembled unigenes were categorized as biological process (30,027 unigenes, 30.3%), cellular component (49,526 unigenes, 50%) and molecular function (19,490 unigenes, 19.7%) ([Fig. 1](#page-3-0)E). In each of these categories, the major subcategories were as follows: six for biological process (metabolic process, cellular process, response to stimulus, localization, establishment of localization and biological regulation); four for cellular component (cell, cell part, organelle and macromolecular complex); and three for molecular function (catalytic activity, binding and transporter activity). Only a few genes were clustered in terms of 'biological adhesion', 'locomotion', 'nitrogen utilization', 'pigmentation', 'rhythmic process', 'viral reproduction', 'cell junction', 'extracellular region part', 'protein binding transcription factor activity', and 'translation regulator activity' ([Fig. 1](#page-3-0)E). Thus, the results revealed that most of the sequenced barley cDNA was related with genes involved in the regulation of biological processes and cellular components.

## 4. Discussion

## 4.1. Identification and annotation of differentially-expressed genes

In this study, we focused on differentially expressed genes which may have the potential to gain B-tolerance. Most of those genes were related to defense and stress mechanisms. For instance, phospholipase transcripts were highly accumulated upon B-induction. Likewise, previous study conducted by [Hassan \(2007\)](#page-9-0) demonstrated lipase activity in B-stressed barley. It is known that lipases have role in jasmonic acid (JA)-dependent signaling [\(Jakab et al., 2003](#page-9-0)) and stress responses. Another highly up-regulated gene in tolerant barley was a formin like protein. Formins could play a role in cytoskeleton, plasma membrane and cell-wall linkages related to defense responses [\(Cushman, 2001\)](#page-9-0). Additionally, peroxidases are involved in oxidative-stress responses. In an agreement with this, we found that the expression level of peroxidase was strongly induced by excess B. The other differentially-expressed gene encodes the multi-antimicrobial extrusion (MATE)-efflux family protein, which is a member of membrane-localized efflux-protein family, and is involved in the removal of multi-drug and toxic compound from the cells ([Rogers and Guerinot, 2002](#page-10-0)).

Yet another highly differentially-expressed gene was calmodulin/ Ca2+-binding protein, which was previously reported as a key player in various types of abiotic stresses [\(Knight, 1999\)](#page-9-0). The Ca2+-calmodulin system is also involved in responses to heavy-metal toxicity. In this study, it was demonstrated that the expression of Ca2+-calmodulin (Unigene15346\_All) and Ca2+-binding protein (CL37120.Contig1\_All) transcripts were significantly induced due to high B concentrations. In animal models, it has been suggested that B interacts with other nutrients and plays a regulatory role in the metabolism of some minerals, such as calcium [\(Eckhert et al., 2007](#page-9-0)). However, the reports are limited with animals, and not much is available for plants. In plants, cytosolic calcium concentration changes with drought and salinity stresses ([Knight et al., 1997;](#page-9-0) [Dodd et al., 2010](#page-9-0)). Recently, [Yuan et al. \(2014\)](#page-10-0) reported a protein, OSCA1, which mediates osmotic stress-dependent Ca+2 releases in Arabidopsis. In our study, B-induced expression of Ca+2–Calmodulin genes was highly up-regulated. Thus, Ca+2-signaling, and -related mechanisms may be important as a potential target for further studies. In addition, many stressand cell-wall mechanisms-related genes were up-regulated upon high levels of B (1000 μM). For instance, the phospholipases, serine/ threonine-protein kinases, NADH-flavin oxidoreductases, peroxidase and MATE-efflux family proteins were found to be remarkably accumulated during B-excess condition.

In leaves, some of the most-annotated differentially-expressed genes are the ones encoding the chitin-binding lectin precursor, ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase, serine/threonine-protein kinase AFC2, cytochrome P450 and Methionine s-methyltransferase (Online Resource Suppl. Table 3b). All of these genes were strongly induced by B-excess stress. Chitin-binding lectin precursor may be involved in cell-wall structure and it is mediated to stress responses [\(Millar et al.,](#page-9-0) [1992\)](#page-9-0). Another protein is a serine/threonine-protein kinase, which is involved in the regulation of metabolism, development, and stress responses ([Gong et al., 2004\)](#page-9-0). Eventually, these genes are involved in defense-related cell-wall modifications/reinforcements. Also, several genes belonging to the cytochrome-P450 family were detected in barley exposed to B excess, and two of them were included in the previously mentioned in top-50 list. The expressions of these genes were strongly induced by B-excess stress. Cytochrome P450 is a large protein family in animals, plants, fungi, and bacteria. ## 4.2. Analyses of GO functional categories and KEGG pathways

The Gene Ontology functional analyses were carried out to predict the B-excess-responsive genes in barley. The GO classification enhances our understanding of gene classification in terms of their associated biological processes, cellular components and molecular functions [\(Conesa et al., 2005](#page-9-0)). A total of 12,709, 16,874, and 16,368 genes belonging to biological process, cellular component and molecular function categories were identified from the 50 μM B and 1000 μM B transcriptome libraries (Online Resource Suppl. Table 6). In roots, most of the genes in the category of biological process were sub-grouped into gene expression, protein metabolic process and response to stimulus. According to GO term analyses, related to B-excess-induced gene expression, protein metabolic process, response to stimulus, response to stress, programmed cell-death, response to hormone stimulus, response to osmotic stress and cell wall organization subgroups were mainly enhanced with DEG in biological processes of B-tolerant barley roots. In obvious manner, the Ca2+-calmodulin system, MATE-efflux proteins and transcription factors were found to be related with stress responses. Thus, the most-annotated differentially-expressed genes were confirmed by GO analyses. The other major gene-ontology subgroups relevant to B-excess stress were related to cellular component membrane, molecular-function protein kinase activity and antioxidant activity.

In leaves, protein metabolic process, nitrogen compound metabolic process and establishment of localization were the major subgroups involving most of the induced genes. Meanwhile, response to stress was the most remarkable subgroup in both tissues. Besides, in the cellularcomponent classification, membrane-related genes were considerably enriched among the transcripts. And also, genes involved in the protein-kinase-activity were highly found in the "molecular function" group for both root and leaf tissues. Besides this, the most-annotated and most differentially-expressed top-50 genes were confirmed with the GO analysis results. For instance, in the current study, the induction of the gene encoding phospholipase enzyme was enhanced by B-excess stress. As in the case of previous studies, lipases have been related to hormone signaling ([Jakab et al., 2003\)](#page-9-0). Indeed, we have found in the GO term analyses that they were related to hormone stimuli responses. Also, genes encoding the formin-like proteins were the other most remarkable DEG, and they could play role in cytoskeleton, plasma membrane and cell wall linkages. In our GO term analysis, they were subgrouped into cytoskeleton and membrane in cellular component, as well as into cell wall organization or biogenesis in biological process. Another significant difference was found in the serine/threonineprotein kinase regulation. They are related to regulation of metabolism, development and stress responses ([Gong et al., 2004](#page-9-0)). In the GO term analyses, they were also significantly enriched, suggesting that serine/ threonine-protein kinases were activated during B-excess stress and could be important in the regulation of B-excess tolerance mechanisms. Similarly, various highly-annotated genes were confirmed in the significantly-enriched GO terms.

To better understand the function of sequenced genes, KEGG pathway analysis was used. KEGG is a database this is analyzing the gene products during metabolism processes and related gene-functions in the cellular processes. Pathways with p-values b0.05 and q-values b0.05 indicate that they are significantly enriched in differentiallyexpressed genes. By doing so, we identified 5939 and 6669 DEG from the two libraries (50 μM B/1000 μM B for root; and 50 μM B/1000 μM B for leaf). Our KEGG-enrichment analyses showed that in root tissue, the most significant pathway groups were related to ribosome (13%), plant–pathogen interaction (7.4%), spliceosome (6.8%) and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (3.2%). On the other hand, in leaves, they were involved in metabolic pathway (31.5%), RNA transport (19.4%), mRNA surveillance pathway (16.5%) and glycerophospholipid metabolism (12.6%).

The KEGG mapping indicated that 771 genes were altered in ribosome and 55 genes in ABC-transporter pathways (Online Resource Suppl. Table 7). The ABC-transporter genes were also differentially expressed in roots and leaves under B-excess stress. Spliceosome was the other enriched KEGG pathway in B-tolerant barley roots. Thus, the stress-induced changes influenced alternative splicing of target transcripts. In leaves, the most remarkable enrichment was seen in the metabolic pathways (31.5%). Also, the glycerophospholipid metabolism, functional in the plasma membrane, was another important pathway in leaves. (Online Resource Suppl. Table 7). Additionally, numerous genes altered in the membrane were also highly annotated.

## 4.3. Barley transcriptome has eight constitutive and B-independent Bot1 copies

The Bot1 (GenBank accession EF660435) gene has been previously reported to provide B-toxicity tolerance in barley ([Sutton et al., 2007](#page-10-0)). Its expression was found ~160- and 18-fold higher in roots and leaf blades of the Sahara B-tolerant cultivar, respectively, as compared to the Clipper B-sensitive one and also its over-expression conferred Btolerance to yeast [\(Sutton et al., 2007](#page-10-0)). In our study, eight sequences were annotated as corresponding to the Bot1 gene with a significant identity. As suggested by the Bot1 discoverers [\(Sutton et al., 2007\)](#page-10-0), the gene expansions might have contributed to the increased Bot1 transcript levels that have been observed in B-tolerant barley. Yet, as in the case of the previous report ([Sutton et al., 2007](#page-10-0)), both roots and leaf transcript levels of Bot1 were not dramatically changed by toxic B exposures. Interestingly, in our study the transcripts decreased about three to four times for all Bot1 paralogs (except CL15116 and CL27458) in the leaf samples, instead of the expected increases (Fig. 5), which is consistent with the previous results. Sequence analyses of those genes revealed high identity (N80). Interestingly, it was observed that their expression did not seem to be B-excess dependent. As it was shown in Fig. 5, they were consistently accumulated both in control (50 μM) and B-stressed (1000 μM) plants. Thus, we propose that the Bot1, Bexcess tolerance-responsible gene has seven duplicated paralogs in the barley genome and their expressions are not B-excess dependent. We assume that these genes are paralogs and contribute to the Btoxicity tolerance with consistent co-segregation. However, further research is needed to determine their functional identity and precise roles in B-excess toxicity tolerance.
