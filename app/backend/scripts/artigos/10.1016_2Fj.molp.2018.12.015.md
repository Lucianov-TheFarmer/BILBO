# ABSTRACT

Gene expression variation is a key component underlying phenotypic variation and heterosis. Transcriptome profiling was performed on 23 different tissues or developmental stages of two maize inbreds, B73 and Mo17, as well as their  $F_1$  hybrid. The obtained large-scale datasets provided opportunities to monitor the developmental dynamics of differential expression, additivity for gene expression, and regulatory variation. The transcriptome can be divided into  $\sim$ 30 000 genes that are expressed in at least one tissue of one inbred and an additional  $\sim$ 10 000 "silent" genes that are not expressed in any tissue of any genotype, 90% of which are non-syntenic relative to other grasses. Many ( $\sim$ 74%) of the expressed genes exhibit differential expression in at least one tissue. However, the majority of genes with differential expression do not exhibit consistent differential expression in different tissues. These genes often exhibit tissue-specific differential expression with equivalent expression in other tissues, and in many cases they switch the directionality of differential expression in different tissues. This suggests widespread variation for tissue-specific regulation of gene expression between the two maize inbreds B73 and Mo17. Nearly 5000 genes are expressed in only one parent in at least one tissue (single parent expression) and 97% of these genes are expressed at mid-parent levels or higher in the hybrid, providing extensive opportunities for hybrid complementation in heterosis. In general, additive expression patterns are much more common than non-additive patterns, and this trend is more pronounced for genes with strong differential expression or single parent expression. There is relatively little evidence for non-additive expression patterns that are maintained in multiple tissues. The analysis of allele-specific expression allowed classification of cis- and trans-regulatory variation. Genes with cis-regulatory variation often exhibit additive expression and tend to have more consistent regulatory variation throughout development. In contrast, genes with trans-regulatory variation are enriched for non-additive patterns and often show tissue-specific differential expression. Taken together, this study provides a deeper understanding of regulatory variation and the degree of additive gene expression throughout maize development. The dynamic nature of differential expression, additivity, and regulatory variation imply abundant variability for tissue-specific regulatory mechanisms and suggest that connections between transcriptome and phenome will require expression data from multiple tissues.

**Key words:** maize, gene expression, heterosis, differential expression, non-additive expression, allele-specific expression

# INTRODUCTION

Plant breeding generally relies upon the variation found among individuals of the same species. Crosses between genetically distinct individuals provide opportunities to combine variation, and progeny can be subjected to selection to drive shifts toward favorable traits. Importantly, in many species the combination of the variation present in two parents can also lead to heterosis in the F1 offspring. Heterosis refers to phenotypic performance of the F1 that is outside the range of the parents. While heterosis is widely utilized in maize and other plant species, we are still seeking to understand the contribution of mechanisms through which the molecular variation present in two parents combines to determine hybrid performance.

An important link between DNA sequence variation and phenotypic variation is gene expression variation. Gene expression differences among individuals of the same species can drive phenotypic variation ([Belting et al., 1998; Wang et al., 1999;](#page-13-0) [Frary et al., 2000; Schadt et al., 2003; Chen et al., 2005;](#page-13-0) [Cookson et al., 2009](#page-13-0)). Transcriptome profiling has been used to characterize gene expression variation in maize ([Guo et al.,](#page-13-1) [2006; Stupar and Springer, 2006; Swanson-Wagner](#page-13-1) [et al., 2006; Meyer et al., 2007; Stupar et al., 2008; Hansey](#page-13-1) [et al., 2012; Hirsch et al., 2014; Waters et al., 2017;](#page-13-1) [Baldauf et al., 2018; Kremling et al., 2018\)](#page-13-1). In many cases >10% of expressed genes are differentially expressed in a comparison of two distinct inbreds ([Stupar and Springer, 2006](#page-14-0)). These differences in expression include many quantitative differences in which there is detectable expression in both parents. There are also examples in which there is expression in only one of the two lines being compared [\(Hansey et al.,](#page-13-2) [2012; Paschold et al., 2014; Marcon et al., 2017; Baldauf et al.,](#page-13-2) [2018\)](#page-13-2) sometimes referred to as single parent expression (SPE). These examples of SPE could reflect differences in genome content or differences in regulation of shared genes. Interestingly, the genes that exhibit SPE are typically expressed at mid-parent levels in the hybrid, resulting in an increase in the numbers of genes expressed in the hybrid as they express the set of genes expressed in either of the parents ([Paschold et al.,](#page-14-1) [2012, 2014; Schnable and Springer, 2013; Baldauf et al., 2018](#page-14-1)).

Comparative genomics can be used to classify maize genes as syntenic or non-syntenic based on comparisons with the genome structure and content of related species such as sorghum [\(Schnable et al., 2012; Schnable, 2015\)](#page-14-2). In general, many maize genes defined by mutational analyses or phenotypic changes are syntenic relative to other grass species ([Schnable and](#page-14-3) [Freeling, 2011](#page-14-3)). Many of the genes that exhibit SPE are nonsyntenic [\(Paschold et al., 2014; Baldauf et al., 2016, 2018](#page-14-4)), and the contribution of these genes to phenotypic variation is unclear.

The differences in gene expression between different inbred lines can be the result of *cis*-acting regulatory variation that are due to linked polymorphisms (allele specific and local to the regulated gene) or *trans*-acting changes that are due to diffusible products not necessarily linked with the target gene (in diploids, affecting both alleles equally). The relative contribution of *cis*and *trans*-acting regulatory changes can be investigated using allele-specific expression (ASE) analysis in the F1 relative to parental ratios ([Wittkopp et al., 2004\)](#page-15-0) or through expression

Both of these approaches have been used to investigate the sources of variable expression in maize. ASE assays for targeted sets of genes [\(Stupar and Springer,](#page-14-0) [2006; Springer and Stupar, 2007b\)](#page-14-0) or genome-wide surveys ([Lemmon et al., 2014; Waters et al., 2017](#page-13-4)) have revealed significant numbers of *cis*-regulatory variants that can lead to large differences in gene expression levels. The analysis of gene expression levels in recombinant inbred lines has been used to identify eQTL in maize ([Schadt et al., 2003; Swanson-](#page-14-5)[Wagner et al., 2009; Holloway et al., 2011; Li et al., 2013](#page-14-5)). Many examples of *cis*- and *trans*-acting eQTL have been documented in these studies. In general, within a population, *trans*regulatory effects are more common than *cis* regulation, but have more subtle effects on gene expression levels ([Wittkopp](#page-15-0) [et al., 2004; Gruber et al., 2012; Metzger et al., 2016](#page-15-0)), while large differences in expression levels are primarily maintained by *cis*-regulatory variation ([Stupar and Springer, 2006; Li et al.,](#page-14-0) [2013](#page-14-0)).

A number of studies have investigated the extent and sources of variable gene expression among maize genotypes. These have included comparisons of parental lines ([Stupar et al., 2008\)](#page-14-6), allele-specific regulatory studies [\(Guo et al., 2004; Stupar and](#page-13-5) [Springer, 2006; Springer and Stupar, 2007b; Hawkins et al.,](#page-13-5) [2014; Lemmon et al., 2014; Aguilar-Rangel et al., 2017\)](#page-13-5), eQTL analyses ([Holloway et al., 2011; Li et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2017\)](#page-13-6), and studies of additivity for gene expression in hybrids relative to parents ([Swanson-Wagner et al., 2006; Paschold et al., 2012;](#page-14-7) [Baldauf et al., 2016, 2018](#page-14-7)). In general, these studies have largely focused on a single tissue or a small set of tissues, and it has not been possible to monitor how dynamic the patterns of differential expression, additivity, or regulatory variation are throughout development. This is an important topic for several reasons. First, one goal of many transcriptomic studies is to connect gene expression with phenotype. However, it is quite difficult to determine the appropriate tissue to be profiled for many phenotypes. For example, yield is affected by metabolism and growth patterns in many different tissues across many different stages. It is not clear which tissues are most closely associated with yield. Even for a simpler trait such as plant height, it is not clear which stage and set of tissues are most important for sampling. Therefore, many researchers often rely on transcriptome data from a single tissue and attempt to associate expression with traits (such as seedling to model juvenile to adult vegetative transition; [Hirsch et al.,](#page-13-7) [2014](#page-13-7)). This study provides a framework for considering how conserved or dynamic the patterns of transcriptome variability are throughout maize development. Another goal of transcriptome analyses in maize inbreds and hybrids has been to associate the patterns or properties of the transcriptome with heterosis. There are rare examples of ''overdominant'' (above high-parent [AHP] or below low-parent [BLP]) expression that have been documented ([Swanson-Wagner et al., 2006;](#page-14-7) [Stupar et al., 2008; Paschold et al., 2012\)](#page-14-7). However, it is not clear whether these patterns would be reproducible in multiple tissues or whether they might simply be outliers due to the large set of genes being studied. In this study we have generated transcriptome data for two parental lines and the F1 from 23 diverse tissues or developmental stages. The patterns of differential expression and gene action are quite dynamic across different developmental stages and tissues. # RESULTS

## Transcriptome Profiling of Diverse Tissues of B73, Mo17, and their F1 Hybrid

The generation of transcriptomes for matched tissue samples (note that we refer to the samples as tissues although in some cases these samples represent complex organs comprised of multiple cell types) from numerous developmental stages in two inbreds and their F1 hybrid provided an opportunity to study the developmental dynamics of variable expression, hybrid-specific patterns, additivity and allelic regulatory variation in maize [\(Figure 1A](#page-2-0)). A total of 207 maize samples were collected for RNA-seq profiling, including 23 distinct tissues in three genotypes (B73, Mo17, and B733Mo17), each with three biological replicates ([Figure 1](#page-2-0)A and [Supplemental Table 1](#page-12-0)). These tissues included different portions of the maize leaf or stem, samples of specific tissues from the male or female inflorescence, and different sections of root, as well as embryo and endosperm samples [\(Supplemental Table 1](#page-12-0)). Tissues at the same developmental stage were harvested from all three genotypes on the same day using plants sown on different dates to ensure that there were no environmental differences among genotypes due to sampling times. It should be noted, however, that there could be subtle environmental differences due to the fact that we are sampling plots located in different regions of the field. Biological replicates were collected on different dates to allow for day-to-day variation within each genotype/tissue to be represented (see [Methods\)](#page-11-0). Approximately 10–15 million read pairs were obtained for each sample [\(Supplemental Table 1](#page-12-0)

### Differentially Expressed Genes between Parents

We identified differentially expressed (DE) genes between B73 and Mo17 for each tissue and characterized the relative frequency of conserved or variable differences in expression across tissues. There are 3944–9381 DE genes between B73 and Mo17 per tissue, including genes with higher expression in either B73 or Mo17 ([Figure 2A](#page-4-0) and [Methods\)](#page-11-0). A large fraction (22.5%–54.7% depending on tissue) of the DE genes shows modest levels of expression change (i.e., less than 2-fold change), while a subset of genes show much greater levels of difference in transcript abundance [\(Figure 2A](#page-4-0)). Many genes are only classified as DE in a small number of tissues, but a subset of the genes exhibit DE in many different tissues [\(Figure 2](#page-4-0)B and 2D). There are 26 301 genes that exhibit DE in at least one tissue although many of these genes are only DE in a handful of tissues ([Figure 2](#page-4-0)B). Among all 19 819 genes with differential expression in multiple (R3) tissues, 19.6% and 21.6% show consistently higher expression in B73 or Mo17, respectively. The remaining 58.8% of genes exhibit mixed directionality of DE such that B73 has higher expression for some tissues while Mo17 exhibits higher expression in other tissues ([Figure 2](#page-4-0)B). This observation suggests varied tissue-specific regulation for many of these genes rather than differences in basal promoter strength. ## Gene Expression Levels Are Largely Additive with Rare Tissue-Specific Non-additivity

The availability of RNA-seq data for the F1 hybrid plants enabled two additional sets of analyses of expression, additivity analysis of the relative levels of expression in the F1 compared with parents, and ASE for *cis*/*trans* regulatory variation analyses. Additive expression refers to cases in which the F1 expression levels are not significantly different from the average of the two parents while non-additive expression occurs when the hybrid expression deviates significantly from mid-parent value. Since additivity/non-additivity could reflect different properties for genes that are differentially expressed between two parents and those that are not, we took two different approaches to assess the additivity and/or dominant/recessive patterns of hybrid expression depending on whether the two parents exhibit DE for the assessed gene [\(Figure 1A](#page-2-0) and [Methods\)](#page-11-0).

An analysis of additivity for genes that are not DE between the parents identified some examples of non-additivity, but these were primarily genes with moderate levels of differential expression and were quite tissue specific ([Supplemental Figure 7](#page-12-0)). ### Identifying cis- and trans-Regulatory Genes

The high single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) density between B73 and Mo17 ([Gore et al., 2009; Waters et al., 2013, 2017](#page-13-12)) provides an opportunity to monitor the relative expression of the B73 and Mo17 alleles for many genes in their F1 hybrid. ASE could be monitored (>30 reads assigned to a specific allele) for 55%–69% (11 068–16 013) of the expressed genes in each tissue ([Supplemental Figure 10\)](#page-12-0). The combined analysis of relative expression levels in the two parents and allelic expression ratios in the F1 can be used to infer the sources of regulatory variation ([Wittkopp et al., 2004; Springer and Stupar,](#page-15-0) [2007b](#page-15-0)). Genes that are differentially expressed between the parents will have unbalanced parental ratios [\(Figure 4A](#page-7-0)). If the allelic ratio in the F1 is similar to the parental proportions, it is likely that the differential expression in the parents is due to *cis*-acting differences that lead to different transcript abundance of the two alleles (''*cis*-only''). This would include changes to *cis*-regulatory sequences affecting transcription as well as sequence differences that might influence transcript stability. In contrast, some genes show differential transcript abundance in the parents but the two alleles are expressed at similar levels in the F1 (''*trans*-only''). There are some genes with intermediate levels that are classified as having both *cis*and *trans*-acting influences (''*cis* + *trans*: opposite direction'') as well as some genes that have even greater bias in the F1 than predicted by the parents (''*cis* + *trans*: same direction''). There is also a subset of genes that have allelic bias in the F1 that is in the opposite direction to the bias of the parental expression (''unexpected bias''). The distribution of these classifications is shown for root tissue in [Figure 4](#page-7-0)A, and there are relatively similar distributions in the other tissues ([Supplemental](#page-12-0) [Figure 11A\)](#page-12-0). We also assessed the allelic expression for genes showing similar levels of expression in the parents [\(Figure 4B](#page-7-0)). The majority of non-DE genes were assigned an ''unbiased'' pattern whereby the two alleles in the hybrid do not exhibit significant bias. However, we do find examples of biased (either B73-biased or Mo17-biased) allelic expression even though the transcript abundance levels are similar in the parents ([Figure 4](#page-7-0)B and [Supplemental Figure 12](#page-12-0)A). # DISCUSSION

There are examples of developmental transcriptome atlases for many plant species ([Schmid et al., 2005; Libault et al., 2010;](#page-14-10) [Wang et al., 2010; Sekhon et al., 2011; Stelpflug et al., 2016;](#page-14-10) [Walley et al., 2016; Palovaara et al., 2017](#page-14-10)). These have been useful for documenting the tissue-specific patterns of gene expression and have been widely used to construct coexpression networks ([Swanson-Wagner et al., 2012; Downs](#page-15-2) [et al., 2013; Schaefer et al., 2014; Walley et al., 2016; Huang](#page-15-2) [et al., 2017\)](#page-15-2). However, there are no datasets that have provided comparable developmental profiles for multiple genotypes. The transcriptome dataset described here provides many insights into the dynamic nature of gene expression variation within maize.

One key observation in this work is the differences in expression, and variable expression, for syntenic and non-syntenic genes. The set of genes that have collinear orthologs in rice and sorghum (syntenic genes) are much more likely to be expressed and are depleted for variable expression. Approximately 40% of the gene models in the maize reference genome annotation are not syntenically conserved to any other grass genomes, including sorghum and rice [\(Schnable and Freeling,](#page-14-3) [2011; Freeling et al., 2015; Cheng et al., 2018](#page-14-3)). Examples of structural variation such as copy-number variation or presence-absence variation (PAV) are much more prevalent at non-syntenic genes than at syntenic genes ([Swanson-Wagner](#page-14-11) [et al., 2010; Schnable et al., 2011; Brohammer et al., 2017\)](#page-14-11). A detailed analysis of the maize proteome reveals that nonsyntenic genes are much less likely to produce detectable levels of protein [\(Walley et al., 2016\)](#page-15-3). There are also substantial differences in the profile of chromatin modifications at nonsyntenic genes ([Eichten et al., 2011](#page-13-13)). The analysis of the causal loci behind classical mutant screens finds very few examples on non-syntenic genes with mutant phenotypes ([Schnable and Freeling, 2011](#page-14-3)). It has thus been suggested that most of the non-syntenic genes have small effects or no effect on phenotype and fitness ([Schnable, 2015](#page-14-12)). It is worth noting that there certainly are some non-syntenic genes that produce proteins [\(Walley et al., 2016\)](#page-15-3) and there will be some examples of non-syntenic genes that influence phenotypes but, on the whole, this set of genes likely includes a large number of pseudogenes or genes with redundant function. In this study we find that non-syntenic genes are several times more likely to be transcriptionally silent than syntenic genes. For the set of non-syntenic genes that are transcriptionally active we find enrichments for DE and SPE patterns. It is worth highlighting how different some of the patterns would be if we had focused analyses only on syntenic genes. We are not suggesting that this should be a preferred approach, but rather highlight the fact that differences in annotation approaches in different species could lead to differences in rates of differential expression or other patterns due to the inclusion, or exclusion, of non-syntenic genes.

We were struck by the variability of differential expression and regulatory variation among different tissues. Given the

substantial role of *cis*-regulatory variation ([Stupar and Springer,](#page-14-0) [2006; Li et al., 2013](#page-14-0)) we had expected higher levels of conservation of differential expression in tissues that had expression of a gene. However, we find many examples of genes that are expressed in many tissues but only show differential expression in a subset of these tissues. Even for tissues with differential expression we find that 46% of DE genes had at least one tissue with DE in the direction of Mo17 and at least one tissue with DE in the direction of B73. This mixture of effects is also seen for 5.7% of the SPE genes. The analysis of ASE also reveals substantial variability for *cis*- and *trans*-regulatory variation in different tissues. This dynamic variability for gene expression in different tissues has several important implications. First, this highlights the extensive role of regulatory variation in leading to differential expression. Examples of varied expression that are due to PAV of the gene itself or differences in the basal promoter (such as indels) would be expected to lead to differences in transcript abundance in all tissues with expression of the gene. While these undoubtedly exist, they seem to only account for a minority of the examples of differential expression (i.e., 4.3% of SPE genes). Second, the variability of differential expression and frequent mixed effects of the two genotypes in different tissues will lead to major complexities in linking varied expression from a single tissue to trait predictions. Many studies have attempted to connect transcriptome variation with phenotypes, with varying levels of success. While it is clear that there is predictive power in transcriptome data, it could be that much of this power is based on using *cis*-regulated expression variation as ''markers'' to tag a genomic region rather than having expression level per se leading to phenotypic consequences. It can be difficult to precisely match a single tissue with a quantitative phenotype. If the differences in gene expression are stable throughout development, the transcriptional variation from one tissue can be used to predict traits in multiple tissues. However, our observations reveal substantial variability in differential expression, additivity, and regulatory variation among tissues. This suggests that it will be important to consider transcriptome data from multiple tissues when predicting traits. This also implies that the bulk of variability in gene expression is due to variation in tissue-specific regulation rather than differences in basal promoter strength. It will be critical to develop prediction models that utilize expression from multiple tissues to predict traits. Third, the observation of many genes with mixed differential expression with higher levels for B73 in some tissues and Mo17 in other tissues has potential implications for heterosis. The concepts of dominance and overdominance have been widely considered for heterosis ([Hochholdinger and Hoecker, 2007; Lippman and Zamir, 2007;](#page-13-14) [Birchler et al., 2010\)](#page-13-14). It has been difficult to consider how overdominance may operate at the molecular level. [Guo et al.](#page-13-1) [\(2006\)](#page-13-1) suggested that two alleles may have different patterns of expression during development or in response to environmental conditions. This could lead to cases in which the full pattern of expression was only exhibited when both alleles are present. This would suggest that neither allele has the full potential present within the two alleles and could be a potential explanation for overdominance at the molecular level. In this study we find that these types of expression patterns are quite prevalent, and for many genes the full

pattern of developmental expression is only seen in the hybrid containing both alleles.

Any analysis of gene expression in two maize inbreds and the F1 hybrid can be considered from the perspective of heterosis. Heterosis, the superior performance of the F1 relative to the parents, is prevalent for many traits and crosses in maize ([Flint-Garcia et al., 2009\)](#page-13-15). Many studies have examined the patterns of expression in hybrids relative to the parents for specific tissues ([Schnable and Springer, 2013\)](#page-14-13). Given the unique phenotype of the F1, it is somewhat surprising that additive expression patterns are quite prevalent in maize hybrids with relatively rare non-additive expression and very few examples of expression significantly outside the parental range. Several potential explanations for this have been proposed. One suggestion, the ''Goldilocks hypothesis,'' suggests that perhaps the additive level of expression is advantageous ([Springer and Stupar, 2007a\)](#page-14-14). This would posit that one parent has expression that is too high and one parent has expression that is too low, with the hybrid being just right. A second hypothesis suggests that differences in presence and absence of transcripts between lines is complemented in the F1 [\(Paschold et al., 2012](#page-14-1)). Many genes exhibit SPE but the vast majority of these genes are present at additive levels in the F1 hybrid. This leads to a situation whereby hybrid transcriptomes include more genes than are present in either parent and exhibit substantial potential complementation. In this study we find many examples of SPE that are also expressed in the F1 hybrid at additive levels. By assessing SPE patterns across many different tissues we find that there are nearly 5000 genes that exhibit SPE patterns in at least one tissue. This accounts for 16% of all expressed genes and suggests widespread potential for hybrid complementation. A third model has proposed that hybrids can selectively utilize the best (biochemically most stable) allele for a given environment, resulting in higher efficiency of protein folding and degradation in the hybrids ([Goff, 2011;](#page-13-16) [Goff and Zhang, 2013](#page-13-16)). This hypothesis would predict that hybrids could selectively express, stabilize, or translate the transcripts of a preferred allele in the F1 hybrid. One prediction of this hypothesis is that the F1 would exhibit biased allelic ratios for an appreciable number of genes that have similar abundance in the two parents. Indeed, among the set of genes that do not exhibit DE between parents we do find a number of examples with biased allele abundance (30%). We do not have evidence to assess whether this bias is also observed for protein abundance or whether the allele with higher abundance has higher biochemical stability and folding efficiency ([Goff and Zhang, 2013\)](#page-13-17). Future experiments of proteome profiling and enzymatic assays will be needed to document further support for the biased allele usage hypothesis.

This study provides a rich dataset for considering variable expression, regulation, and additivity throughout maize development. An increased understanding of the dynamic nature of expression patterns and variability could lead to the development of improved models for connecting the transcriptome to phenotype. In addition, this study provides a fruitful dataset for considering potential models for heterosis at the molecular level.

## Plant Material

Plant materials were grown in the field for collection of 17 adult tissues and in the lab for collection of six seedling tissues (sampling location for each tissue is noted in [Supplemental Table 1](#page-12-0)). For field sampling B73, Mo17, and B73xMo17 seeds were planted every 3 days for a period of 3 weeks in May of 2016 in St Paul, Minnesota. For a single replicate of a specific tissue we identified plants for each of the three genotypes that were at the same vegetative or reproductive stage and sampled on the same day, but not necessarily from the same planting date due to differences in developmental rates of these genotypes. This resulted in sampling plants from slightly different regions of the field, which could introduce some variation between samples not necessarily due to genotype. Tissue from at least four plants was pooled for each of the replicates and the three biological replicates were sampled on different dates. Tissues were all collected between 10 and 11 a.m. For seedling tissues, seeds were imbibed for 2 days in distilled water and then manually examined to select uniformly germinating seedlings based on radical emergence and the lack of mold. Seedlings were grown in growth chambers at 25C in continuous dark for etiolated tissues and under 12:12-h light/dark cycles for collection of green tissues. A total of 207 samples were collected for profiling: three biological replicates 3 23 distinct tissues 3 3 genotypes (B73, Mo17, and B73xMo17). Two samples did not meet the minimum cDNA content requirement during RNA-seq library preparation and were substituted with additional replicates of another two samples. This resulted in two tissues with only two replicates and two tissues with four replicates in the final dataset [\(Supplemental Table 1\)](#page-12-0).

### Mo17 Variant Calling

Genome resequencing of Mo17 was done as part of the Biomass Association Panel (BiomAP) project [\(Mazaheri et al., 2019](#page-14-15)). 477 million 100-bp paired-end reads were generated for Mo17 giving an average of 953 coverage. Reads were first trimmed by Trimmomatic ([Bolger et al.,](#page-13-18) [2014\)](#page-13-18) and mapped to the maize B73 genome AGPv4 [\(Jiao et al., 2017\)](#page-13-9) using BWA-MEM ([Li and Durbin, 2010\)](#page-14-16). PCR duplicates were marked and removed using GATK ([McKenna et al., 2010](#page-14-17)). Variants were called by GATK haplotypecaller and filtered using different filters for SNPs (QD > 2, FS < 60, MQ > 40, MQRankSum > 12.5, ReadPosRankSum > 8, SOR < 4) and for indels (QD > 2, FS < 200, ReadPosRankSum > 20, SOR < 10). Moreover, variants located in regions with unusually high coverage (DP > mean + 2SD) and heterozygous calls (GT = = '0/1') were also removed. The final variant file containing 8.04 million variants with 164,000 CDS variants was deposited in the Data Repository for University of Minnesota (DRUM, <https://doi.org/10.13020/D6T38X>).

## RNA-Seq Data Processing

RNA-seq (HiSeq 2500 High Output Mode with 50-bp paired-end reads) was performed to collect 10–15 million reads for each sample. Reads were trimmed by Trimmomatic [\(Bolger et al., 2014\)](#page-13-18) and aligned to the B73 maize reference genome (AGPv4, Ensembl Plant release 32) using Hisat2 ([Kim et al., 2015\)](#page-13-10), which also takes the Mo17 variants into account for mapping bias. Uniquely aligned reads were then counted per feature by HT-Seq [\(Anders et al., 2015\)](#page-13-19). Raw read counts were then normalized by library size and accounted for the effect of extremely differentially expressed genes using the TMM normalization approach [\(Robinson and Oshlack, 2010\)](#page-14-18) to give CPM values for each gene in each sample. CPM values were then normalized by gene CDS lengths to give FPKM (fragments per kilobase of exon per million reads) values. Hierarchical clustering and principal component analysis were used to explore sample cluster patterns. ## Identification of DE and HC Genes

Genes with differential expression between B73 and Mo17 were identified for each tissue using the DESeq2 package in R (tissue-wise false discovery rate adjusted *P* value <0.01) [\(Love et al., 2014](#page-14-19)). DEGs were further classified into different fold-change categories (i.e., DE1–2, DE2–4, DE4–8, and DE8+) based on the log2 fold change of mean expression abundance in Mo17 over B73. SPE genes (i.e., genes actively expressed in one genotype but not expressed in another genotype, also called genotype-specific genes or ePAV genes) were identified as having CPMB73 R 1 and CPMMo17 < 0.1 (which we denote SPE\_B), or genes having CPMB73 < 0.1 and CPMMo17 > 1 (which we denote SPE\_M). Most of these genotype-specific genes were also expressed in the hybrid, indicating the complementation on the gene expression level in the hybrid. These hybrid complementation (HC) genes were designated HC\_B and HC\_M using the following criteria:

HC\_B: CPMB73 R 1 and CPMMo17 < 0.1 and CPMBxM R min(1,(CPMB73 + CPMMo17)/2)

HC\_M: CPMB73 < 0.1 and CPMMo17 R 1 and CPMBxM R min(1,(CPMB73 + CPMMo17)/2)

### Characterization of Non-additive Expression Pattern in the Hybrids

To identify the mode of inheritance (additive, dominance, overdominance, etc.) of gene expression, we first obtained the normalized read counts for each gene across different samples as previously mentioned. We then fitted data to statistical models reflecting either additive or dominant/ recessive inheritance patterns. We assumed that read counts followed negative binomial distributions:

$$\begin{split} x_{\max,i} &\sim \mathsf{NB}\bigg(r, \ \frac{S_{\mathit{pmax}}}{1 - S_{\mathit{pmax}}}\bigg), \ x_{\min,i} \ \sim \mathsf{NB}\bigg(r, \ \frac{S_{\mathit{pmin}}}{1 - S_{\mathit{pmin}}}\bigg), \ \text{and} \ y_i \\ &\sim \mathsf{NB}\bigg(r, \ \frac{S_h}{1 - S_h}\bigg) \end{split}$$

Where *x*max,*<sup>i</sup>* is the normalized read count in the *i*th replicate from the parental showing the higher median expression level among replicates, *x*min,*<sup>i</sup>* is the normalized read count in the *i*th replicate from the parental showing the lower median expression level among replicates, and *yi* is the normalized read count in the *i*th F1 hybrid.

The dispersion parameter (*r*) for each gene was estimated using the ''estimateDispersions'' function within DESeq2 with local regression fit ([Love et al., 2014](#page-14-19)). *r* was used as the reciprocal of the fitted dispersion value from DESeq2. # Characterization of Allele-Specific Expression Pattern in the Hybrids

All hybrid reads were mapped to the B73 (AGPv4) reference database built by Hisat2 using Mo17 variants. As a result, at the  $\sim$ 1.1 million exonic variant positions where B73 and Mo17 differ, reads can be assigned unequivocally to one of the two alleles. In practice, a significant mapping bias was observed for indels where the proportion of B73-supporting reads (out of all the reads spanning these indel sites) was systematically higher than 0.5 (median of 0.55). On the other hand, such mapping bias was not observed (or much reduced) for SNP variants. This is an indication that reference mapping bias is much higher for indels than for SNPs. In the downstream analysis of ASE, only SNP variants were used for inference.

Reads spanning more than one variant position might give contradictory predictions (i.e., a read may support B73 allele in one variant position but Mo17 in another variant position). Such "conflicting reads" (accounting for <0.06% of allele-specific reads), as well as the number of B73-supporting reads and Mo17-supporting reads, were counted for each gene. Only genes with at least 30 allele-specific reads and less than 10% conflicting reads were used to infer allele ratio of hybrid expression.

# Identification of Genes Showing *cis*- and *trans*-Regulatory Patterns

To classify gene expression levels into different regulatory categories, for each gene we introduce the following notation:

- $x_i$  = expression of the gene in the *i*th B73 parent
- $y_i$  = expression of the gene in the *i*th Mo17 parent
- $n_j$  = number of reads mapping across both alleles in the *j*th F<sub>1</sub> hybrid
- $z_i$  = number of reads mapping to the B73 allele in the *j*th F<sub>1</sub> hybrid

$$x_i \sim \mathsf{NB}\left(r, \frac{p_{\mu}}{1-p_{\mu}}\right), y_i \sim \mathsf{NB}\left(r, \frac{p_{\nu}}{1-p_{\nu}}\right), \text{ and } z_j \sim \mathsf{BB}(\alpha, \beta, n_i)$$

The marginal distributions of  $x_i$ , and  $y_i$  are negative binomial, and the marginal distribution of  $z_j$  is beta-binomial. Additionally, we note that *r* reflects the overdispersion (relative to a Poisson distribution); this parameter is estimated a priori using the approach of Anders et al. (2015). Subsequently, different constraints upon the parameters can be imposed to describe the following biological situations:

Conserved: 
$$p_{\mu} = p_{\nu}$$
 and  $\alpha = \beta$   
 $cis: p_{\mu} \neq p_{\nu}$  and  $\alpha/\beta = (p_{\mu}/(1 - p_{\mu}))/(p_{\nu}(1 - p_{\nu}))$   
 $trans: p_{\mu} \neq p_{\nu}$  and  $\alpha = \beta$   
 $cis \& trans: p_{\mu} \neq p_{\nu}$  and  $\alpha \neq \beta$ 

Each gene was allocated into one of these four categories by first fitting the four models described above to the data by maximizing the likelihood function, then calculating the BIC to determine which of the four models best fitted the data for each gene. Genes allocated to the *cis* & *trans* class were further subdivided into three classes using the following approach. For the *i*th gene we defined  $x_i$  as the average log<sub>2</sub> fold change for the parent values and  $y_i$  as the average log<sub>2</sub> allelic ratio for the F<sub>1</sub> alleles. Subsequently, we used the following criteria:

- (1) opposite *cis* stronger:  $(0 < y_i < x_i)$  OR  $(0 > y_i > x_i)$
- (2) opposite *trans* stronger:  $(x_i < 0 < y_i)$  OR  $(y_i < 0 < x_i)$
- (3) same:  $(0 < x_i < y_i)$  OR  $(0 > x_i > y_i)$

### Gene Ontology Enrichment and Homology Analysis

GO annotation was downloaded from maize-GAMER (Wimalanathan et al., 2018) including domain assignments from InterProScan5 and significant homolog hits to the *Arabidopsis* and UniProtKB databases. GO enrichment was performed in R, and *P* values were calculated by the "phyper" function and corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg) (BH) approach. Only GO terms with *P* values less than 1e–4 were reported.

### Comparison of ASE-Based and eQTL-Based Gene Regulatory Classification Approaches

Shoot apex meristem (SAM) eQTLs have been mapped previously using the IBM populations of maize B73 and Mo17 lines (Li et al., 2013). These eQTLs were either assigned as *cis*-acting or *trans*-acting based on the physical distance between the mapped QTL and the target gene (*cis*: within 1 Mb apart; *trans*: more than 1 Mb apart). For the subset of genes that overlap between the eQTL dataset and our ASE-based dataset (i.e., have at least one *cis*- or *trans*-acting eQTL, and are DE between B73 and Mo17 and have ASE calls in this dataset), we determined the consistency of regulatory pattern classification between the two datasets. Seedling meristem tissue in this dataset was first used, since it is closest to SAM that was used previously. The analysis was then repeated for three other tissues (flag leaf, root, and ear) to check whether there was a tissuespecific effect in the comparison.
