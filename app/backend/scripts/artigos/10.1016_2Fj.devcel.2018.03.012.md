#### **SUMMARY**

DNA methylation on  $N^6$ -adenine (6mA) has recently been found to be a potentially epigenetic mark in several unicellular and multicellular eukaryotes. However, its distribution patterns and potential functions in land plants, which are primary producers for most ecosystems, remain largely unknown. Here we report global profiling of 6mA sites at single-nucleotide resolution in the genome of Arabidopsis thaliana at different developmental stages using single-molecule real-time sequencing. 6mA sites are widely distributed across the Arabidopsis genome and enriched over the pericentromeric heterochromatin regions. 6mA occurs more frequently in gene bodies than intergenic regions. Analysis of 6mA methylomes and RNA sequencing data demonstrates that 6mA frequency positively correlates with the gene expression level and the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth in Arabidopsis. Our results uncover 6mA as a DNA mark associated with actively expressed genes in Arabidopsis, suggesting that 6mA serves as a hitherto unknown epigenetic mark in land plants.

#### INTRODUCTION

DNA methylation occurs by the addition of a methyl group (CH<sub>3</sub>) to all four types of DNA nucleotides, and affects many biological processes that depend on DNA structure and topology. Methylations on the fifth position of the pyrimidine ring of cytosine (5-methylcytosine [5mC]) and on the sixth position of the purine ring of adenine ( $N^6$ -methyldeoxyadenosine [6mA]) are the most abundant DNA modifications in eukaryotes and prokaryotes (Ratel et al., 2006a; Wion and Casadesus, 2006; Vanyushin et al., 1968, 1970). 5mC, which is widely distributed in the genomes of most eukaryotes, including plants, animals, and fungi, is commonly regarded as an important epigenetic mark closely relevant to transcription (Jones, 2012; Law and Jacobsen, 2010; Zilberman et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2006). In contrast, the distribution and function of 6mA in eukaryotes had remained

largely unknown until several recent studies reported 6mA distribution patterns in the genomes of the unicellular green algae *Chamydomonas reinhardtii*, fungi, and several multicellular animals, including *Drosophila melanogaster*, *Caenorhabditis elegans*, *Xenopus lavis*, *Mus musculus*, and *Danio rerio* (Mondo et al., 2017; Koziol et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2016; Fu et al., 2015; Greer et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2015).

Although it has long been known that 6mA is present in prokaryotes and ancient eukaryotes, research on 6mA has so far been intensively focused on its functions in prokaryotes, where 6mA serves as a major DNA mark to discriminate invasive foreign DNA (Luo et al., 2015; Ratel et al., 2006b). In addition, prokaryotic 6mA also affects various cellular processes, such as gene expression, DNA replication and repair, and cell-cycle regulation (Collier et al., 2007; Campbell and Kleckner, 1990; Messer and Noyer-Weidner, 1988). Although 6mA research in eukaryotes was early focused on unicellular protists (Luo et al., 2015; Ratel et al., 2006b; Wion and Casadesus, 2006), its functions were far less understood than in prokaryotes until a recent report on the whole-genome 6mA landscape in Chamydomonas (Fu et al., 2015). 6mA is present in nearly 84% of all Chamydomonas genes, and exhibits a periodic distribution pattern near transcription start sites (TSSs) associated with actively expressed genes, implying regulatory roles of 6mA in gene expression in Chamydomonas.

Besides unicellular eukaryotes, more recently evolved eukaryotes also contain 6mA modification in their DNA, albeit at very low levels (Ratel et al., 2006b). Thanks to the development of sensitive detection techniques (Luo et al., 2015), recent studies on 6mA in genomic DNA (gDNA) of D. melanogaster and C. elegans, have shed light on distribution patterns and potential functions of 6mA in multicellular eukaryotes. Modification of 6mA is dynamic in various tissues and required for regulation of development in D. melanogaster (Zhang et al., 2015), while 6mA is broadly distributed in the C. elegans genome and has crosstalk with histone modification in carrying epigenetic information (Greer et al., 2015). Furthermore, 6mA has also been discovered in vertebrates, such as X. laevis and M. musculus, with much lower abundance than that in D. melanogaster and C. elegans (Koziol et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2016). 6mA is commonly depleted from exons in X. laevis and M. musculus (Koziol et al., 2016), and plays a role in gene repression in *M. musculus* embryonic stem cells (Wu et al., 2016).

Although the presence of 6mA in plastid, mtDNA, or nuclear DNA has been detected in several higher plants mostly using the restriction enzyme digestion approach since 1971 [\(Fedor](#page-10-2)[eyeva and Vanyushin, 2002; Dhar et al., 1990; Vanyushin et al.,](#page-10-2) [1971, 1988\)](#page-10-2), the whole-genome distribution patterns and potential functions of 6mA in land plants remain largely unknown. In this study, we applied several methods, including DNA dot blot using a specific 6mA antibody, liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), and single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing, to measure the levels and global distribution patterns of 6mA in gDNA of the model plant *Arabidopsis thaliana* (ecotype Columbia-0; Col). 6mA modification is broadly distributed across the Col genome at considerable levels, and displays a dynamic change in its levels in various *Arabidopsis* tissues and at different developmental stages. Nearly 30% of 6mA sites are present in gene bodies. Comparison of 6mA methylome and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data in Col plants revealed that 6mA is positively correlated with actively transcribed genes. Taken together, our findings suggest that 6mA is a hitherto unknown DNA mark associated with actively expressed genes in *Arabidopsis*, and could play a fundamental role as an epigenetic mark in land plants.

#### RESULTS

#### 6mA Is a Dynamic DNA Modification in Arabidopsis

To directly examine the presence of 6mA in *Arabidopsis*, we first performed dot blot analysis using an antibody specifically against the 6mA base to detect the 6mA signal in *Arabidopsis* gDNA extracted from Col wild-type plants. 6mA signals were detectable with different intensities in gDNA extracted from 9-day-old whole seedlings and from all tissues examined, including roots, rosette leaves, cauline leaves, stems, open flowers, flower buds, and siliques ([Figures 1](#page-2-0)A, left panel, S1A, and S1B). Furthermore, dot blot analysis of gDNA extracted from 3- to 21-day-old Col wild-type plants revealed a gradual increase in the 6mA signal intensity from vegetative to reproductive stage [\(Figure 1A](#page-2-0), right panel). To accurately quantify 6mA levels, we applied LC-MS/MS assays using the pure 6mA nucleoside as an external standard. The 6mA level in gDNA of 9-dayold Col whole seedlings was 0.048% (6mA/A) [\(Figures 1B](#page-2-0) and 1C). Among various tissues examined, gDNA of roots and rosette leaves displayed the highest (0.138%) and lowest level (0.006%) of 6mA, respectively ([Figures 1](#page-2-0)B and S1C). In addition, 6mA levels gradually increased in gDNA of 3- to 15-day-old Col whole plants, and displayed a dramatic increase in 21-day-old plants at the reproductive stage ([Figures 1A](#page-2-0), 1C, and S1B). Since PCR analyses using universal 16S primers excluded the bacterial contamination in our plant materials (Figures S1C and S1D), the dot blot and LC-MS/MS assays both suggest that 6mA DNA modification is present in *Arabidopsis*, and that there are dynamic changes in 6mA levels in various tissues and at different developmental stages.

#### Genome-Wide Mapping of 6mA in Arabidopsis

To precisely understand 6mA distribution and localization in the *Arabidopsis* genome, we carried out SMRT sequencing to directly interrogate unamplified gDNA extracted from 9-dayold Col wild-type plants. SMRT sequencing uniquely detects strand-specific DNA modifications at single-nucleotide resolution by virtue of variations in the interpulse duration between two successive base incorporations, which is altered by the presence of a modified base in the DNA template ([Flusberg](#page-10-3) [et al., 2010\)](#page-10-3). This sequencing approach has been successfully applied to the genome-wide mapping of 6mA in a number of organisms ([Greer et al., 2015; Fang et al., 2012](#page-11-6)). As this sequencing approach does not distinguish 6mA versus DNA methylation on *N*<sup>1</sup> -adenine (1mA) ([Flusberg et al., 2010](#page-10-3)), we applied LC-MS/MS assays to examine whether 1mA is also present in *Arabidopsis*. Our results demonstrated that 1mA was not detectable in various tissues and at different developmental stages of Col wild-type plants (Figure S1). Thus, SMRT sequencing is suitable for specifically detecting 6mA signals in *Arabidopsis*.

We first mapped the raw data from SMRT sequencing with the bacterial database, and the negative results excluded the possibility of bacterial contamination. After trimming off adapters, low-quality regions, and short reads, our SMRT sequencing generated approximately 1.2 million reads with a mean read length of 10.3 kb and a mean read quality of 0.845, which was much higher than 0.75, a threshold value indicating the high quality of the sequencing data. The SMRT sequencing dataset yielded approximately a 103-fold coverage of the Col genome, which was higher than the 100-fold coverage recommended for reliably detecting 6mA (PacBio Methylome Analysis Technical Note), indicating high-confidence levels of detection achieved by SMRT sequencing. After considering the minimum sequencing coverage per strand at 25-fold, we detected statistically reliable 6mA on 29,811 adenines in nuclear, chloroplast, and mitochondrial genomes of 9-day-old Col wild-type seedlings ([Figures 2](#page-4-0)A, S2A, and S2B; Table S1). The 6mA/A percentage representing the number of detected 6mAs to the total number of adenines in all sequencing reads is 0.04%, which was comparable with the value revealed in the same materials by our LC-MS/MS assays (0.048%) [\(Figure 1](#page-2-0)B). Furthermore, 6mA-immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative real-time PCR analyses independently validated 29 6mA-methylated loci and 25 negative loci randomly selected from 5 chromosomes (Figure S2C). These results suggest that our SMRT sequencing analysis of 6mA modification in the Col genome is robust.

Through analyzing SMRT sequencing datasets, we profiled the density of 6mA sites normalized to total adenines in each 50-kb bin by grouping nearly 98% of the total 6mA sites identified in the Col genome into the following four fractions with different methylation levels, including low (20%–50%, green ring), intermediate (50%–70%, yellow ring), high (70%–100%, blue ring), and full (100%, purple ring) fractions [\(Figure 2B](#page-4-0)). Among these 6mA sites, the intermediately and highly methylated sites were dominant, and were prevalently distributed across all five chromosomes in the Col genome. Furthermore, compared with unmethylated adenines (innermost red ring), all methylated fractions were dense to different extents over the pericentromeric heterochromatin regions [\(Figure 2](#page-4-0)B), which is generally consistent with an increased enrichment of 6mA sites near the centromeres ([Figure 2](#page-4-0)C). In contrast, the average methylation levels of 6mA sites were comparable along chromosome arms with a slight reduction toward centromeres in the Col genome ([Figure 2](#page-4-0)D). In addition, both 6mA-methylated and -unmethylated adenines were depleted at the centromeric gaps ([Figure 2B](#page-4-0)). Notably, the general 6mA distribution pattern across the five chromosomes, including its enrichment in pericentromeric heterochromatin regions and depletion at centromeric gaps, is similar to the pattern of 5mC distribution in *Arabidopsis* [\(Zilberman et al., 2007\)](#page-11-9).

#### Analysis of 6mA-Methylated Genes

To infer putative biological functions of 6mA, we examined 6mA distribution in Col genomic regions divided into gene bodies, promoters (within 2 kb upstream of TSS), 5<sup>0</sup> intergenic (within 2–7 kb upstream of TSS), 3<sup>0</sup> intergenic (within 5 kb downstream of transcription stop site), and other intergenic regions. In particular, 32% of 6mA sites were located within gene bodies, which was significantly higher than the expected number (Figures S3A and [3](#page-5-0)A, left panel). We further examined 6mA distribution within gene bodies divided into 5<sup>0</sup> and 3<sup>0</sup> UTRs, exons (excluding 50 and 3<sup>0</sup> UTRs in protein-coding genes), and introns, and revealed that most of the 6mA sites were present in exons [\(Fig](#page-5-0)[ure 3A](#page-5-0), right panel). These sites also displayed higher methylation levels than other sites in gene bodies (Figure S3B).

Through interrogating the sequences in gene bodies containing 6mA sites, we found that around 16% of the annotated genes in Col genomes were 6mA-methylated. Protein-coding and transposable element genes were the two largest groups of 6mA-methylated genes, with comparable methylation levels, while there was only a small number of methylated non-coding RNA genes and pseudogenes ([Figures 3B](#page-5-0), left panel, and S3C; [Table 1\)](#page-6-0). Protein-coding genes contained more than half of the 6mA sites in all methylated genes [\(Fig](#page-5-0)[ure 3B](#page-5-0), right panel). Notably, protein-coding genes were evenly distributed over all chromosome arms, while transposable element genes were mostly enriched over the pericentromeric heterochromatin regions ([Figure 2](#page-4-0)B, ring 6). In addition, most of the methylated protein-coding genes contained one 6mA site ([Figure 3](#page-5-0)C).

We further analyzed the distribution pattern of 6mA sites in the vicinity of methylated genes by aligning protein-coding, transposable element, and all genes at the TSS, and plotting 6mA occupancy representing the relative number of genes containing 6mA sites against the total gene number for each 50-bp window throughout 5 kb upstream and downstream of the TSS [\(Figure 3](#page-5-0)D). Although 6mA sites displayed a general trend of enrichment near the TSS, this pattern was less obvious among 6mA sites in protein-coding genes. In contrast, 6mA sites in transposable element genes were greatly enriched near the TSS with a local depletion at the TSS followed by a clear summit of peaks immediately after the TSS ([Figure 3D](#page-5-0)). Furthermore, we plotted the numbers of 6mA-methylated, unmethylated, and total adenine sites in each 50-bp window around the TSS. Although all these adenines showed a trend of enrichment around the TSS, the distribution patterns of 6mA-modified adenines around the TSS were different from the identical pattern displayed by unmethylated and total adenines (Figure S3D). These observations imply that enrichment of 6mA sites around the TSS in genes is correlated with, but not determined by, the number of adenines around the TSS.

To provide functional insights about 6mA DNA modification in *Arabidopsis*, we categorized 6mA-methylated and unmethylated genes using the *Arabidopsis* gene ontology (GO) resource ([Be](#page-10-4)[rardini et al., 2004](#page-10-4)). There were significant differences in fraction percentages of many GO categories between methylated and unmethylated genes (p < 10-5 , Fisher's exact test) (Figure S3E). In general, the total percentages of methylated genes with annotated functions were higher than those of unmethylated genes. The largest group of 6mA-methylated genes were involved in interacting selectively and non-covalently with other protein(s) (protein binding).

#### 6mA DNA Methylomes at Different Developmental Stages and Ecotypes

To investigate dynamic changes in 6mA methylomes at different developmental stages, we further performed genome-wide mapping of 6mA in gDNA from 21-day-old Col wild-type plants at the reproductive stage by SMRT sequencing, and revealed approximately 2.4 million high-confidence reads with a mean read length of 7.0 kb, a mean read quality of 0.848, and a 116 fold coverage of the Col genome. This resulted in the identification of 184,633 6mA sites (Table S2), among which 9,909 sites were overlapped with those in 9-day-old plants [\(Figure 4A](#page-7-0)). This dramatic increase in the number of 6mA sites in 21- versus 9-day-old plants is consistent with LC-MS/MS results [\(Fig](#page-2-0)[ure 1](#page-2-0)C), implying that 6mA DNA methylation positively correlates with the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth.

Interestingly, despite a remarkable change in the number of 6mA sites, the 6mA distribution patterns in 21- and 9-day-old plants shared the following key features. First, 6mA sites were widely distributed across the five chromosomes, but relatively enriched in pericentromeric heterochromatin regions ([Figures](#page-4-0) B and [4B](#page-7-0)). Second, 6mA occurred more frequently in gene bodies, particularly exons, than intergenic regions ([Figures 3](#page-5-0)A and [4C](#page-7-0)). Third, in both 21- and 9-day-old plants, protein-coding genes were the largest group of 6mA-methylated genes [\(Figures](#page-5-0) B and [4](#page-7-0)D) and evenly distributed over the five chromosome arms [\(Figures 2B](#page-4-0) and [4](#page-7-0)B). In addition, the number of proteincoding genes containing one 6mA site was the highest among those containing various numbers of 6mA sites ([Figures 3C](#page-5-0) and [4E](#page-7-0)). Last, the consensus 6mA motifs found in 9-day-old plants were also enriched among 6mA sites found in 21-dayold plants ([Figures 3](#page-5-0)E and S3F).

Notably, the percentage of 6mA-methylated genes increased from 16% in 9-day-old plants to 60% of the annotated genes in Col genomes in 21-day-old plants, in which 6mA sites in all genes including protein-coding and transposable element genes displayed a more obvious trend of enrichment near the TSS than those in 9-day-old plants ([Figures 3D](#page-5-0) and [4F](#page-7-0)). In 21-day-old plants, GO analysis revealed that the overall percentages of methylated genes with annotated functions were much higher than those of unmethylated genes (Figure S3E).

### DISCUSSION

Recent discovery of 6mA in several multicellular eukaryotes and the unicellular algae *Chamydomonas* has shed light on potential regulatory roles of this DNA modification in eukaryotes [\(Koziol](#page-11-7) [et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2016; Fu et al., 2015; Greer et al., 2015;](#page-11-7) [Zhang et al., 2015\)](#page-11-7). Here we have reported a genome-wide evaluation of 6mA modifications in a land plant *Arabidopsis*. 6mA sites are broadly distributed across the *Arabidopsis* genome at different developmental stages, and are present in a large number of gene bodies encoding various proteins and transposable elements. We have further revealed that 6mA DNA modification could be a universal regulatory mechanism pertaining to gene transcription in eukaryotes.

The distribution patterns of 6mA sites in methylated genes are diverse in various eukaryotic organisms so far examined. Our results have shown that 6mA sites in *Arabidopsis* genes are mostly located in exons. In addition, 6mA sites in transposable element genes exhibit a local depletion at the TSS followed by an immediate summit of peaks. This distribution pattern is generally similar to that in *Chlamydomonas* genes, which additionally display a unique bimodal distribution centered on the TSS ([Fu](#page-10-1) [et al., 2015](#page-10-1)). In contrast, 6mA distribution patterns in animals are noticeably different from those in *Arabidopsis* and *Chlamydomonas*. In *C. elegans* 6mA sites are evenly distributed in genomic regions, including exons, introns and TSS regions [\(Greer et al., 2015\)](#page-11-6), while 6mA sites in *X. laevis* and *M. musculus* are predominantly excluded from coding regions with a strong decrease in 6mA levels after the TSS [\(Koziol](#page-11-7) [et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2016](#page-11-7)). In *D. melanogaster* 6mA sites are enriched in transposable elements [\(Zhang et al., 2015\)](#page-11-5), whereas 6mA sites in *Arabidopsis* are predominately located within protein-coding genes.

The consensus motifs for 6mA are only partially conserved in different eukaryotic organisms ([Figure 6\)](#page-9-0), implying that multiple enzymes could have evolved to catalyze 6mA modification in the evolutionary process. GATC is likely the most ancient 6mA motif, which exists in both lower eukaryotes and bacteria, but is lost in higher eukaryotes ([Fu et al., 2015](#page-10-1)). GAGG may arise from a common ancestor of bikonts and unikonts as it is present in both plantae and animalia, while AGAA might be a specific motif in animals [\(Wu et al., 2016; Greer et al., 2015\)](#page-11-8). *Arabidopsis* contains two specific motifs, ANYGA and ACCT, which are so far not found in other organisms.

Notably, despite different distribution patterns and consensus motifs of 6mA sites in methylated genes in various eukaryotic organisms, 6mA modification preferentially correlates with highly expressed genes in several evolutionarily distant organisms, including *Arabidopsis*, *Chlamydomonas*, *Drosophila*, as well as several fungi species ([Figure 6](#page-9-0)) ([Mondo et al., 2017; Fu et al.,](#page-11-2) [2015; Zhang et al., 2015](#page-11-2)). The correlation of 6mA with gene repression was only reported in mouse ([Wu et al., 2016](#page-11-8)). Since addition of a methyl group at the sixth position of the purine ring of adenine could affect base-pairing energy and protein-DNA interactions ([Luo et al., 2015\)](#page-11-3), it is possible that 6mA could affect transcription by influencing recruitment of transcription factors, RNA polymerases, histones or other components in the relevant chromatin context. The concrete correlation between 6mA and gene expression in various organisms might be dynamically regulated in different developmental contexts.

In this study, we have found that highly expressed genes correlate with higher frequencies of 6mA modification in *Arabidopsis*. Consistently, the same genes with a change in the status of 6mA modification at different developmental stages are expressed at different levels, which is positively correlated with 6mA modification. Whether the correlation between 6mA modification and gene expression is relevant to the role of 6mA in positioning of nucleosomes around the TSS, as revealed in *Chamydomonas* [\(Fu et al., 2015](#page-10-1)), will be an interesting topic for further investigation.

Taken together, our results uncover that 6mA is a DNA mark widely distributed across the *Arabidopsis* genomes at different developmental stages and associated with actively expressed genes. It is noteworthy that the overall 6mA distribution pattern is similar to that of 5mC distribution in *Arabidopsis* chromosomes ([Zilberman et al., 2007\)](#page-11-9). Moreover, both 6mA and 5mC correlate with gene transcription, but in a different manner. These observations imply that these two DNA marks could have certain interdependence. Interestingly, although enrichment of 6mA sites near the TSS is obviously observed in transposable element genes, nearly all these genes are silenced. Since these transposable element genes accumulate over the pericentromeric heterochromatin in *Arabidopsis* ([Ara](#page-10-5)[bidopsis Genome Initiative, 2000](#page-10-5)), it is possible that expression of these genes is affected by multiple factors, including various types of DNA methylation and the chromatin context. Further investigation of this sort of questions will contribute to understanding of the holistic impact of DNA methylation on gene expression.

#### <span id="page-13-0"></span>EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS

#### Plant Materials

*Arabidopsis thaliana* Col-0 wild-type plants were grown under long day conditions (16 h light/8 h dark) at 23 C±2 C under cool white fluorescent light (100 mmol m-<sup>2</sup> sec-1 ). *Arabidopsis* seedlings used for dot blot, LC-MS/MS and SMRT sequencing analyses were grown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium.

#### <span id="page-13-1"></span>METHOD DETAILS

#### Dot Blot Analysis of 6mA Levels

Purified genomic DNA was treated with RNase and denatured by heating at 95C for 3 min, followed by chilling on ice directly. DNA was spotted on a Hybond-N+ membrane (Amersham) optimized for nucleic acid transfer. After UV crosslinking, the membrane was washed by 13PBST buffer, blocked with 5% of non-fat milk in PBST, and incubated overnight with anti-6mA antibody (1:250; Synaptic Systems 202003) at 4 C. After incubating with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Santa Cruz), the membrane was visualized by ECL Western Blotting Detection Kit (Thermo Scientific).

#### Measurement of the 6mA/A Ratio

Genomic DNA samples were digested into single nucleosides in a digestion cocktail containing nuclease P1 (1 U), phosphodiesterase I (0.01 U) in 30 mM sodium acetate and 2mM zinc chloride at pH 6.8 for 3 h at 37C, and dephosphorylated with bacterial alkaline phosphatase (10 U) for 1 h at 37C. Enzymes were removed by filtration (Amicon Ultra 10K MWCO). Individual nucleosides were resolved on a Hypersil GOLD aQ reverse phase column (Thermo Scientific), and samples were then subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis on an Agilent 6490 Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer. Nucleosides were quantified using the nucleoside-to-base ion mass transitions of 266.1 to 150.0 for 6mA and 252.1 to 136.0 for A.

#### SMRT Library Preparation

Genomic DNA was extracted from 9-day-old and 21-day-old *Arabidopsis* Col wild-type seedlings using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The amount and quality of native DNA were examined by gel electrophoresis, Nanodrop (OD 260/280, 1.8-2.0; OD 260/230, 2.0-2.2) and Qubit DNA Assay Kit (Life Technologies). DNA libraries were prepared and sequenced on 13 SMRT cells according to the PacBio instructions (Pacific Biosciences). The SMRT bell was produced using the DNA Template Prep Kit 1.0, and the resulting templates were bound to polymerases using the DNA/Polymerase Binding Kit P6 v2 (Pacific Biosciences). The polymerase-template complexes were then bound to magbeads by using Magbead Binding Kit, and sequencing was carried out on the PacBio RSII using the DNA Sequencing Kit 4.0. Movie lengths were 240 min for each of 13 SMRT cells.

### SMRT Sequencing Analysis

The raw data of Col plants were generated by SMRT sequencing in this study, and filtered by SFilter (Pacific Biosciences) to remove reads containing adapters, short reads and the other low quality reads with cutoffs (read quality % 0.75, read length % 500 nt, and subread length % 50 nt). Read quality refers to the de novo prediction of the accuracy of subreads from a single zero-mode waveguide (ZMW), and the value above 0.75 is considered as high quality (Pacific Biosciences Terminology). 6mA was detected using RS\_Modification\_Detection.1 protocol (Pacific Biosciences) according to previously published methods [\(Flusberg et al., 2010\)](#page-10-3). Briefly, the clean reads were aligned to the *Arabidopsis* TAIR10 genome using pbalign. The polymerase kinetics information was further loaded after being processed by loadChemistry.py and loadPulses. Then post-aligned datasets were sorted by cmph5tools. 6mAs were finally identified using ipdSummary.py script. IPD ratio was determined by calculating the ratio of the IPD at a site in the native sample to the IPD at a site in an in silico control, which used a predicted value of the mean IPD at each reference position. 6mA was only called if the IPD ratio at a position was significantly different from the expected background (P-value < 0.01, Welch's *t*-test). We then filtered out 6mA sites with less than 25-fold coverage to ensure reliable detection of 6mA ([Clark et al., 2012](#page-10-6)). The genomewide 6mA profiles are generated using the Circos software [\(Krzywinski et al., 2009\)](#page-11-15). Part of the analysis was done by custom-made R and Perl scripts. The upstream and downstream 4-bp sequences from the 6mA sites were extracted for 6mA motif identification using MEME-ChIP ([Ma et al., 2014\)](#page-11-17).

### Measurement of Significance of Genomic Distribution of 6mA Sites

We obtained the genome-wide methylation rate of adenine sites by calculating the mean of 6mA sites from all adenine sites. The expected number of methylation sites in each category was the total number of adenines in that category multiplied by the methylation rate. A binomial test was performed to assess statistical significance of over- or under-representation of the observed number of 6mA sites in each category compared to the corresponding expected number. A comparison was considered statistically different when p-value < 10-5.

#### RNA-Seq

RNA-seq was performed as previously described [\(Shen et al., 2016\)](#page-11-18). Briefly, 9-day-old and 21-day-old wild-type *Arabidopsis* Col-0 seedlings were harvested for total RNA extraction using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen). The amount and quality of RNA were

tested by Qubit RNA Assay Kit (Life Technologies), gel electrophoresis and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Ribosomal RNA was removed by Ribo-ZeroTM rRNA Removal Kit (Epicentre). NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB) was used to construct the library, and sequencing was performed on Illumina Hiseq 2500 platform. Clean reads were aligned to the *Arabidopsis* reference TAIR10 using TopHat v2.0.9 [\(Trapnell et al., 2012](#page-11-16)). The mapped reads were assembled by Cufflinks v2.1.1. Cuffdiff was used to calculate FPKMs of coding genes ([Trapnell et al., 2012](#page-11-16)).

#### 6mA-IP-qPCR

Genomic DNA was extracted from 9-day-old wild-type *Arabidopsis* seedlings (Col) and sonicated to produce fragments of 250 bp. Fragmented DNA was incubated for 2 h at 4 C with anti-6mA polyclonal antibody (Synaptic Systems) in immunoprecipitation buffer (10 mM sodium phosphate, 2 mM potassium phosphate, 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 0.05% Triton X-100, pH 7.4). The mixture was then immunoprecipitated by incubation with Protein A/G Plus-Agarose (Santa Cruz) that was pre-bound with bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 4 C for additional 2 h. After extensive washing, bound DNA was eluted from the beads in elution buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 1% SDS, 10 mM EDTA) at 65 C, treated with proteinase K and purified using QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen). Fold-enrichment of each fragment was determined by quantitative real-time PCR as previously described [\(Li et al., 2008](#page-11-19)). Briefly, relative enrichment of each fragment was calculated first by normalizing the amount of a target DNA fragment against a genomic fragment of *TUBLIN2* as an internal control, and then by normalizing the value for the immunoprecipitated sample against that for the input. Primers pairs used for 6mA-IP-qPCR assays are listed in Table S4.
