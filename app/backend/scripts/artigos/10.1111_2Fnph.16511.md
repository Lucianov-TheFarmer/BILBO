Received: *31 October 2019* Accepted: *18 February 2020*  DNA methylation is a widespread epigenetic mark that contributes to transcriptome reprogramming during plant-pathogen interactions. However, the distinct role of DNA methylation in establishing resistant and susceptible responses remains largely unexplored.

Here, we developed and used a pair of near-isogenic lines (NILs) to characterize DNA methylome landscapes of soybean roots during the susceptible and resistant interactions with soybean cyst nematode (SCN, *Heterodera glycines*). We also compared the methylomes of the NILs and their parents to identify introduced and stably inherited methylation variants.

The genomes of the NILs were substantially differentially methylated under non-infected conditions. This difference was associated with differential gene expression that may prime the NILs responses to SCN infection. In response to SCN infection, the susceptible line exhibited reduced global methylation levels in both protein-coding genes and transposable elements, whereas the resistant line showed the opposite response of increased global methylation levels. Heritable and novel non-parental differentially methylated regions overlapping with genes associated with soybean response to SCN infection were identified and validated using transgenic hairy root system.

Our analyses indicate that DNA methylation patterns associated with the susceptible and resistant interactions are highly specific and that novel and stably inherited methylation variants are of biological significance.

**Keywords:** DNA methylation, Epigenetic inheritance, RNA-seq, Soybean cyst nematode, Susceptible and resistant interactions, Syncytium

# INTRODUCTION

In soybean (*Glycine max*), soybean cyst nematode (SCN, *Heterodera glycines*) is the most damaging pathogen, causing significant yield and quality losses. SCN induces vascular root cells to fuse and form a permanent feeding structure, the syncytium, essential for nematode development and maturity. While the molecular mechanism underlying syncytium formation is far from being understood, recent studies indicate that epigenetic modifications mediated by small RNAs and miRNAs play key regularly role in establishing syncytium transcriptome (Hewezi *et al.*, 2008a; Hewezi *et al.*, 2012; Hewezi & Baum, 2015; Hewezi *et al.*, 2016; Piya *et al.*, 2017). Recently, DNA methylation has emerged as pivotal epigenetic mark that can shape the dynamic response of plants to infection by various phytopathogens including cyst nematodes (reviewed in Hewezi *et al.* (2018)).

DNA methylation is a consequential epigenetic mark that impacts gene expression, transposon mobility, genomic stability and imprinting (He et al., 2011). Previous studies identified key enzymes that carry-out cytosine DNA methylation in various sequence contexts. After DNA replication, methylation in CG and CHG is maintained through the activity cytosine of METHYLTRANSFERASE1 (MET1) and CHROMOMETHYLASE3 (CMT3), respectively. Also, a small portion of CHH methylation can be maintained by CMT2 following DNA replication, but the large majority of CHH methylation sites are reestablished de novo. De novo DNA methylation in CG and non-CG contexts is carried-out through the synchronized activity of the RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) pathway and DOMAINS REARRANGED METHYLTRANSFERASE 2 (Matzke & Mosher, 2014). Mutants defective in DNA methylation pathway clearly pointed into a role of DNA methylation in regulating plant immune response against bacterial, fungal and nematode pathogens (Hewezi et al., 2008a; Dowen et al., 2012; Yu et al., 2013; Le et al., 2014; Lopez Sanchez et al., 2016). These analyses also revealed that hyper- and hypomethylation exert distinct effects against different pathogens and biotic treatments (Dowen et al., 2012; Yu et al., 2013; Le et al., 2014; Lopez Sanchez et al., 2016). Profiling DNA methylation patterns at a single nucleotide resolution revealed that infection of Arabidopsis by Pseudomonas syringae induced genome-wide demethylation, which altered plant basal defense responses (Dowen et al., 2012; Yu et al., 2013). Similarly, cyst nematode infection of Arabidopsis and soybean induced dramatic changes in plant methylomes that impacted the expression of a significant number of genes involved in essential cellular processes required for successful nematode parasitism (Rambani *et al.*, 2015; Hewezi *et al.*, 2017). Despite the current progress in our understanding of the role of DNA methylation in plant-pathogen interactions, the distinct role of DNA methylation in establishing resistant and susceptible responses remains largely unexplored.

Despite cytosine methylation is a metastable mark, failure to faithfully maintain DNA methylation status by the maintenance enzymes after fertilization may cause spontaneous DNA methylation polymorphisms referred to as epialleles (Mosher et al., 2009; Slotkin et al., 2009; Schmitz et al., 2011). Epialleles can be induced by developmental and environmental stimuli as well as through transposon insertions and genome rearrangements that induce directed chromatin modifications (Hollister & Gaut, 2009; Taudt et al., 2016; Springer & Schmitz, 2017). DNA methylation patterns of Arabidopsis lines generated through single-seed descent for 30 generations with their ancestral lines revealed that spontaneous loss and gain of DNA methylation at individual cytosine sites occur at high levels mainly in the genic regions in the CG sequence context (Becker et al., 2011; Schmitz et al., 2011). Regions with continuous cytosine methylation polymorphisms were also found but at relatively lower rate (Becker et al., 2011; Schmitz et al., 2011). Studies of inheritance and stability of DNA methylation patterns in maize and soybean using populations of recombinant inbred lines (RILs) provided additional evidences for transgenerational inheritance of DNA methylation variants over several generations in the segregating populations (Eichten et al., 2013; Regulski et al., 2013; Schmitz et al., 2013). However, information about occurrence of stably inherited and novel nonparental methylation variants and their potential associations with phenotypic traits, particularly disease resistance, remains very limited.

In this study, SCN–resistant and –susceptible near-isogenic soybean lines were generated and used to compare genome-wide DNA methylation profiles of soybean roots during the susceptible and resistant interactions with SCN. The results indicated that susceptible and resistant responses to SCN employ distinct DNA methylation patterns. We also compared the methylomes of the isogenic lines with the methylomes of their parents and identified heritable and novel non-parental differentially methylated regions overlapping with genes whose functions in plant-SCN interactions were validated.

## **MATERIALS AND METHODS**

# Developing near-isogenic soybean lines differing in GmSHMT08 gene

Two Soybean (*Glycine max*) near-isogenic lines (NILs), TN09-16 (NIL-S) and TN09-29 (NIL-R), which exhibit susceptible and resistant responses, respectively, to SCN HG type 0 (race 3) were developed. These NILs are highly homozygous recombinant inbred lines resulting from individual  $F_{13}$  generation single plants from a cross between the SCN-resistant cultivar Fowler and the SCN-susceptible cultivar Anand (Anand *et al.*, 2001; Young, 2001). SCN resistance in Fowler is derived from Plant Introduction (PI) 437654. Genome similarity between the isogenic lines was determined using SoySNP6K Illumina Infinium BeadChip array containing 5,986 SNPs (Akond *et al.*, 2013). Simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers associated with *rhg1* (Satt309) and *Rhg4* (Satt162 and Satt632) (Kazi *et al.*, 2010) were initially used to examine the genetic differences between these two NILs. In addition, the SCN resistance genes *soluble NSF attachment proteins* (*GmSNAP18*) and the *serine hydroxymethyltransferase* (*GmSHMT08*) at the *rhg1-b* and *RHg4* loci, respectively, were amplified, ligated into pGMT-easy vector (Promega), and sequenced to further confirm the genetic differences between the NILs at *GmSHMT08* locus.

# Nematode inoculation and collection of root tissues

Surface-sterilized soybean seeds were germinated on wet germination paper in the dark at 26 degrees for three days. Healthy 3-day-old seedlings were inoculated with about 3000 second-stage juvenile (J2s) of SCN HG type 0 (race 3) per seedling, by spreading the nematodes across the whole root of a seedling as previously described (Rambani *et al.*, 2015). Control and inoculated plants were arranged in replicates, each containing at least six plants and maintained in a controlled plant growth chamber at 26°C with 16-h light/8-h dark conditions. Five days post SCN-inoculation, roots tissues were collected from both inoculated and non-inoculated soybean roots in three biologically independent replicates. Uniformity of successful infection of each replicate was confirmed by examining one-fourth of the infected seedlings using acid fuschin stain (Daykin & Hussey, 1985). The two parental

lines (Fowler and Anand) were only mock-inoculated in the same experimental sittings and a total of 6 biologically independent samples were similarly collected five days later. DNA and RNA were extracted from the samples and used to construct methylC-seq and RNA-seq library.

# MethylC-seq library preparation, identification of differentially methylated regions, and overlapping genomic regions

Genomic DNA of the infected and non-infected root samples was extracted using DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) and used to construct methylC-seq libraries using Illumina TruSeq Library Prep kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA) as previously described (Rambani et al., 2015). Unmethylated lambda phage DNA was spiked in all samples before treatment with sodium bisulfite in order to determine the cytosine to uracil conversion rate. After quality control, the libraries were sequenced using Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. High quality paired-end reads were aligned to the soybean reference genome (Wm82.a2.v1) using Bismark with default parameters (Krueger & Andrews, 2011). Alignment files generated by Bismark were analyzed by the R bioconductor package methylKit (Akalin *et al.*, 2012) to identify differentially methylated cytosines. For each of the three biological replicate, three methylation call files for the CG, CHG, and CHH contexts were generated and used to identify differentially methylated regions (DMRs) as previously described (Rambani et al., 2015; Hewezi et al., 2017). DMRs were mapped to various genic regions including promoter (1 kb upstream of the transcription start site), 5' and 3' untranslated regions (UTRs), and gene body (transcribed region) using Bioconductor package rtracklayer in a custom R script (Lawrence et al., 2009). Methylation cytosine report files generated by Bismark were used to visualize global methylation levels over protein-coding genes and transposable elements (TEs) using ViewBS package (Huang et al., 2018).

The most recent assembly of soybean genome (Wm82.a2.v1) was released without annotation of TEs. Thus, sequences of previously annotated and known TEs in soybean assembly were obtained from SoyTEdb (Du, JC *et al.*, 2010). The TE sequences were used to mask the new soybean assembly using RepeatMasker (Smit *et al.*, 2013) and output file with TE coordinates in the new assembly was created. Then, overlaps of DMRs with TEs belonging to various families were reported. BEDtools

closest (Quinlan & Hall, 2010) were used to calculate the distance from DMR-associated TEs to the nearest gene.

# **RNA** library preparation and transcriptome analysis

mRNA was isolated using magnetic mRNA isolation kit (NEB). NEBnext mRNA library prep master mix (NEB) was used to build libraries following manufacturer's protocol. RNA-seq libraries were sequenced on Illumina Hiseq 2500 platform. Quality of paired-ended reads was verified with FastQC (version 0.11.4) and qualified reads were then mapped to the soybean reference genome (Wm82.a2.v1) using TOPHAT v.2.0.13 with default parameters (Trapnell *et al.*, 2009). Number of reads assigned to each gene was determined using HTSeq (Anders *et al.*, 2015) and used to determine differentially expressed genes using the R bioconductor package edgeR (Robinson *et al.*, 2010). Genes with false discovery rate of 0.1 or 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Separate count files were generated using the python-based package QoRTs for counting sequencing reads spanning exons for every gene (Hartley & Mullikin, 2015). The count files were used with the bioconductor R package JunctionSeq to determine differentially spliced transcripts (Hartley & Mullikin, 2016). Chi square tests of independence were used to determine the significance of the overlaps between different gene sets as previously detailed in Hewezi *et al.* (2012).

## Gene Ontology terms enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) terms enrichment analysis of differentially methylated genes and differentially expressed genes were determined using soybase tools and AgriGO database (Du, Z *et al.*, 2010). Statistically significant enriched GO terms were calculated using Fisher's exact test and Bonferroni multi-test adjustment with a q value less than 0.05. GO terms were clustered based on semantic similarity to other GO terms in the Uniprot database using REVIGO (Supek *et al.*, 2011).

Generation of transgenic hairy roots and nematode infection assays

The coding sequences of soybean genes were amplified from root cDNA and cloned under the control of a soybean polyubiquitin promoter and the RuBisCO small subunit terminator in the binary vector pG2RNAi2, which contains the superfolded green fluorescent protein (sGFP) to facilitate identification of transgenic hairy roots. All constructs were confirmed by sequencing. The constructs as well GFP-only control vector were transformed into Agrobacterium rhizogenes strain K599 and used for generation of transgenic soybean hairy roots. Transgenic hairy roots overexpressing these constructs were generated in seven-day-old seedlings of NIL-S using the method previously described by (Kereszt *et al.*, 2007). Transgenic hairy roots expressing the empty vector were used as control. The composite transgenic plants were planted in 656 cm<sup>3</sup> cone-tainers (6.4 cm  $\times$  25.4 cm) containing steam-sterilized sand mixed with top soil (3:1) and arranged in a randomized complete block design. Each composite plant was inoculated with about 3000 eggs of SCN race 3 (HG Type 0). The plants were maintained under controlled growth conditions (16-h day length and 26 °C) in Percival reach-in plant growth chambers. Five weeks after inoculation, the cysts were extracted from each plant separately and counted under a stereoscope. The number of cysts was used to calculate the female index as a percentage of the average number of cysts determined on the test lines relative to those determined on the control line. For each overexpression construct, cyst counts of at least 8 independent replicates were used to calculate female index.

Nematode susceptibility assays of the isogenic lines and their parents were conducted in the greenhouse using SCN HG type 0 (race 3). Seeds of each line were planted in pots (2 seeds per pot) containing soil : sand (1:1) mixture and organized in a randomized complete block design. Each pot was inoculated with approximately 4,000 eggs at seeding. Approximately 5 weeks after planting the cysts were blasted off the roots and counted under the microscope. The experiment was repeated twice each included at least 10 independent replicates per line. Statistically significant differences between the lines were calculated using t-tests with P value < 0.01.

# RESULTS

# **D**eveloping SCN–resistant and –susceptible near-isogenic soybean lines

Two near isogenic lines (NILs), TN09-16 and TN09-29, respectively containing the susceptible and resistant allele of Rhg4 (GmSHMT08), were generated. These NILs are highly homozygous recombinant inbred lines obtained from individual F<sub>13</sub> generation single plants from a cross between the SCN-resistant variety Fowler and the SCN-susceptible variety Anand. Homozygosity level of these NILs was estimated to be 0.9998 based on the number of inbreeding generations after the creation of F<sub>1</sub>. Using the SoySNP6K Illumina Infinium BeadChip genotyping array the NILs showed 91.2% genome similarity. SCN resistance in the parental line Fowler was acquired from the Plant Introduction (PI) 437654. PI 437654, which exhibits 'Peking-type' resistance, has been shown to carry-out the SCN resistance gene Rhg4 and three copies of rhg1a (Concibido et al., 2004; Cook et al., 2014). Initially, we used simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers associated with rhg1 (Satt309) and Rhg4 (Satt162 and Satt632) (Kazi et al., 2010) to test potential genetic differences between these two NILs at rhg1-a and Rhg4 loci. The resistant line TN09-29 (NIL-R) inherited the two resistant alleles from its 'Hartwig' ancestry. The susceptible line TN09-016 (NIL-S), however, did not inherit the *Rhg4* allele but did receive the *rhg1-a* resistant allele. We next cloned and sequenced the SCN resistance soluble NSF attachment proteins (GmSNAP18) genes and the serine hydroxymethyltransferase (GmSHMT08) at the rhg1-a and Rhg4 loci, respectively from the isogenic lines. While the nucleotide sequences of GmSNAP18 were identical in both lines, GmSHMT08 showed two single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), leading to R130P and Y358N amino acid substitutions between the NILs (Fig. S1). These two amino acid substitutions were previously reported to establish the difference between the resistant and susceptible alleles of *GmSHMT08* (Liu et al., 2012).

The parental lines and the NILs were also evaluated against SCN race 3 in greenhouse assays. The parental line Anand showed very high level of SCN susceptibility with more than 100 cysts per plant.

In contrast, Fowler showed high level of resistance with only one or two cysts occasionally seen in the inoculated plants (Fig. S2). Similarly, the two NILs showed completely opposite responses to SCN infection. NIL-S showed very high level of susceptibility, whereas NIL-R showed complete resistance to SCN (Fig. S2). It may be important to mention that the parental line Fowler and NIL-R showed a typical Peking-type resistance of rapid syncytium degeneration (Mitchum, 2016). These data confirm the results of the USDA Southern Uniform Soybean Test program (Gillen, 2012).

## The genomes of the near isogenic lines are substantially differentially methylated

To examine potential methylome differences between the NILs under non-infected conditions, methylC-seq libraries of root tissues of non-infected 8-day--old seedlings were generated and sequenced. From the three biological replicates, we obtained a total of 1.190E+09 100-bp reads for NIL-S and 1.159E+09 reads for NIL-R. After considering only uniquely mapped reads and elimination of duplicates,  $25.28 \times$  and  $36.46 \times$  coverage were attained for the NIL-S and NIL-R, respectively. Bisulfite conversion rate was estimated using the  $\lambda$  phage genome and found to be greater than 99.7%. Differentially methylated cytosines in the CG, CHH and CHG sequence contexts were determined for each biological sample separately and used to determine global methylation levels and differentially methylated regions (DMRs). We compared global methylation levels between NIL-S and NIL-R over genes and transposable elements (TEs) in the CG, CHG, and CHH contexts. Interestingly, differences in the global methylation levels between the two lines were detected in the body of protein-coding genes as well as upstream and downstream regions in all sequence contexts (Fig. 1a-c). Differences in global methylation levels between the two lines were also observed over the body and flanking regions of TEs (Fig. 1d-f). These initial analyses indicate that the genomes of the NILs are considerably differentially methylated.

To identify genomic regions with the most significant methylation differences, the genomes of these two lines were divided into 200-bp non-overlapping bins and then DMRs with at least 50% methylation differences were identified using a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.01. Using these stringent criteria, 21,852 unique DMRs between NIL-S and NIL-R were identified. Of these 21,852 DMRs, 4,180 and 11,211 DMRs overlapped with 3,666 protein-coding genes and 6,033 TEs, respectively (Fig. S3, and Tables S1 and S2). The remaining DMRs are located in unannotated

regions of the genome. When the 4,180 DMRs were mapped to various annotated features of proteincoding genes we found that CG-DMRs occur mostly in gene body and to a much lesser extent in gene promoters and 5' and 3' untranslated regions (UTRs) (Fig. S4). Notably, 70% of CHG-DMRs were found in gene body (Fig. S4). In contrast to CG- and CHG-DMRs, the number of CHH-DMRs overlapping with gene features was relatively small (Fig. S1). TE-associated DMRs were mapped to various transposon families. Remarkably, 88% of these DMRs were associated with long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons (Fig. S4). DMRs overlapping with the DNA transposons Helitron and Terminal Inverted Repeats (TIR) constituted about 10% of the total number of TE-associated DMRs (Fig. S5).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the 3,666 differentially methylated genes (DMGs) identified between NIL-S and NIL-R under non-infected conditions revealed overrepresentation of genes involved in various biological processes (Fig. 2a). Of note is that ontologies with functions related to chromatin silencing, RNA interference, DNA repair, production of siRNAs, and histone H3-K9 methylation were significantly overrepresented. GO term analysis of the DMGs were performed using hyper- and hypomethylated genes (in NIL-S vs NIL-R) separately to determine the association between hyper- and hypomethylated genes and ontologies related to epigenetic modifications. While ontologies related to chromatin silencing, production of siRNAs, and chromosome organization were enriched among the hypermethylated genes in NIL-S.

# Differential DNA methylation between the isogenic lines may prime their responses upon SCN infection

To examine the degree to which cytosine methylation impacts gene expression in the isogenic lines, RNA-seq libraries were generated from the same non-infected root samples used for DNA methylation analysis. The principal component analysis (PCA) of RNA-seq data clearly separated the biological replicates of the NIL-S from that of NIL-R (Fig. S6). The first two principal components are responsible for 83% of the variance in the data. We identified 948 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between NIL-S and NIL-R at a FDR of 0.05 (Table S3). Of these 948 DEGs, 587 were upregulated and 361 were downregulated (Table S3). GO term analysis revealed statistically

significant enrichment for categories related to wounding response, defense response, membrane disassembly, and intracellular signal transduction (Fig. 2b). This finding suggests that differences in priming response between NIL-S and NIL-R may contribute to their contrasted response to SCN infection. To examine this suggestion we compared the 948 DEGs with our list of previously identified syncytium DEGs (6,903) (Itha et al., 2007; Klink *et al.*, 2009; Klink *et al.*, 2011; Kandoth *et al.*, 2011). Interestingly, 267 genes were common between these two gene lists (Fig. 2c). This significant overlap (28.16%,  $\chi 2 = 38.91$ , P = 1.81E-08) further supports our suggestion. We also compared the 3,666 DMGs with both of the 948 DEGs and the previously identified syncytium DEGs, and an overlap of 52 and 463 genes was detected, respectively (Fig. 2d, e). Together, these data indicate that under non-infected conditions differential DNA methylation contributes to differential gene expression between the NILs that may prepare soybean plants to be to more quickly or aggressively responding to SCN infection.

# The susceptible and resistant isogenic lines exhibit contrasted DNA methylation patterns in response to SCN infection

We examined whether the methylomes of the isogenic lines are similarly altered in response to SCN (race 3) infection. MethylC-seq libraries were constructed from SCN-infected roots at 5 day post infection (dpi) and compared with the corresponding non-infected control samples. The 5 dpi was selected because differences between NIL-S and NIL-R were observed at this time point. Both infected and non-infected libraries were prepared from root tissues collected from the same experiments at the same time. Uniquely mapped reads represented about 26.19 and 35.84× genome coverages for the NIL-S and NIL-R, respectively. Differentially methylated cytosines were identified as described above and global methylation levels were compared between infected and non-infected samples over genes and TEs in all sequence contexts. In response to SCN infection, NIL-S showed reduced methylation levels over protein-coding genes and TEs in all sequence contexts compared with non-infected control (Fig. 3a-f). In sharp contrast, the SCN-infected samples of the resistant line showed increased methylation levels over protein-coding genes in all sequence contexts compared with non-infected control (Fig. 3g-i). Differences in global methylation patterns over TEs in response to SCN infection were also observed between the NILs (Fig. 2j-l). These analyses indicate that the

NILs exhibit contrasted DNA methylation patterns in response to SCN infection with hypomethylation being more predominant in the susceptible line, whereas hypermethylation being more predominant in the resistant line.

# DNA methylation patterns associated with the susceptible and resistant responses are highly specific

To localize the genome-wide DNA methylation profiles induced by SCN in both lines we identified DMRs as indicated above and mapped them to the annotated protein-coding genes (Fig. S7) and TEs (Fig. S8). In response to SCN infection we identified 50,040 DMRs in NIL-S in comparison with noninfected control. Of these, 7,584 DMRs (15.16%) overlapped with 6,252 protein-coding genes, and 28,100 DMRs (56.16%) overlapped with TEs (Fig. 4a, and Tables S4 and S5). Of the 28,100 TEassociated DMRs 1,676 (5.96%) were located in genes. The number of DMRs in NIL-R was dramatically lower (Fig. 4b). A total number of 5,080 DMRs were identified in the NIL-R infected samples compared with the NIL-R control samples (Fig. 4b). Of these, 1,296 DMRs (25.51%) overlapped with 1,293 protein-coding genes, and 2,356 DMRs (46.38%) overlapped with TEs (Fig. 4b, and Tables S6 and S7). Of the 2,356 TE-associated DMRs 178 (7.55%) were located in genes. The finding that the number of DMRs identified in the NIL-S is about 10 times more than those identified in the NIL-R indicates that DNA methylation reprogramming occurs prominently during the susceptible interaction and to a much lesser extent during the resistant interaction. When the DMRs overlapping with protein-coding genes and TEs were compared between the two lines, only 74 DMRs were common (Fig. 4c and Table S8). Of these 74 DMRs, 40 showed the same methylation patterns in both lines and the remaining 34 showed opposite patterns. Together, these findings indicate that DNA methylation patterns associated with the susceptible and resistant interactions are highly specific. This indication was further supported by GO term enrichment analysis showing the association of the DMGs in NIL-S and NIL-R with different GO biological process categories (Fig. 4d,e).

Consistent with the global methylation patterns shown in Figure 3, we found that the numbers of hypo-DMRs associated with protein-coding genes and TEs in various sequence contexts in NIL-S were higher than hyper-DMRs (Fig. 4f-k). In NIL-R, however, we observed the opposite trend with

hyper-DMRs being more predominant than hypo-DMRs (Fig. 4f-j). This finding further demonstrates the specificity of the patterns of DNA methylation associated with the susceptible and resistant interactions.

# DNA methylation reprogramming during susceptible and resistant interactions contributes to gene expression changes

We further studied gene expression changes in the NIL-S and NIL-R in response to SCN using RNAseq approach. The RNA-seq libraries were prepared from the same tissue samples used for DNA methylation analysis to facilitate examining the potential link between DNA methylation and transcriptome changes. PCA of RNA-seq data showed that the infected biological replicates of the NIL-S can be separated from those of NIL-R and that the first and second principal components explained 76% of the total variance in the data (Fig. S9). Because of the heterogeneity nature of SCNinfected roots we used a less stringent P value cutoff of < 0.05 and a FDR < 0.1 to identify DEGs. We identified 1,668 and 112 DEGs in NIL-S and NIL-R, respectively at 5 d post SCN infection in comparison with the corresponding non-infected control (Table S9 and S10). The low number of the DEGs identified in the resistant lines may reflect the localized response to SCN infection compared with the susceptible line in which localized and systemic responses may occur both in the developing syncytium as well as in cells far from the infection sites. GO analysis revealed a significant enrichment of three biological process terms associated with plant responses to oxidative stress, chemical stimulus, and oxidation reduction among the NIL-R DEGs. Among the NIL-S DEGs we noted a significant enrichment of biological process terms corresponding to plant response to stimulus, and signaling of various phytohormones, including ethylene, salicylic acid, jasmonic acid, and abscisic acid (Fig. S10a). Genes involved in ROS-mediated defense response, secondary cell wall biogenesis, cell wall loosening, membrane disassembly, and cellular responses to wounding and chitin were also overrepresented among the NIL-S DEGs (Fig. S10a).

Next, we determined the potential association between DNA methylation and gene expression changes in both lines in response to SCN infection. We compared the 1293 DMGs and the 112 DEGs

identified in the NIL-R upon SCN infection and only one gene (Glyma.04G180400) was common between the two gene lists (Fig. S10b). When the 1293 DMGs were compared with syncytium DEGs, 188 genes overlapped. These results imply an impact of DNA methylation on gene transcription during the resistant interaction. Similarly, we compared the 6252 DMGs and the 1668 DEGs identified in the NIL-S after SCN infection, and 123 genes were common between the two gene lists (Fig. S10c). Furthermore, we identified 50 DEGs containing differentially methylated TEs in their gene body or promoters, 2 kb upstream of the TSS, resulting in a unique list of 147 differentially expressed DMGs (Fig. S10c and Table S11). When the 6252 DMGs were compared with syncytium DEGs, 756 genes overlapped. The finding that several DMGs identified in the NIL-S and NIL-R are among syncytium DEGs suggest that DNA methylation reprogramming during SCN infection may have an impact on gene expression changes.

## **Identification of stably inherited DMRs**

To identify stably inherited DMRs in the genic regions with potential association with SCN resistance/susceptibility we searched for differential methylation in the isogenic lines that are inherited from the parents using non-infected samples. In other words, we were looking for DMRs with the exact genomic coordinates that are hypermethylated in the susceptible parent (Anand) and NIL-S but hypomethylated in the resistant parent (Fowler) and NIL-R, and vice versa (hypomethylated in Anand and NIL-S but hypermethylated in Fowler and NIL-R). Therefore, we generated methylC-seq libraries from the two parental lines Fowler and Anand using non-infected root tissues collected from the same experimental settings described above. A significant number of DMRs between the parental lines were also detected. Of the 45,603 DMRs detected, 7,000 mapped to protein-coding genes and 21,667 mapped to TEs (Tables S12 and S13). We then compared the methylomes of the parental lines and that of the isogenic lines under non-infected conditions. We identified 58 DMRs in the isogenic lines with differential methylation patterns that were inherited from the parents (Fig. 5a, and Table S14). As shown in Figure 5a, the 58 DMRs are grouped into two main clusters. The first cluster contains 38 regions that were hypomethylated in Fowler and NIL-R but hypermethylated in Anand and NIL-S. An example of these regions is highlighted in Figure 5b.

The second cluster contains 20 regions that were hypermethylated in Fowler and NIL-R but hypomethylated in Anand and NIL-S (Fig. 5a). These regions showed differential methylation in the CG (36) and non-CG contexts (22) and are located in the gene body (38), promoter (16) and 5'UTR (4) (Fig. 5c,d). These 58 DMRs overlapped with 57 unique protein-coding genes, four of them were previously reported as differentially expressed in soybean syncytium (Table S14).

## Identification of novel non-parental DMRs specific to the isogenic lines

We further extended these analyses by comparing the methylomes of the parental lines and the isogenic lines under non-infected conditions in order to identify novel non-parental DMRs unique to NIL-S or NIL-R. Interestingly, 56 DMRs unique to NIL-S were identified (Table S15). Gain or loss of DNA methylation in these regions occurred to a similar extent and was opposite of that detected in the parental lines and NIL-R (Fig. 6a). An example of these regions is provided in Figure 6b. The opposite methylation patterns were detected in CG (37), non-CG (16), and both CG and non-CG contexts (3) (Fig. 6c). The DMRs were located in gene body (42), gene promoters (9), and UTRs (5) (Fig. 6d). The 56 DMRs overlapped with 55 protein-coding genes (Table S15). This gene list included 9 of the previously identified syncytium DEGs (Table S15), implying a role in soybean-SCN interaction. We also identified a homolog of Arabidopsis mRNA splicing factor (Glyma.20G189700) among this gene list. This finding promoted us to examine our RNA-seq data for differential usage of exon and exon-exon junctions using JunctionSeq package (Hartley & Mullikin, 2016). Using a FDR cut-off of 0.1, we identified 12 alternatively spliced genes with 13 differentially used exons/junctions when the transcriptomes of NIL-S and NIL-R were compared under non-infected conditions (Fig. 6e, and Table S16). Of these exons/junctions, 10 were significantly highly used in NIL-S as in the case of Glyma17G149600 (Fig. 6f), whereas the remaining 3 were significantly highly used in NIL-R. Interestingly, we found that 4 out of these 12 differentially spliced genes are among the syncytium DEGs (Table S16), suggesting that novel non-parental hypomethylation of the splicing factorencoding gene in NIL-S may affect alternative splicing of syncytium DEGs, and thus modulate plant response to SCN infection. To examine this possibility, we overexpressed the splicing factor-encoding gene in NIL-S using soybean transgenic hairy root system and assayed the composite plants for SCN (race 3) resistance/susceptibility. Interestingly, overexpression of the splicing factor significantly enhanced plant susceptibility resulting in a female index of 285%, compared with the control plants expressing the empty vector (Fig. 6g).

Similarly, we compared the methylomes of the parental lines and the isogenic lines under noninfected conditions in order to identify novel non-parental DMRs unique to NIL-R. Interestingly, 102 DMRs specific to NIL-R were identified (Fig. 7a, and Table S17). Hyper- and hypomethylation in these 102 regions occurred to a similar extent and was opposite of that detected in the parental lines and NIL-S (Fig. 7a). An example of hypermethylated region specific to NIL-R is provided in Figure 7b. Differential methylation of these DMRs was found in CG (58) and non-CG (44) (Fig. 7c). The opposite methylation patterns were detected in gene promoter/5'UTR (23) and gene body/3'UTR (79) (Fig. 7d). The 102 DMRs overlapped with 100 protein-coding genes (Table S17), 11 of them previously reported to change expression in SCN-induced syncytium (Fig. 7e,f). Together, these data suggest that novel non-parental DMRs specific to the isogenic lines may impact gene expression in the nematode feeding sites and hence soybean-SCN interaction.

# Genes with introduced differential DNA methylation are of biological significance

We finally examined if genes associated with the identified novel non-parental DMRs are of biological significance and impact plant response to SCN infection. Four genes with introduced differential DNA methylation specific to NIL-S (Glyma.17G216000 and Glyma.16G174500) or NIL-R (Glyma.09G198900 and Glyma.02G071300) were functionally characterized. These genes, which code for an aspartate aminotransferase5 (Glyma.17G216000), a receptor-like protein kinase (Glyma.16G174500), a disease resistance-responsive (dirigent-like protein) family protein (Glyma.09G198900), and a transcription regulator of the NOT2/NOT3/NOT5 family protein (Glyma.02G071300) were overexpressed in NIL-S using soybean transgenic hairy root system. The composite transgenic plants were assayed for SCN (race 3) response. Interestingly, overexpression of the *aspartate aminotransferase5* and the *NOT2/NOT3/NOT5 family transcription regulator* dramatically increased plant susceptibility to SCN resulting in a female index of more than 300% (Fig. 8). In contrast, overexpression of the *dirigent-like protein* and the *receptor-like protein kinase* 

significantly increased plant resistance compared with the susceptible control, resulting in a female index of about 30% (Fig. 8). These results indicate that genes associated with regions that acquired novel non-parental DNA methylation patterns are likely to play role in soybean-SCN interaction.

## DISCUSSION

Our analyses demonstrated substantial differences in DNA methylation patterns and gene expression levels between the isogenic lines both under non-infected and SCN-infected conditions (Table S18). Under non-infected conditions, we observed a general trend of increased global DNA methylation in NIL-R in comparison with NIL-S over the body of protein-coding genes and TEs in the CG context (Fig. 1a,d). However, NIL-S exhibited increased DNA methylation over protein-coding genes and TEs in CHG and CHH contexts (Fig. 1b-f). This finding suggests that global decrease in CG methylation in NIL-S was complemented with an increase of CHG and CHH methylation. Indeed, the global increase of CHG and CHH methylation in the susceptible line could be the result of decreasing demethylation activity and/or increasing de novo methylation in order to equilibrate whole genome methylation level. In agreement with this assumption, our analysis revealed a significant number of CHG-DMRs in the body of protein-coding genes when the methylomes of the two isogenic lines were compared under non-infected conditions (Fig. S3b). The isogenic lines also exhibited high level of differential DNA methylation in TEs, particularly LTR-type in the CHG context (Fig. S5). This result may reflect differences in the transposition dynamics of Copia and Gypsy retrotransposons, which tend to be more vulnerable to DNA methylation than other types of TEs (Wang et al., 2015; Hewezi et al., 2017).

Methylome changes in the isogenic lines under non-infected conditions appeared to impact the methylation patterns of several genes involved in epigenetic regulation (Fig. 2a). It is well known that various epigenetic components including DNA methylation, histone modification and siRNA accumulation are highly interconnected (Zilberman *et al.*, 2008; Saze *et al.*, 2012; Du *et al.*, 2015). Thus, differential methylation of key genes involved in these pathways may be part of epigenetic

feedback regulatory mechanisms that maintain epigenetic information and may explain the increased level of global non-CG methylation in the susceptible line. Maintenance of epigenetic information could contribute to priming of plant defense responses (Holoch & Moazed, 2015; To et al., 2015). Our RNA-seq analysis of the NILs under non-infected conditions pointed into a possible difference in some type of defense priming between the lines. This possibility was further reinforced by our finding that the DEGs were highly enriched for genes involved in defense responses and biological processes associated with nematode susceptibility (Fig. 2b). Interestingly, many of these genes showed higher expression in the NIL-R relative to NIL-S. In addition, about one-third of the DEGs were previously shown to change expression in the SCN-induced syncytium. Furthermore, the DEGs included numerous marker genes of defense priming, including several WRKY transcription factors, ROSrelated genes, and *lipoxygenases* (Martinez-Medina et al., 2016; Mauch-Mani et al., 2017). Recently, it has been shown that Arabidopsis hyper- and hypo-methylated mutants developed opposite responses to pathogen infection that were dependent on the mutants' ability to prime salicylic acidmediated defense responses and callose deposition (Lopez Sanchez et al., 2016). In this context, DNA methylation can prime defense responses in a way that alters chromatin structure to expedite gene transcription (Martinez-Medina et al., 2016; Mauch-Mani et al., 2017).

The differences between the isogenic lines became more evident when the methylomes of the isogenic lines were compared under SCN-infected conditions. Consistent with the role of SHMTs in cellular methylation (Locasale, 2013; Mitchum, 2016), the susceptible line, which contains a non-functional allele of *GmSHMT08*, exhibited reduced global methylation levels in both protein-coding genes and TEs, whereas the resistant line showed the opposite response of increased global methylation levels (Fig. 3). This trend of reduced global DNA methylation levels was observed in all three methylation contexts, suggesting that increased flux of one-carbon units from functional GmSHMT08, as a result of its increased activity upon SCN infection (Liu *et al.*, 2012; Kandoth *et al.*, 2017; Lakhssassi *et al.*, 2019; Patil *et al.*, 2019), may indirectly impact cellular methyltransferase activity and subsequently the whole DNA methylation pathway. However, other genetic factors that contributed to methylome changes between the isogenic lines can't be ruled out. Also, our results show that the methylome of

the susceptible line is more dynamic than that of the resistant line in response to SCN infection, as 50,040 DMRs were identified in the susceptible line compared to only 5,080 DMRs in the resistant line (Fig. 4a,b). This dynamic can be explained by the many cellular processes that accompany syncytium formation and development during the susceptible interaction compared to localized cell death that occurs during the resistant interaction.

Differential DNA methylation in the NIL-S appears to influence cellular functions that are directly modulated by cyst nematode effectors. For example, genes involved in pectin demethylesterification, polyamine oxidation, histone deacetylation, which are targeted by the cyst nematode effectors cellulose binding protein, 10A06, and 32E03, respectively (Hewezi *et al.*, 2008b; Hewezi *et al.*, 2010) were among the 147 differentially expressed/DMGs. The potential effect of DNA methylation on gene transcription was obvious in many situations as in the case of an *adaptin family protein* gene, which was hypomethylated in the promoter, gene body, and 3'UTR in various sequence contexts and highly induced in response to SCN infection. Adaptin family proteins are involved in intracellular protein trafficking (Boehm & Bonifacino, 2001) and thus hypomethylation-mediated upregulation of this gene may facilitate assimilate flow to the syncytium. Another example of the impact of DNA methylation is the downregulation of cycling DOF factor 2 (CDF2), which was hypomethylated in gene body in the CG context, but was hypermethylated in the promoter region in the CHH context. In Arabidopsis, CDF2 has been reported to regulate the expression of a number of miRNA genes at both transcriptional and posttranscriptional levels by direct binding to miRNA promoters or through modulation of DCL1-mediated processing of primary miRNA transcripts (Sun et al., 2015). Thus, reprogramming of DNA methylation patterns may function in concert with other epigenetic pathways during SCN parasitism. Interestingly, among the 756 syncytium DMGs identified in the NIL-S we found several mediators of gene splicing, cell differentiation and morphogenesis, inter-and intercellular transport functions, and cell redox homeostasis. Thus, modulation or stabilization the transcriptional activity of these genes in the syncytium via DNA methylation may facilitate SCN parasitism.

The methylome analysis of the parental lines and the two isogenic lines allowed us to identify 56 and 102 regions that exhibit novel non-parental methylation patterns unique to NIL-S or NIL-R,

respectively (Fig. 6a and Fig. 7a). Our data suggest that the novel DNA methylation patterns introduced to the isogenic lines was faithfully maintained probably through the activity of MET1 and CMT3. In contrast, loss of DNA methylation in certain genic regions could be the result of absence of corrective DNA methylation mechanisms that can restore DNA methylation. Failure to reestablish DNA methylation in specific genic regions may be owing to the loss of the repressive histone mark H3K9me2, which has been shown to be directly or indirectly linked to the activity of MET1 and CMT3-mediated DNA methylation in CG and non-CG contexts (Bernatavichute *et al.*, 2008; Inagaki *et al.*, 2010; Du *et al.*, 2012).

The non-parental methylation patterns in these genic regions could be the results of DNA methylation changes in TEs located in proximity to genes, specifically those transposons that are prone to DNA methylation change under infected conditions. In agreement with this view we found that 12 of the 56 DMRs that are unique to NIL-S were located within 2 Kb of differentially methylated TEs identified in this line in response to SCN infection. Previously, it has been shown that TEs can induce heritable epialleles by bringing neighboring genes under their own regulation (Slotkin & Martienssen, 2007). We also examined whether these 56 DMRs are particularly vulnerable to DNA methylation changes under SCN infection. Interestingly, we found that 18 of these DMRs are among those identified in NIL-S in response to SCN infection. Similarly, 20 of the 102 DMRs that are unique to NIL-R are among those showing differential DNA methylation under infected conditions. Together, these findings suggest that certain non-parental methylation patterns occur in regions that are vulnerable to methylation changes. Recently, it has been reported that a small portion of stress-induced DNA methylation changes can be faithfully transmitted to next generations (Wibowo et al., 2016). The biological significance of the newly acquired DNA methylation patterns is manifested by our finding that some genes associated with these regions were previously shown to change expression in the SCN-induced syncytium (Fig. 7e). More importantly, overexpression of 4 genes overlapping with these regions significantly altered soybean response to SCN infection (Fig. 8). Thus, we conclude that non-parental methylation patterns in the NILs may be biased towards loci associated with plant resistance/susceptibility to SCN.
