#### 38 **Abstract**

39 Phosphate (Pi) starvation induces a suite of adaptive responses aimed at recalibrating cellular 40 Pi homeostasis. Plants harboring a mutation in *OVARIAN TUMOR DOMAIN-CONTAINING*  41 *DEUBIQUITILATING ENZYME5* (*OTU5*) showed altered DNA methylation of root hair-42 related genes and altered Pi-responsive root traits. Unlike the wild type, homozygous *otu5* 43 mutants did not respond to Pi starvation by increased lateral root formation and increased root 44 hair length, but formed very short root hairs when grown on low Pi media. Under Pi-replete 45 conditions, *otu5* plants developed more root hairs than the wild type due to attenuated primary 46 root growth, a phenotype which resembled that of Pi-deficient plants. Growth of plants on 47 low Pi media altered both H3K4 and H3K27 trimethylation levels at the transcriptional start 48 site of a subset of genes encoding key players in Pi homeostasis, which was correlated with 49 mRNA abundance changes of these genes. Pi starvation had a minor impact on DNA 50 methylation. Differentially methylated regions were enriched in transposable elements, 51 suggesting that DNA methylation associated with low Pi supply is required for maintaining 52 genome integrity. It is concluded that DNA methylation and histone methylation constitute 53 critical, interdependent regulatory components which orchestrate the activity of a subset of Pi-54 responsive genes.

#### 57 **Introduction**

58 As a key component of nucleic acids, membrane phospholipids, signaling cascades and 59 energy metabolism, phosphorus (P) is essential for plant development and reproduction. 60 Plants take up P in the form of inorganic phosphate (Pi). Due to numerous interactions with 61 soil components, the phyto-availability of Pi can drop to levels well below the requirement of 62 plants, impairing plant growth and productivity. Pi starvation triggers a suite of plant 63 responses that improve the uptake, internal use and recycling of Pi. This is achieved through a 64 combination of reprogrammed energy and lipid metabolism, expression of Pi transporters, and 65 altered root development (Ticconi and Abel, 2004; Desnos, 2008; Giehl et al., 2014). In 66 response to low Pi availability, primary root growth is attenuated while the development of 67 lateral roots is enhanced to improve foraging of Pi in topsoil layers enriched in Pi. Low Pi 68 conditions further increases the length and density of root hairs by expanding the duration of 69 tip growth and reduced elongation of root epidermal cells, thereby enhancing the radial reach 70 of the root (Bates and Lynch, 2000; Gilroy and Jones, 2000; Salazar-Henao et al., 2016).

71

72 Root hair morphogenesis and the expression of Pi starvation responsive (PSR) genes 73 are affected by alterations in chromatin structure. For example, compromised deposition of 74 the histone variant H2A.Z in mutants defective in the expression of *ARP6*, a subunit of the 75 SWR1 ATP-dependent chromatin-remodeling complex, was associated with constitutive 76 activation of a suite of PSR genes (Smith et al., 2010). Also, *arp6* plants formed longer and 77 more abundant root hairs under Pi-replete conditions, a phenotype that is typical of Pi-78 deficient plants. Similarly, mutants defective in the expression of the histone deacetylase 79 *HDA19* showed alterations in Pi-responsive root traits and deregulation of a subset of PSR 80 genes (Chen et al., 2015).

81

82 DNA cytosine methylation (mC) is an epigenetic modification that is important for 83 transcriptional regulation such as silencing of transposable elements (TEs) (He et al., 2011). 84 In plants, DNA methylation can respond rapidly and dynamically to environmental stimuli 85 (Labra et al., 2002; Aina et al., 2004; Meaney and Szyf, 2005; Choi and Sano, 2007). In rice, 86 Pi starvation increases DNA methylation in TEs close to highly induced genes (Secco et al., 87 2015). In *Arabidopsis*, different effects of Pi starvation on global DNA methylation were

88 reported (Secco et al., 2015; Yong-Villalobos et al., 2015). While Secco *et al.* (2015) 89 observed very limited effects of Pi starvation on the DNA methylation landscape in 90 *Arabidopsis*, which were mainly located in TEs or in genes with undetectable expression 91 level, Yong-Villalobos *et al.* (2015) reported highly dynamic changes in DNA methylation 92 levels upon Pi starvation in genic regions, which were associated with transcriptional 93 regulation. Furthermore, mutants harboring defects in DNA methylation were found to be 94 unable to regulate a subset of PSR genes, suggesting that some PSR components are 95 epigenetically controlled by Pi deficiency-induced changes in mC (Yong-Villalobos et al., 96 2015).

97

98 Deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) are specialized proteases that remove individual 99 ubiquitin molecules or ubiquitin chains attached to target proteins. DUBs are involved in 100 important biological processes such as proofreading ubiquitin–protein conjugates, chromatin 101 remodeling, transcriptional regulation, cell-cycle regulation, DNA repair and signal 102 transduction (Reyes-Turcu et al., 2009). Although clear roles in cellular processes have been 103 defined for OTU deubiquitinases in *Drosophila*, *S. cerevisiae* and *H. sapiens* (King and 104 Storto, 1988; Steinhauer et al., 1989; Lee et al., 2000; Stanisic et al., 2009; Nakada et al., 105 2010), in plants a biological role has only been described for OTLD1, which functions as a 106 direct interactor of the histone lysine demethylase complex KDM1C to suppress the 107 expression of a subset of genes *via* deubiquitination of histone H2B (Krichevsky et al., 2011). 108

109 Phenotypic analysis of plants defective in the expression of members of the OTU 110 family revealed that similar to *arp6*, Pi-replete *otu5* plants display a root hair phenotype that 111 resembles that of Pi-deficient plants. To understand the impact of Pi starvation on the 112 epigenome and the role of OTU5 in root development, we performed comprehensive surveys 113 of histone modifications, genome-wide DNA methylation and transcription profiles of the 114 *otu5* mutant and its wild type under Pi-replete and Pi-deficient conditions. We found that gene 115 regulation by OTU5 is conditionally dependent on the availability of Pi. Gene expression 116 patterns validated significant interaction of root phenotypes with the environment. It is 117 hypothesized that the function of OTU5 is associated with local alterations in DNA 118 methylation, which directly or indirectly affect the activity of genes involved in root hair

- 119 morphogenesis and primary root growth, traits that are also responsive to Pi starvation.
- 120 Growth on low Pi media altered transcriptional profiles, which was partly correlated with
- 121 both H3K4 and H3K27 trimethylation at promoters. Intriguingly, differentially histone
- 122 methylated genes encode key players in Pi acquisition and cellular Pi homeostasis. Our data
- 123 suggest that DNA and histone methylation act interdependently to interpret environmental
- 124 information.

#### 127 **Results**

128 **The** *otu5* **mutant shows genotype-specific responses to low Pi availability** 

129 As anticipated from previous studies, wild-type plants responded to Pi starvation with an 130 increase in root hair density and length, a decrease in primary root length, and an increase in 131 the number of lateral roots (Fig. 1). By contrast, homozygous *otu5* mutants formed longer and 132 denser root hairs than the wild type under Pi-replete conditions, but produced short root hairs 133 with a density typical of Pi-deficient wild-type plants (Fig. 1A and B). Similarly, under Pi-134 replete conditions, the primary root length of *otu5* plants was comparable to that observed for 135 Pi-deficient wild-type plants (Fig. 1C). In *otu5*, primary root length was further decreased 136 upon exposure to low Pi media, but to a lesser extent than in the wild type. No increase in the 137 number of lateral roots was observed in *otu5* plants (Fig. 1D). Interactions between genotype 138 and environment (G × E; E = Pi conditions) were statistically significant for both the length 139 and density of root hairs and for the number of lateral roots (two-way ANOVA, *P* <0.05) 140 (Table S1). Transgenic lines containing a wild-type copy of the *OTU5* gene in the *otu5* 141 background showed a phenotype resembling that of the wild type in all the above-mentioned

142 traits.

143

144 Next, we analyzed the gene expression profiles of wild-type and *otu5* plants grown 145 under Pi-replete and low Pi conditions by RNA-seq (Table S2). Differential gene expression 146 was observed between all four groups (*i.e.* two growth conditions and two genotypes). 147 Hierarchical clustering of these transcriptomes shows that both the Pi supply and the mutation 148 in *OTU5* impacted the transcriptome of the roots (Fig. 2A). It can also be inferred from the 149 analysis that the Pi supply had a much broader impact on the transcriptional profile compared 150 to that of the *OTU5* mutation (Fig. 2A).

151 Principal component analysis (PCA) shows high variation between plants supplied with two 152 different Pi concentration (PC1) as well as between *otu5* and the wild type under both growth 153 conditions (low Pi and Pi-replete), which is in line with the hierarchical clustering. In 154 addition, PCA also revealed higher variation among the triplicates from plants grown under 155 low Pi conditions than in those derived from plants grown on Pi-replete media, indicative of a 156 higher level of transcriptional noise under the former conditions (Fig. 2B). This conclusion is 157 confirmed by the fact that the mean expression standard deviations (SDs) of plants grown in

158 low Pi condition were higher when compared to those grown on Pi-replete media (Table S3).

- 160 In order to understand the relationships between these groups, we performed a weighted gene
- 161 co-expression network analysis (WGCNA; Langfelder and Horvath, 2008). This is an
- 162 unbiased and unsupervised analysis which identifies modules corresponding to clusters of co-
- 163 expressed transcripts in each group. This analysis identified three major modules containing
- 164 tightly co-expressed genes (Fig. S1; Dataset S1). Two of the modules are specific to *otu5* and
- 165 low Pi, respectively, suggesting that despite producing similar root phenotypes, Pi deficiency
- 166 and non-functional OTU5 affected the expression of different sets of genes.

### 168 **OTU5 modulates the transcriptional response to low Pi**

169 Growth on low Pi media increased the number of genes that were differentially expressed 170 (DEGs) between *otu5* mutant and wild-type plants (Fig. 2C and Fig. S2). Of the 633 DEGs, 171 only 118 (19%) were differentially expressed between *otu5* and the wild type under both 172 growth conditions, indicating that the effect of OTU5 on gene expression is dependent on the 173 availability of Pi (Fig. 2D; Dataset S2). Gene ontology analysis showed that among the 319 174 OTU5-dependent genes that were only differentially expressed between *otu5* and wild-type 175 plants when plants were grown under low Pi conditions, 25 genes are related to root hair 176 development (Fig. 2E; Table S4). This subset includes several genes that play key roles in 177 root hair development, *e.g. MRH6*, *XTH14*, *COW1*, and several *ROOT HAIR SPECIFIC* 178 (*RHS*) genes that carry the RHE (Root Hair Element) consensus sequence in their promoters 179 (Kim et al., 2006). Of particular interest is the presence of *ROOT HAIR DEFECTIVE 6-* 180 *LIKE2* (*RSL2*) in this subset, which encodes a functional homolog of RSL4 that acts as an 181 integrator of developmental and environmental signals and controls the elongation of root 182 hairs (Datta et al., 2015). Consistent with the short root hair phenotype of Pi-deficient *otu5* 183 plants, all of the root hair genes were down-regulated in the mutant under Pi-deficient 184 conditions. In addition to the low Pi condition, we also identified 314 OTU5-dependent DEGs 185 under Pi-replete condition (Fig. 2C). Of which, 2 genes *IRON-REGULATED TRANSPORTER*  186 *1* (*IRT1*) and AT5G62340 are related to root hair development. These OTU5-dependent 187 DEGs specific to low and replete Pi-conditions are associated with root hair initiation and 188 root development, potentially resulted in the observed root phenotypes. 189

190 Two-way ANOVA identified 48 genes with statistically significant interaction in their

191 expression in *otu5 vs.* wild type and the two Pi conditions (Fig. 2F; Table S5). Several genes

192 from this subset have predicted or experimentally verified roles in root hair formation

194 derived from genes encoding proteins involved in cell wall organization and cell wall

## 198 **OTU5 is critical for the establishment of DNA methylation patterns**

199 To investigate whether the root phenotype of *otu5* plants is caused by differential mC, we 200 conducted whole genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) of roots from wild-type and *otu5*  201 mutant plants grown under Pi-replete and low Pi conditions (see Table S7 for mapping 202 statistics and methods for WGBS). Methylation of cytosines was categorized into CG, CHG, 203 and CHH contexts (where H denotes A, T or C) for subsequent analyses.

204

205 Global methylation levels were calculated from 3.7 M, 3.9 M and 14.6 M of common 206 sites in the CG, CHG and CHH context, respectively, and found to be similar in wild-type and 207 *otu5* plants in all three contexts (Fig. 3A). On average, the methylation level of *otu5* roots was 208 approximately 31% for CG, 12% for CHG, and 3% for CHH sites. Although the global 209 differences in methylation levels (∆ methylation) between *otu5* and wild type were minor 210 (0.35%, 0.4% and 0.05% in CG, CHG, and CHH, respectively), highly variable mCs were 211 observed locally in several chromosomal regions. Methylation levels of large genomic 212 regions were compared using tiling windows of 100 K bp. Figure 3B marks the top 10% of 213 the regions with differential CG methylation between *otu5* and the wild type (both ∆ 214 methylation and log2 ratio of methylation levels) in more than three serial windows. The 215 pericentromeric regions of chromosome 2 contain clusters of hypo-methylated regions in *otu5* 216 relative to the wild type, and several hyper-methylated regions were observed throughout the 217 *otu5* genome. Hierarchical clustering revealed marked OTU5-driven differences in 218 methylation patterns (Fig. 3C and Fig. S3A). Together the results suggest that OTU5 defines a 219 distinct DNA methylation profile by targeting local genomic regions. 220

## 242 **Pi deficiency-induced changes in the methylome**

243 Environmental conditions such as Pi deficiency can alter both gene expression and DNA

244 methylation patterns (Yong-Villalobos et al., 2015). In total, we identified 62, 31, and 76

245 DMRs between low Pi and Pi-replete conditions in the CG, CHG, and CHH contexts,

246 respectively (Dataset S5). The distribution of differentially methylated cytosines did not

247 enrich towards any specific sequence contexts (Fig. S6A) when compared to the genome

248 distribution of cytosines in CG, CHG, and CHH contexts of common cytosines among the

249 samples (Fig. S6B). CG-DMRs associated with Pi supply were enriched in intergenic regions

250 and TEs, a pattern that is very different from what was observed for DMRs between *otu5* and

- 251 wild type in which DMRs were mainly observed within genes (Fig. 3I). This suggests that
- 252 genetic (*otu5*) and environmental (low Pi) conditions differentially impact DNA methylation.

253 Only 27 protein-coding genes were differentially methylated between Pi-replete and Pi-

- 254 deficient plants (Table S9). Both DMGs and genes in the vicinity of intergenic regions in
- 255 which DMRs were observed showed little alterations in gene expression (Dataset S6). No
- 256 overlap of DEGs and DMGs was observed between Pi-replete and Pi-deficient plants (Fig.
- 257 S4B), suggesting that transcriptional regulation of Pi-responsive genes is not linked to
- 258 differential methylation.

## 260 **Pi starvation induces differential trimethylation of H3K4 of a suite of Pi starvation genes**

261 DNA and histone lysine methylation systems are highly interrelated, resulting in an epigenetic 262 code that orchestrates gene activity. In particular, DNA methylation is correlated with the

263 absence of methylation of lysine 4 of histone 3 (H3K4) (Cedar and Bergman, 2009). To

264 investigate whether the alterations in DNA methylation are correlated with changes in histone

265 3 methylation pattern, we determined the level of trimethylated H3K4 (H3K4me3), which is 266 enriched in transcriptionally active promoters, and of trimethylated H3K27 (H3K27me3) as a

267 repressive histone modification in wild type and *otu5* plants under Pi-replete and Pi-deficient 268 conditions using ChIP-seq. Globally, major differences were neither observed between plants

269 grown with different Pi supply nor between the genotypes (Fig. 4A and Fig. S7), indicating

270 that both Pi supply and the *otu5* mutation did not alter the genome-wide distribution of these

271 two histone marks. Although no global changes of H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 abundance

272 were observed, it is possible that local changes are associated with the expression or DNA

273 methylation of individual genes. Plotting expression changes against alterations in H3K4me3

274 and H3K27me3 induced by growth on low Pi revealed a subset of DEGs in which H3K4me3

275 was positively and H3K27me3 negatively correlated with transcriptional changes in both

276 wild-type and *otu5* backgrounds (Fig. 4B and Fig. S8). Among 39 low Pi-induced DEGs that

277 are all upregulated in low Pi condition with clear change in histone marks, we observed at the

278 transcriptional start site (TSS) a clear enrichment of H3K4me3 (active mark) in low Pi

279 condition, and a slightly less clear enrichment of H3K27me3 (repressive mark) in high Pi

280 condition (Fig. 4C and Fig. S9). Interestingly, there is no clear change of H3K4me3 and

281 H3K27me3 abundance was observed in low Pi-induced DMGs, indicating that DNA

282 methylation is not tightly associated with H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 (Fig. 4D).

283

284 Very few differentially methylated genes between *otu5* mutant and the wild type did

285 show difference in H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 abundance (Fig. S10), but significant

286 alterations in H3K4 and H3K27 trimethylation were observed for a small subset of DEGs

287 (Table S10). Growth on low Pi media yielded the smallest difference between the two

288 genotypes. Under both Pi-replete and Pi-deficient conditions, none of the genes relevant for

289 root hair formation were differentially trimethylated at H3K4 between *otu5* and the wild type.

290 Only a small subset of genes that showed marked anti-correlation between DNA methylation

291 and mRNA expression exhibited differential H3K4me3 abundance of (Fig. S11A).

292

293 Within the 450 low Pi-induced DEGs in wild-type plants, 47 genes showed 294 differential H3K4me3 marks at the TSS between Pi-replete and Pi-deficient wild-type plants, 295 the majority (37 genes) of which showed increased abundance of H3K4 trimethylation 296 (Dataset S7). This group comprises several highly Pi deficiency-induced key regulators of Pi 297 homeostasis such as the non-coding RNAs *AT4* and *IPS1* as well as the SPX 298 (SYG1/Pho81/XPR1)-domain proteins SPX1 and SPX3. Root-specific co-expression analysis 299 of genes with both differential expression and differential H3K4me3 in low Pi (Dataset S7) 300 revealed a cluster of putatively co-regulated genes encoding proteins involved in lipid 301 metabolism such as *MGD3*, *GDPD1*, *PLD*ζ*2*, the phosphatase *PFA-DSP4,* and three proteins 302 of unknown functions, which may represent novel players in this process (Fig. 5A and Fig. 303 S11B). A subset of these genes have proven roles in membrane lipid remodeling (Fig. 5B), a 304 process that recycles Pi from phospholipids, which is strongly induced upon Pi starvation 305 (Fig. S11B) (Nakamura, 2013). Among the genes that showed both decreased expression and 306 H3K4me3 upon Pi starvation, three genes, *IRP1*, *IRP6* and *bHLH39*, have been shown to be 307 strongly induced by iron starvation (Rodríguez-Celma et al., 2013) and may constitute a 308 regulatory unit that prevents the accumulation of toxic iron levels under conditions of Pi 309 starvation (Fig. 6A and Fig. S11C). A subset of 9 low Pi DEGs was differentially 310 trimethylated at H3K27 (Dataset S7). Most interestingly, a suite of 7 low Pi DEGs showed 311 anti-correlated H3K4 and H3K27 trimethylation at their promoters (Dataset S7), including 312 *NPC4*, *PFA-DSP2, PFA-DSP4*, *IPS1* and the Pi transporter *Pht1;4*. It can thus be concluded 313 that differential trimethylation of H3K4 and H3K27 is involved in the transcriptional 314 regulation of genes with critical function in Pi homeostasis. 315

### 317 **Discussion**

318 Homozygous *otu5* plants exhibit a pleiotropic phenotype that comprises alterations in root 319 architecture and root hair development. Chromatin-level regulation of root traits, particularly 320 those responsive to Pi-deficient conditions, has been inferred from previous studies. A 321 phenotype similar to that of *otu5* was observed for *arp6*, a mutant which harbors a defect in a 322 subunit of the chromatin remodeler SWR1 that mediates eviction of the canonical histone 323 H2A-H2B dimer and deposition of the H2A.Z-H2B pair (Smith et al., 2010). Furthermore, the 324 histone deacetylase HDA19 was identified as regulator of epidermal cell length, root hair 325 frequency and several Pi-responsive genes (Chen et al., 2015).

326

327 A suite of genes that are likely to contribute to the root hair phenotype was found to 328 have significant G × E interactions (Table S6). This group comprised several proteins that are 329 essential for the assembly, modification and growth of cell walls. Notably, several genes 330 involved in DNA methylation such as *MET1, NRPD2A, DCL3* and *DCL4* (Chan et al., 2005; 331 Matzke and Mosher, 2014) were differentially methylated in *otu5* (Fig. 6B). This may 332 introduce a feed forward loop that amplifies the methylation of target genes. Similarly, 333 several genes involved in chromatin remodeling (*INO80*, *ARP5*, *CHR3*, *CHR4*, *CHR6*, 334 *CHR20*, *SDG3*) were differentially methylated and may alter the activity of downstream 335 genes at the transcriptional level or indirectly *via* impacting the expression of regulatory 336 proteins (Fig. 6C). It can thus be speculated that effects on chromatin structure are amplified 337 and diversified in the *otu5* mutant by altering methylation levels of genes that mediate such 338 changes.

339

340 Notably, the expression of the genes which showed significant G × E interactions does 341 not appear to be regulated by mC or histone lysine methylation. Inspection of GC meta plots 342 shows that GC methylation is similar among all four groups with minor differences between 343 wild-type and mutant plants downstream of the TSS (Fig. S3B). Meta plots of H3K4me3 also 344 show similarity between the four sample groups with a slightly higher trimetylation 345 abundance under low Pi conditions (Fig. S3C). H3K27me3meta plots reveal a slightly higher 346 level of trimethylation in *otu5* samples, which was, however, not negatively correlated with 347 gene expression. It can thus be inferred that the observed G × E interactions are not directly 348 caused by changes in the DNA or histone lysine methylation patterns.

| 355 | al., 2015), the phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate phosphatase RHD4 (Stenzel et al., 2008;         |
| 356 | Thole et al., 2008; Wada et al., 2015), the ubiquitin-specific protease UBP14 (Li et al., 2010), |
| 357 | the patterning protein TRN1 (Kwak et al., 2015) and the P5-type ATPase PDR2, a key player        |
| 358 | in root Pi sensing (Wang et al., 2010; Müller et al., 2015).                                     |
| 359 |                                                                                                  |
| 360 | Our study showed a very limited impact of Pi starvation on DNA methylation levels                |
| 361 | which did not correlate with changes in transcriptional profiles, indicating that DNA            |
| 362 | methylation has a minor effect on gene activity at the transcriptional level under these         |
| 363 | conditions. Similar to what has been observed by Secco et al. (2015) for rice, Pi deficiency     |
| 364 | induced DMRs were mainly located in TEs or intergenic regions. However, subtle differences       |
| 365 | in the abundance of proteins caused by differential methylation and/or other changes at the      |
| 366 | chromatin level may still affect the phenotypic readout. For example, a synonymous               |
| 367 | substitution in the third position of a conserved proline in the 12th<br>exon of UBP14 led to a  |
| 368 | minor decrease in protein abundance, which compromised root hair elongation under Pi             |
| 369 | deficient but not under Pi-replete conditions (Fig. 6D) (Li et al., 2010). In both mammals and   |
| 370 | plants, DNA methylation is closely interlinked with the positioning of nucleosomes               |
| 371 | (Chodavarapu et al., 2010; Zhu et al., 2013). A plausible mechanism for the action of OTU5       |
| 372 | in roots could thus lie in the positioning of nucleosomes, probably as a component of a          |
| 373 | chromatin remodeler complex, which may affect post-transcriptional processes that alter the      |
| 374 | activity of root hair-related genes.                                                             |
| 375 |                                                                                                  |
| 376 | Whereas changes in mC were largely incongruent with alterations in gene expression               |
| 377 | between genotypes and growth conditions, Pi starvation induced differential H3K4me3              |
|     |                                                                                                  |

- 378 profiles that corresponded to changes in the abundance of transcripts of a subset of Pi-
- 379 responsive genes. Alterations in H3K4me3 abundance and expression level were

380 homodirectional, indicating that these changes could be functionally linked. Moreover, for a 381 subset of low Pi DMRs such homodirectional changes in H3K4me3 and transcription were 382 associated with anti-correlated changes in H3K27me3. This subset comprised several 383 regulatory factors of crucial importance for Pi homeostasis, suggesting that H3K4 384 methylation is a critical component of the regulatory machinery of the Pi deficiency response. 385 It should also be noted that the majority of transcriptionally Pi-responsive genes did not show 386 alteration in histone lysine methylation, indicating that Pi starvation-induced histone 387 modification is not necessarily associated with changes in transcription.

388

389 Differential H3K4 and H3K27 trimethylation affected disparate components of the 390 PSR. The recycling of Pi *via* membrane lipid remodeling plays a central role in the 391 recalibration of Pi homeostasis. This is of particular interest as it has been shown that 392 mutations in several genes involved in this process, *i.e. NPC4*, *PS2* or *SQD2,* resulted in Pi-393 specific root hair defects (Chandrika et al., 2013). Expression of these genes was partially 394 compromised in mutants with defects in the plant homeodomain (PHD) protein ALFIN-395 LIKE6 (AL6). AL6 was identified in a forward genetic screen for Pi-specific defects in root 396 hair development and shown to bind to di- and trimethylated H3K4 with its PHD domain ( 397 Chandrika et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2009). Similar to Pi-deficient *otu5* plants, *al6* mutants 398 develop very short root hairs when grown on low Pi media. It was hypothesized that AL6 act 399 by stabilizing transcripts encoding proteins required for the rapid tip growth of root hairs from 400 Pi-deficient plants (Chandrika et al., 2013). Together, these results underline a close linkage 401 of Pi deficiency-induced H3K4/H3K27 trimethylation, reprogramming of lipid metabolism, 402 and root hair morphogenesis.

403

404 Differential H3K4me3 abundance was not restricted to up-regulated genes. Three of 405 the down-regulated genes with reduced H3K4me3 levels upon Pi deficiency, *i.e.* the 406 transcription factor *bHLH39* and two genes from a recently identified subset of highly iron-407 responsive small proteins designated as *IRON-RESPONSIVE PROTEINS* (*IRP1* and *IRP6*), 408 were reportedly strongly induced in response to iron deficiency (Rodríguez-Celma et al., 409 2013). For *bHLH39*, also differential H3K27 trimethylation was observed that was anti-410 correlated with H3K4me3. In Pi-deficient plants, iron acquisition genes are generally down411 regulated to compensate for increased iron levels (Misson et al., 2005; Lan et al., 2012).

412 Down-regulation of iron acquisition genes is a consequence of oxidatively precipitated iron,

413 which is deposited in the meristematic and elongation zones of the root as a prerequisite of Pi

414 deficiency-induced determinacy of primary root growth (Müller et al., 2015; Hoehenwarter et

415 al., 2016, Mora-Macías et al., 2017). While a molecular function has yet to be assigned to the

416 two IRPs, bHLH39 was shown to form a heterodimer with the master regulator of iron

417 homeostasis FIT (bHLH29), which controls the expression of a large subset of genes involved

418 in the acquisition and homeostatic control of iron (Colangelo and Guerinot, 2004; Jakoby et 419 al., 2004; Yuan et al., 2008). It can thus be assumed that the small iron-related cluster

- 420 identified here is involved in repressing iron acquisition genes in response to Pi starvation.
- 421

422 In summary, it can be deduced from the present data that OTU5 is required for the 423 coordinated expression of genes involved in root hair morphogenesis and critical for the 424 orchestration of Pi signaling on gene expression, thereby impacting the root phenotype of 425 both Pi-replete and Pi-deficient plants. Whole genome bisulfite sequencing revealed that 426 OTU5 defines a distinct DNA methylation profile by targeting local genomic regions, altering 427 the methylation of genes with functions in root hair morphogenesis and Pi signaling. The 428 methylation patterns and gene expression profiles are not directly associated with each other, 429 suggesting that subtle changes in post-transcriptional processes such as mRNA maturation or 430 stability may contribute to the phenotype. Several, but not all Pi-responsive genes showed 431 anti-correlated H3K4 and H3K27 methylation marks, demonstrating that histone 432 modifications are important for the activity of a subset of PSR genes. For Pi-depemdemt 433 DNA methylation, no such correlation with gene expression was observed. In addition, DMRs 434 were enriched in transposable elements and depleted from coding regions, indicating that mC 435 has a minor or indirect effect on the activity of PSR genes and is likely to be critical for 436 genome stability and integrity.

- 439 **Plant growth conditions**

440 *Arabidopsis thaliana* (Col-0) was used as wild type. *otu5-1* is a T-DNA-inserted knockout

441 mutant (Salk\_032407) requested from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center at Ohio

442 State University (Columbus, OH). No full-length transcript or protein expression could be

- 444 OTU5 was made by PCR-amplification and mobilization of the OTU5 coding region from a
- 445 pET28a expression vector into pKNGSTAP (VIB, Belgium) using the gateway cloning
- 446 method (Radjacommare et al., 2014). The *A. thaliana* transformation was performed as
- 447 previously described and homozygous complementation lines were selected from T3 plants
- 448 (Clough and Bent, 1998). Seeds were surface sterilized and germinated on media containing
- 449 KNO3 (5 mM), MgSO4 (2 mM), Ca(NO3)2 (2 mM), KH2PO4 (2.5 mM), H3BO3 (70 µM),
- 450 MnCl2 (14 µM), ZnSO4 (1 µM) CuSO4 (0.5 µM), CoCl2 (0.01 µM), Na2MoO4 (0.2 µM) and
- 451 FeEDTA (40 µM), solidified with 0.4% Gelrite pure (CP Kelco, Atlanta, Georgia, USA),
- 452 1.5% sucrose and 1 g/L MES hydrate. The pH was adjusted to 5.5. Seeds were sown on Petri
- 453 plates and stratified for 2 d at 4°C in the dark and then transferred to a growth chamber at
- 21°C under continuous illumination (50 µmol m-2s -1 454 ). Low Pi conditions were obtained by
- 455 growing plants on media containing 2.5 μM KH2PO4, while the Pi concentration of Pi-
- 456 replete medium was 2.5 mM KH2PO4. The lower concentration of potassium due to the
- 457 reduced KH2PO4 concentration was compensated for by the addition of KCl. Plants were
- 458 grown on Pi-sufficient (2.5 mM) media for 7 days and transferred to Pi-sufficient and low
- 459 (2.5 μM) Pi media, respectively, for another 7 days.

# 466 **Interaction between genotype and Pi availability**

- 471 least a 2-fold change in expression level among all samples were included in the analysis.

472 Genes with *P* < 0.01 in the interaction between genotype and Pi availability were deemed to 473 have robust G × E interaction.

## 475 **Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)**

476 WGCNA was performed using the R package on transcriptome data following the standard 477 method described on the authors' website (Langfelder and Horvath, 2008). This unsupervised 478 and unbiased analysis identifies distinct co-expression gene modules by clustering transcripts 479 with similar expression patterns across samples. Transcriptionally variable genes with at least 480 2-fold changes between samples in the analysis were selected for the analysis. The genes were 481 hierarchically clustered based on a dissimilarity measure of topological overlap matrix. The 482 resulting dendrogram was used for module detection (minimum size 40; height cutoff 0.8).

483 Gene modules were labeled in unique colors. Unassigned genes were labeled in gray.

## 485 **RNA-seq library preparation and data analysis**

486 Total RNA was isolated from roots of 14-day-old plants grown under Pi-replete and Pi-

487 deficient conditions using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the

488 manufacturer's instructions. Equal amounts of total RNA were collected from three

489 independent experiments for library construction. cDNA libraries for sequencing were

490 prepared from 5 μg of purified RNA using Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation kit

491 following the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA libraries were sequenced with Illumina

- 492 HiSeq 2000 system using 101-bp single-end reads. Transcript abundance was calculated by
- 493 mapping reads to the *Arabidopsis* TAIR10 genome using Tophat2 (Kim et al., 2013). The

494 expression value of a gene was computed as RPKM (reads per kilobase of exons per million

- 495 mapped reads). Differentially expressed genes between samples were identified using the
- 496 Cuffdiff program (Trapnell et al., 2013), with at least 2-fold change in expression and a false
- 497 discovery rate (FDR) < 5% as criteria.

498

## 499 **Profiling genome-wide DNA methylation**

500 Genomic DNA was extracted by grinding root tissue in liquid nitrogen followed by

- 501 purification with the DNeasy Plant Mini kit (Qiagen). For whole genome bisulfite
- 502 sequencing, 1 µg of fragmented DNA was first ligated with pre-methylated TruSeq DNA

503 adapters (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The DNA fragments were then converted using the 504 EZ DNA Methylation-Gold kit (Zymo Research) using the manufacturer's protocol. The 505 resulting libraries were sequenced using the Illumina/Solexa sequencing technology (HiSeq 506 2000 sequencer; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Bisulfite converted reads were aligned to 507 the Arabidopsis reference genome (TAIR10) using BS-Seeker2 (Guo et al., 2013). Genome-508 wide DNA methylation profiles were generated by determining methylation levels for each 509 cytosine in the genome. Because bisulfite treatment converts unmethylated cytosines (Cs) to 510 thymines (Ts) after PCR amplification, the methylation level at each cytosine was estimated 511 as #C/(#C+#T), where #C is the number of methylated reads and #T is the number of 512 unmethylated reads. The methylation level per cytosine serves as an estimate of the 513 percentage of cells that have a methylated cytosine at this locus. We only included cytosines 514 that are covered by at least four reads. By spike in Lambda Phage DNA we estimated the

515 bisulfite conversion rate to be ≥99.6% in our libraries (See Table S7).

516

## 517 **Identifying differentially methylated regions**

518 To identify putative differentially methylated regions (DMRs), a non-overlapping tiling 519 window approach was used for genome-wide screening. Regions (200 bp) containing at least 520 5 cytosines, each of which covered by least 4 reads in all compared samples, were included in 521 the analysis. To be considered as DMRs, the region had to (1) show a difference in average 522 methylation level of ≥ 20% between the two groups, and (2) a FDR of less than 10%. To 523 estimate the FDR, we constructed simulated methylomes with the same coverage as the real 524 samples (Chen et al., 2013). For each cytosine site in each simulated sample, we simulated the 525 reads (C as methylated and T as unmethylated) based on the average methylation level (Pm) 526 of this cytosine site and coverage (n) from all real samples. The number of methylated reads 527 Cs is a random number based on binomial distribution B (n, Pm). Since the simulated 528 methylomes are constructed to have no methylation difference between two comparison 529 groups, DMRs identified from the comparison of the simulated methylomes are considered 530 false positives. FDR was estimated as the number of DMRs found in simulated methylomes 531 (false positives) divided by that from the real samples (positive prediction). See Table S11 for 532 the cutoffs of Δ methylation level corresponding to FDR ≤ 10%.

#### 534 **ChIP-seq library preparation and data analysis**

535 Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed using root (∼1.5 g) of *A.*  536 *thaliana* (Col-0) following the published protocols(Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). For 537 immunoprecipitation, 5 μg of antibodies against H3-core (Ab1791; Abcam), H3K4me3 538 (Ab8580; Abcam), and H3K27me3(07-499; Upstate) were added to a 600-μL solution 539 containing diluted chromatin extracts (~25 μg) and incubated overnight at 4°C with gentle 540 rotation. Immunoprecipitated chromatin-DNA (IP-DNA) and ~5 μg of input chromatin-DNA 541 (without IP) were subjected to reverse cross-link and protease K treatment. DNA was purified 542 using the MinElute PCR purification kit (QIAGEN) for sequencing libraries preparation. 543 DNA was quantified by Qubit and analyzed using the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA analysis 544 chip (5067-4626, Agilent). Libraries for ChIP-seq were prepared following KAPA Library 545 Preparation Kits Illumina series (#KK8201, KAPA Biosystems) according to the 546 manufacture's protocol. To summarize, 2-5 ng ChIPed DNA was end-repaired, "A" base was 547 added to the 3'-end of DNA fragment and then ligated to the barcoded adapters. The products 548 are purified and enriched with 12 cycles of PCR to create the final double stranded cDNA 549 library. Final libraries were analyzed using the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA analysis chip to 550 estimate the quality, and were then quantified by qPCR using the KAPA Library 551 Quantification Kit (KK4824, KAPA BioSystems). The ChIP-seq libraries were sequenced 552 with the Illumina HiSeq 2500 system. The reads were aligned to the TAIR10 Arabidopsis 553 genome using Bowtie2 version 2.2.4 (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012) (see Table S12 for 554 mapping statistics). Alignments were converted into coverage files (bigWig format) using the 555 bamCoverage function in deepTools (Ramirez et al., 2014), and normalized to 1X. 556 Correlation plots of ChIP-seq were generated using the multiBigwigSummary function 557 followed by plotCorrelation. To generate heatmaps of reads abundance associated with TSS, 558 the reads density around TSS (-500 to +1500bp) was computed using the computeMatrix 559 function, followed by the plotHeatmap. Genes with differentially binding of histone marks 560 were detected by t-test *P* value of the average density. A cutoff of *P* < 0.05 with differential 561 abundance >= 2-fold and abundance >=1 RPKM in one or more samples were used to select 562 genes showing differential histone modification binding. 563
