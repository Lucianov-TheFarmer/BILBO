**Abstract** Determining the molecular mechanism of fruit tree architecture is important for tree management and fruit production. An apple mutant 'McIntosh Wijcik', which was discovered as a bud mutation from 'McIntosh', exhibits a columnar growth phenotype that is controlled by a single dominant gene, *Co*. In this study, the mutation and the *Co* gene were analyzed. Fine mapping narrowed the *Co* region to a 101 kb region. Sequence analysis of the *Co* region and the original wild-type *co* region identified an insertion mutation of an 8202 bp long terminal repeat (LTR) retroposon in the *Co* region. Segregation analysis using a DNA marker based on the insertion polymorphism showed that the LTR retroposon was closely associated with the columnar growth phenotype. RNA-seq and RT-PCR analysis identified a promising *Co* candidate gene (91071-gene) within the *Co* region that is specifically expressed in 'McIntosh Wijcik' but not in 'McIntosh'. ## **Introduction**

Fruit tree architecture is an important factor for tree training, orchard management and fruit production (Costes et al. [2006](#page-16-0)). In apples (*Malus* × *domestica* Borkh.), a number of spur-type mutants, characterized by increased spur production and reduced vegetative growth, have been discovered. The most distinct mutant is 'McIntosh Wijcik', a columnar mutant of 'McIntosh', discovered in Canada. The columnar growth phenotype of 'McIntosh Wijcik' is characterized by short internodes, reduced lateral branches and increased spurs (Kelsey and Brown [1992](#page-16-1)). Test crosses within the progeny of 'Golden Delicious' ('GD') × 'McIntosh Wijcik' revealed that the columnar growth phenotype of 'McIntosh Wijcik' is controlled by a single dominant gene, *Co* (Lapins [1976](#page-17-0)). Therefore, 'McIntosh Wijcik' has been used as an important genetic resource to develop compact cultivars for high density planting, as well as a model system to understand the molecular and genetic mechanisms associated with the determination of tree architecture (Hollender and Dardick [2015;](#page-16-2) Kelsey and Brown [1992](#page-16-1)).

To identify the *Co* gene and develop DNA markers for the columnar growth phenotype, apple genetic linkage maps have been constructed, which showed that the *Co* gene is located on linkage group 10 (Conner et al. [1997](#page-16-3); Moriya et al. [2009;](#page-17-1) Tian et al. [2005](#page-17-2)). Recently, a draft genome sequence of the apple cultivar 'GD' was reported by Velasco et al. ([2010\)](#page-17-3). The apple genome sequence enabled us to design new simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers, and accelerated the fine mapping of the *Co* gene. Moreover, next-generation sequencing technologies have enabled researchers to analyze large-scale genomic sequences and transcriptomes. As a result, several research groups have reported the fine mapping of the *Co* gene (Bai et al. [2012;](#page-16-4) Baldi et al. [2013](#page-16-5); Moriya et al. [2012\)](#page-17-4), genome sequences and mutations of the *Co* region (Otto et al. [2014](#page-17-5); Wolters et al. [2013](#page-17-6)) and candidates for the *Co* gene (Otto et al. [2014](#page-17-5); Petersen et al. [2015](#page-17-7); Wolters et al. [2013](#page-17-6)). However, some of these results differ among studies.

In terms of fine mapping, Moriya et al. [\(2012](#page-17-4)) delimited the *Co* region to a 196 kb region between markers Mdo. chr10.11 and Mdo.chr10.15 (Chr10: 18766–18962 kb of the apple genome). Similarly, Bai et al. ([2012\)](#page-16-4) delimited the *Co* region to 193 kb between markers C1753-3520 and C7629-22009 (Chr10: 18905–19098 kb of the apple genome), and Baldi et al. ([2013\)](#page-16-5) delimited the *Co* region to 393 kb between markers Co04R10 and Co04R13 (Chr10: 18512–18905 kb of the apple genome). The 196 kb region identified by Moriya et al. ([2012\)](#page-17-4) overlapped with portions of the regions identified by Bai et al. ([2012\)](#page-16-4) and Baldi et al. [\(2013](#page-16-5)). However, the 193 kb region identified by Bai et al. [\(2012](#page-16-4)) did not overlap with the 393 kb region identified by Baldi et al. [\(2013](#page-16-5)). Therefore, the correct position of the *Co* region is not clear.

In terms of the genome sequence and mutation of the *Co* region, Wolters et al. ([2013\)](#page-17-6) reported the 388 kb sequence of the *Co* region covered by two contigs (WiC1/ WiC2; accession number KF530876), which could not be joined, and an insertion mutation of 1956 bp in 'McIntosh Wijcik'. In contrast, Otto et al. ([2014\)](#page-17-5) reported the 201 kb sequence of the *Co* region (accession number HF968765), and an insertion mutation of 8200 bp in 'McIntosh Wijcik'. Besides the difference in the insertion sequence length, the sequence of KF530876 was also a little different from that of HF968765.

In terms of the *Co* gene, several candidates have been proposed. Wolters et al. ([2013\)](#page-17-6) analyzed the expression patterns of the six predicted genes (*MdCo27*–*32*) located within the 50 kb region surrounding the 2 kb insertion sequence, and revealed upregulation of the *MdCo31* gene [putative 2OG-Fe(II) oxygenase] in axillary buds of 'McIntosh Wijcik' compared with 'McIntosh'. Otto et al. ([2014\)](#page-17-5) mapped RNA-seq data sets of leaves of 'McIntosh Wijcik' and 'McIntosh' to the 'GD' apple genome, and revealed that two candidate genes, *MDP0000934866* (bHLH155 like) and *MDP0000878773* (ACC1-like), were noticeably upregulated in 'McIntosh Wijcik'. Otto et al. ([2014\)](#page-17-5) also mapped RNA-seq data sets of shoot apical meristems (SAMs) of branches of a noncolumnar cultivar 'A14- 190-93' (A14) and SAMs of the spur of a columnar cultivar 'Procats 28' (P28) (Krost et al. [2012](#page-16-6), [2013](#page-16-7)) to the 'GD' apple genome, and identified two candidate genes; *MDP0000934866* (bHLH155-like), which showed approximately 2-fold upregulation in P28 compared with A14, and *dmr6*-*like* (downy mildew resistance 6-like), which showed exclusive expression in P28 and not in A14. Furthermore, Petersen et al. [\(2015](#page-17-7)) showed that two candidate genes, *MDP0000927098* (ATL5K-like) and *MDP0000163720* (ACC1-like), were also upregulated in the SAMs of P28.

As mentioned above, fine mapping of the *Co* gene; genome sequences and mutations of the *Co* region; and candidates for the *Co* gene differ among studies, and the mutation and the *Co* gene controlling columnar growth phenotype remain a puzzle (Hollender and Dardick [2015](#page-16-2)). In this study, initially, fine mapping of the *Co* gene was performed using new SSR markers and additional cross seedlings. Second, the complete sequencing of the *Co* and *co* region, and long PCR analysis were carried out to identify the mutation in the *Co* region. Third, transcriptome analysis and generation of transgenic plants (tobacco and apple) were conducted to identify a candidate *Co* gene. The comprehensive data presented here provided a better understanding of the mutation and the *Co* gene controlling the columnar growth phenotype. Moreover, a DNA marker specific for the *Co* region was developed for markerassisted selection of columnar apples.

## **Materials and methods**

#### **Plant materials and DNA extraction**

The plant materials comprised seven columnar apples ('McIntosh Wijcik', 'Telamon', 'Trajan', 'Tuscan', 'Maypole', *Co*-homozygote and 5-13097), 20 noncolumnar apples ('McIntosh', 'GD', 'Greensleeves', 'Baskatong', 'Starking Delicious', 'Fuji', 'Granny Smith', 'Gala', 'Jonathan', 'Jonagold', 'Rome Beauty', 'Braeburn', 'Elstar', 'Cox's Orange Pippin', 'Tsugaru', 'Orin', 'Shinano Sweet', 'Shinano Gold', 'Mutsu' and *co*-homozygote), and three trees with recombination in the vicinity of *Co* gene (7-4121, 7-4189 and 9), described in Moriya et al. ([2012\)](#page-17-4). The *Co*homozygote (*Co/Co*) was selected from cross seedlings of 5-12786 (*Co/co*) × 'McIntosh Wijcik' (*Co/co*) using the SSR markers CH03d11 and Hi01a03 (Moriya et al. [2009](#page-17-1)). The 5-13097 was selected from cross seedlings of 'Sansa'  $(co/co) \times 8$ H-2-26 (Co/co) by columnar growth phenotype and fruit quality. The *co*-homozygote (co/co), which has the original wild-type progenitor (co allele) of the *Co* allele, was selected from cross seedlings of 'McIntosh'  $(co/co) \times$  'Kitakami' (co/co) [('McIntosh'  $\times$  'Worcester Pearmain')  $\times$  'Redgold'] using the SSR markers CH03d11 and Hi01a03. Their genomic DNAs were extracted from young leaves using a DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA).

Apple seedlings from four cross combinations [177 trees from 'Shinano Gold' (co/co) × 8H-5-3 (Co/co), 197 trees from 8H-5-3 (Co/co) × 'Morinokagayaki' (co/co), 149 trees from 'Shinano Gold' (co/co) × 8H-2-26 (Co/co), 134 trees from 'Morinokagayaki'  $(co/co) \times 8H-2-26 (Co/co)$ ] were used for fine mapping. Apple seedlings from three cross combinations [66 trees from 'Fuji' (co/co) × 5-12786 (Co/co), 65 trees from 'Fuji' (co/co)  $\times$  5-263 (Co/co), and 100 trees from 6-837 (co/co) × 5-8246 (Co/co)] were also used to evaluate a DNA marker specific for the Co region. Their genomic DNAs were extracted from young leaves using the PI-50a DNA-isolation system (Kurabo, Osaka, Japan), according to the method of Moriya et al. (2009). Their growth phenotypes were evaluated visually and categorized as columnar or noncolumnar, according to the criteria of Moriya et al. (2012).

The genotypes of 'Telamon', 8H-5-3, 8H-2-26, 5-12786, 5-263 and 5-8246 were determined by Moriya et al. (2009).

#### SSR analysis

Five SSR markers (Mdo.chr10.11, Mdo.chr10.12, Mdo. chr10.13, Mdo.chr10.14 and Mdo.chr10.15) (Moriya et al. 2012) and three SSR markers (C1753-3520, C18470-25831 and C7629-22009) (Bai et al. 2012) were used. Four new SSR markers (Mdo.chr10.11-1, Mdo.chr10.11-2, Mdo. chr10.13-2 and Mdo.chr10.14-1) were designed by searching for SSRs containing five or more repeats of a 2 bp motif from the 'GD' apple genome sequence (Table S1). SSR-PCR and detection of these markers were performed according to the method of Moriya et al. (2012).

# Bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) library construction and shotgun sequencing

BAC libraries of 'Telamon' (*Co/co*) ['McIntosh Wijcik' (*Co/co*) × 'GD' (*co/co*)] and 'McIntosh' (*co/co*) were constructed using nuclear DNA prepared from apple leaves. DNA was partially digested with *Hin*dIII and size-selected (100–180 kb) before being ligated into the BAC vector, pIndigoBAC-5 (Epicentre, Illumina, Madison, WI, USA), and then transformed into *Escherichia coli* Electro-MAX DH10B Cells (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific,

Waltham, MA, USA). BAC clones were stocked into 384well plates and kept at -80 °C (Baba et al. 2000). Selected BAC clones were subjected to random shearing and shotgun sequencing to obtain sequence data with 10-fold redundancy, using an ABI 3730xl sequencer (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with forward and reverse universal primers and the dye-terminator method. The shotgun sequences were assembled using PHRED and PHRAP software packages (Ewing and Green 1998; Ewing et al. 1998). To complete the BAC sequence, additional shotgun sequences and other methods were used, such as dye-primer and transposon-tag sequencing (Sasaki et al. 2002).

#### PCR analysis

Fourteen primer pairs were designed from the BAC-end sequences (Table S2). PCR with the primer pairs was performed using the Expand High Fidelity PCR system (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Two primers "Long-F" (5'-TCGGCGGGAGTAGGCT-GAACCACTG-3') and "Long-R" (5'-ATTAGTAATTC-CTCTGCTACTGCTGATGGCCTCTC-3') were designed from the sequence of the BAC-end and 'GD' apple genome. Long PCR using the two primers was performed using the KOD FX Neo enzyme (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). Amplification was performed as follows: an initial denaturation step at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 30 cycles at 94 °C for 15 s and 68 °C for 20 min.

Three primers "Normal-F" (5'-GAATTCTAAACGCT ATTGTCCTTGG-3'), "Normal-R" (5'-CTGAGAATGAGAG CTTTGAGAAGG-3') and "Columnar-R" (5'-GCTAGTT AATTCTCGCATCAGTCC-3') were designed from the sequence of BAC clone 116H3. PCR using the three primers was performed using the GoTaq Hot Start Master Mix (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Amplification was performed as follows: an initial denaturation step at 94 °C for 2 min; 30 cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min; followed by a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min.

#### **RNA-seq analysis**

Shoot apexes of vegetative shoots of 'McIntosh Wijcik' and 'McIntosh' were collected from two independent grafted trees in the orchard, which were more than 10 years old, for biological replicates of each cultivar. Total RNAs were isolated from the shoot apexes using an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen). The RNA-seq analysis was carried out by the Genome Network Analysis Support Facility, RIKEN Center for Life Science Technologies. RNA-seq libraries were generated from the total RNAs using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). 100 bp paired-end sequencing of the RNAseq libraries was conducted using the HiSeq 2500 system (Illumina). The reads from each library were trimmed using CLC Genomics Workbench Version 6.0.4 (Clc Bio, Qiagen, Aarhus, Denmark) with the default settings. Contigs were created by the de novo assembly process of CLC Genomics Workbench, integrated by scaffolding and updated based on the subsequent mapping of the input reads back to the contigs generated by the de novo assembly. The resultant contigs were mapped to the reference sequence of BAC clone 116H3, allowing gaps for introns, using the Large Gap Mapper in the CLC Genomics Workbench. Parameters for the Large Gap Mapper were as follows: non-specific matches: ignore; mismatch cost: 2; insertion cost: 3; deletion cost: 3; similarity: 0.9; length fraction: 0.9. Expression levels of the contigs were calculated based on the reads per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (RPKM) values, normalized by quantile normalization in the CLC Genomics Workbench. Differentially expressed contigs were defined by *t*-tests, using a cutoff of *p* value <0.05.

### RT-PCR

Shoot apexes of vegetative shoots of the Co- and cohomozygotes in the greenhouse were collected from 2-year-old own-root trees. Shoot apexes of vegetative shoots of 5-13097, 'McIntosh Wijcik' and 'McIntosh' were collected from grafted trees in the orchard, which were more than 10 years old. Leaves of transgenic tobaccos were collected just before or during flowering. Leaves of transgenic apples were collected from culture shoots. Total RNAs were extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide method (Wada et al. 2002). The extracted RNAs were used for reverse transcription with a ReverTra-Plus kit (Toyobo). Two primers "CoO19F" (5'-TGGAGACATTA-GATCAGAA-3') and "CoO19R" (5'-TTAGCTACTCGA-GAGGCTTA-3') were designed at the site of the start and stop codon of the 91071-gene. PCR with the two primers was performed using the KOD-Plus polymerase (Toyobo). Amplification was performed as follows: an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 30 cycles at 98 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 68 °C for 1 min. The apple ubiquitin MdUBQ and tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L. Cv. Petit Havana SR1) GAPDH genes were amplified as controls (Quiapim et al. 2009; Takos et al. 2006).

#### Nucleotide and amino acid sequence analysis

Nucleotide sequences were determined using an ABI 3730xl sequencer with an ABI BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems). Nucleotide and amino acid sequences were analyzed using GENE-TYX-MAC Ver. 18 and ATSQ Ver. 5 (Genetyx, Tokyo,

Japan). Nucleotide sequences were analyzed using LTR\_ Finder (http://tlife.fudan.edu.cn/ltr\_finder/) to find LTR retrotransposons. Homology searches of the nucleotide sequences were performed by BLAST (Altschul et al. 1997). Deduced amino acid sequences were analyzed by Pfam 27.0 (http://pfam.xfam.org) to identify conserved domains.

## **Phylogenetic analysis**

The amino acid sequences of the experimentally characterized 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases (2OGDs) were obtained from GenBank by referring to Appendix S2 in Kawai et al. (2014). The amino acid sequences were aligned using ClustalW (Fig. S1), and a phylogenetic tree was constructed by using the Maximum Likelihood method based on the LG + G model in MEGA7 (Kumar et al. 2016). The LG + G model with the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion scores was selected as the best amino acid substitution model.

# Development of a DNA marker specific for the *Co* region

To develop a DNA marker specific for the *Co* region, new primers 'Normal-F' and 'Normal-R' were designed from the regions flanking the insertion breakpoints, and 'Columnar-R' was designed from the insertion sequence (Fig. 3a). Under PCR conditions using GoTaq, the primer pair of 'Normal-F' and 'Columnar-R' should amplify a 128 bp product from the Co region of 'McIntosh Wijcik', and the primer pair of 'Normal-F' and 'Normal-R' should amplify a 251 bp product from the original wild-type co region of 'McIntosh', but should not amplify an 8453 bp product from the Co region of 'McIntosh Wijcik'. As expected, genomic PCR using the three primers successfully amplified a ~250 bp product from 'McIntosh' (co/co), and 128 and ~250 bp products from 'McIntosh Wijcik' (Co/co) (Fig. 3c, lanes 1-2). The presence of the 128 bp product discriminated 'McIntosh Wijcik' from 'McIntosh'. Similarly, genomic PCR using the three primers amplified the 128 and ~250 bp products from four columnar cultivars ('Telamon', 'Trajan', 'Tuscan' and 'Maypole') (Fig. 3c, lanes 3-6), but only the ~250 bp product from 18 noncolumnar cultivars (Fig. 3c, lanes 7-24). This indicated that the 128 bp product is useful as a specific DNA marker for the Co region, and that the  $\sim 250$  bp product is useful as a control to check the success of PCR, because the primer regions of 'Normal-F' and 'Normal-R' are conserved in the co region of all the cultivars used in this study.

To confirm the reliability of the 128 bp product for marker-assisted selection of columnar apples, seedlings from three cross combinations were analyzed by genomic PCR using the three primers. The 128 bp product was detected in 29 of 66 trees from 'Fuji'  $(co/co) \times 5-12786$  (Co/co), 41 of 65 trees from 'Fuji'  $(co/co) \times 5-263$  (Co/co), and 46 of 100 trees from 6-837  $(co/co) \times 5-8246$  (Co/co) (data not shown). All 116 trees with the 128 bp product showed the columnar growth phenotype, suggesting that the 128 bp product was completely consistent with the columnar growth phenotype and could be used in marker-assisted selection of columnar apples. In addition, these results supported the close relationship between the columnar growth phenotype and the presence of the insertion sequence.

## Transcriptome analysis of the Co region

To identify the candidate for the Co gene, differentially expressed genes between 'McIntosh Wijcik' and 'McIntosh' were analyzed using RNA-seq. Next generation sequencing of the four mRNA samples of shoot apexes of 'McIntosh Wijcik' (two biological replicates) and 'McIntosh' (two biological replicates) generated 438.4 million reads, with an average of 109.6 million reads per sample (Table S3). After trimming of the reads, de novo assembly reconstructed 109451 contigs from 432.6 million reads. A "contig" here means a reconstructed mRNA sequence (gene). The contig lengths ranged from 158 to 15981 bp, with average of 697 bp (Table S4). To identify contigs located at the Co region, all contigs were mapped to the reference sequence of the BAC clone 116H3, allowing gaps for introns. Fifty-eight contigs with >95 % identity to the 116H3 sequence were extracted as candidates for the Co gene (Table 1; Fig. S4); the other 109393 contigs were removed from the list of candidates. To extract the differentially expressed genes between 'McIntosh Wijcik' and 'McIntosh' from the 58 contigs, the processed reads from each sample were mapped separately to the 109451 contigs (Table S5), and the expression levels of the 58 contigs were calculated as the normalized RPKM values (Table 1). Thus, the RT-PCR result was consistent with that of RNA-seq and demonstrated that columnar apples have upregulated expression of the 91071-gene.

[2014](#page-17-5); Petersen et al. [2015](#page-17-7); Wolters et al. [2013](#page-17-6)). These candi date genes corresponded to the 91071-, 335-, 95062-, 104499- and 100016-genes in this study, respectively. In this study, no differential expression between 'McIntosh Wijcik' and 'McIntosh' was detected for the 335-gene (*MDP0000934866*), 104499-gene (*MDP0000927098*), 100016-gene (*MDP0000163720*) (Table [1](#page-8-0)) and 95062-gene (*MDP0000878773*) (Table S6). Petersen et al. [\(2015](#page-17-7)) also reported that *MDP0000934866* (335-gene) showed simi lar expression levels in columnar and noncolumnar apples. Therefore, the 335-gene (*MDP0000934866*), 95062-gene (*MDP0000878773*), 104499-gene (*MDP0000927098*) and 100016-gene (*MDP0000163720*) are unlikely to be related to the columnar growth phenotype. On the other hand, the 91071-gene (*MdCo31/dmr6* -*like*) is the most promising candidate for the *Co* gene because the gene was differen tially expressed between columnar and noncolumnar apples in various tissues and locations. The 91071-gene (*MdCo31/ dmr6*-*like*) showed expression in SAMs of spur and shoot apexes of vegetative shoots of columnar apples, but no expression in those of noncolumnar apples in Germany and Japan (this study and Otto et al. [2014](#page-17-5)). The 91071-gene (*MdCo31/dmr6*-*like*) also showed upregulated expression in axillary buds (14-fold) and SAMs (30–200-fold) of columnar apples compared with noncolumnar apples in Italy and Germany (Petersen et al. [2015;](#page-17-7) Wolters et al. [2013\)](#page-17-6). Thus, upregulated expression of the 91071-gene (*MdCo31/dmr6 like*) in columnar apples seems to be sufficient to change the phenotype. The mechanism of the upregulated expression of 91071 gene in 'McIntosh Wijcik' is not well understood. Recently, it was reported that promoter and enhancer elements within LTR retroposons could alter plant gene expression (Grandbastien [2015;](#page-16-14) Lisch [2013\)](#page-17-16). For example, in blood oranges (*Citrus sinensis*) with red fruit flesh, insertion of retroposon LTRs in the region upstream of the *Ruby* gene caused transcriptional activation of the *Ruby* gene, which encodes a MYB transcription factor for anthocyanin production (Butelli et al. [2012](#page-16-15)). In maize (*Zea mays* ssp. *mays* L.), a retroposon (*Hopscotch*) inserted ~60 kb upstream of the *teosinte branched1* (*tb1*) gene acts as an enhancer of the *tb1* gene expression, which contributes to apical dominance by repressing branch outgrowth (Studer et al. [2011](#page-17-17)). Therefore, upregulated expression of the 91071-gene in 'McIntosh Wijcik' may be caused by promoter and enhancer elements within the LTR of the insertion sequence, which are able to activate adjacent apple genes. In our analysis, two contigs (59013- and 74068-genes) were located nearer to the insertion sequence than the 91071-gene (Fig. S4). However, the 59013- and 74068-genes showed ≤96.5 % identity with the 116H3 sequence (Table [1](#page-8-0)), suggesting that the two genes are likely to derive from a region other than the *Co* region, namely an allele or a homolog. Moreover, the LTR\_Finder program predicted two LTR retroposons (retroposon2 and 4) in the region (Fig. [3b](#page-6-0)). Therefore, the 91071-gene seems to be the nearest apple gene to the insertion sequence, and might be affected by the promoter and enhancer activities of the LTR. However, further investigations are necessary to verify this hypothesis.

## **Characterization of the 91071‑gene**

91071-gene encodes a putative protein of 339 amino acids that was predicted to have two conserved domains by a Pfam sequence search: non-heme dioxygenase in morphine synthesis N-terminal (DIOX\_N) (E-value 9.9e−19) in the N-terminal region, and a 2OG-Fe(II) oxygenase superfamily (2OG-FeII\_Oxy) (E-value 3.2e−22) motif in the C-terminal region (Fig. S6). Therefore, the protein product of 91071-gene was regarded as a member of the 2OGD superfamily. In plants, the 2OGD superfamily is involved in various oxygenation/hydroxylation reactions, and comprises three functionally distinct classes: DOXA, DOXB and DOXC (Kawai et al. [2014](#page-16-12)). The domain organization of the protein product of the 91071-gene suggested that it belongs to the DOXC class, because it harbors the DIOX\_N domain. DOXC-class 2OGDs are classified into 57 phylogenetic clades (DOXC1–57) and are involved in specialized metabolism of various phytochemicals (Kawai et al. [2014](#page-16-12)). To infer the biological function of the protein product of the 91071-gene, a phylogenetic tree was constructed with 91 functionally characterized 2OGDs in the DOXC class. The phylogenetic analysis revealed that the protein product of the 91071-gene could be classified into the DOXC41 clade (Fig. [4](#page-12-0)). DOXC41 contains functionally diverse 2OGDs involved in various specialized metabolisms, such as hyoscyamine 6β-hydroxylase (H6H) and *Hordeum vulgare* (barley) iron-deficiency-specific clones 2 and 3 (HvIDS2 and HvIDS3) (Kawai et al. [2014\)](#page-16-12). H6H in *Hyoscyamus niger* (HnH6H) catalyzes hydroxylation of hyoscyamine in the biosynthetic pathway leading to a tropane alkaloid, scopolamine (Matsuda et al. [1991](#page-17-18)). HvIDS2 and HvIDS3 are involved in the biosynthesis of mugineic acid family phytosiderophores (Nakanishi et al. [2000](#page-17-19)).

## **Analysis of transgenic tobaccos and apples overexpressing 91071‑gene**

To investigate the effect of the 91071-gene on phenotype, tobaccos and apples were transformed with the 91071 gene. In tobacco, 25 transgenic lines (T0 generation) were obtained and divided into two groups according to their phenotypes. Group A (15 lines) had a short plant height of 8–20 cm, whereas group B (10 lines) had a normal plant height of 25–37 cm, which was the same as the nontransgenic tobacco 'Petit Havana SR1' (data not shown). To characterize the phenotype of the transgenic tobaccos in detail, selfed seeds from each T0 line were germinated on MS medium with kanamycin, and surviving seeds were grown in soil (T1 generation). T1 transgenic tobaccos from each T0 line showed similar phenotypes to the parental T0 line. For example, T1 transgenic tobaccos from group A (T0 lines 4, 9 and 12) were significantly shorter in plant height and internode length than the nontransgenic tobacco 'Petit Havana SR1'. In contrast, T1 transgenic tobaccos from group B (T0 lines 2 and 6) had the same plant height and internode length as the nontransgenic tobacco 'Petit Havana SR1' (Table [2](#page-13-0); Fig. [5a](#page-14-0)). T1 transgenic tobaccos with an extremely small phenotype from the T0 lines 4 and 12 showed strong expression of the 91071-gene. T1 transgenic tobaccos with moderately small phenotype from the T0 line 9 showed weak expression of the 91071-gene. T1 transgenic tobaccos with normal growth phenotype from the T0 lines 2 and 6 showed no expression of the 91071 gene (Fig. [5](#page-14-0)b). These results indicated that expression of 91071-gene was associated with the short plant height and short internode length in tobacco.

## **Conclusions**

To provide a comprehensive view of the genetic and molecular control of the columnar growth phenotype, the present study used genetic, transcriptomic, gene expression and transformation approaches. These results strongly suggested that the short internodes of the columnar growth phenotype result from the upregulated expression of the 91071-gene, encoding a 2OGD, which was closely related to the insertion mutation of an 8202 bp LTR retroposon in the *Co* region. Previous reports suggested that phytohormones might be related to the columnar growth phenotype (Watanabe et al. [2004,](#page-17-21) [2006\)](#page-17-22). However, the phylogenetic analysis of 2OGDs (Fig. [4\)](#page-12-0) revealed that the protein product of 91071-gene was classified into the DOXC41 clade, not into the clades involved in the metabolism of phytohormones. Therefore, the function of the 91071-gene remains unknown. Our transgenic tobaccos and apples represent useful model systems to examine the function of the 91071-gene, providing important information to better understand the differences in tree architecture between 'McIntosh Wijcik' and 'McIntosh'.

The *Co* gene derived from 'McIntosh Wijcik' has been used as a genetic resource to breed columnar cultivars. In many segregating populations, the selection of columnar seedlings by visual evaluation takes at least 2 years, because the columnar growth phenotype is difficult to identify in trees <2 years old (Hemmat et al. [1997\)](#page-16-16). Therefore, SSR markers linked to the *Co* gene have been used to screen columnar seedlings; however, these SSR markers could not distinguish the *Co* allele from the original wild-type *co* allele of 'McIntosh'. Moreover, SSR analysis is expensive and complicated. In this study, a DNA marker specific for the *Co* region was developed based on the insertion polymorphism for early selection of columnar seedlings. This marker is superior to previous SSR markers by virtue of its simple and low-cost detection, and utilization in the cross seedlings of 'McIntosh' and its offspring, because it can distinguish the *Co* allele from the original wild-type *co* allele. Furthermore, the ~250 bp product is useful as a control to check the success of PCR. In the NARO Institute of Fruit Tree Science, a breeding program is ongoing to develop new columnar apples with high fruit quality. The DNA marker specific for the *Co* region provides an early and reliable selection system for columnar seedlings, and will promote the breeding of columnar apples with high fruit quality.
