The elucidation of epigenetic responses of salt-responsive genes facilitates understanding of the underlying mechanisms that confer salt tolerance in rice. However, it is still largely unknown how epigenetic mechanisms are associated with the expression of salt-responsive genes in rice and other crops. In this study, we reported tissue-specific gene expression and tissue-specific changes in chromatin modifications or signatures between seedlings and roots in response to salt treatment. Our study indicated that among six of individual mark examined (H3K4me3, H3K27me3, H4K12ac, H3K9ac, H3K27ac and H3K36me3), a positive association between salt-related changes in histone marks and the expression of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was observed only for H3K9ac and H4K12ac in seedlings and H3K36me3 in roots. By contrast, chromatin states (CSs) with combinations of six histone modification marks played crucial roles in the differential expression of salt-responsive genes between seedlings and roots. Most importantly, CS7 containing the bivalent marks H3K4me3 and H3K27me3, with a mutual exclusion of functions with each other, displayed distinct functions in the expression of DEGs in both tissues. Specifically, H3K27me3 in CS7 mainly suppressed the expression of DEGs in roots, while H3K4me3 affected the expression of down- and up-regulated genes, possibly by antagonizing the repressive role of H3K27me3 in seedlings. Our findings indicate distinct impacts of the chromatin states on the differential expression of salt-responsive genes between seedlings and roots in rice, which provides an

important background for understanding chromatin-based epigenetic mechanisms that might confer salt tolerance in plants.

## **Introduction**

Physiological, biochemical, genetic and epigenetic changes frequently occur in plants upon exposure to environmental stresses. These stresses mainly include drought, salinity and cold. Stress-related epigenetic changes can be achieved at different regulatory layers, including modifications of DNA and chromatin, differential gene expression, generation of small RNAs and dynamic changes of chromatin structure ([Boyko and Kovalchuk 2008,](#page-30-0) [Covarrubias and Reyes 2010](#page-31-0), [Kim et al. 2015,](#page-33-0) Pecinka and Mittelsten Scheid 2012, Probst and Mittelsten 2015, Sahu et al. 2013, Sokol et al. 2007, Vriet et al. 2015, Yuan et al. 2013). Local or global changes in H3K4me1/2/3 and H3K9ac have been documented during drought stress in *Arabidopsis* and rice ([Chinnusamy 2009,](#page-31-1) [Dijk et al. 2010](#page-31-2), [Kim et](#page-33-1)  [al. 2008a](#page-33-1), Zong et al. 2013). Cold treatment can cause global changes in DNA demethylation, and H3/H4 deacetylation in maize ([Hu et al. 2011,](#page-32-0) Steward et al. 2002). Local or global accumulation of histone marks, such as H3K9/14ac, H3K4me3, H3Ser-10 phosphorylation and H4 acetylation, has been detected during salt treatment in *Arabidopsis* and soybean [\(Chen and Wu 2010](#page-31-3), Sokol et al. 2007, Song et al. 2012). The dynamics of histone H2A variant H2A.Z-nucleosomes help plants to cope with thermal or drought stress [\(Boden et](#page-30-1) 

[al. 2013,](#page-30-1) [Kumar and Wigge 2010](#page-34-0), Sura et al. 2017), indicating the involvement of histone variants in the stress response. These findings suggest that epigenetic changes in DNA and histone modifications might occur in a plant species- or stress type-dependent manner.

The tissue-specific gene expression has already been reported in rice and other plants in response to stresses [\(Huang et al. 2016](#page-32-1), [Liu et al. 2007](#page-34-1), Smita et al. 2013, Tillett et al. 2011, Yu et al. 2017, Zhang et al. 2017); however, the association between the expression of these genes and changes in histone modifications is still poorly understood. In particular, it remains elusive how an individual mark or the combined actions of a panel of marks function in the transcription of stress-responsive genes in a tissue-dependent manner in plants, especially in crops. Here, the results of our study indicated that salt treatment induced tissue-specific gene expression and tissue-specific changes in chromatin modifications between seedlings and roots. Particularly, chromatin modifications or signatures played distinct roles in regulating the differential expression of salt-responsive genes between seedlings and roots in rice. Thus, our findings facilitate understanding of chromatin-based epigenetic mechanisms that confer salt tolerance in plants.

## **Results**

# **Differential gene expression occurs between seedlings and roots under salt treatment**

To investigate global transcriptional changes in response to salt treatment between seedlings and roots, we performed mRNA-seq with two independent biological replicates from each tissue with a one-day treatment (24 h) with 150 mM NaCl and mock (without NaCl addition) (Supplementary Table S1). Pearson correlation coefficients (R) calculated using FPKM from two biological replicates between mock and NaCl treatment were 0.9480441(mock)/0.9884847(salt) for seedlings and 0.9827507(mock)/0.9849431(salt) for roots (Supplementary Fig. S1), indicating a good correlation between biological replicates. Thus, each replicated data set was analyzed using cuffdiff to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in response to salinity. 3,272 genes in seedlings and 3,652 genes in roots were differentially expressed with fold changes equal to or greater than 2 (*p*<0.05) (Supplementary Table S2). 1,665/1,607 DEGs in seedlings and 2,153/1,499 DEGs in roots were up- and down-regulated, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). To validate the reliability of DEGs identified using the RNA-seq data, fourteen up-regulated and thirteen down-regulated genes from seedlings, and eleven up-regulated and fourteen down-regulated genes from roots were chosen randomly for the RT-qPCR assay. We found that 26/27 (Supplementary Table S3) and 25/25 genes (Supplementary Table S4) matched well with the RNA-seq assay in each respective tissue, which confirmed the reliability of DEGs identified using the RNA-seq.

To investigate tissue-related gene expression under salt treatment, we performed a pairwise comparison using up- or down-regulated genes between seedlings and roots. We found that 526 genes were up-regulated, and 402 genes were down-regulated in both tissues under the salt condition. In addition, 176 genes exhibited an opposite expression trend between seedlings and roots, including 47 genes up-regulated in seedlings but down-regulated in roots, and 129 genes up-regulated in roots but down-regulated in seedlings (**Fig. 1**), which were further

validated by the RT-qPCR assay (Supplementary Table S5). The rest exhibited tissue-related transcriptional changes in response to salt stress, which included 1,092/1,076 in seedlings, and 1,498/1,050 genes in roots with up-/down-regulation, respectively (**Fig. 1**). After performing GO term enrichment analyses, we found that the up-regulated genes mainly functioned in response to stimuli or stresses, while the down-regulated genes exhibited functions in response to stimuli or stresses and metabolic processes in seedlings. In contrast, the up-regulated genes displayed functions mainly associated with metabolic processes, catalytic activities, cell wall and membrane, and the down-regulated genes primarily functioned in responses to stimuli or stresses in roots (Supplementary Fig. S2). The primary functions of 176 genes, with an opposite expression trend between seedlings and roots, were mainly associated with oxidation-reduction and lipid metabolic processes (**Fig. 2D**) while functions in lipid metabolic processes were only detected in 17 out of 129 genes up-regulated in roots but down-regulated in seedlings (**Fig. 2E**). We did not see GO terms enriched in other genes with opposite expression between seedlings and roots under salt treatment, including 47 genes up-regulated in seedlings but down-regulated in roots.

To assess the representativeness of our RNA-seq data sets, we compared our DEGs with 17 typical transcription factors (family) (OsDREB1A/ B/ F; OsABF2; OsABI5; bZIP23; NAC6/SNAC2; OsNAC5; ZFP252/179; OsWRKY45-2; OsTIFY11a; OsSOS1/2/3 and OsMYB3R-2) that are responsible for regulating the expression of salt-responsive genes in rice . We detected twelve of them in

seedlings, including nine up-regulated genes and three down-regulated genes, but only six of them in roots, including one gene up-regulated and five genes down-regulated (Supplementary Table S6). Particularly, OsSOS2/3 were up-regulated in seedlings while OsSOS1/2 were down-regulated in roots under salt treatment (Supplementary Table S6), which is consistent with previous findings about involvement of SOS pathway in salt tolerance in rice ([Martínez-Atienza et al.](#page-35-0)  [2007](#page-35-0)) . Moreover, we found that the majority of TFs in seedlings exhibited similar transcriptional changes when compared with previous reports under salt treatment [\(Hossain et al. 2010](#page-32-2), [Ito et al. 2006,](#page-33-2) Todaka et al. 2012). Consistently, we observed more TF interaction networks occurred in seedlings than roots (**Fig. 2A**). We observed more DEGs in roots than in seedlings under salt treatment (Supplementary Fig. S3A). According to GO term analyses, we found that functions related to photosynthesis, thylakoid, oxygen binding, lipid metabolic processes and plastids were overrepresented in genes only expressed in seedlings, while functions associated with carbohydrate binding, response to stresses and transcription regulatory or factor activities were dominantly enriched in genes only expressed in roots (Supplementary Fig. S3B). As expected, distinct protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were observed between seedlings and roots (**Fig. 2**), for instance, salt responsive genes (*OsRC2-7*) interacted with dehydrin in seedlings (**Fig. 2B**), while supernodes with interactions among cellulose, chitinase, GPI anchored protein, actin-binding protein or auxin-responsive protein were observed in roots, which may affect cell wall or membrane functions during salt treatment

(**Fig. 2C**). PPI networks further reflected differential tissue-related responses occurred in rice. Collectively, our transcriptomic analyses demonstrate that salt stress causes global transcriptional changes in seedlings and roots, and majority of DEGs display tissue-related changes in rice.

# **Dynamic changes in histone modifications between seedlings and roots under salt treatment**

Dynamic changes of histone marks with concomitant transcriptional changes in stress responses have been widely documented in various plant species ([Kim et](#page-33-0)  [al. 2015,](#page-33-0) [Meyer 2015](#page-35-1), Probst and Scheid 2015). To investigate tissue-specific dynamics of histone marks in response to stresses in plants, we performed ChIP experiments followed by Illumina sequencing (ChIP-seq) with a panel of commercial antibodies against six histone marks, including H3K4me3, H3K36me3, H3K27me3, H3K9ac, H3K27ac and H4K12ac. After global peak callings, we found that the majority of peaks overlapped in the same tissue between mock and NaCl treatment (Supplementary Table S7), reflecting salt treatment did not cause dramatic changes in histone modifications. To characterize the effects of salt treatment on dynamic chromatin changes, we designated peaks of each mark specifically present in the mock or only related to salt treatment as salt-induced losses and gains of peaks, respectively (Supplementary Table S7). In general, among six histone marks examined, all marks displayed dramatic losses in roots, by contrast, more peaks of H3K9ac and H4K12ac, but less peaks of the other four marks were observed in seedlings under salt treatment (Supplementary Table S7).

In addition, when compared to each respective mock we observed more peaks of all histone marks examined in seedlings under salt treatment, but dramatic less peaks of H3K4me3, H3K27ac, H3K9ac and H3K27me3, and almost no change of H3K36me3 and H4K12ac in root under salt treatment (Supplementary Table S8). Thus, our analyses show that salt treatment induces differential changes in chromatin levels between seedlings and in roots, indicating salt-induced tissue-related chromatin changes in rice.

To investigate the genomic distribution of salt-induced chromatin changes, we divided the whole genome into five sub-genomic functional loci (promoters, exons, introns, 1 kb downstream of the TESs and intergenic regions with at least 1 kb away from either the TSSs or the TESs of any genes) (**Fig. 3**). In general, we noticed distinct genomic distribution of marks examined in mock or salt treatment within each tissue. However, dramatic changes occurred in serval marks, like H3K4me3, H3K36me3, H3K27ac and H3K27me3, and certain sub-genomic loci in seedlings and roots between mock and salt treatment (**Fig. 3**). For example, when compared with mock, H3K4me3, an active mark, decreased dramatically in exons, but increased in introns in seedlings, by contrast, it reduced significantly in exons in roots. H3K27me3, a repressive mark, increased dramatically in intergenic regions in roots, but decreased significantly in exons in both tissues. H3K36me3 increased significantly in promoters but decreased dramatically in 1kb downstream of TES in roots only. Taken together, all the above analyses demonstrate that salt treatment induces local or global chromatin changes. Especially, salt treatment

induces mark or tissue-related chromatin changes on the genome-wide scale, suggesting that tissue-dependent epigenetic mechanisms might be responsible for the salt response in rice.

# **Salt treatment induces differential changes in occupancies of modified nucleosomes between seedlings and roots**

The presence of H3K4me3 and acetylation at diverse lysine residues of H3 or H4 is usually correlated with gene activation (Shahbazian and Grunstein 2007). In contrast, enrichment of H3K27me3 usually leads to the repression of gene transcription ([Kouzarides 2007](#page-34-2), [Liu et al. 2010](#page-34-3)). Similarly, the correlation analysis using deepTools (Ramirez et al. 2014) in our study demonstrated that active marks (H3K4me3, H3K36me3, H3K27ac, H4K12ac and H3K27ac) were positively correlated with each other, but reversely correlated with the repressive mark. In contrast, H3K27me3 displayed a weak positive correlation with H3K4me3, but a negative correlation with other active marks in the mock of roots. However, salt treatment caused a positive correlation between all active marks and H3K27me3, further indicating the differential changes in histone modifications between seedlings and roots under salt treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4). To test the quality of our ChIP-seq data sets, we divided all expressed non-TE genes from seedlings and roots into five bins (from the top 20% to the bottom 100%) according to their expression levels (FPKM values). After association analyses, we observed that the elevation of active mark enrichment levels (H3K4me3 and H4K12ac etc.) was closely related to the expression levels of genes either in

12

seedlings or roots, by contrast, enrichment levels of H3K27me3, a repressive mark, exhibited opposite changes to the expression levels of genes (Supplementary Fig. S5). In addition, we randomly selected 12 and 11 genomic loci displaying co-localization of H3K4me3 with H4K12ac in both tissues, respectively, for the ChIP-qPCR assay using two biologically replicated ChIPed DNA. We noticed that ChIP-qPCR matched well with ChIP-seq in the change trend (Supplementary Table S9). These analyses confirmed that the quality of our ChIP-seq data sets were justified for further analyses. We then found that salt treatment reduced the genic enrichment levels of all marks in both tissues, and more reduction occurred in roots than in seedlings, especially for H3K27me3 (Table 1). After plotting the normalized reads count from each mark across the ± 1 kb of the TSSs of all non-TE genes and random control in each tissue, we found that H3K4me3 or H3K9ac was significantly enriched in mock or salt treatment in seedlings or roots when compared with each respective random control (Supplementary Fig. S6A). After checking enrichment levels of all marks in association with up- or down-regulated genes in both tissues, we observed a significant reduction in enrichment levels of all active marks in down-regulated genes in both tissues. However, we found that a significant elevation of H3K9ac and H4K12ac only occurred in up-regulated genes in seedlings, but not in roots, which is opposite to a widely accepted concept that active marks positively correlate with gene expression in eukaryotes (**Fig. 4**). # **Impacts of chromatin states on the differential expression of salt-responsive genes between seedlings and roots**

Sequential actions or combinations of diverse histone marks, referred to as chromatin states (CSs), define distinct genomic activities based on the readout of epigenomic indexing features during development or stress responses in eukaryotes (Roudier et al. 2011, Sanz et al. 2016). Overall, we did not observe an elevation trend in the majority of individual marks with enrichment examined in relation to up-regulated genes in seedlings and roots under salt treatment (Supplementary Fig. S5). We suspected whether certain CSs instead of an individual mark might play

14

vital roles in the differential transcription of salt-responsive genes in both tissues. We then performed the Hidden Markov Model (ChromHMM) assay and divided global chromatin structures into fifteen states according to the combinations of marks examined (**Fig. 6E**). In general, three main types of CSs were observed according to enrichment levels of mark combinations: no enrichment with any of the tested marks (CS4/5), low enrichment of certain marks (CS3/14/15) and high enrichment of certain marks (CS1/2/6/7/8/9/10/11/12/13). We next observed two distinct profiles of CSs: either dominantly enriched around the TSSs of DEGs, or primarily covered the upstream region of the TSSs and the downstream region with 1 kb away from the TSSs of DEGs. CS1/2/3/9/10 and CS1/3/5/9/10 displayed a significant elevation associated with up-regulated genes in seedlings and roots, respectively, when compared with the corresponding mock (**Fig. 6A, B**). By contrast, a significant reduction in CS2/3/9/10 and CS3/4/9 occurred within the down-regulated genes in seedlings and roots, respectively (**Fig. 6C, D**), supporting that CS2/3/9/10 in seedlings and CS3/9 in roots functioned as active chromatins during salt treatment in rice. Interestingly, we noticed that certain CSs exhibited a distinct function in affecting the expression of DEGs between seedlings and roots. For example, CS2 affected the expression of DEGs in seedlings but not in roots, while CS5 and CS4 were mainly responsible for up- and down-regulation of DEGs in roots, respectively. When compared with random controls, significant changes in CSs were observed in DEGs in both tissues (Supplementary Fig. S8**)**. These

results imply that salt-responsive genes might be differentially regulated at chromatin levels between seedlings and roots.

# **Functions of H3K27me3-related chromatin states in differential regulation of salt-responsive genes between seedlings and roots**

The presence of H3K27me3 is usually associated with chromatin compaction, leading to gene silencing in eukaryotes [\(Kouzarides 2007,](#page-34-2) [Liu et al. 2010\)](#page-34-3). Thus, H3K27me3 usually displays a negative correlation with gene expression in eukaryotes. Although H3K27me3 exhibited a slightly higher correlation with H3K4me3 than H3K36me3 in both tissues from mock or salt treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4), we noticed that CS6 contained a high enrichment of H3K27me3 and CS7 contained a bivalent mark, H3K27me3 and H3K4me3, with antagonistic functions with each other in the regulation of gene expression (**Fig. 7A-D**). To investigate possible effects of these two CSs on the expression of salt-related DEGs between seedlings and roots, we conducted an association analysis, and found that the enrichment of CS6 exhibited a trend opposite to the transcriptional changes of up- or down-regulated genes in seedlings (**Fig. 7A-D**). However, CS6 distributed in the upstream of the TSSs and 1 kb away from the downstream of the TSSs may regulate the expression of the up-regulated genes, by contrast, CS6 encompassing the 1 kb downstream of the TSSs may be responsible for the regulation of down-regulated gene transcription in roots (**Fig. 7A-D**). These analyses indicated that H3K27me3-dominated CS6 mainly functioned as a repressive mark in regulating DEGs between seedlings and roots, especially it

might exert genomic position-dependent roles in roots. Unlike CS6, CS7 enrichment displayed a trend opposite to the expression levels of DEGs in roots, exhibiting functions of repressive chromatin. However, we observed that CS7 enrichment exhibited the same trend with changes of the expression of DEGs in seedlings, implying that it may act active chromatin to affect the expression of DEGs in seedlings. Thus, our analyses indicate that CS6 and CS7 display tissue-related functions, which are responsible for the expression of DEGs between seedlings and roots.

To further investigate how changes of chromatin signatures affect differential gene transcription under salt treatment, we performed the self-organization mapping (SOM) analysis according to ChromHMM segments results. We used the resulting matrix of 24-dimensional data vectors to train a SOM with a map size as 1,350 units (30 x 45). We then compared unit changes associated with six different chromatin marks during salt treatment (detailed results deposited in Supplementary dataset: https://pan.baidu.com/s/1NgZqFBKbdOFeM9UsRE\_sFQ**)**. Specifically, we detected changes of units associated with H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 in both tissues under salt treatment (**Fig. 7E**). In general, salt treatment caused little more units decreased than increased in chromatin densities in both tissues (Supplementary Table S10). After extracting genes associated with different segments for gene ontology term analyses (Supplementary Table S11), different GO terms were observed in genes with differential chromatin signatures in each tissue (Supplementary Table S12). For example, genes associated with gains of

H3K4me3-related chromatin signatures in seedlings and roots under salt treatment were found to mainly function in cellular/ biological processes, and cellular processes or protein binding, respectively. In contrast, genes associated with losses of H3K27me3-related chromatin signatures in seedlings and roots under salt treatment were mainly overrepresented in metabolic processes or cytoplasm membrane-bounded vesicle, and cellular processes or protein/nucleotide binding etc., respectively. Taken together, all the above analyses are the first to demonstrate that chromatin states, rather than an individual mark, play dominant roles in regulating the differential expression of genes in response to salt treatment between seedlings and roots.

#### **Discussion**

#### **Tissue-dependent transcriptional changes in response to salt treatment**

Stress-inducible transcriptional changes on a genome-wide scale predominantly occur in plants in response to various abiotic stresses, representing one of the essential molecular mechanisms for facilitating the ability of plants to cope with stresses for better survival. Global transcriptional changes have been extensively investigated in different plant species in response to various abiotic stresses, such as drought, dehydration, salt, cold, and high light ([Chao et al. 2005](#page-31-4), [Dijk et al.](#page-31-2)  [2010](#page-31-2), [do Amaral et al. 2016,](#page-31-5) [Humbert et al. 2013](#page-33-3), Rasmussen et al. 2013, Yang et al. 2015, Zhao et al. 2015, Zhou et al. 2016, Zong et al. 2013). Through transcriptomic investigations, various numbers of stress-responsive genes have

been detected within different species or under different stresses, helping to elucidate the molecular basis of stress tolerance in plants, especially for crops. How stress-related chromatin changes occur between different tissues, however, is still understudied. It has been documented that Na+ usually accumulates in root and shoot parts when plants were under exposure to the salt condition [\(Nieves-Cordones et al. 2016\)](#page-35-2). Thus, our study mainly focused the difference of DEGs and changes in histone marks between seedlings and roots. It showed that tissue-dependent transcriptional changes occurred in rice seedlings and roots, in response to salt treatment. These changes included some of transcription factors, which are mainly involved in the regulation of salt-responsive gene expression in rice (Todaka et al. 2012). A possible explanation is that adaptation-related mechanisms could drive salt-related transcriptional changes between seedlings and roots. In fact, roots usually grow in the liquid culture medium, while seedlings grow above the water and expose to air. Once rice seedlings were subject to salt stress by addition of NaCl to the liquid medium, for better survival, the plants will initiate protective mechanisms to either prevent absorbance of Na<sup>+</sup> through roots or reduce the Na<sup>+</sup> absorbance rate. Simultaneously it will reduce the evaporation rate on the seedling surface at the early stage. The distinct responses to salt stress between seedlings and roots potentially associate with the differential expression of certain salt-responsive genes in each respective tissue. This hypothesis can be evidenced by distinct functional GO terms for DEGs within the same tissue or between different tissues during salt treatment in our study.

#### **Dynamic changes of histone marks in response to salt treatment**

Covalent modifications of histones play important roles in regulating gene expression through affecting the packing states of the corresponding nucleosomes [\(Jenuwein and Allis 2001](#page-33-4)). Emerging evidence demonstrates that the chromatin structure undergoes either local or global dynamic changes during plants in response to various abiotic stresses (Pandey et al. 2016, Yuan et al. 2013), suggesting that chromatin-based mechanisms are involved in the plant stress response. It has been reported that the enrichment levels of H3K4me3 are dependent on the expression levels of drought-responsive genes in rice (Zong et al. 2013). Salt conditions have been reported to induce H3K4me3 and H3K9ac more enriched in stress-inducible genes in *Arabidopsis* and soybean [\(Chen and Wu 2010](#page-31-3), Song et al. 2012). Current progress indicated that stress-induced chromatin changes are either plant species or combined types of stresses-related. Similar to previous findings, our study demonstrated that salt treatment can induce tissue or mark-related dynamic changes of histone marks in rice. Similarly, it has been documented that cold treatment leads to a global decrease in acetylation of H3 and H4 in maize ([Hu et al. 2011\)](#page-32-0); in contrast, a local reduction of H3K27me3 occurs in several cold-responsive genes in *Arabidopsis* ([Kwon et al. 2009](#page-34-4)). We assume that there are two possibilities for explaining the dramatic reduction in modified nucleosomes we observed in rice under salt treatment. One possibility is that the changes are directly the biological relevance-related, which is similar to other documented stresses. More likely, changes of histone occupancies may play

pivotal roles in regulating the transcription of salt-responsive genes, thereby conferring salt-tolerance in rice. Of course, we cannot rule out the possibility that changes of certain histone marks are a consequence of transcriptional changes in the corresponding genes. In our current study, it is difficult to determine the consequence and cause between changes of gene expression and dynamics of histone marks in response to salt treatment, they more likely interact with each other. Another possibility is that the phenomenon is caused by high concentrations of salt, which can destabilize nucleosomes or decrease their solubility, thereby reducing the actual concentrations of modified nucleosomes interacting with the antibody of interest, and ultimately affecting the ChIP efficiency. It has been reported that high salt conditions can indeed destabilize nucleosomes and increase their insolubility *in vivo* [\(Ni and Cole 1994](#page-35-3), Russev et al. 1980). Distinct ChIP-seq-based chromatin landscapes were observed when chromatin was extracted with different salt solutions, and the nucleosome occupancies around the 5' end of the active genes displayed a reverse correlation with salt concentration-related chromatin extracts in the *Drosophila* genome ([Henikoff et al.](#page-32-3)  [2009](#page-32-3)). The high salt solution has been found to result in depletion of H3.3/H2A.Z double-mark-related nucleosomes positioned in nucleosome-free regions of active promoters in *HeLa* S3 cell lines ([Jin et al. 2009\)](#page-33-5). After comparing stress-related changes of histone marks in different plants or different stresses, we found that H3K27me3 reduction occurred in *Arabidopsis* in response to cold [\(Kwon et al.](#page-34-4)  [2009](#page-34-4)) or salt stress (Sani et al. 2013), which is similar to the change we observed

in rice in response to salt treatment. In contrast, H3K4me3 can be elevated in *Arabidopsis* in response to salt ([Chen et al. 2010](#page-31-6), [Chen and Wu 2010](#page-31-3)), drought [\(Kim et al. 2008b\)](#page-33-6) or heat (Pecinka et al. 2010), which is opposite to the reduction of H3K4me3 in response to salt or drought stress in rice. Reduction or elevation of H3K9ac seems to be dependent on the stress treatment or plant species, which is similar to increases or decreases of histone acetylation at different residues in maize in response to salt treatment [\(Li et al. 2014](#page-34-5), Zhao et al. 2014) or reductions of histone acetylation in rice in response to drought ([Fang et al. 2014](#page-32-4)). Thus, stress-inducible changes of histone marks is plant species or stress-dependent. Of course, characterization of time-point related changes of histone marks in response to salt stress will help to elucidate how the corresponding modifiers respond to environmental cues.

# **Chromatin states function in regulating the transcription of salt-responsive genes**

Recent findings regarding functions of histone modifications in plant stress response are still very limited. In particular, it remains unclear how the combined actions of various histone marks affect gene expression in plants responding to abiotic stresses. Additionally, the chromatin-based mechanisms responsible for tissue-dependent transcription related to stress treatment are still largely unknown. Our study implied that chromatin states rather than an individual mark might primarily function in regulating the transcription of salt-responsive genes in rice. Similarly, our previous study showed that chromatin states are responsible for

22

regulating DEGs in rice in response to ionizing radiation (Pan et al. 2017). Consistent with our findings, it has been reported that H3K27me3 combined with its antagonistic mark H3K4me3 or H3K36me3 confers distinct roles in the regulation of genes in response to different stresses, including modulation of memory genes in the dehydration response ([Liu et al. 2014\)](#page-34-6), and the regulation of the *Arabidopsis FLC* gene during vernalization (Yang et al. 2014). Our findings about functions of chromatin states in response to salt stress can be evidenced by the overrepresented functions of DEGs associated with various chromatin states (**Fig. 6**; Table 2; Supplementary Fig. S9; Supplementary Table S13**)**. These functions are either necessary to enhance plant tolerance to salt stress, or they are responsible for adjusting biological processes to facilitate the ability of plants responding to stresses for improved survival. Interestingly, functions of some CSs might be conserved in regulating the expression of DEGs in response to various stresses in rice since they displayed the same change trend between salt and ionizing radiation treatment in rice (Pan et al. 2017). Therefore, salt related DEGs are differentially regulated by chromatin states, which might be one of the possible underlying chromatin-based mechanisms for the differential response to salt stress between seedlings and roots in rice. More importantly, our study can provide some additional valuable information about salt-inducible changes of histone marks or chromatin states in plants.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated tissue-dependent changes of gene expression and histone modifications between seedlings and roots during salt

23

treatment. In addition, our study showed that chromatin states (CSs) play crucial roles in regulating differential gene expression in response to the salt condition between seedlings and roots. Most importantly, the CS7-containing bivalent marks H3K4me3 and H3K27me3, with mutual functional exclusion, displayed distinct functions in the regulation of DEG expression between both tissues. H3K27me3 in CS7 mainly regulated the expression of DEGs in roots, but H3K4me3 in CS7 functioned in the expression of down-regulated genes in seedlings. Thus, our findings are important for an improved understanding of chromatin-based epigenetic mechanisms that confer salt tolerance in plants.

#### **Material and methods**

#### **Plant material, growth conditions and salt treatment**

Uniformly germinated rice cultivar "Nipponbare" seeds were transferred into 96-well PCR plates with a trimmed bottom. The plants were grown in the liquid culture medium containing the following components: NH4NO3 (1.40 mM), NaH2PO4 (0.32 mM), K2SO4 (0.51 mM), CaCl2.2H2O (1 mM), MgSO4.7H2O (1.7 mM), H3BO3 (19 μM), ZnSO4.7H2O (0.15 μM), CuSO4.5H2O (0.15 μM), (NH4)6Mo4O2.4H2O (0.14 μM), FeSO4•7H2O (0.04 mM), EDTA-Na2 (0.04 mM) and MnCl2.4H2O (9.47 μM). The plants were grown with a mean 28/22 ℃ (day/night) in a greenhouse with a 12 h/12 h light-dark cycle for two weeks. Thirty PCR plates of two-week hydro-cultured seedlings were split into two halves, one with continued growth in the normal condition without addition of NaCl (mock)

and the other was transferred into the liquid culture medium supplemented with NaCl to a final concentration of 150 mM for salt treatment. After 24 h treatment with NaCl, at which the phenotypic changes were caused by the salt stress rather than earlier salt-related osmotic shock, seedlings and roots from mock and salt-treated plants were collected and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until use. Two independent biological replicates for salt treatment and mock were performed for some of experiments specified below.

#### **Reverse transcription and quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) assay**

Total RNAs were extracted from seedlings and roots of mock and salt-treated plants, respectively, using the RNeasy plant Mini kit (Qiagen, 74904). Following DNase I treatment at 37 ℃ for 30 min for completely eliminating genomic DNA contamination, the total RNAs were reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNAs using the SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, 18080-044). qRT-PCR was performed using the Roche LightCycler 480 Real-time system (Roche). The PCR program was as follows: 95 ℃ x 30 sec, 40 cycles of 95 ℃ x 5 sec, 55-65 ℃ x 5 sec and plate reading, 65 ℃ x 31 sec, 60 cycles of 65 ℃ x 5 sec with 0.5 ℃/cycle with a ramp of 0.5 ℃/s and plate reading. The qRT-PCR primer sequences corresponding to each DEG randomly selected from seedlings and roots are listed in Supplementary Table S3, S4.

#### **NGS library preparation, Illumina sequencing and data analyses**

25 **RNA-seq assay:** Total RNAs were extracted from seedlings and roots 24 h post salt treatment, and the corresponding mock using the RNeasy plant Mini kit (Qiagen, 74904). Total RNA was treated with DNase I to completely remove genomic DNA contamination. mRNA was specifically enriched using magnetic beads coupled to oligo(dT). The enriched mRNA was subsequently used for RNA-seq library preparation following the standard protocol for preparing Illumina RNA-seq libraries. RNA-seq libraries from mock or salt treatment of seedlings or roots were biologically replicated. Finally, four total RNA-seq libraries for each tissue were sequenced using the paired-end mode on the Illumina HiSeq platform. The sequencing data sets were analyzed using Tophat (Trapnell et al. 2009) and Cufflink (Trapnell et al. 2010) software as previously described (Zhang et al. 2011). Briefly, sequencing reads were mapped to the version 7 pseudo-molecules (<http://rice.plantbiology>.msu.edu/pub/data/Eukaryotic\_Projects /o\_sativa/annotation\_dbs/pseudomolecules/version\_7.0/) using Tophat 2 with default parameters. The total number of reads (TotR), the number of mapped reads (MapR) and the percentage of mapped reads (% Map) are summarized in Table S1. Expression values (FPKM) of annotated genes were calculated using Cufflink with parameter -p –L. Sample A\_ck, Sample B\_salt sample A\_ck\_rep1.sam, sample A\_ck\_rep2.sam, sample B\_salt\_rep1.sam and sample B\_salt\_rep2.sam to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Genes with a significant difference in expression between mock and salt treatment were called DEGs. All DEGs with a fold change equal to or greater than 2, including up- and down-regulated genes, were used for further analyses.

**ChIP-seq assay:** ChIP experiments were conducted following a previously described method (Zhang et al. 2011). The key steps include the isolation and purification of nuclei, fragmentation of chromatin using MNase, incubation of fragmented chromatin with the antibody of interest overnight at 4 ℃, recovery and purification of DNA associated with antibody-bound nucleosomes for ChIP-seq library preparation. Six commercial ChIP-grade antibodies, H3K9ac (Millipore, 07-352), H3K27ac (Abcam, ab4729), H3K27me3 (Millipore, 07-449), H3K36me3 (Abcam, ab9050), H4K12ac (Millipore, 07-595) and H3K4me3 (Abcam, ab8580), were used for ChIP experiments with seedlings and roots. The ChIP-seq and corresponding input libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, E7645S). All ChIP-seq and input libraries were sequenced using the paired-end mode on the Illumina HiSeq platform. All ChIP-seq and input data sets were analyzed using a pipeline as previously described (Zhang et al. 2011). Any significant peaks were determined as enriched genomic loci associated with the mark examined.

**ChIP-qPCR assay:** ChIP-qPCR assay was conducted using two biologically replicated ChIPed DNA collected from anti-H4K12ac and anti-H3K4me3 ChIP experiments in seedlings and roots between mock and NaCl treatment. The summary information for targeted genes and primers used for ChIP-qPCR analysis are listed in Supplementary Table S9. One primer set was repeated three times in each qPCR assay.

#### **Reads count normalization**

The ± 1 kb upstream and downstream of the TSSs from all involved genes were divided into 50 bp per window for normalization. The number of uniquely mapped ChIP-seq reads per sliding window was first divided by the window length (50 bp) and then by the number of all uniquely mappable reads within the genome (Mb). All normalized ChIP-seq reads count was used for detailed analyses.

## **Western blotting assay**

Seedlings and roots of mock and salt-treated plants, which were the same as the RNA-seq and ChIP-seq experiments, were used for extracting total proteins using protein extraction buffer (1 M Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 5 M NaCl, 0.1% NP40, 24% [w/v] urea, 1 mM PMSF and 1 mM DTT). Approximately 40 µg of extracted proteins were denatured by heating at 98 ℃ for 10 min, followed by fractionation by 12% SDS-PAGE. Fractionated proteins were transferred to the blot using the semi-dry technique for 1 h at 100 V. After pre-blocking with 3% albumin in 1 x PBS for 2 h with constant shaking at RT, the blot was incubated with the primary antibodies of interest at dilutions ranging from 1:4,000-6,000 in 1 x PBS at 4 ℃ overnight on the shaker. After sequential washing with 1 x PBS and 1 x PBS plus 0.1% Tween-20 three times for 10 min each, the blot was incubated with 1 x PBS+0.1% Tween with 1:10,000 diluted secondary antibody conjugated to HRP for 2 h at RT. After washing three times in 1 x PBS plus 0.1% Tween-20, the blot was developed, and the immuno-signals were digitally recorded. H3K4me3 (Abcam, ab8580), H3K27ac (Abcam, ab4729) and H4K12ac (Millipore, 07-595) were used to detect the total amount of proteins extracted from seedlings and roots

between mock and NaCl treatment. For a relative comparison, the same blot was sequentially probed with the primary antibody against H3 (Abcam, ab1791).

**Chromatin States:** Chromatin states were analyzed using ChromHMM ([Ernst and](#page-32-5)  [Kellis 2012\)](#page-32-5) as previously described. Fifteen types of chromatin states were generated using LearnModel parameters similar to the results previously published [\(Ernst and Kellis 2012\)](#page-32-5); each state contained a distinct combination of six examined marks, including H3K9ac, H3K27ac, H4K12ac, H3K27me3, H3K4me3 and H3K36me3. The program 'Neighborhood Enrichment' with parameter -b 50 -r 50 -l 50 was used to calculate the enrichment levels for each state around ± 5 kb of the TSSs of DEGs.

## **Protein-protein interaction networks**

Salt-related differential expressed genes were used for construction of protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks. PPI networks were constructed following the published R package STRINGdb (Szklarczyk et al. 2015). Briefly, there were three main steps for making the networks. First, salt-related up-and down-regulated genes were selected for downstream analyses; secondly, all selected genes were mapped to the string database with parameters as belows: version = "10", species = 39947 and score\_threshold = 700 for the network construction; finally, the output was imported to the cytoscape (version 3.6.0) for reshaping the network, which was followed by clustering using the module MCODE.

## **Self-organization mapping training**

Chromosome stats segment regions from ChromHMM segments were used for the self-organization mapping analysis. Self-organization mapping (SOM) training was conducted according to the published procedures ([Liu et al. 2018\)](#page-34-7). Briefly, SOM training was conducted using average signal values of epigenomic data in each segment, which were calculated using the bigWigAverageOverBed program of UCSC Genome Browser. The SOM training was performed using ERANGE software (version 3.3). A series of parameters for SOM training were determined as below: the size of SOM maps as 30 by 45; the trails parameter as 10, and the timestep parameter as 6,000 and 24-dimensional data vectors used for SOM training. For two epigenomic data sets-related SOM training, the BW file and bigwigAverageOverBed program were used for calculating the average signal values of the epigenomic data in each segment. Finally, the mapsom program in ERANGE software was used to map the new data to the trained SOM map [\(Mortazavi et al. 2013\)](#page-35-4).

## **Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis**

The enriched GO terms were analyzed using an online tool called agriGO [\(http://bioinfo.cau.edu.cn/agriGO](http://bioinfo.cau.edu.cn/agriGO)) [\(Du et al. 2010](#page-31-7)).

### **Significance test**

To determine whether the difference in CSs or histone marks between mock and salt treatment was significant, we performed a two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test as previously described [\(Fang et al. 2016\)](#page-32-6). The CSs distributed across ± 5 kb of the TSSs of DEGs or a specific region in each

figure as indicated were chosen for the K-S test. R was used for all the two-sample K-S tests within groups, and "two.sided" was selected as the alternative hypothesis. The output of a two-tailed *p*-value less than 0.05 was considered as a significant difference between two samples. The whole region or a certain specified region as indicated was chosen for conducting the significance test.
