# Transcriptome analysis of genes related to resistance against powdery mildew in wheat-*Thinopyrum* alien addition disomic line germplasm SN6306

# Quanquan Li<sup>1</sup>, Zubiao Niu<sup>1</sup>, Yinguang Bao<sup>1</sup>, Qiuju Tian, Honggang Wang, Lingrang Kong, Deshun Feng\*

#### ABSTRACT

Wheat powdery mildew, which is mainly caused by Blumeria graminis f. sp. tritici (Bgt), seriously damages wheat production. The wheat-Thinopyrum intermedium alien addition disomic line germplasm SN6306, being one of the important sources of genes for wheat resistance, is highly resistant to Bgt E09 and to many other powdery mildew physiological races. However, knowledge on the resistance mechanism of SN6306 remains limited. Our study employed high-throughput RNA sequencing based on next-generation sequencing technology (Illumina) to obtain an overview of the transcriptome characteristics of SN6306 and its parent wheat Yannong 15 (YN15) during Bgt infection. The sequencing generated 104,773 unigenes, 9909 of which showed varied expression levels. Among the 9909 unigenes, 1678 unigenes showed 0 reads in YN15. The expression levels in Bgt-inoculated SN6306 and YN15 of exactly 39 unigenes that showed 0 or considerably low reads in YN15 were validated to identify the genes involved in Bgt resistance. Among the 39 unigenes, 12 unigenes were upregulated in SN6306 by 3-45 times. These unigenes mainly encoded kinase, synthase, proteases, and signal transduction proteins, which may play an important role in the resistance against Bgt. To confirm whether the unigenes that showed 0 reads in YN15 are really unique to SN6306. 8 unigenes were cloned and sequenced. Results showed that the selected unigenes are more similar to SN6306 and *Th. intermedium* than to the wheat cultivar YN15. The sequencing results further confirmed that the unigenes showing 0 reads in YN15 are unique to SN6306 and are most likely derived from Th. intermedium (Host) Nevski. Thus, the genes from Th. intermedium most probably conferred the resistance of SN6306 to Bgt.

#### 1. Introduction

Wheat powdery mildew is caused by *Blumeria graminis* f. sp. *tritici* (*Bgt*) and is one of the most important wheat diseases in areas with cool or maritime climates. This disease significantly affects winter wheat production. Given the use of intensive wheat cultivation systems, including semi-dwarf cultivars and improved irrigation and fertilization, powdery mildew severities have also increased in dry and warm areas (Hua et al., 2009). The most feasible means of controlling this disease and reducing yield loss is to breed resistant wheat varieties (He et al., 2009). However, some powdery mildew resistance genes are rapidly overcome by new or virulent *Bgt* races; thus, searching for new sources of resistance genes is necessary (Hua et al., 2009). *Th. intermedium* (Host) Barkworth and Dewey (2n = 6x = 42,  $E^eE^eE^bE^bS^tS^-$ <sup>t</sup>) is an allopolyploid containing three diploid genomes, namely,  $E^e$ ,  $E^b$  (also designated as E and J, respectively), and S<sup>t</sup> (Liu and Wang, 2005). *Th. intermedium* is an important potential donor of disease resistance

and other traits for common wheat improvement. Moreover, Th. intermedium has been hybridized with wheat and has been proven a valuable wild resource of disease resistance genes. For example, excellent resistance to *Fusarium* head blight was found on a *Th. intermedium* chromosome (Han et al., 2003). Resistance to powdery mildew was also found in a partial amphiploid (Liu et al., 2005) and a substitution line (Liu and Wang, 2005) derived from Th. intermedium. The powdery mildew resistance gene Pm43 was introgressed from Th. intermedium into wheat (He et al., 2009). The wheat cultivar Yannong 15 (YN15) is highly susceptible to Bgt race E09; however, the hybrid progenies of a cross between the common wheat YN15 and Th. intermedium (i.e., the wheat germplasm SN6306 with 2n = 44 (wheat alien disomic addition line germplasm)) is resistant to Bgt race E09 and to many other physiological races. The powdery mildew resistant trait of SN6306 is consistent with that of Th. intermedium. However, map-based cloning is not useful in cloning gene from wheat alien disomic addition line germplasm SN6306.

Wheat leaf transcriptome data analysis during *Bgt* E09 pathogen infection elucidates the mechanism of powdery mildew resistance and allows the identification of powdery mildew resistance genes. The purpose of the present work is to propose effective strategies for breeding resistant varieties and ultimately achieve improved control of wheat powdery mildew. In previous years, next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology has emerged as a frontier approach to high-throughput sequencing, drastically improving the efficiency and speed of gene discovery and immensely reducing time, human labor, and cost. NGS can also locate unannotated transcript isoform switching (Trapnell et al., 2010). A large number of sequence data, which represent the gene expression pattern of a tissue at a specific condition, can be easily obtained using this technology (Xiao et al., 2013).

A large number of distinct sequences (designated as unigenes) from the equally mixed total RNA of YN15 at five infection stages [0, 12, 24, 48 and 72 h after inoculation (hai) of *Bgt*] were obtained using the Illumina sequencing technology, and SN6306 at five infection stages were as above. The unigenes were then assigned to putative functions, classifications, or pathways on the basis of their similarity to the sequences in public database resources. The assembled and annotated transcriptome sequences provide a global view of the gene expression of YN15 and SN6306 during Bgt infection. These sequences are also the basis for Bgt resistance gene discovery. On the basis of our transcriptome data, we compared the gene expression patterns of YN15 and SN6306 after Bgt inoculation. This comparison revealed the molecular mechanism underlying the different resistance phenotypes between YN15 and SN6306 and elucidated the resistance mechanisms of wheat in response to Bgt infection. The gene expression profile analysis of YN15 and wheat-Thinopyrum alien addition disomic line germplasm SN6306 may aid in the identification of Bgt resistance-associated genes isolated from Th. intermedium. Our results may assist breeders in utilizing the genes isolated from Th. intermedium for future wheat breeding. Our results have also revealed that Th. intermedium is an important gene source for improving wheat powdery mildew resistance.

#### 2. Materials and methods

#### 2.1. Plant materials and wheat powdery mildew race

The wheat alien disomic addition line germplasm SN6306 (2n =44), which is highly resistant to powdery mildew and originates from the intergeneric hybridization between wheat cultivar YN15 (2n =42) and Th. intermedium, was stored in State Key Laboratory of Crop Biology. YN15 is a high-yield, high-quality winter wheat but is nonresistant to powdery mildew. The seeds were transferred into a mixture of substrate and vermiculite (1:1) after soaking in water for 24 h at 24 °C. The plants were grown in a phytotron set at 24 °C during the day and at 20 °C at night at long-day conditions (16 h light and 8 h dark), 75% relative humidity, and approximately 12,000 LX (white fluorescent lamps). The Chinese Bgt isolate E09, a prevalent pathotype in the Beijing area, was used in the pathogen inoculation test. The Bgt isolate E09 was also stored in State Key Laboratory of Crop Biology. To prepare the Bgt powder for inoculation into wheat leaves, we maintained the powdery mildew fungi on the sensitive wheat cultivar YN15 by transferring it weekly into fresh plants.

# 2.2. Genomic in situ hybridization (GISH) technology for SN6306 identification

The total St genomic DNA from *Pseudoroegneria strigosa* was labeled with digoxigenin-11-dUTP with the nick translation method and was used as a probe. The sheared genomic DNA from the Chinese Spring (ABD genomes, 2n = 42) was used as a blocker. The detailed procedures in preparing the hybridization mixture were described previously by Chen et al. (1998a) and Han et al. (2004). Chen et al. (1998b) employed the St-genomic probe from *Ps. strigosa* to study the genomic structure of *Th. intermedium*, which was redesignated as JJJ<sup>S</sup>J<sup>S</sup>StSt. The GISH signals were detected with fluorescein-conjugated *anti*-digoxigenin antibodies, and the slides were mounted in Vectrashield antifade solution (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA) containing propidium iodide. Photographs were captured using an Olympus BX-60 fluorescence microscope equipped with a charge-coupled device camera.

#### 2.3. Coomassie brilliant blue staining of wheat leaves

The wheat leaves were cut into pieces of a few centimeters in length and then fixed overnight in 2 ml centrifuge tubes containing 75% ethanol/25% glacial acetic acid. The leaves were subsequently rinsed twice with deionized water. The Coomassie brilliant blue stain (0.05% Coomassie brilliant blue R250, 10% acetic acid, 50% methanol) was then added into the tubes to stain the leaves for 10–15 min and was then either examined immediately or stored in buffer (20% glycerol and 5% acetic acid) for up to 48 h before observation under the Olympus BX60-32FB microscope (Yuan et al., 2011).

#### 2.4. Preparation of biological samples for RNA and DNA extraction

The inoculation of SN6306 and YN15 with the powdery mildew fungi was conducted using seven-day-old seedlings at a spore density of 150–250 conidia mm<sup>-2</sup>. Bgt was inoculated by brushing over the spores of the test plants collected from wheat plants that had been infected a week earlier. The plant material was collected from the leaves at 0, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hai. Inoculations and sampling were conducted at 8 a.m., except for those in the 12 hai sample, the inoculation and sampling of which were conducted at 8 p.m. Ten samples (one treatment, two kinds of wheat, and five time points) were prepared for RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated using the EasyPure™ Plant RNA Kit (TransGen, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Genomic DNA was extracted from the leaves of SN6306, YN15, and Th. intermedium using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method (Saghai-Maroof et al., 1984). The concentration and purity (A260/A280) of the extracted DNA and RNA were subsequently measured with the NANOPHOTOMETER P360 (Implen GmbH, München, Germany). The integrity of the genomic DNA and total RNA was evaluated via electrophoresis on 0.8% agarose gels at 180 V; they were then stained with ethidium bromide. The isolated RNA was used for RT-qPCR.

#### 2.5. Construction of cDNA library and Illumina sequencing

Mixture of equal amounts of wheat leaves from SN6306 at 0, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hai was used as one sample and mixture of equal amounts of wheat leaves from YN15 at 0, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hai was used as another sample for the transcriptome analysis. The data of the two samples would be compared with each other. Illumina sequencing was performed at Shanghai Hanyu Bio-Tech, Shanghai, China (http://www. hanyubio.com/) using the Illumina platform according to the manufacturer's instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Briefly, poly(A) RNA was isolated from the total RNA using the Micropoly (A) Purist<sup>™</sup> mRNA purification kit (Ambion, America). The double-stranded cDNA was then synthesized with the improved method of Ng et al. (2005) using the Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, America) with Gsul-oligo dT. The 5' cap structure of the synthesized cDNA was subjected to oxidation with NaI04 (Sigma, America), and then a biotin group was added to the RNA ends; Dynal M280 beads (Invitrogen, America) were used to select the mRNA/cDNA linked by biotin. The cDNA was purified from mRNA/cDNA using the alkaline lysis method; 5' adaptor was added to the purified cDNA DNA by ligase (TaKaRa, Japan), and the second strand cDNA was synthesized with Ex Tag polymerase (TaKaRa, Japan). The polyA and 5' adaptor was finally removed via digestion with Gsul. The

#### 2.6. Amplification of selected unigenes by common PCR and cloning

Ten unigene-specific primer pairs were used to identify the different unigenes in the final volume of 20 μl containing 2 μl diluted first-strand cDNA, 1 μl sense primer (10 μM), 1 μl anti-sense primer (10 μM), 6 μl double-distilled water, and 10 μl 2 × Power Taq PCR Master Mix® (BioTeke). The PCR program was separated into two stages. The first stage involved 40 cycles of a reaction at 94 °C for 40 s, at 55 °C for 40 s, at 72 °C for 90 s, and an extension at 72 °C for 5 min as the final stage. The amplified PCR products were separated using 1% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide. The gel was then screened with the Tanon 3500 Gel imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). The target bands were extracted from the agarose gel using a gel extraction kit (Biomiga). The cloning vector pMD18-T (TaKaRa, Biotech, Dalian, China) was used to subclone the extracted genomic DNA using T4 DNA ligase and then transformed into competent DH5a Escherichia coli. The plasmids, which were positively inserted, were sequenced with Biosun (Shanghai, China).

#### 2.7. Quantitative RT-PCR assay

To validate the expression pattern of the selected genes, we performed the SYBR Green real-time RT-PCR. The qRT-PCR reaction (20 μl) included 20 ng cDNA template and 0.25 μM each of primer and 1 × SYBR Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa Bio Inc., Japan). The reactions were performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) under the following conditions: 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s and at 60 °C for 30 s to calculate the cycle threshold (Ct) values, and a reaction at 95 °C for 10 s and then at 65–95 °C with 0.5 °C increment for 5 s to obtain the melt curves. The actin gene was selected for the endogenous control. The primer sequence of the selected genes and actin gene are listed in the Additional file. Real-Time PCR was performed in triplicate for each sample. The fold changes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method ([Livak and Schmittgen, 2001\)](#page-12-0).

#### 3.1. Cytologic identification of germplasm SN6306

GISH technology was used to analyze the karyotype of SN6306 using digoxigenin-labeled St-genomic DNA from Ps. strigosa as probe and wheat Chinese Spring DNA as blocker. The GISH pattern of the SN6306

#### 3.2. Determination of wheat powdery mildew resistance in SN6306 and YN15

SN6306 and YN15 were inoculated with Bgt race E09. One week later, the leaves of YN15 were covered with spores of wheat powdery mildew, which were visible to the naked eye. By contrast, the leaves of SN6306 showed only a few necrotic lesions; no spores were observed [\(Fig. 2](#page-2-0)). After inoculation with Bgt, the leaves of SN6306 and YN15 were collected at 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 168 h (7 days) and were stained with Coomassie brilliant blue. The stained leaves were microscopically observed, and the results showed that hyphae were obvious in YN15 but were absent in SN6306 at 72 h after inoculation with Bgt. After 7 days of inoculation, YN15 was covered with hyphae, and spores had formed, whereas SN6306 did not show hyphae and only had a few blue spots [\(Fig. 3\)](#page-2-0). These results confirmed that the wheat-Thinopyrum alien addition disomic line germplasm SN6306 was more resistant to wheat powdery mildew than any of its YN15 parents. This result laid the foundation for the subsequent research.

#### 3.3. Transcriptome comparison between SN6306 and YN15 after Bgt infection

To investigate the transcriptome of the Bgt-infected SN6306 and YN15, we prepared the RNA samples of the leaves obtained at four infection stages (12, 24, 48, and 72 hai), as well as the RNA samples of the non-inoculated leaves. Equal quantities of leaves were mixed and sequenced using the Illumina sequencing technology. We used a mixture of RNA samples from the pathogen-challenged and non-challenged plants to compare the transcriptomes of the two lines. Our objective was to identify not only the Bgt resistance-related genes in SN6306 but also the genes unique to SN6306 that may have been derived from Th. intermedium. Transcriptome de novo assembly was performed using the shortread assembly program Trinity; the results revealed 104,773 unigenes, 35,740 of which were longer than 500 bp [\(Fig. 4](#page-3-0)). The generated unigenes were analyzed with the "GetORF" function of the EMBOSS kit to determine their cDNA coding sequence ([Rice et al.,](#page-12-0) [2000](#page-12-0)). For annotation, all of the unigenes were aligned with Blastp to the NCBI NR protein, GO, KOG, and KEGG databases using a cutoff E-value of 10−<sup>5</sup> . GO enrichment analysis was performed to classify the gene function of the unigenes. According to sequence homology, 10,526 sequences can be categorized into 58 functional groups consisting of three domains: biological process ([Fig. 5](#page-4-0)A), cellular component ([Fig. 5](#page-4-0)B), and molecular function ([Fig. 5C](#page-4-0)). The total GO term of the 10,526 sequences was 57,971 (Supplementary Table S1). In each of the three main categories of the GO classification, the cardinal terms are "Catalytic activity" (6036, 21.6%), "Cell" (8686, 40.0%), and "Cellular process" (7552, 28.0%). We also noticed a high percentage of genes in the following categories: "binding (other binding)" (5551, 19.8%), "intracellular" (7131, 25.4%), and "macromolecule metabolism" (4783, 17.7%). Only a few genes were classified under "integrase activity" (1, 0.004%), "extracellular matrix" (10, 0.04%), "extracellular structure organization" (5, 0.02%), and "pathogenesis" (8, 0.03%) ([Fig. 5](#page-4-0)).

The KEGG database was used to systematically analyze the pathway annotation of the unigenes and to describe the complicated biological behaviors of the transcriptome. A total of 104,641 sequences with open reading frame (ORF) were assigned to 38 <span id="page-7-0"></span>KEGG pathways [\(Fig. 6\)](#page-5-0), and the KO number of these sequences was 5173. The most representative pathways of unigenes were the Metabolic pathways (2878, 35.9%) and Genetic information processing (1605, 19.9%). These annotations provided a valuable clue in investigating the specific processes and in identifying the genes that maybe involved in the Bgt-resistance of SN6306.

The numbers of reads of the two samples were converted into RPKM values to normalize the number of reads of each unigene in the RNA-seq data and to exclude the impact of RNA load and data volume [\(Mortazavi et al., 2008](#page-12-0)). The statistics significantly different unigenes between the samples of SN6306 and YN15 were 9909; the unigenes with zero reads in YN15 reached 1678. Compared with those in YN15, 5483 (55.3%) unigenes were upregulated and 4426 (46.7%) unigenes were downregulated in SN6306. [Fig. 7](#page-6-0) shows the changed KEGG pathway of the significantly up- and downregulated unigenes; these results provide information for effectively elucidating the function of the significantly changed unigenes and for easily identifying the wheat powdery mildew resistance genes. We deduced that the addition of a pair of chromosomes from Th. intermedium in SN6306 could cause the differential expression of genes in SN306 and YN15.

#### 3.4. Difference in the gene expression patterns of SN6306 and YN15 as revealed by qRT-PCR analysis during Bgt infection

In our data, we selected 39 unigenes that are related to plant disease resistance but are rarely or are not expressed in YN15. We also examined the expression of these genes in the Bgt-infected SN6306 and YN15 leaves. We focused not only on the genes related to plant disease resistance but also on the genes that are expressed in SN6306 but not in YN15 because these plant disease resistance genes might be unique to SN6306 and may have been derived from Th. intermedium (Host) Nevski. We aimed to determine the contribution of the exogenous genes to the resistance of SN6306 to wheat powdery mildew. The unigene comp37846\_c0\_seq1 was also predicted to encode aspartic proteinase nepenthesin (AP) and showed a high number of reads of RNA-seq in SN6306; the qRT-PCR result showed a 9-fold increase in its expression.

Moreover, the unigene comp42778\_c0\_seq1, a putative disease resistance RGA4 like-protein coding gene, showed a 12-fold increase in its expression. The unigene comp44411\_c0\_seq1 (which putatively encodes integral membrane proteins) showed an approximately 4-fold increase in expression. The comp47969\_c0\_seq1 showed the highest increase (49-fold) among the 39 unigenes; this unigene encoded entcopalyl diphosphate synthase (ent-CPS). The comp55281\_c0\_seq1 encoded an E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase At4g11680-like isoform 1, and the increase in its expression level was low (approximately 2-fold). The unigenes comp63597\_c0\_seq1 and comp63972\_c2\_seq1 putatively encoded protein kinase superfamily protein and protein kinase domaincontaining protein, respectively; comp63597\_c0\_seq1 showed an approximately 4-fold increase, whereas comp63972\_c2\_seq1 showed a higher expression level (approximately 5-fold increase). The 22 other unigenes, whose expression did not change, encoded NBS-LRR disease resistance proteins, hypothetical proteins, some disease resistance proteins, and probable LRR receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase, among others. Some of these 22 unigenes, such as comp40082\_c0\_seq1 (hypothetical protein), comp69339\_c0\_seq1 (a predicted salicylate Omethyltransferase-like protein), and comp69991\_c0\_seq1 (a putative 22 kDa kafirin cluster; Ty3-Gypsy type), showed a high expression level in SN6306, although these unigenes were not induced by the Bgt infection. The rest of the unigenes, including comp39098\_c0\_seq1 (hypothetical protein), comp61017\_c0\_seq1 (p450 domain-containing protein), and comp76419\_c3\_seq1 (beta-fructofuranosidase hypothetical protein), were downregulated. We also used the sequences of these unigenes for searching in the URGI database [\(http://urgi.versailles.inra.](http://urgi.versailles.inra.fr/srs83/displayTool.do?tool) [fr/srs83/displayTool.do?tool](http://urgi.versailles.inra.fr/srs83/displayTool.do?tool) Name=BlaBla) using the Blastn program. The results showed that the similarity of the sequences of most of the unigenes to those of wheat was less than 90%. It indicated that most of the 39 unigenes might have originated from Th. intermedium. Some sequences of the unigenes were also highly similar to those of wheat (e.g., comp47969\_c0\_seq1 and comp62548\_c0\_seq1). The similarity of these two unigenes to those of wheat genes was more than 98%, and the unigenes were upregulated by Bgt infection. Supplementary Table S3 shows the detailed information on the unigenes, qRT-PCR results, and unigene similarity in URGI.

#### 3.5. Identification of some unigenes expressed in SN6306 but not in YN15

We cloned eight unigenes to confirm whether the expression of the unigenes in SN6306 but not in YN15 was caused by a pair of additional chromosomes in SN6306 from Th. intermedium (Host). These eight unigenes were obtained from the 39 unigenes, whose expression levels were analyzed via qRT-PCR. These unigenes were comp225549\_c0\_seq1, comp44530\_c0\_seq1, comp66805\_c0\_seq1, comp76196\_c0\_seq1, comp37846\_c0\_seq1, comp69991\_c0\_seq1, comp44411\_c0\_seq1, and comp504470\_c0\_seq1. These genes included those that encoded a predicted disease resistance RPP8-like protein, a predicted disease resistance RPM1-like protein, a predicted probable LRR receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase, an extracellular invertase, an aspartic proteinase nepenthesin, a putative 22 kDa kafirin cluster protein, a putative integral membrane protein, and a predicted G-type lectin S-receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase. We used a primer sequence (Supplementary Table S4) to amplify the genomic DNA of SN6306, YN15, and Th. intermedium. The amplified fragments of these unigenes were then sequenced. We found that all the unigenes were amplified in the genomic DNA of SN6306 and that the amplified sequences of these unigenes were similar to the RNA-seq sequence results. By contrast, only some unigenes in the genomic DNA of YN15 were amplified; in addition, the amplified sequences were not similar to the SN6306 sequences and to the unigenes of RNA-seq. All of the selected unigenes were amplified in the genomic DNA of Th. intermedium; the amplified sequences were more similar to the SN6306 and RNA-seq sequence than to the amplified sequence in YN15. Table 1 shows the amplification results (the detailed data on the sequences and the results of the agarose gel electrophoresis are not shown). By analyzing the sequences of these unigenes, we speculated that these cloned genes, which were expressed in SN6306 but not in YN15, were most likely located in a pair of additional chromosomes from Th. intermedium found in SN6306. The sequencing results of the unigenes also verified the reliability of the RNA-seq results.

#### 4. Discussion

4.1. Transcriptome of SN6306 and YN15 leaves after Bgt infection provided an efficient way to discover endogenous and exogenous candidate resistance genes against wheat powdery mildew

The wheat cultivar YN15 is susceptible to wheat powdery mildew, whereas Th. intermedium is immune to this disease. Unlike its parent YN15, SN6306, which is a wheat alien disomic addition line germplasm, is resistant to wheat powdery mildew. The insufficient genetic or genomic information specifically on leaves infected with the Bgt race E09 hampers the further identification of wheat powdery mildew resistance-related genes. Our study found that more than 50% of these unigenes with ORF have Blastx hits in the NR database; the absence of annotation and ORF in some of the unigenes might have been caused by the relatively short length of their assembled sequences. However, some unigenes longer than 800 bp also did not show match hits with any known sequence, and some of these unigenes possibly represented novel wheat genes, which may serve as supplemental genomic information for wheat. In addition, some of these unigenes might have originated from Th. intermedium and may serve as supplemental genomic information for Th. intermedium as well.

On the basis of the comparison in the URGI website of the selected 39 unigenes for qRT-PCR, we found that most of them are possibly exogenous genes from Th. intermedium. However, only 12 of the 39 unigenes were upregulated by Bgt infection. Moreover, 2 of the 12 unigenes were highly similar to the wheat genes. We speculated that the wheat powdery mildew resistance of SN6306 was most likely caused by the genes derived from Th. intermedium and wheat. The wheat genes might have been affected by the exogenous genes; thus, their expression was higher in SN6306 than in YN15 and was induced by Bgt infection. Other unigenes that were not induced but were highly expressed in SN6306 might not have important relationships with wheat powdery mildew resistance, and these genes were only constitutive expressions in SN6306.

Although a large number of potentially interesting genes were obtained from the transcriptome data, we must mention that most of them were part of the corresponding gene sequences. Most of the wheat genome sequences can be obtained from the URGI website and are advantageous for transcriptome sequence assembly and for easily obtaining long unigenes in the future. We can also accurately and easily find exogenous genes from Th. intermedium by comparing the transcriptome of SN6306 with that of YN15. In summary, our result provided an overview of the gene expression pattern of Bgt-inoculated leaves of SN6306 and YN15. Our results also offered an important resource of sequence data for the further discovery of endogenous and exogenous resistant candidate genes.

Wall-associated kinase 1 (WAK1) is a transmembrane protein containing a cytoplasmic Ser/ Thr kinase domain and an extracellular domain that is in contact with the pectin fraction of plant cell walls [\(Decreux and Messiaen, 2005](#page-11-0)). Wak1 induction requires the positive regulator NPR1/NIM1; thus, Wak1 is a pathogen-related protein. There are also results provide a direct link between a protein kinase that could mediate signals from the ECM and the events precipitated by a pathogenic infection ([He et al.,](#page-12-0) [1998](#page-12-0)). Moreover, a receptor-like kinase gene (GbRLK) from cotton enhances the salinity and drought-stress tolerance of Arabidopsis [\(Zhao](#page-12-0) [et al., 2013](#page-12-0)). The unigene sequence with a 98% similarity to the wheat sequence was comp62548\_c0\_seq1. The sequences of the four other unigenes were approximately 80% similar to the wheat sequences; these unigenes might have come from the Th. intermedium chromosomes and were transferred into YN15, or they were possibly new genes in wheat. The Bgt-induced high expression and upregulation of the unigenes in SN6306 possibly conferred the resistance of SN6306 to wheat powdery mildew. Although the role of RLKs in wheat powdery mildew resistance has not been reported, we speculated that RLKs participate in the pathogen resistance of wheat on the basis of studies on other plants and on induced unigene expression. The RLK family is believed to play crucial roles in saccharide signaling, stress perception, and biotic/abiotic stress regulation ([Vaid et al.,](#page-12-0) [2013; Wang et al., 2013](#page-12-0)). For these reasons, we speculated that the RLK unigenes play an important role in wheat powdery mildew resistance and that RLKs could sense the signal from Bgt infection, thereby enabling the plant to respond to the pathogen infection.

#### 4.3. Ent-CPS and AP may also play an important role in wheat powdery mildew resistance

Ent-CPS is critical enzymes that can catalyze GGDP into ent-CDP in the biosynthesis of phytoalexins and phytohormones gibberellins [\(Otomo et al., 2004\)](#page-12-0). Some studies have reported that ent-CPS participates in phytoalexin biosynthesis and is associated with crop disease resistance. In rice, the ent-CPS, which are named OsCPS2 and OsCPS4, are involved in the biosynthesis of phytoalexins [\(Otomo et al., 2004; Prisic](#page-12-0) <span id="page-11-0"></span>[et al., 2004\)](#page-12-0). Another study reported that OsCPS2 and OsCPS4 induced by TvX/EIX and OsCIPK (calcineurin B-like protein-interacting protein kinases) 14/15 possibly regulates TvX/EIX-induced phytoalexin biosynthesis [\(Kurusu et al., 2010](#page-12-0)). In another cereal (corn), An2 gene expression is induced by a Fusarium attack, and the fungal-induced gene encodes an ent-CPS [\(Harris et al., 2005\)](#page-12-0). Moreover, An2 transcript accumulation precedes the highly localized kauralexin (ent-kaurane–related diterpenoid phytoalexin) production [\(Schmelz et al., 2011\)](#page-12-0). This report confirmed that An2 gene has an important impact on disease resistance in maize. However, no report so far associates ent-CPS with wheat powdery mildew resistance. Our result showed that the ent-CPS gene was induced by Bgt in SN6306 but not in YN15. This gene may be involved in the wheat powdery mildew resistance pathway by promoting phytoalexin production. Phytoalexin accumulation may confer resistance to wheat powdery mildew. This gene is 99% similar to the wheat gene and is thus likely a wheat gene. However, the high expression of this gene in SN6306 was probably caused by the pair of extra chromosomes in SN6306 that came from Th. intermedium; the extra chromosomes possibly affected the transcriptional regulation of the ent-CPS gene in SN6306 and contributed powdery mildew resistance to SN6306.

AP is one of the proteinase classes involved in several physiological processes in plants, including protein processing, senescence, and stress response; they also share many structural and functional features with mammalian and microbial APs. Plant APs are believed to participate in the processing and degradation of storage protein necessary for seed germination; in addition, they have been implicated in defense mechanisms against pathogens in tobacco, tomato, and potato leaves [\(Mazorra-Manzano et al., 2010](#page-12-0)). Some studies reported that APs exhibit antimicrobial activity, which is induced after infection. In potato, the changes in AP levels in response to Phytophthora infestans infection and wounding were studied, and the results showed that AP exerts a direct inhibitory effect on the germination of cysts of P. infestans and conidia of Fusarium solani; such outcome suggests that AP plays a role in the defense response of potato and that they are components of the plant defense response (Guevara et al., 2002; Guevara et al., 2005). A recent study showed that Solanum tuberosum aspartic proteases (StAPs) can kill the spores of F. solani and P. infestans in a dose-dependent manner; in addition, one report suggested that the presence of the PSI domain in mature StAPs is related to their antimicrobial activity [\(Mendieta et al., 2006; Muñoz et al., 2010](#page-12-0)). New APs exhibiting antimicrobial activity were also found in potato (Guevara et al., 2004). In Arabidopsis, the overexpression of an apoplastic AP causes dwarfing and resistance to the virulent Pseudomonas syringae [\(Xia et al., 2004](#page-12-0)). However, the association of AP with powdery mildew resistance in wheat has not been reported. Our study revealed that the AP gene is induced by Bgt infection.We then analyzed the transcriptome sequence of the AP unigene and found that its sequence was not similar to the wheat sequence or to that in the database; the similarity of this gene to the wheat sequence was less than 70%. We speculated that the unigene is a new gene in wheat or that it might have been transferred into YN15 from a pair of chromosomes of Th. intermedium. Given that we cloned the unigene of SN6306 and Th. intermedium but not the unigene of YN15, we speculated that the gene was likely from Th. intermedium. The antimicrobial activity of APs was ascribed to AP proteolysis; thus, AP may also exhibit proteolytic activity against wheat powdery mildew by killing the Bgt spores, thereby conferring the resistance of SN6306 to the disease.

In summary, the transcriptome data provided a global insight into the gene expression patterns in Bgt-infected SN6306 and YN15, as well as elucidated the molecular mechanism of Bgt resistance in wheat. Twelve candidate genes involved in Bgt resistance were identified, and characterizing their roles in wheat powdery mildew resistance is of great significance. We suggested some reasons explaining the resistance and sensitivity of SN6306 and YN15 to wheat powdery mildew, respectively. The genes that came from Th. intermedium might have affected the genome of common wheat and conferred wheat powdery mildew resistance to SN6306. Moreover, we speculated that the genes from wheat and Th. intermedium synergistically induced the wheat powdery mildew resistance. However, the unigenes that are new in wheat and those from Th. intermedium remain unclear. In addition, the impact of the exogenous genes from Th. intermedium on the resistance of SN6306 remains a subject of future studies. The cloning and functional analysis of two genes, an AP and an ent-CPS gene, are underway in our laboratory, and six other genes are currently under BSMV-VIGS functional analysis.
