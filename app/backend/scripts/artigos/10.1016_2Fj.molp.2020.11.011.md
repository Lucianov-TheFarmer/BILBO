Running title: Auxin-ERF13-KCS16-VLCFAs module regulates lateral root development

## Short summary

Auxin plays a critical role in lateral root (LR) formation. Recently studies suggest that Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinases3/6 (MPK3/6) play important roles in auxin-regulated LR development. Its downstream regulators are still largely unknown and besides MPK3/6, it is not known whether other MPKs are invovled in auxin-regulated LR development. Our study uncovers a novel molecular mechanism of MPK14-mediated auxin signaling in LR development via ERF13-regulated VLCFAs biosynthesis.

## Abstract

Auxin plays a critical role in lateral root (LR) formation. The signaling module composed of auxin responsive factors (ARFs)-lateral organ boundaries-domain transcription factors (LBDs) mediates auxin signaling to control almost every stage of LR development. Here, we show that auxin-induced degradation of the APETALA2/Ethylene Responsive Factor (AP2/ERF) transcription factor ERF13, dependent on MITOGEN-ACTIVATED PROTEIN KINASE MPK14-mediated phosphorylation, plays an essential role in LR development. Overexpression of ERF13 results in restricted passage of the LR primordia (LRP) through the endodermal layer, leading to highly reduced LR emergence. ERF13 inhibits the expression of 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase16 (KCS16), which encodes a fatty acid elongase involved in very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) biosynthesis. Overexpression of KCS16 or exogenous VLCFAs treatment rescues the LR emergence defects in *ERF13* overexpression lines, indicating a role downstream of the auxin-MPK14-ERF13 signaling module. In conclusion, this study uncovers a novel molecular mechanism of MPK14-mediated auxin signaling in LR development via ERF13-regulated VLCFAs biosynthesis.

**Keywords**: lateral root development, auxin, ERF13, MPK14, KCS16, very-long-chain fatty acids

## Introduction

Higher plants are immobile and must cope with diverse abiotic stresses. Plant roots, especially lateral roots (LRs), play crucial roles in the adaptation to various conditions (Casimiro et al., 2003; Lavenus et al., 2013; Nibau et al., 2008; Petricka et al., 2012). Unlike primary roots, LRs are postembryonic organs and initiate from pericycle founder cells within the primary roots in *Arabidopsis* (Casimiro et al., 2001). LR formation involves priming, initiation, patterning and emergence (Malamy and Benfey, 1997; Peret et al., 2009). On their way to the surface, LRs must cross three different cell layers: the neighbouring endodermis, cortex, and epidermis. Thus, LR formation requires intercellular communication between LR primordia (LRP) and the overlying cells. The emergence of LRs relies on LRP cell divisions and expansion, along with changes in the cell walls and the shapes of the overlying cells (Kumpf et al., 2013; Lucas et al., 2013; Malamy and Benfey, 1997; Peret et al., 2012; Swarup et al., 2008; Zhu et al., 2019). Cell death in the layers overlying LRP was also recently shown to regulate LR emergence (Escamez et al., 2020).

Very long chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) are fatty acids with an acyl chain length of 20 carbons or more synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum by the fatty acid elongase complex, which consists of 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase, 3-ketoacyl-CoA reductase, 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydratase, and trans-2,3-enoyl-CoA reductase (Bach and Faure, 2010). VLCFAs are not only components of cell membrane and extracellular lipids to form protective barriers, but also act as signaling molecules to regulate various biological processes. VLCFAs influence membrane dynamics during cell division and are thought to act as signal messengers involved in signal communication between organs during induction of cell pluripotency, which is essential for LRP formation and callus generation (Boutte and Jaillais, 2020; Morineau et al., 2016). The 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase1 (KCS1) mediated VLCFAs production has been shown to regulate pericycle cell proliferation and LRP development partly via the nuclear protein *Arabidopsis* ABERRANT LATERAL ROOT FORMATION 4 (ALF4) (Shang et al., 2016; Trinh et al., 2019). The

competence of pericycle cells for callus formation and plant in vitro regeneration capacity in plants are also regulated by VLCFAs (Shang et al., 2016). The production of VLCFAs is precisely thought to be regulated in a time and space dependent manner during LR development (Boutte and Jaillais, 2020). The AP2/EREBP transcription factor PUCHI targets the spatial expression of genes involved in VLCFAs synthesis, such as *KCS1*, *KCS20*, *KCR1*, *PAS2* and *ECR/CER10* to control cell proliferation during the early stages of LR development (Hirota et al., 2007; Trinh et al., 2019). Extracellular lipids, such as cuticle and suberin both contain VLCFAs (Boutte and Jaillais, 2020). Recent study indicated that unusual cutin-like compounds were found to be enriched in a cell layer overlaying the LRP and played an important role in LR emergence (Berhin et al., 2019).

Several studies suggest that Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinases (MPKs) play important roles in auxin-regulated LR development. The MKK4/5-MPK3/6 module was recently shown to be essential for LR development (Fernandez et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2019). Auxin activates the MKK4/5-MPK3/6 cascade through TMK1/4 to control the cell division pattern during LR morphogenesis (Huang et al., 2019). In addition to TMK1/4, auxin also activates MPK3/6 in an IDA-HAE/HSL2-dependent manner to regulate LR emergence by regulating pectin degradation and cell wall separation (Zhu et al., 2019). Furthermore, MPK6 was recently reported to act downstream of GOLVEN peptide signalling to restrict the number of initial asymmetric cell divisions during LR initiation (Fernandez et al., 2020). Although MPK3/6-regulated LR development and its upstream signaling pathway have been well studied, its downstream regulators are still largely unknown.

We recently showed that auxin activates the MPK14 kinase, leading to the stabilization of the non-canonical IAA33 protein and regulation of auxin signaling and root stem cell niche identity (Lv et al., 2020). Here, we discovered that MPK14 which belongs to group C MAPK family, inhibits LR development by regulating the protein stability of the APETALA2/Ethylene Responsive Factor (AP2/ERF) transcription factor ERF13. Overall, our work supports a model whereby MPK14-mediated auxin signaling promotes LR development by alleviating

ERF13-mediated repression of very-long-chain fatty acids biosynthesis.

#### Results

#### Group C MPKs positively regulate lateral root development.

Auxin has been shown to activate MKK4/5-MPK3/6 signaling via TMKs to regulate the cell division pattern during LR development (Huang et al., 2019). We previously showed that auxin activates MPK14, a group C MPK (Lv et al., 2020). Here, we found that auxin (1-Naphthaleneacetic acid, NAA) activates MPK14 and two other group C MPKs, MPK1 and MPK2, even in the presence of PEO-IAA, an inhibitor of the auxin receptor TRANSPORT INHIBITOR RESPONSE 1 (TIR1) (Figure 1A and Supplemental Figure 1). To investigate the role of MPKs in auxin-mediated LR development, we treated *Arabidopsis* seedlings with U0126, an inhibitor of MAP kinase kinases (Favata et al., 1998; Lee et al., 2001), or with epidermal growth factor (EGF), which can activate the MAPK pathway (Gao et al., 2005). We found that auxin-induced LR development was strongly reduced in the presence of U0126 (Figure 1B and 1C), whereas EGF treatment was sufficiently to increase LR development (Figure 1D and 1E).

During LR development, LR primordia (LRP) must pass through three cell layers: the overlaying endodermis, the cortex, and the epidermis. This process has been classified into eight stages (Figure 1F) (Malamy and Benfey, 1997). We found that U0126 mainly inhibited the transition from stage IV to V of LR development (Figure 1D-F), restricting the passage of the LRP through the endodermal layer. This result is in contrast to MPK3/6, which regulates the cell division pattern and restricts passage of the LRP through the overlaying cell layers (Fernandez et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2019).

U0126 prevents the activation of MPK3/6 as well as group C MPKs and other sub-group MPKs. Our reporter analysis indicated that both *MPK1* and *MPK14* are mainly expressed in stele and LRP (Supplemental Figure 2). To verify that the group C MPKs are involved in LR development, we examined wild-type, *mpk14*, *mpk1/2/14* 

and a transgenic *A. thaliana* line that overexpresses an MPK14 kinase-activated version ( $35:MYC:MPK14^{DD}$ ). To synchronize LR formation in seedlings, we applied a 90° gravitropic stimulus and LRP were quantified and staged at 18 and 48 h post gravitropic induction (pgi) (Figure 1F). LRP development was not affected at early stages (18 h pgi) but at 48 h pgi was delayed in the *mpk14* and *mpk1/2/14* mutants compared to wild-type and  $35:MYC:MPK14^{DD}$ , which were displaying similar phenotypes (Figure 1G and Supplemental Figure 3). Most LRPs of the *mpk1/2/14* mutant were blocked at stage IV (Figure 1G). Auxin treatment accelerated LRP development, such that at 48 pgi the wild-type and the *mpk1/2/14* mutant were similar and the  $35:MYC:MPK14^{DD}$  was faster, mostly at stage VIII (Figure 1G and 1H). These data further confirm the role of group C MPKs in LR development.

#### MPK14 interacts with ERF13.

To elucidate how group C MPKs regulate LR development, we searched for the interacting proteins. Given that ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR6 (ERF6), an AP2/ERF domain-containing transcription factor (Nakano et al., 2006), acts as a substrate of MPK3/6 to regulate fungal pathogen resistance and leaf growth in plants (Dubois et al., 2013; Meng et al., 2013), and that MPK3/6 also regulates LR development (Fernandez et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2019), we examined whether MPK1-14 interacts with ERF6 or with its homologous protein ERF13. ERF6 interacted with most MPKs except MPK8/12 in a yeast two-hybrid, whereas ERF13 interacted mainly with the group C MPKs in yeast (Supplemental Figure 4 and Figure 2A). In addition, MPK14-YFP co-immunoprecipitated with ERF13-MYC from A. thaliana mesophyll protoplasts (Figure 2B). A bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay revealed that ERF13-<sup>C</sup>YFP yielded a strong fluorescence signal in A. thaliana mesophyll protoplasts co-expressing MPK14-<sup>N</sup>YFP or MPK1-<sup>N</sup>YFP, whereas no signal was observed with the empty vector controls (Figure 2C and Supplemental Figure 5). Finally, MPK14-MBP-His directly interacted with ERF13-GST in an in vitro pull-down assay (Figure 2D). Therefore, we infer that ERF13 interacts with MPK14 in vitro and in vivo.

#### ERF13 inhibits lateral root development.

We found that *ERF13*, like *MPK1* and *MPK14*, was expressed mainly in stele and LRP (Supplemental Figure 6). To examine whether ERF13 is also involved in LR development, we examined erf13 mutants (two T-DNA insertion knock out mutants and a SRDX fusion line) and transgenic A. thaliana lines overexpressing ERF13 (35S:ERF13:MYC) (Supplemental Figure 7A). Compared with wild-type, the 35S:ERF13:MYC lines showed a significant reduction in emerged LR (LRE), whereas the erf13 mutants showed an increase in LRE (Figure 3A and 3B). The total LR density (LRP+LRE) was similar in wild-type, erf13 mutants and ERF13 overexpression lines (Figure 3B), suggesting the involvement of ERF13 in LR development, not in LR initiation. Regarding these features, the 35S:ERF13:MYC is reminiscent of the shy2-101/iaa3 mutant which produces more LRPs through endodermal auxin signaling feedback but very few LREs (Goh et al., 2012b). Unlike SHY2/IAA3 mediated endodermal auxin signaling feedback to inhibit the initiation of LRP, ERF13 had no effect on the LRP initiation (Figure 3B). To address whether the LR development phenotype is due to the abortion of primordia or a delay of development, we counted the numbers of LRE at 1 cm root zones from the hypocotyl-root junction in 12-day-old seedlings and found that most of the LRP can emerged (Supplemental Figure 7A and 7B). The process of LR development will be promoted when the root tip was cut off. We found that WT and 35S:ERF13:MYC seedlings had similar numbers of LRE 4 days after cutting off root tips (Supplemental Figure 7C and 7D). These results indicated that ERF13 just delayed the process of LR development. We next performed a detailed inspection of LRE stage VIII by cryo-scanning electron microscopy (SEM). In wild-type, the epidermis cells on each side of the emerged LR were nicely rounded and curved like a pair of lips (Figure 3C), similar to the previous reports (Kumpf et al., 2013; Zhu et al., 2019). The same phenotype was observed between mutants and overexpression lines of *ERF13* (Figure 3C), indicating that ERF13 did not affect the epidermis cell separation during LR emergence.

To gain further insight into how ERF13 regulates LR development, we examined the stage distribution of LRP in wild-type, *erf13* mutants and *ERF13* overexpression lines. Whereas about 10% of all LRP were at stage IV in wild-type seedlings, about 40% LRP were at this stage in *35S:ERF13:MYC* seedlings, indicating that overexpression of *ERF13* delays the transition of LRPs from stage IV to stage V (Figure 3D). We observed a significant increase of LRPs trapped underneath the endodermis layer in *35S:ERF13:MYC* roots compared to wild-type roots (Figure 3D), suggesting that LRPs in *35S:ERF13:MYC* roots are constrained inside the endodermis layer. The unsignificant phenotype in *erf13* mutants might be result from the redundant role of ERF13 with other AP2/ERF transcription factors (Figure 3D). In addition, flat shaped LRP were found both in WT and *35S:ERF13:MYC* plants, we noticed that the flat shaped LRPs in *35S:ERF13:MYC* roots were significant more than those in WT (Figure 3E). Taken together, we conclude that ERF13 negatively regulates the normal progression of LRPs through the endodermis layer during LR development.

## ERF13 inhibits LR development by repressing very-long-chain fatty acids biosynthesis.

To identify potential downstream targets of ERF13 during LR development, we performed RNA-Seq of roots from wild-type, *35S:ERF13:MYC* and 35S:ERF13:SRDX plants. We found that 108 genes were both down-regulated in 35S:ERF13:MYC and up-regulated in 35S:ERF13:SRDX compared to wild-type, whereas 63 genes were both up-regulated in 35S:ERF13:MYC and down-regulated in 35S:ERF13:SRDX compared to wild type (Supplemental Figure 8A). Of these genes, KCS16, which encodes 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase, had reduced expression in 35S:ERF13:MYC compared to wild-type, and increased expression in the 35S:ERF13:SRDX compared to wild-type (Supplemental Figure 8B). In addition, we found that *KCS16* expression was induced by auxin treatment in wild-type seedlings, and this induction was eliminated in 35S:ERF13:MYC lines and enhanced in 35S:ERF13:SRDX (Supplemental Figure 8B). Further analysis suggested that KCS16

is not a direct target of ERF13 (Supplemental Figure 8C). Two homologous genes *KCS8* and *KCS18* were also found repressed by ERF13 (Supplemental Figure 8D).

To examine if *KCS16* regulates LR development, we examined *KCS16* mutants. LRP development was similar in wild-type and *KCS16* mutants after 18 h gravitropic stimulus, but was delayed in both *kcs16-1* and *kcs16-2* mutants and accelerated in *35S:KCS16:MYC* lines compared with wild-type after 48 h gravitropic stimulus (Figure 4A and Supplemental Figure 9). In addition, *KCS16* reporter expression was detected mainly in LRPs, consistent with a role in LR development (Supplemental Figure 10).

To determine if ERF13 inhibits lateral root development by reducing *KCS16* expression, we overexpressed *KCS16* in the *35S:ERF13:MYC* overexpression line. We found that the inhibition of LR development in *35S:ERF13:MYC* was partly recovered when combined with *35S:GFP:KCS16* (Figure 4B and 4C). Thus, overexpression of *ERF13* inhibited LR development partly due to reduced expression of *KCS16*.

3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase is a subunit of the fatty acid elongase complex and catalyzes a rate-limiting step in very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) biosynthesis. Therefore we examined whether overexpression of *ERF13* affected VLCFAs contents. We analyzed the amount and proportion of VLCFAs in *ERF13* overexpression lines, which had highly reduced *KCS16* expression levels. Consistent with the known function of KCS16 in VLCFAs biosynthesis (Supplemental Figure 11), the saturated VLCFAs levels of C18:1, C22:0 and C24:0 were reduced in *35S:ERF13:MYC #6*, and partly recovered in *35S:GFP:KCS16* (*35S:ERF13:MYC #6*) *#3* compared to wild-type (Figure 4D). We treated *35S:ERF13:MYC* seedlings with a mixture of VLCFAs (C20:0, C22:0 and C24:0) and observed partial recovery of LR development (Figure 4E and 4F and Supplemental Figure 11).

We found that auxin-induced expression of *KCS16* was also partly dependent on ARF7/19 and LBDs (Supplemental Figure 12A). However, the LR phenotype of *lbd16/18/33* was not recovered by overexpression of *KCS16* (Supplemental Figure 12B and 12C), and treatment with exogenous VLCFAs did not recover the LR

phenotype of *arf7/19* and *lbd16/18/33* seedlings (Supplemental Figure 12D and 12E).

VLCFAs are incorporated into various classes of lipids including suberin to function as a barrier to regulate water and nutrient transport and protect plants from environmental stresses (Li-Beisson et al., 2013). To test whether there are any differences in suberin quantity among WT, 35S:ERF13:MYC #6 and the kcs16-1 mutant, fluoral yellow 088 staining was performed (Baxter et al., 2009). We found that the suberin lamellae appears very similar to WT in 35S:ERF13:MYC #6 and kcs16-1 (Supplemental Figure 13A and 13B), a similar observation which was also reported in puchi when another AP2/ERF transcription factor mutated (Trinh et al., 2019). These results indicate that the ERF13-KCS16-VLCFAs module regulates LR development via a mechanism independent from suberin deposition. Besides suberin, degradation of pectin is also important to the cell wall separation process, which played an essential role during LR development (Micheli, 2001). A pectin-staining assay using propidium iodide (PI) was performed to evaluate pectin breakdown during LR development (Kumpf et al., 2013; Rounds et al., 2011). In the WT, reduced PI staining of cell walls between the endodermis and cortex cells directly overlaying LRP was observed (Supplemental Figure 13C and 13D), consistent with an expected pectin dissolution during cell separation (Cai and Lashbrook, 2008). In contrast, the PI intensity of the endodermis/cortex cell walls overlaying LRP in 35S:ERF13:MYC #6 seedlings was similar to the intensity at positions without emerging LRP, where no pectin dissolution occured (Supplemental Figure 13C and 13D), suggesting a defect in pectin degradation during LR development in 35S:ERF13:MYC seedlings.

#### Auxin induces ERF13 degradation.

We showed that ERF13 interacts with MPK1/2/14 (Figure 2), and that MPK1/2/14 are activated by auxin and promote LR formation (Figure 1A). We hypothesized that auxin destabilizes ERF13 to positively regulate LR development. Indeed, we found that treating *35S:ERF13:MYC* seedlings with auxin reduced ERF13 levels in roots over time (Figure 5A). We inhibited translation with cyloheximide, and found that auxin treatment still led to reduced ERF13 levels (Figure 5B), indicating that auxin

triggers ERF13 degradation. Moreover, we found that treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 prevented the reduction in ERF13 levels in the presence of auxin, revealing that degradation is dependent on the 26S proteasome pathway (Figure 5C). Inhibiting auxin biosynthesis enzymes by treating seedlings with yucasin (an inhibitor of YUCCA) and KYN (L-Kynurenine, a competitive inhibitor of TAA1) stabilized the ERF13 protein (Figure 5D).

To visualize ERF13 protein, we performed confocal analysis of roots expressing *35S:GFP:ERF13*, which had similar LR phenotype to the *35S:ERF13:MYC* line (Supplemental Figure 14). We found that ERF13-GFP protein accumulates at the early stages of (stage II- stage III) of LRP formation (Figure 5E). As LR development progresses, ERF13-GFP gradually degrades whereas auxin accumulates (Figure 5E). However, the accumulated GFP signals with LRP progressing and auxin induced GFP degradation were not observed in the *35S:GFP* control (Figure 5E). Overall, our data strongly suggest that auxin induces the degradation of ERF13 protein.

## Auxin-mediated degradation of ERF13 is regulated by MPK14-mediated phosphorylation.

Based on our data so far, we hypothesized that ERF13 is regulated by phosphorylation. To test this hypothesis, we extracted total proteins from roots treated with or without auxin, and incubated them with ERF13-GST in the presence of MG132. We found that ERF13-GST is phosphorylated, and that phosphorylation was higher in lysate from auxin-treated roots compared to untreated roots (Figure 6A and 6B).

To investigate whether MPK14 directly phosphorylates ERF13, we performed *in vitro* phosphorylation assays with ERF13-GST and recombinant MPK14<sup>DD</sup>-MBP-His (constitutively active MPK14). MPK14<sup>DD</sup>-MBP-His phosphorylated ERF13 *in vitro*, and treatment with calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase (CIP) abolished this phosphorylation (Figure 6C). We identified three potential MPK phosphorylation sites (Thr66, Ser67 and Thr124) within the amino acid sequence of ERF13 (Enslen and Davis, 2001; Sharrocks et al., 2000). We mutated all three Thr/Ser residues to Ala (ERF13<sup>AAA</sup>) and found that MPK14<sup>DD</sup>-MBP-His did not phosphorylate

ERF13<sup>AAA</sup>-GST *in vitro* (Figure 6C), suggesting that at least one of these sites is directly phosphorylated by MPK14.

Next, we tested whether MPK14-mediated phosphorylation of ERF13 affected its stability. We expressed kinase dead (MPK14<sup>AA</sup>-YFP), constitutively active (MPK14<sup>DD</sup>-YFP), and wild-type (MPK14<sup>WT</sup>-YFP) versions of MPK14 in *Arabidopsis* mesophyll protoplast cells generated from *35S:ERF13:MYC* seedlings. We found that ERF13 protein levels were highest in protoplasts expressing MPK14<sup>AA</sup> and lowest in protoplasts expressing MPK14<sup>DD</sup> (Figure 6D). These data suggest that MPK14-mediated phosphorylation of ERF13 triggers its degradation.

In addition, we manipulated MPK signaling in *35S:ERF13:MYC* seedlings and found that activating MPKs with EGF led to reduced ERF13 levels, whereas inhibiting MPKs with U0126 increased the levels of ERF13 (Supplemental Figure 15). Importantly, auxin-induced degradation of ERF13 was reduced in *mpk1/2/14* mutant compared to wild-type protoplasts (Figure 6E).

To examine the consequences of ERF13 phosphorylation *in vivo*, we expressed ERF13<sup>WT</sup>, ERF13<sup>AAA</sup> and ERF13<sup>DDD</sup> (ERF13 phosphomimetic version) in protoplasts. Auxin treatment led to a greater reduction in the levels of ERF13<sup>WT</sup> compared to ERF13<sup>AAA</sup> (Figure 6F). ERF13<sup>DDD</sup> levels were lower than those of ERF13<sup>WT</sup> and ERF13<sup>AAA</sup> even in the absence of auxin treatment (Figure 6F). These results further suggest that auxin treatment induces MPK14-mediated phosphorylation of ERF13, which in turn triggers ERF13 degradation. Moreover, LR development was inhibited in *ERF13<sup>AAA</sup>* overexpression lines but not in *ERF13<sup>DDD</sup>* overexpression lines, though both lines had similar *ERF13* transcript levels (Figure 6G and Supplemental Figure 16), further indicating that MPK14-mediated ERF13 phosphorylation and destabilization support LR development.

## Discussion

Based on our results, we propose a model for MPK14-mediated LR development via ERF13-regulated VLCFAs biosynthesis (Figure 6H). The canonical pathway of

auxin-regulated LR development is implemented through auxin-induced degradation of AUX/IAA proteins such as IAA14, eventually derepressing ARF proteins such as ARF7/19, which activate the expression of downstream *LBDs* to regulate LR formation. Auxin can also activate MPK3/6 through the TMK1/4-MKK4/5 module to regulate cell division and cell wall remodeling (CWR) during LR development. Based on the results of our current study, we propose that auxin activates the group C MAP kinases including MPK14, which interact with and phosphorylate ERF13. Phosphorylated ERF13 is degraded, releasing the repression of *KCS16*, and eventually inducing LR development by promoting very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) biosynthesis (Figure 6H).

ERF13 is an APETALA2/Ethylene Responsive Factor (AP2/ERF) transcription factor (Nakano et al., 2006). ERF109 mediates the crosstalk between jasmonic acid signaling and auxin biosynthesis to regulate LR formation by directly regulating the expression of *ASA1* and *YUC2*, which encode two key enzymes in auxin biosynthesis (Cai et al., 2014). The *PUCHI* gene, which encodes a putative AP2/ERF transcription factor, acts downstream of auxin signaling and is required to coordinate the pattern of cell divisions during the early stages of LRP development (Hirota et al., 2007). The ERF-VII transcription factors, which mediate hypoxic responses, were recently shown to inhibit LR development by repressing the expression of the auxin induced genes *LBD16*, *LBD18* and *PUCHI* (Shukla et al., 2019), in contrast with ERF109 and PUCHI, which both promote LR development. Here, we found that MPK14-mediated auxin signaling controls LR development by regulating ERF13 protein stability. Overall, these results indicate that AP2/ERF transcription factor families play various roles during LR development.

The mutant of  $\beta$ -ketoacyl reductase (KCR), which catalyzes the first reduction during VLCFAs elongation, displays reduced LR and closer observation revealed that LRPs formed but did not emerge (Beaudoin et al., 2009). Exogenous application of VLCFAs does not rescue the LR phenotype of *arf7/19* and *lbd16/18/33* seedlings because the *arf7/19* mutant has almost no LRPs and the LRPs in *lbd16/18/33* remain mainly at stage II and III (Goh et al., 2012a; Okushima et al., 2007). This result

suggests that ERF13-mediated VLCFAs biosynthesis restricts a different step of LR formation, the passage of LRP through endodermis layer during LR development. The *kcs1* mutant exhibits retarded LR formation and SLR/IAA14-ARF7/19-mediated LR initiation is necessarily required for VLCFA-regulated LR formation (Trinh et al., 2019). These observations are consistent with our results that VLCFAs regulate LR development independent of SLR/IAA14-ARF7/19-mediated LR initiation (Figure 6H).

Our work reveals the upstream regulatory factors that control VLCFAs during LR development, but the downstream regulatory mechanism remains of interest for future studies. VLCFAs are constituents of suberin, a cell wall barrier. A suberin layer was recently described overlying developing LRP (Li et al., 2017). However, we found that suberin lamellae formation was not affected in 35S:ERF13:MYC #6 and kcs16-1 compared to the WT control (Supplemental Figure 13A and 13B). The unchanged suberin lamellae formation was also reported in the *puchi* mutant which was defective in another AP2/ERF transcription factor and displaying affected LR development (Trinh et al., 2019). These results indicated that VLCFAs regulate LR development via a mechanism independent from suberin deposition. Our results show a defect in pectin degradation in LRP of 35S:ERF13:MYC (Supplemental Figure 13C and 13D), which suggests that VLCFAs could regulate LR development through pectin degradation. In addition, given that auxin activates MPK3/6 via TMKs to control the cell division pattern during LR development (Huang et al., 2019); it will be of interest to elucidate how auxin activates MPK14, and the potential role of TMKs. Finally, our results and previous studies indicate a complex regulation of VLCFAs synthesis during LR development notably by AP2/ERF transcription factors such as the positive regulator PUCHI and the negative regulator ERF13 (Trinh et al., 2019). VLCFAs play important roles in LR development and the production of VLCFAs is precisely regulated both in time and space during LR development. Whether PUCHI is regulated by phosphorylation like ERF13 is also worth studying in the future.

## Methods

#### Plant materials and growth conditions.

All of the Arabidopsis thaliana mutants and transgenic lines utilized are in the ecotype Columbia (Col-0) background. The following have been described elsewhere: arf7/19 (Okushima et al., 2005), lbd29 (Liu et al., 2010), lbd18-1, lbd16/18/33 (Goh et al., 2012a), DR5rev:GFP (Friml et al., 2003), and the T-DNA mutants including, erf13-1 (GK\_724B09), erf13-2 (GK\_121A12), kcs16-1 (SALK\_110690), kcs16-2 (SALK\_035139), mpk1 (SALK\_063847), mpk2 (SALK\_047422), mpk14 (SALK\_022928) were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC, Columbus, OH, USA). The mpk1/2/14 triple mutant was obtained by crossing of *mpk1*, *mpk2* and *mpk14*. The 3170-bp, 2105-bp and 1353-bp upstream regions from the start codon of ERF13, KCS16 and MPK14 were amplified using I-5TM 2× High-Fidelity Master Mix (Beijing TsingKe Biotech Co., Ltd, catalog number TP001) and linked to the GUS reporter in a gateway vector *pKGWFS7.1* to obtain ERF13pro::GUS, KCS16pro::GUS and MPK14pro::GUS reporter constructs, respectively. The 35S:ERF13:MYC, 35S:KCS16:MYC and 35S:MPK14:MYC constructs were constructed by introducing the ERF13, KCS16 and MPK14 coding region (CDS) into the binary vector *pGWB17* and subjected to plant transformations, respectively. The 35S:GFP:ERF13 and 35S:GFP:KCS16 constructs were constructed by introducing the ERF13 and KCS16 coding region (CDS) into the binary vector pB7WGF2.0 and subjected to plant transformations, respectively. The 35S: ERF13:SRDX construct was constructed by introducing the CDS of ERF13 following with an EAR domain into the binary vector pGWB2 and subjected to the plant transformations. Prior to the germination, seeds were surface sterilized by fumigation in chlorine gas, held for 2 days at 4°C on solidified half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium, then transferred to the growth room providing a 16 h photoperiod and a constant temperature of 20°C. To examine the effect of exogenous VLCFAs on LR development, seedlings were treated with or without exogenous 200 µM VLCFAs (a mixture of C20:0, C22:0 and C24:0) (Sigma-Aldrich) for 8 days and

checked the LR development. All primers used for the generation of the constructs are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

#### **RNA-Seq.**

Roots of nine-day-old WT, 35S:ERF13:MYC and 35S:ERF13:SRDX plants with corresponding treatment were collected for RNA isolation. RNA was isolated using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and treated with DNase I to remove contaminating genomic DNA. The extracted RNA was sent to BGI (Shenzhen, China) for RNA-seq library preparation and whole transcriptome sequencing. RNA-seq methods were reported in our earlier study (Lv et al., 2018). The differentially transcribed genes were identified using the thresholds FDR $\leq$ 0.001 and  $|\log_2|\geq 1$  (Audic and Claverie, 1997).

#### qRT-PCR.

For qRT-PCR, RNA was isolated from the roots using an RNeasy Plant Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's protocol. DNase I was used to remove contaminating genomic DNA, and a 2  $\mu$ g aliquot was reverse-transcribed using a Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), following the manufacturer's protocol. The subsequent qRT-PCRs were run on a MyiQTM Real-time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master mix (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Each sample represented three biological replicates, and each biological replicate includs three technical replicates. The *AtACTIN2* (*At3g18780*) was used as the reference control. Primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

#### Microscopy, SEM and histochemical GUS staining.

For LR observation, seedlings were treated with a series of solutions as described in our early study (Zhang et al., 2020). The LRP and LR emergence were observed using an Olympus BX53 microscope. The stages of LR development have been described (Malamy and Benfey, 1997). For GFP scanning, roots were imaged by an LSM-880 laser-scanning confocal microscopy (Zeiss, German). The process was performed according to Ding et al. (Ding and Friml, 2010). Scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis was performed according to the method described by Zhu et al. (Zhu et al., 2019). For histochemical GUS staining, seedlings were infiltrated with 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.2), 0.1% Triton X-100, 2 mM potassium ferricyanide and potassium ferrocyanide, 10 mM EDTA and 2 mM 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl- $\beta$ -d-glucuronide (X-gluc), and incubated at 37°C for 3 hours. Samples were cleared in chloral hydrate and visualized with Olympus BX53 microscopy.

#### Analysis of very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs).

For the analysis of VLCFAs in roots, freshly collected roots were immersed in 4 mL of hot isopropanol for 30 min at 85°C. The isopropanol phase was collected and roots were further extracted with 4 mL of chloroform:methanol (2:1, v/v). Add 100 mg glass beads, put them into a high-throughput tissue grinder, vibrate at 60 Hz for 1 min, repeat twice, ultrasonic for 30min at room temperature, centrifuge at 13000 rpm for 5 min, take all the supernatant into a 15 mL centrifuge tube, add 2 mL 1% sulfuric acid methanol solution, fully mix and shake for 1 min and then for water bath at 80°C for 30 min. Extract with 1 mL of n-hexane, and wash with 5 mL distilled water along with 100 mg anhydrous sodium sulfate to remove excess water in the supernatant. Take 500 µL supernatant with 25 µL 37 fatty acid methyl esters mixed standard solution as internal standard for test. Samples were injected into the gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) chain composed of an analytic gaseous phase chromatograph (7890A; Agilent) and a mass detector (MSD 5975; Agilent). The Agilent MassHunter Workstation software was used for the data analyzing. Spectra were analyzed manually and confronted to the NIST mass spectral library to identify fatty acid compounds. Areas of peaks were normalized against corresponding standards to determine the quantity of each fatty acid. The experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates.

#### Co-immunoprecipitation and western blotting.

Co-IP was performed according to our previous study (Lv et al., 2020). Mesophyll cells were harvested and lysed in cell lysis buffer (0.5 mM EDTA,10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM PMSF and 150 mM NaCl) on ice for 30 minutes with pipetting every 10 minutes. Cell lysates were centrifuged and the supernatant was

incubated with MYC-Trap magnetic agarose beads (Chromotek, catalog number ytma-20) at 4°C for 2h. The beads were washed three times with dilution buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA) and we resuspended the beads in SDS loading buffer. The resuspended beads were boiled for 10 minutes at 99°C and western blotting was followed using anti-MYC (Abclonal, catalog number AE010) or anti-GFP (TransGen Biotech, catalog number HT801-02) antibody. For western blotting, proteins were denatured and subjected to SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Bio-Rad) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, catalog number IPVH00010). The membranes were blocked in 1× TBST with 5% milk for 2 hours, immunoblotted with indicated antibodies at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation for 2 hours with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature. Blots were visualized by SuperSignal West Pico Luminol Enhancer Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Yeast two-hybrid assays.

Yeast two-hybrid assay was performed according to the manufacturer's manual of Matchmaker GAL4 Two-Hybrid System 3 (www.clontech.com). The coding sequence of *MPK14* was inserted into the *Eco*RI-*Xho*I cloning site of *pGADT7* (Takara, USA), and the coding sequence of *ERF13* was inserted into the cloning site of the bait plasmid *pGBKT7*. Each of the constructs (including an empty vector for control) was transferred separately into yeast Y2HGold using the PEG/LiAc method. After culturing on synthetic medium plates (SD medium) lacking Trp and Leu (SD-Trp-Leu) for 2 days, the transformants were transferred onto SD-Trp-Leu-His for analysis. Primers used for yeast two-hybrid are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

### Yeast one-hybrid assays.

Yeast one-hybrid assay was performed according to the manufacturer's manual of Matchmaker One-Hybrid Library Construction and Screening Kit (www.clontech.com). The coding sequence of *ERF13* was inserted into the *Eco*RI-*Xho*I cloning site of *pGADT7-Rec2* (Takara, USA), and the target DNA sequence of *KCS16* promoter was inserted into the cloning site of the bait plasmid *pHIS2.1*. Each of the constructs (including an empty vector for control) was

transferred separately into yeast Y187 using the PEG/LiAc method. After culturing on synthetic medium plates (SD medium) lacking Trp and Leu (SD-Trp-Leu) for 2 days, the transformants were transferred onto SD-Trp-Leu-His for analysis. Primers used for yeast one-hybrid are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

#### Suberin lamellae analysis.

Suberin lamella was observed in 5-day-old roots after fluorol yellow 088 staining. Seedlings were incubated in a freshly prepared solution of Fluorol Yellow 088 (0.01% w/v, in lactic acid) at 70°C for 30 min, rinsed in water (three baths of 5 min each), and counter-staining with aniline blue (0.5% w/v, in water) at room temperature for 30 min in darkness. Washed the samples in water for, at least, 30 min (change the bath to fresh water every 10 minutes). Mount on slides using glycerol 50% prior to microscope examination.

#### **BiFC** assays.

To generate the constructs for the BiFC assays, full-length cDNA fragments of *ERF13* and *MPK14* were amplified and cloned into the *pDONRZeo* (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) vector for fusion with the C-terminus of YFP and the N-terminus of YFP by LR reaction, respectively. The recombinant constructs were co-transferred into the mesophyll protoplasts of *Arabidopsis thaliana* and incubated in dark for 12 hours at 25°C before observation. The mesophyll cells were then imaged under Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope. About forty cells of three biological repetitions were analyzed in each combination. The combination with the empty vector was the negative control.

#### **Pull-down** assays.

For pull-down assays, recombinant GST-tagged ERF13 protein was purified using glutathione beads (GE Healthcare) and MPK14-MBP-His recombinant protein was purified using Ni-NTA agarose (Qiagen). Glutathione beads containing 5  $\mu$ g ERF13-GST was incubated with 5 $\mu$ g MPK14-MBP-His or MBP-His protein at 4 °C for 2 hours. The proteins were detected by immunoblotting with anti-GST or anti-His antibody after the proteins eluted from beads.

## Phosphorylation assays.

To examine how auxin treatment affects ERF13, recombinant GST-tagged ERF13 was incubated with total proteins extracted in degradation buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 4 mM PMSF, 5 mM DTT, and 10 mM ATP) from 8-day-old seedlings that had been treated with or without auxin. For the *in vitro* phosphorylation assay, recombinant activated MPK14<sup>DD</sup>-MBP-His was used to phosphorylate recombinant GST-tagged ERF13 protein (1:10 enzyme substrate ratio) in the reaction buffer (20mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 25 mM ATP and 1 mM DTT) at 37 °C for 30 min. The reactions were stopped by the addition of  $2\times$  SDS loading buffer. The phosphorylated ERF13 was separated and detected using phos-tag (Kinoshita et al., 2009).

#### Statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis was performed by two-sided Student's *t*-test and Duncan's test. All data were represented by means and standard error.

(H) A proposed model of MPK14-mediated auxin signaling to control LR development. In the canonical pathway, auxin induces the degradation of IAA14 and eventually the derepression the ARF7/19, which activates the expression of downstream LBDs to regulate LR initiation. Auxin can also regulate cell division and cell wall remodeling (CWR) during LR development through the TMK1/4-MKK4/5-MPK3/6 module.
