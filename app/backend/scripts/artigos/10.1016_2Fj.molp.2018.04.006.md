## A Molecular Framework for Auxin-Controlled Homeostasis of Shoot Stem Cells in Arabidopsis

Linjie Luo<sup>1</sup>, Jian Zeng<sup>1</sup>, Haijun Wu<sup>1</sup>, Zhaoxia Tian<sup>1,\*</sup> and Zhong Zhao<sup>1,\*</sup>

<sup>1</sup> CAS Center for Excellence in Molecular Plant Sciences, School of Life Sciences, University of Science and Technology of China, Huangshan Road 443, Hefei 230027, China.

\*Corresponding authors (email: zxtian@ustc.edu.cn or zhzhao@ustc.edu.cn)

Keywords: stem cell homeostasis; auxin; MONOPTEROS; DORNROSCHEN.

Running title: Auxin regulates plant stem cell pool

**Short Summary:** In the shoot apical meristem, auxin accumulates in both stem cells in the central zone and differentiated cells in the periphery zone. Here we show a direct role of *MP-DRN*-mediated auxin signaling in regulation of stem-cell pool by interfering with the *WUS-CLV3* feedback loop, and provide a mechanistic framework for auxin control of stem-cell homeostasis by balancing maintenance and differentiation.

#### Abstract

The classic phytohormone auxin plays essential roles in priming meristematic cell differentiation in the shoot apical meristem to promote lateral organ initiation. Recently, several lines of evidence have suggested that auxin not only is transported to new primordia but also descends in stem cells in the central zone. However, the function of auxin in stem cell regulation has remained elusive. Here, we show that auxin signaling in stem cells is mediated, at least in part, by AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR5 / MONOPTEROS (MP), which directly represses DORNROSCHEN/ENHANCER the transcription of **OF** SHOOT REGENERATION 1 (DRN/ESR1). DRN expressed in stem cells positively regulates CLV3 expression and has important meristematic functions. Our results provide a mechanistic framework for auxin control of shoot stem cell homeostasis and demonstrate how auxin differs in the control of plant stem cell maintenance and differentiation.

## Introduction

The postembryonic development of aboveground organs in *Arabidopsis thaliana* relies on the continuous differentiation of shoot stem cells throughout their lifespan. Maintaining a relatively stable stem cell pool in the shoot apical meristem (SAM) is crucial for plants to fulfill this developmental program. Stem cell fate in the SAM of *A. thaliana* is controlled by a complex regulatory network including transcription

factors and phytohormones (Brand et al., 2000; Fletcher et al., 1999; Gordon et al., 2009; Leibfried et al., 2005; Mayer et al., 1998; Schoof et al., 2000). The negative feedback loop between the peptide ligand CLAVATA3 (CLV3) and the homeodomain transcription factor WUSCHEL (WUS) is essential to maintain stem cell fate where embedded in the central zone (CZ) of the SAM (Brand et al., 2000; Fletcher et al., 1999; Mayer et al., 1998; Schoof et al., 2000). WUS proteins are expressed in the organizing center (OC) just beneath the CZ and migrate to stem cells to maintain stemness and activate the transcription of *CLV3* (Daum et al., 2014; Yadav et al., 2011). However, the peptide ligand CLV3 secreted from stem cells represses *WUS* transcription via the *CLAVATA1/2(CLV1/2)*- and *CORYNE* (*CRN*)-mediated signal transduction cascade to define the limits of stem cell number (Clark et al., 1997; Muller et al., 2008; Ogawa et al., 2008).

The classic phytohormones cytokinin and auxin play crucial roles in determining plant stem cell fate in the SAM via functional interactions with multiple regulators (Cheng et al., 2006; de Reuille et al., 2006; Heisler et al., 2005; Reinhardt et al., 2003; Vernoux et al., 2011; Yamaguchi et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2010). Cytokinin signaling has been shown to be required for maintaining stem cell fate by activating *WUS* expression (Gordon et al., 2009), and four closely related A-type *ARABIDOPSIS RESPONSE REGULATOR* (*ARR*) genes, which are negative regulators of cytokinin signaling (To et al., 2004), are negatively controlled by WUS (Leibfried et al., 2005). The other classic phytohormone auxin, however, has opposite roles in priming meristematic cell differentiation and organogenesis (Cheng et al., 2006; Heisler et al.,

2005; Leibfried et al., 2005; Yamaguchi et al., 2013). The local accumulation of auxin in the periphery zone (PZ) is required for new organ initiation, which is facilitated by auxin polar transport of PIN1 protein (Reinhardt et al., 2003). Auxin-dependent organ initiation is mediated by the *AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR5/MONOPTEROS (MP)* transcription factor (Berleth and Jurgens, 1993; Hardtke and Berleth, 1998), which recruits SWI/SNF chromatin-remodeling ATPases and activates *LEAFY (LFY)*, *AINTEGUMENTA (ANT), AINTEGUMENTA-LIKE 6 (AIL6), FILAMENTOUS FLOWERS (FIL)* and *TARGET OF MONOPTEROS 3 (TMO3)* at the transcriptional level (Wu et al., 2015; Yamaguchi et al., 2013).

In contrast to the current notion of cell differentiation, free active IAA has also been reported to accumulate in stem cells in the central zone using GCMS detection and immunochemical staining with mono-antibodies directly against auxin (de Reuille et al., 2006). The distribution patterns of the novel auxin signaling sensor DII-VENUS, which monitors the local degradation of Aux/IAA, also demonstrate high auxin signaling input in stem cells (Vernoux et al., 2011). We have previously reported that MP protein is not only located in differentiated cells but also forms a gradient from PZ to CZ, suggesting that MP may also be involved in stem cell regulation (Zhao et al., 2010). However, the function of MP-mediated auxin signaling in stem cell regulation.

DORNROSCHEN (DRN) was previously identified as a direct target of MP during embryonic development when expressed in the tip of the cotyledon (Chandler et al.,

2007; Cole et al., 2009). In contrast, its homologous gene *DORNROSCHEN-LIKE* (*DRNL*) is also expressed in founder cells of lateral organs and modified by auxin (Chandler et al., 2011; Chandler and Werr, 2014; Chandler and Werr, 2017; Comelli et al., 2016). Therefore, knocking out *DRNs* or their homolog *LFS* in tomato causes severe defects in cotyledon initiation (Capua and Eshed, 2017; Chandler et al., 2007). Moreover overexpression of *DRN* or *DRNL* in Arabidopsis and tobacco affects leaf and flower development (Kirch et al., 2003; Marsch-Martinez et al., 2006).

Although DRN is expressed in the CZ of SAM, the single mutant of drn shows no visible defects in meristem development (Kirch et al., 2003). Interestingly, overexpression of DRN shows meristem arrested phenotypes in vegetative growth and enhances shoot generation efficiency in tissue culture (Banno et al., 2001; Kirch et al., 2003). Although DRN/DRNL functions during embryonic development have been reported to be linked to auxin signaling (Chandler et al., 2007; Chandler et al., 2009; Cole et al., 2009), whether DRN mediates auxin signaling in the regulation of plant stem cells remains unclear. In this study, we showed that MP repressed the transcription of DRN in plant shoot stem cells by directly binding to the conserved auxin response element in the DRN promoter. The knockout of DRN and its homologous gene DRNL caused severe meristem defects and down-regulation of CLV3 expression. Our results provide evidence to support a new mechanistic framework of auxin that acts to maintain stem cell proliferation in the central zone of the meristem while promoting cell differentiation in organ primordia.

#### Results

#### MP directly represses DRN transcription in stem cells

MP protein was previously reported to be located in both stem cells and differentiated cells (Zhao et al., 2010). However, the presence of very few stem cells in the SAM causes a major problem for profiling MP-specific targets in stem cells. To investigate with increased resolution the functions of MP in stem cells, we crossed the mp mutant with the stem cell overproliferating mutant *clv3* and performed RNA-Seq on *clv3* and mp clv3 shoot tips. A total of 1497 genes were up-regulated in mp clv3 (>2-fold), while 1679 genes were down-regulated. Among the genes with altered expression, eleven previously identified stem cell-specific genes were up-regulated (Anderson et al., 2004; Kirch et al., 2003; Mayer et al., 1998; Nole-Wilson et al., 2005; Yadav et al., 2009) (Supplemental Figure 1). To test if the genes were potential MP targets in stem cells, we performed dexamethasone (DEX) induction on RPS5A::GR-bdl transgenic plants (Weijers et al., 2006), which drove strong expression of mutated GR-bdl protein in the SAM and blocked floral primordia initiation 7 days after DEX treatment (Supplemental Figure 2). After DEX induction, accompanying the IAA treatment to eliminate endogenous BDL and other potential Aux/IAA inhibitors (Schlereth et al., 2010), the nuclear translocated GR-bdl bound to and inactivated MP, which cannot be degraded by intracellular auxin. Among the 11 aforementioned genes, only DRN expression was significantly up-regulated (Figure 1A), which was further confirmed by in situ hybridization (Figure 1B and 1C). Moreover, we observed a gradient of

DRN expression from the CZ to the PZ (Figure 1D). To further confirm this observation, we generated the DRN::mCherry-N7 transgenic plant with the same upstream and downstream sequences of DRN that were used in the previous study (Seeliger et al., 2016). We crossed the DRN::mCherry-N7 transgenic plant with the CLV3::GFP plant to compare the expression domain of DRN and CLV3. Consistent with the *in situ* hybridization (Figure 1D), we observed not only strong signals of DRN in the CZ colocalized with CLV3 but also weak signals that spread to the PZ and formed a gradient in the SAM (Figure 1E-1G, Supplemental Figure 3). Consistently, which were the DRN protein localization patterns, detected in the DRN::DRN-GFP/drn rescue lines, also formed a gradient from the CZ to the PZ (Supplemental Figure 4). The expression pattern of DRN was complementary to the MP protein distribution in the SAM, which was highly accumulated in the PZ but weakly in stem cells (Supplemental Figure 5) (Zhao et al., 2010), suggesting that DRN was under the negative control of MP. Consistent with this idea, we observed a dramatic increase in *DRN* expression in the *mp* mutant by comparing DRN::mCherry-N7 signals with the wild-type background (Figure 1E and 1H) and by performing qRT-PCR on wild-type and mp mutant plants (Supplemental Figure 6), indicating that MP repressed DRN in the meristem at the transcriptional level. To precisely examine whether MP repressed DRN expressions in the central zone and rule out morphological influences between mp and wild-type plants, we generated CLV3::MP-GR transgenic plants that increased MP only in stem cells and observed a significant decrease in DRN expression after DEX induction in the presence of cycloheximide (Figure 1I).

Interestingly, in the tips of embryonic cotyledons, *DRN* expression was activated by MP through auxin response elements (AuxREs) (Cole et al., 2009). Thus, we examined whether MP could associate with the *DRN* promoter in stem cells using chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) in inflorescence apexes to exclude the binding signals of the cotyledons, and we observed an interaction with a fragment containing an AuxRE element (TGTCTC) (Figure 1J). Using electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs), we found that MP bound to the ChIP-positive fragment specifically *in vitro*, but not if this AuxRE was mutated to TGGCTC (Figure 1K). Furthermore, we performed a reporter gene analysis to check the transcriptional activities of three putative AuxREs in the *DRN* promoter *in vivo* by mutating individual motifs (Cole et al., 2009). We demonstrated that only EMSA- and ChIP-positive AuxRE motifs were required for the repression levels of *GUS* in three transgenic plants with single AuxRE mutations (Supplemental Figure 7).

# DRN regulates the shoot apical meristem by interfering with the WUS-CLV3 feedback loop

Because the *drn* null mutant showed no visible defects (Kirch et al., 2003), we generated a double mutant with *drnl* containing a point mutation in its closest homologous gene, *DORNROSCHEN-LIKE (DRNL)* (Supplemental Figure 8A-8D). Although *DRNL* expressions were too weak to be detected in the CZ (Supplemental

Figure 8E and 8F), genetically, we observed a significantly enlarged SAM in the drn drnl double mutant in both reproductive (Figure 2A and 2B) and vegetative stages (Supplemental Figure 8G-8J), which was further confirmed by sectioning with DAPI staining (Figure 2C and 2D). Because of the different mutant backgrounds. *drn* in Col-0 and *drnl* in Ler, we further quantified the SAM size in single and double mutants. We observed no significant size differences in single mutants compared with wild type (Supplemental Figure 9A-9D, 9F). However, the drn drnl double mutant showed a significantly enlarged meristem (Figure 2E; Supplemental Figure 9E, 9F and 8J). Moreover we also observed a significantly enlarged floral meristem in the drn drnl double mutant (Supplemental Figure 9G-9J), indicating that DRN and DRNL were functionally redundant for regulating meristem size. At the molecular level, we observed a remarkable reduction of CLV3 mRNA in the drn mutant in both reproductive (Figure 2F and 2G) and vegetative stages (Supplemental Figure 10A and 10B), while WUS expression moderately increased (Figure 2K, 2L and Supplemental Figure 10C, 10D). Consistently, quantitative measurements by qRT-PCR also confirmed that CLV3 expression was significantly down-regulated in the drn mutant, while WUS was up-regulated (Supplemental Figure 10E and 11A). Likewise, similar trends in CLV3 and WUS expression were also observed in the drnl mutant (Figure 2H, 2I, 2M, 2N; Supplemental Figure 10F-10J and Supplemental Figure 11B). Given the functional redundancy of DRN and DRNL in meristem regulation, we observed a drastic reduction of CLV3 (Figure 2J; Supplemental Figure 8H, 8I, 8K) and apparently ectopic expression of WUS (Figure 2O) in the drn drnl double mutant, which was

consistent with the observed enlarged meristem. These findings suggested that *DRN* and *DRNL* were both required for *CLV3* expression. The enlarged shoot apical meristems observed in the *drn drnl* double mutant were caused by reduced CLV3 signaling and ectopically expressed *WUS* activity.

#### CLV3 expression is under direct positive control of DRN

To determine if DRN was sufficient to activate *CLV3* expression, we transiently transformed *CLV3::3xGFP-N7(NLS)* into tobacco leaves. We found that GFP signals highly accumulated in leaves when *DRN* was overexpressed (Figure 3A and 3B). We also determined GFP expression levels under the same circumstances and noticed a substantial increase in GFP mRNA by overexpression of *DRN* (Supplemental Figure 12), suggesting that the *CLV3* promoter could be activated by DRN. Using a *CLV3*-specific ethanol-inducible promoter, we generated a stem cell-inducible transgenic line in *Arabidopsis thaliana* to increase *DRN* expression in stem cells (*CLV3::AlcR, AlcA::DRN*). After 1% ethanol induction, we observed a dramatic increase in not only *DRN* expression (Figure 3C) but also *CLV3* expression (Figure 3D) by qRT-PCR, suggesting that *DRN* activates *CLV3* expression in stem cells.

To test if DRN could directly regulate *CLV3* expression, we generated *CLV3::DRN-GR* transgenic plants. After DEX and cycloheximide treatment, nuclear entry of DRN-GR resulted in a significant increase in *CLV3* (Figure 3E and 3F). To further confirm this finding, we performed a quantitative analysis of *CLV3::DRN-GR* treated with the transcriptional inhibitor cordycepin. Consistent with the above

observation, *CLV3* transcription was highly activated by DEX induction in *CLV3::DRN-GR* transgenic plants, while cordycepin was sufficient to block the increase in *CLV3* expression in *CLV3::DRN-GR* plants after DEX induction, indicating a direct role of DRN in the positive regulation of *CLV3* transcription (Figure 3G). To genetically examine the interaction between *DRNs* and *CLV3*, we generated a *drn drnl clv3* triple mutant. The meristem size of the *drn drnl* double mutant was significantly enlarged compared with the wild-type plant (Figure 2E, 3J), while the *drn drnl clv3* triple mutant showed no difference from *clv3* single mutants (Figure 3H-3J), suggesting that *DRN*, *DRNL* and *CLV3* act in the same genetic pathway to control meristem size.

To test whether DRN could directly bind to *CLV3*, we performed ChIP assays to screen its 1.5-kb promoter, as shown in Figure 3, which responded to DRN activation. However, we failed to detect direct binding of DRN to the *CLV3* promoter *in vivo* across multiple attempts and two different sets of primers (Figure 4A-4C). Because the *CLV3* promoter contains a putative binding site (GCCGGCA) for DRN (Banno, 2006), we further tested this fragment by EMSA. We found that shift binding caused by the addition of bacterially expressed DRN protein was not affected by cold probes, suggesting that DRN could not specifically bind the *CLV3* promoter *in vitro* (Figure 4D). As we showed, DRN was sufficient to activate *CLV3::3xGFP-N7(NLS)* expression in tobacco leaves (Figure 3A and 3B; Supplemental Figure 12). However, it could also activate *CLV3m::3xGFP-N7 (NLS)* expression in tobacco leaves containing a mutation in the putative DRN-binding site (Figure 4E). In addition, we

mutated the 37th Ala to Val in the DRN protein, which is the key amino acid for DNA binding or stability of the ERF/AP2 domain (Liu et al., 2006; Seeliger et al., 2016). We found that this mutated DRN protein could also activate the *CLV3* promoter or even the *CLV3m* promoter (Figure 4E), suggesting that the direct regulation of DRN for *CLV3* expression is not dependent on its DNA-binding activity.

## Genetic interaction between MP-mediated auxin signaling and *WUS-CLV* feedback loop in stem cells

To test the genetic interaction between MP-DRN-mediated auxin signaling and the key stem-cell regulatory circuitry, we ectopically expressed the mutated *bdl* protein under the control of the *CLV3* promoter to specifically block MP function in stem cells. In the resulting transgenic plants, we observed highly increased *DRN* expression in the central zone of the meristem at early stages of flowering (7 days after bolting) (Figure 5U and 5V). Along with the increase in *DRN*, *CLV3* expression levels increased dramatically in stem cells (Figure 5F and 5G), while *WUS* proteins were significantly inhibited (Figure 5K, 5L, 5P and 5Q). As a biological consequence of *WUS* repression, SAM sizes of *CLV3::bdl* transgenic plants were significantly reduced at the early stage of flowering (7 days after bolting) (Figure 5A, 5B, 5W) and eventually terminated 14 days after bolting (Figure 5C). Consistently, we could not detect *CLV3/WUS* expression or a normal SAM structure in these terminated plants (Figure 5H, 5M and 5R).

As we have shown, in the drn drnl double mutant, CLV3 expression was dramatically

reduced (Figure 2J, 5I). Accordingly, expression of both *WUS* mRNA (Figure 2O) and protein (Figure 5N and 5S) were increased, resulting in enlarged SAMs (Figure 2E, 5D and 5W). To genetically test the interaction between MP and *DRNs*, we specifically inhibited MP functions in the stem cells of *drn drnl* double mutants via transformation with *CLV3::bdl*. In contrast to the wild-type background, SAMs of *CLV3::bdl/drn drnl* transgenic plants were not terminated (Figure 5E). At the molecular level, we observed the same trend in expression of *CLV3* (Figure 5I, 5J) and WUS proteins (Figure 5N, 5O, 5S and 5T) in *drn drnl* mutants with or without *CLV3::bdl*. Consistent with this observation, there was no difference in the size of SAMs between *drn drnl* mutants and *CLV3::bdl/drn drnl* transgenic plants (Figure 5W). Thus, we conclude that MP represses *DRN* in the central zone, and they act in a same genetic pathway to control the homeostasis of stem cells in the shoot apical meristem.

#### DRN regulates stem cell homeostasis in a manner dependent on CLV signaling

To further explore whether DRN could regulate stem cells in a CLV3 signaling-dependent manner, we expressed *DRN* from the *CLV3* promoter, which increased its specific expression in stem cells. Surprisingly, we observed dramatic effects in transgenic plants when *DRN* was driven by the *CLV3* promoter; 16.7% of T1 plants of *CLV3::DRN* showed a terminated meristem and stopped growing after producing several flowers (Figure 6A, 6B and 6D). We performed *in situ* hybridization, but no SAM or *CLV3* expression was observed (Figure 6E and 6F),

indicating a complete loss of stem cells. However, the other 12.5% of T1 plants showed a pin-like meristem at the reproductive stage and often displayed trichome initiation near the tip, demonstrating a clear difference from the pin-formed meristems of *mp*, *pin1* and *pid* mutants (Figure 6A, 6C and 6D). Based on *in situ* hybridization, we observed enlarged cells in the tips of transgenic plants, but no *CLV3* expression (Figure 6E and 6G), indicating that stem cells might have differentiated or been terminated.

A possible mechanism underlying the stem cell termination in *CLV3::DRN* transgenic plants could be an interference with the *WUS-CLV3* negative feedback loop by elevated *DRN* levels. Support for this idea came from the observation that *CLV3* expression dramatically increased during the early blooming stages in *CLV3::DRN* transgenic plants (Figure 6E and 6H), whereas *WUS* expression was significantly reduced (Figure 6I and 6J). Consistent with this observation, we also observed progressively higher levels of transcription of *CLV3* in *CLV3::DRN* transgenic plants during the vegetative stage (Supplemental Figure 13).

As *DRN* was also expressed in stem cells, we questioned how could *CLV3::DRN* cause such severe defects in stem cell maintenance. Given that DRN directly activated the *CLV3* promoter (Figure 3), we hypothesized that *CLV3::DRN* formed a positive feedback loop to produce high levels of DRN proteins in the stem cells of transgenic plants. Simultaneously, the high levels of DRN also activated endogenous *CLV3* to markedly repress *WUS* activity and terminate stem cell fate (Figure 6K). To

functionally test this hypothesis, we transformed CLV3::DRN into the clv3-7 mutant background (Supplemental Figure 14A). First, we assessed the activity of the exogenous *CLV3* promoter in the *clv3-7* mutant. Due to the release of inhibition from the CLV3 peptide, WUS was ectopically expressed in the entire meristem of clv3 mutants (Supplemental Figure 14B). We found that CLV3::GUS was highly activated in the entire meristem and in the floral primordia (Supplemental Figure 14C). Furthermore, we also detected those partial transcripts of CLV3 in the enlarged stem cell region of the clv3-7 mutant (Supplemental Figure 14D), suggesting that CLV3 promoter activity was functional in *clv3*. We obtained a total of 108 independent T1 transgenic plants of CLV3::DRN in the clv3 mutant background, and no terminated SAM was ever observed. Among them, 66% transgenes showed *clv3-like* phenotypes, while unexpectedly, the other 34% T1 plants showed even enlarged meristems with no flowers (Figure 6L-6N) and dramatically increased DRN mRNA (Figure 6O) and protein (Figure 6P), suggesting that DRN activated the CLV3 promoter in the clv3 mutant background and that a large number of transcripts and proteins accumulated by self-activation of CLV3::DRN. We could detect STM expression in these meristem-enlarged transgenes (Figure 6Q), implying that the meristem activity was functional in these plants. More importantly, WUS (Figure 6R) and partial transcripts of CLV3 (Figure 6S) were also detected in the meristems of these transgenic plants, suggesting that DRN could not repress WUS expression or meristem activity in the absence of CLV3 functions.

#### MP-mediated auxin signaling controls plant shoot stem cell homeostasis

Because DRN is the direct target of MP and an important regulator in stem cells (Figures 1-3), we were interested in whether modulation of auxin accumulation also has an effect on stem cell maintenance. We first analyzed DRN expression patterns in the *yucca1,2,4,6* quadruple mutant (Cheng et al., 2006), which lacks essential auxin biosynthesis in the SAM, and observed significantly elevated levels of DRN expression compared with wild type (Figure 7A-7C). In contrast, when endogenous auxin levels were specifically increased in stem cells using CLV3::iaaM or CLV3::iaaH (Supplemental Figure 15A-15C), we observed a significant reduction of DRN expression in the resulting transgenic plants (Figure 7D-7F and 7H). To further support this observation, we also generated stem cell-inducible *iaaM* or *iaaH* transgenic plants using the *CLV3*-specific ethanol-inducible promoter. After 36 hours of induction of 1% ethanol, we observed significant decreases in the expression of in DRN and CLV3 but increased expression of WUS (Supplemental Figure 15D-15F). These results demonstrate that DRN is under the negative control of endogenous auxin in stem cells.

In the shoot apical meristem, MP-mediated auxin regulation in stem cells was more complicated than we expected. In *mp* mutants, *CLV3* expression was significantly increased (Supplemental Figure 16A and 16D). However, WUS was not repressed, but its expression domain was increased (Supplemental Figure 16B, 16E, 16C and 16F), leading to enlarge meristems (Supplemental Figure 16G). Because MP was also strongly expressed in the PZ (Supplemental Figure 5), we speculated that the function of MP in the PZ might be involved in the negative control of *WUS*. Support for this

idea was derived from the observation that auxin levels were low in the OC and auxin flow from lateral organs negatively regulated SAM size, which is independent of CLV signaling (Shi et al., 2018). Likewise, in auxin-deficient *yuc1 yuc2 yuc4 yuc6* mutants, although *DRN* (Figure 7B) and *CLV3* (Zhao et al., 2010) expression levels were highly increased, the SAM size in this quadruple mutant remained enlarged (Supplemental Figure 16G) (Shi et al., 2018).

To further explore the functions of MP in stem cells and exclude the influence from the PZ, we specifically modulated MP functions in stem cells by introducing CLV3::MP. DRN transcripts were reduced in CLV3::MP transgenic plants due to increased MP expression in stem cells (Figure 7D, 7G and 7H). Furthermore, CLV3 expression was also reduced in *CLV3::MP* plants (Figure 7I, 7J and 7M), whereas WUS was up-regulated (Figure 7K-7M) and caused enlarged SAMs (Supplemental Figure 16H). Conversely, in *CLV3::bdl* transgenes that MP functions were specifically blocked in stem cells, we observed a dramatic increase in DRN (Figure 5V) and CLV3 (Figure 5G) expression, which abolished expression of WUS (Figure 5L and 5M) and terminated stem cell fate and meristem development (Figure 5C and 5H), demonstrating that MP-mediated auxin signaling modulated the *CLV3/WUS* feedback loop in stem cells by repressing DRN activity. This conclusion was further supported by the observed phyllotaxy defects in CLV3::MP plants concomitant with the repression of DRN (Figure 7N, 7O and 7R), similar to the findings for drn drnl and clv3 mutants (Figure 7P-7R).

## Discussion

In this study, we showed that MP-mediated auxin signaling negatively regulates CLV3 by repressing of *DRN* and plays a role in the core regulatory machinery of plant shoot stem cells (Figure 8). This study fits well with the previously described role of A-type ARRs in stem cells, which are also directly repressed by MP (Zhao et al., 2010). Although that study demonstrated the importance of crosstalk between cytokinin and auxin, herein we showed a direct role of MP-mediated auxin signaling in the regulation of the WUS/CLV network and stem cell homeostasis. Although overexpression of DRN has been shown to cause meristem-arrested phenotypes in seedlings (Kirch et al., 2003), whether DRN mediates auxin signaling in stem cells and the molecular mechanism by which DRN controls meristem function are still largely unknown. Our data demonstrate that DRN regulates plant shoot stem cell homeostasis by interfering with the WUS-CLV3 feedback loop, which is achieved by activating endogenous CLV3 expression. The highly accumulated DRN in CLV3::DRN transgenic plants compared with wild-type plants terminates stem cell fate by repressing WUS activity (Figure 6). However, in 108 independent T1 transgenic plants on the clv3 mutant background, no terminated SAM was ever observed by transformation with the same construct, which defines one of the major functions of DRN in stem cell regulation. Interestingly, more than one-third of CLV3::DRN/clv3 T1 plants showed enlarged meristems (Figure 6N), indicating that DRN might have additional roles in controlling stem cell homeostasis that might be independent of CLV3 signaling.

DRN/ESR1 has been previously classified as a putative AP2/ERF domain transcription factor (Banno et al., 2001). Our data also showed that DRN is involved in the direct control of CLV3 expression (Figure 3). However, we did not detect any specific binding of DRN in the CLV3 promoter using ChIP or EMSA. We showed that the expression of not only CLV3::3xGFP-N7(NLS) but also CLV3m::3xGFP-N7(NLS), which contains a mutated putative DRN-binding site in the CLV3 promoter, was sufficiently activated by DRN in tobacco leaves (Figure 3A and 3B; Supplemental Figure 12; Figure 4E). Although the DRN protein contains a conserved ERF/AP2 domain, mutation of the key amino acid Ala37, which is essential for binding the dehydration responsive element (DRE element) and GCC box (Liu et al., 2006), could still activate *CLV3::3xGFP-N7* expression in tobacco leaves and caused phenotypes similar to those of CLV3::DRN transgenic plants (Figure 4E-4H). These data indicate, at least regarding the regulation of CLV3 expression, that DRN does not directly bind to DNA as other transcription factors but rather acts as a cofactor to facilitate transcription.

MP protein localization in both differentiated and undifferentiated cells in the SAM (Schlereth et al., 2010; Zhao et al., 2010), suggests that auxin acts in a diametrically opposite way in determining cell fate. This effect can be achieved by targeting different sets of downstream genes, such as *LFY*, *ANT*, *AIL6*, *FIL* and *TMO3* (Wu et al., 2015; Yamaguchi et al., 2013) in organ initiation, while targeting *DRN* in stem cells. Alternatively, this behavior could also be mediated by manipulating the transcriptional activity of ARFs in a context-dependent manner. Support for this idea

has been derived from the observation that *DRN* activity can be either activated by MP in embryonic cotyledons (Cole et al., 2009) or repressed in stem cells, indicating that MP can act either as an activator or a repressor. Interestingly, MP was recently found to modulate chromatin structure by recruiting SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling ATPases and facilitates transcriptional activation (Wu et al., 2015). The mechanism by which MP represses downstream targets and responses to auxin-mediated degradation of Aux/IAA protein in stem cells remains unclear.

Auxin has long been believed to promote plant cell differentiation and elongation, as well as the maintenance of root stem cell niche. Given that auxin accumulates in both stem cells and differentiated cells in the shoot apical meristem, our results revealed a new aspect of auxin in the regulation of plant stem cell maintenance and differentiation. In stem cells, *MP-DRN*-mediated auxin signaling negatively regulated *CLV3* expression to maintain a stable stem cell pool, whereas in the differentiated PZ, auxin continuously promoted stem cell differentiation. Therefore, our results provide a mechanistic framework for auxin control of stem cells homeostasis in the shoot apical meristem by balancing the maintenance and differentiation of stem cells.

#### Methods

### Plant materials and treatments

All plants were generated in the Columbia-0 (Col-0) background except *drnl (drnl-2,* Ler background). The seeds of *drn (drn-1)* and *drnl* mutants were kindly provided by

Prof. Wolfgang Werr. The seeds of RPS5A::GR-bdl and MP::MP-GFP in mp rescued plants and mpS319 were kindly provided by Prof. Dolf Weijers (Schlereth et al., 2010). The seeds for CLV3::mCherry-N7/WUS::WUS-GFP were kindly provided by Prof. Jan Lohmann. We generated drn drnl (drn-1 drnl-2) double mutants and drn drn clv3 (drn-1 drnl-2 clv3-7) triple mutants by crossing. The transposon insertion allele clv3-7 and the weak mpS319 T-DNA insertion allele were used. All seeds were sterilized by applying 70% ethanol and 0.5% Tween 20 for 10 min except for the ethanol induction assay, followed by two washes with 95% ethanol and air drying. Seeds were then placed at 4°C for two days before sowing. For the ethanol induction assay, 0.16 M NaClO was used to sterilize seeds, followed by several washes with sterilized water. Plants were grown at  $21^{\circ}$ C under long-day conditions (16 hours of light and 8 hours of darkness). Seedlings for observing phenotypes in the vegetative stage were grown on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 0.8% agar. For ethanol induction, we cut the inflorescence apexes (CLV3::AlcR AlcA::GUS/DRN) and immersed them in 1/2 MS medium with 1% ethanol for 12 hours in the light. For CLV3::AlcR, AlcA::GUS/iaaM/iaaH plants, ethanol inductions were performed by watering with 1% ethanol for 18 or 36 hours under slightly dry soil conditions. For DEX inductions of RPS5A::GR-bdl (Schlereth et al., 2010), inflorescence apexes were pretreated with 15µM DEX for 2 hours, followed by an additional 2-hour treatment with 15µM DEX and 50µM IAA. For DEX inductions of CLV3::DRN-GR and CLV3::MP-GR transgenic plants, inflorescence apexes were treated with 15µM DEX and 50 $\mu$ M cycloheximide in 1/2 MS medium for 2 hours, and 600  $\mu$ M

cordycepin was used to block transcription. Fifty micromolar NPA in 0.1% DMSO and 0.01% Silwet L-77 were used to block auxin transport by spraying Col-0 inflorescence apexes for 5 days. For controls, 0.1% DMSO and 0.01% Silwet L-77 were used. For each induction, three biological replicates were performed, each with 15 apexes. At 7 days after bolting, the apexes were used for induction.

#### **RNA-sequencing**

Shoot apexes from 6-week-old inflorescences of *clv3* or *mp clv3* plants were collected. Total RNA was isolated from the apexes using the mirVana<sup>TM</sup> miRNA Isolation Kit (Life Technology). Total RNA (10 μg) was subjected to DNase I treatment, and mRNA was purified with the Dynabeads® mRNA DIRECT<sup>TM</sup> Purification Kit (Life Technology). Strand-specific RNA-Seq libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra<sup>TM</sup> RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina following the manufacturer's instructions and subjected to Illumina Hi-Seq<sup>TM</sup>2500 for 2 x 100-bp end sequencing. Reads were aligned to the *Arabidopsis* TAIR10 reference genome, and differentially expressed genes were identified using DEGseq (Wang et al., 2010). For RNA sequencing, each material was analyzed as two biological replicates.

#### **Plasmid construction**

For *DRN::DRN-GFP*, a 4.8-kb upstream sequence before the ATG was used as a promoter, and a 1.5-kb downstream sequence of TAG was used as a terminator (Cole et al., 2009; Seeliger et al., 2016). The *DRN::DRN-GFP* construct was then transformed into the *drn* mutant to rescue its single or fusion cotyledon defects. Using the same promoter and terminator, we generated *DRN::mCherry-N7* and used it to

transform Col-0. Overlap PCR was used to mutate putative AuxREs in the *DRN* promoter and *DRNA37V*. *DRN*, *DRNA37V*, *MP*, *bdl*, *iaaM* and *iaaH* coding sequences and  $3 \times GFP$ -*N7* (*NLS*) were subcloned downstream of the *CLV3* promoter and 35S promoter to construct *CLV3::DRN*, *35S::DRN*, *CLV3::MP*, *CLV3::iaaM*, *CLV3::iaaH* and *CLV3::3×GFP*. The 1.4-kb upstream and 1.3-kb downstream regions of the *CLV3* coding sequence were used as the promoter and terminator, respectively. The same promoter and terminator of *CLV3* were also used in the ethanol induction system (*CLV3::AlcR*; *AlcA::GUS/DRN*). To generate *DRNL::3xGFP*, a 4.3-kb upstream sequence before the ATG was used as a promoter. The primer sequences used in the plasmid construction are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

### Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed on inflorescences of the *mp* mutant rescued by *MP::MP-GFP* (Schlereth et al., 2010) and on *drn* mutants rescued by *DRN::DRN-GFP*. ChIP was conducted as previously described (Leibfried et al., 2005; Zhao et al., 2010) with minor modifications, and 500 mg apex was used for each independent experiment. A Diagenode Bioruptor UCD-200 was used for sonication (30 s on, 30 s off, medium, 15 min duration; sonication buffer: 10 mM Na<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>, 100 mM NaCl, 0.5% sarkosyl, 10 mM EDTA, 1 mM PMSF, 1 tablet per 10 ml, pH 7). GFP antibodies (ab290, Abcam) were used to precipitate chromatin, and no antibody and WT groups were used as negative controls. The primer sequences used in the ChIP assays are listed in Supplemental Table 1. Three independent ChIP assays were performed

#### for DRN::DRN-GFP.

#### Microscopy

To detect the fluorescence signals in the SAM, sectioning was performed using a vibratome. First, we removed the old flowers around the meristem and detached the shoot apexes from the plants. Next, we embedded the apexes in 6% LM agarose liquid gel. The block was placed on ice for 15 minutes to solidify the gel. The Leica VT1200S vibratome is used to generate 100-µm sections. Images were obtained using a Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscope. GFP was excited at 488 nm, and the emission spectrum was between 500 and 550 nm. The mCherry was excited at 594 nm, and detected at a wavelength between 590 and 632 nm.

## Total RNA isolation and quantitative RT-PCR

Tripure isolation reagent (Roche 94002420) was used for total RNA isolation, the PrimeScript<sup>TM</sup> RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa RR047A) was used for cDNA synthesis, and SYBR Green (Promega A6002) was used for quantitative PCR. qRT-PCR was performed using a Thermo PIKO REAL96 Real-Time PCR system with the following PCR conditions: Step  $1 - 95^{\circ}$ C for 5 min; Step 2 - 40 cycles of  $95^{\circ}$ C for 10 s, followed by  $57^{\circ}$ C for 30 s and  $72^{\circ}$ C for 30 s; Step  $3 - 72^{\circ}$ C for 10 min. *TUBULIN* was used to normalize mRNA levels. The primer sequences used in the qRT-PCR are listed in Supplemental Table 1. The apexes of plants at 7 days after bolting and 10-day-old seedlings were used to isolate total RNA. Because of the absence of cotyledons in *drn drnl* seedlings, we removed the cotyledons from Col-0 wild type to exclude morphological difference and then used the remainder for qRT-PCR.

#### **EMSA**

The DNA-binding domain of MP (residues120-274) and DRN (residues1-200) were cloned following a 6xHis-MBP tag. The protein was produced in *Escherichia coli* strain Rosetta and purified with nickel Sepharose medium (GE Healthcare). EMSA was performed according to the instructions supplied with the LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA kit (Thermo Scientific 20148), and probes were labeled with biotin. The  $10\times$ ,  $50\times$ ,  $100\times$ , and unlabeled (cold) probes were used as specific competitors. The primer sequences used in EMSA are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

#### in situ Hybridization

*in situ* hybridization was performed according to standard protocols (Andersen et al., 2008; Weigel, 2002). Templates of RNA probes were amplified from cDNAs with gene-specific primers containing T7 or T3 promoter sequences at the 5' end. A complete list of primer sequences is shown in Supplemental Table 1. RNA probes were synthesized using T7/T3 polymerase and labeled with digoxin-UTP.

#### **GUS** staining

Inflorescence apexes were collected in 90% acetone on ice and prefixed at room temperature for 20 min. The apexes were then washed with cold staining buffer (0.2% Triton X-100, 0.5 M NaPO4, 2 mM  $K_4$ Fe(CN)<sub>6</sub>, 2 mM  $K_3$ Fe(CN<sub>6</sub>) ) without X-Gluc, and changed into cold staining solution (staining buffer with 2 mM X-Gluc). The apexes were infiltrated on ice for 15 min and then vacuumed until all contents descended, after which they were incubated at 37°C overnight. The apexes of plants at 7 days after bolting were used for staining.

#### **DAPI** staining

Tissues were fixed and embedded according to standard protocols (Andersen et al., 2008; Weigel, 2002). DAPI at a concentration of 0.1  $\mu$ g/ml in PBS was applied for staining and detected with a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM710). Meristem size was measured according to the maximum width between primordia. ImageJ software was used for analysis.

#### Nicotiana benthamiana infiltration

Agrobacterium tumefaciens that harbored relevant reporters or overexpression constructs were cultured at 28°C for 2 days. The bacteria were then harvested by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 10 min and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 150  $\mu$ M acetosyringone, pH 5.8). The cells were incubated in the medium for 2 hours at room temperature and infiltrated into the abaxial leaf surface of 3-week-old *Nicotiana benthamiana* plants using an injector. Four days after infiltration, the leaves were processed for further analysis consisting of imaging with a Zeiss LSM710.

#### Statistical analysis

Differences between two groups were identified using the Student's t-test, and the p-value level was set at 5%.

Figure 8. Hypothetical regulatory mechanism of MP-mediated auxin signaling in the *WUS/CLV3* network.

In the shoot apical meristem, auxin is not only transported to the periphery zone (PZ) to promote new organ initiation but also the central zone (CZ) to maintain stem-cell homeostasis. The red arrows point out the orientation of auxin flow in the meristem. In stem cells, MP-mediated auxin signaling negatively regulates *CLV3* by repressing

of *DRN* and *ARR7/ARR15*, which maintains the *WUS* expression and the stability of stem cells pool. The output of cytokinin signaling is more abundant in the organizing center (OC), which is also essential for *WUS* expression, suggesting that both auxin and cytokinin are synergetic to regulate stem cell homeostasis in the shoot apical meristem.
