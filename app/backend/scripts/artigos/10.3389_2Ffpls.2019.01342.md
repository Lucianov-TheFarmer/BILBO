## 20 Abstract

21

Seed germination is a complex trait determined by the interaction of hormonal, metabolic, 22 genetic, and environmental components. Variability of this trait in crops has a big impact 23 on seedling establishment and yield in the field. Classical studies of this trait in crops 24 have focused mainly on the analyses of one level of regulation in the cascade of events 25 leading to seed germination. We have carried out an integrative and extensive approach 26 27 to deepen our understanding of seed germination in Brassica napus by generating transcriptomic, metabolic and hormonal data at different stages upon seed imbibition. 28 29 Deep phenotyping of different seed germination associated traits in six winter-type B. napus accessions has revealed that seed germination kinetics, in particular seed 30 germination speed, are major contributors to the variability of this trait. Metabolic 31 32 profiling of these accessions has allowed us to describe a common pattern of metabolic change and to identify the levels of malate and aspartate metabolites as putative metabolic 33 markers to estimate germination performance. Additionally, analysis of seed content of 34 35 different hormones suggests that hormonal balance between ABA, GA and IAA at crucial time points during this process might underlie seed germination differences in these 36 accessions. In this study, we have also defined the major transcriptome changes 37 38 accompanying the germination process in *B. napus*. Furthermore, we have observed that 39 earlier activation of key germination regulatory genes seems to generate the differences 40 in germination speed observed between accessions in *B. napus*. Finally, we have found 41 that protein-protein interactions between some of these key regulators are conserved in 42 B. napus suggesting a shared regulatory network with other plant species. Altogether, our results provide a comprehensive and detailed picture of seed germination dynamics in 43 oilseed rape. This new framework will be extremely valuable, not only to evaluate 44 germination performance of B. napus accessions, but also to identify key targets for crop 45 improvement in this important process. 46

## 53 INTRODUCTION

55 A plant's life cycle starts with seed germination, a process aiming to produce a seedling able to grow, develop and generate viable offspring. Germination is a crucial step for the 56 establishment of crops in the soil and the uniformity of seedling emergence that, in turn, 57 influence crop yield and quality. Seed germination is a complex trait influenced by 58 59 different intrinsic (physiological and hormonal state of the seed) and external factors (environmental conditions during seed development, germination and early seedling 60 61 growth) as well as harvest and posterior storage (Basnet et al, 2015; Finch-Savage and Bassel, 2016). Thus, continuous interaction between environment and genotype allows 62 seeds to germinate when conditions are suitable for plant growth (Kendall et al., 2011; 63 64 Penfield and MacGregor, 2017). In crops, this trait seems to be controlled by several genes adding complexity to its study and their application in biotechnological and 65 breeding programs. Moreover, seed germination encompasses a wide number of 66 metabolic, hormonal and molecular events aimed to timely produce the emergence of the 67 68 embryo radicle through the surrounding seed tissues (Bewley, 1997; Holdsworth et al., 2008). Therefore, improving germination in crops will require integrative approaches 69 70 combining a wide variety of global analyses (metabolic, molecular and genomic analyses) with an array of computational methods. 71

73 Oilseed rape (OSR) is one of the world's most important sources of high-quality vegetable oils for human nutrition and vegetable protein diets for livestock (Paterson et al., 2001). 74 75 Brassica napus productivity is being challenged by variable environmental conditions 76 that affect the reliability of seed germination in the field. Germination is a crucial trait in oilseed rape production because seedlings must quickly attain self-sufficiency in order to 77 survive. Previous studies in *B. napus* focused mainly on the phenotypic and genetic 78 aspects of germination traits, demonstrated that B. napus seed germination has substantial 79 80 potential for improvement (Hatzig et al., 2015). In this context, important efforts have been made to characterize the basic mechanisms regulating this trait using omic 81 approaches such as proteomics (Gu et al., 2016), hormonal profiling (Nguyen et al., 82 2016), oil content profiling (Ding et al., 2019), transcriptomics (Luo et al., 2019) and 83 combination of microarray and proteomic data (Kubala et al., 2015). All these studies 84 have been very useful to uncover some new regulatory mechanisms as well as to identify 85 86 common mechanisms with other plant species. Nevertheless, an integration of all these 87 data to provide a reliable framework to study and improve germination in this crop is still 88 missing

89 90 B. napus is closely related to the model crucifer Arabidopsis thaliana and more than 86% of the protein coding sequences are conserved between them (Cavell et al., 1998). Most 91 studies have taken advantage of the genetic and physiological proximity of both species, 92 to provide valuable guidance to analyze seed germination response in *B. napus* (Zhang et 93 94 al., 2004). Nevertheless, there are clear differences between both species regarding seed size, seed composition, and seed dormancy. Moreover, Arabidopsis has not been 95 subjected to the selection pressures of crop domestication and therefore its seeds have not 96 97 been challenged to perform in an agricultural context (Li et al., 2005, Finch-Savage and Bassel, 2016). Thus, it is expected that the mechanisms underlying the control of 98 germination in this crop could be more complex and/or different to those found in 99 100 Arabidopsis.

101

102 Seed germination begins with water uptake by the quiescent seed (imbibition) and ends with the elongation of the embryonic axis inside the seed. Seed imbibition triggers the 103 104 reactivation of various metabolic, physiological and biochemical processes necessary for 105 the seed-to-seedling transition such as resumption of respiratory activity, acquisition of energy, activation of repair mechanisms, protein biosynthesis from stored and newly 106 synthesized mRNA, and reserve mobilization. Accordingly, initial catabolism of stored 107 reserves of protein, oil or starch accumulated during seed maturation will support the 108 changes related with cell expansion, chloroplast development and root growth that have 109 to take place during this transition from heterotrophic to autotrophic nutrition 110 (Holdsworth et al., 2008; Rajjou et al., 2012). Many of these events are triggered and 111 112 controlled by integration of environmental signals by hormones, mainly abscisic acid 113 (ABA) and gibberellins (GAs), with antagonistic effects on the germination process (Shu et al., 2016). Environmental signals that are favorable or unfavorable for seed germination 114 115 are associated with high GA/ABA or ABA/GA ratios, respectively. There is also a clear 116 crosstalk between ABA and GAs since mutants with decreased or increased ABA content have altered expression of GA biosynthetic genes (Seo et al., 2006). An increased number 117 of regulatory loci, additional hormones and metabolic pathways has been identified as 118 119 important for the germination process, and some of these components have been shown 120 to work similarly in several plant species (Holdsworth et al., 2008; Linkies et al., 2009; 121 Morris et al., 2011; Rajjou et al., 2012; Shu et al., 2016). For instance, the role of ABA 122 in regulating dormancy and germination through ABI3 and ABI5 (Graeber et al., 2010) 123 and the function of GAs, PIL5 and DELLA proteins (Bassel et al., 2008; Daviere and Achard, 2016; Zhou et al., 2017) are well established. However, to our knowledge, other 124 important regulators discovered more recently such as HFR1, NF-YC9 or NAC25, have 125 126 not been investigated in the context of seed germination in plant species other than 127 Arabidopsis (Shi et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2016; Ravindran et al., 2017; Sánchez-Montesino et al., 2019). Although all these mechanisms would presumably be conserved in *B. napus*. 128 differences in some of the regulatory aspects are expected to be different in the polyploid 129 130 context of the crop genome. Other levels of regulation have also been shown to control germination in plants. Several studies have described global changes in mRNA 131 populations during seed germination (Luo et al., 2019; Narsai et al., 2011 and 2017), 132 133 adding the interaction of complex gene regulatory networks to the underlying mechanisms regulating this process. All these events have to be coordinated, temporally 134 regulated and integrated with environmental signals to accomplish a successful seedling 135 establishment. Thus, combined metabolomic and transcriptomic approaches to integrate 136 137 global transcriptional networks with metabolic and hormonal interactions are required to 138 decipher this complex coordination.

139

For that reason, we have decided to build a framework to study seed germination traits in 140 141 B.napus. We have used an integrative approach encompassing all these different analyses based on data from 6 winter-type accessions (Hatzig et al., 2015). Accessions selected 142 by their distinct germination rates were used to quantify hormones, metabolites and RNA 143 species at different stages upon seed imbibition. The robustness of the traits was 144 confirmed by using seeds from glasshouse-grown plants to perform similar germination 145 and seed trait assays. In silico analyses of all these data sets together with quantitative 146 expression analysis and protein-protein interaction assays have allowed us the 147 148 identification of gene regulatory and hormonal networks as well as key genes that may 149 explain the differences observed in germination traits between B. napus accessions. The germination framework reported here would be useful to define and evaluate targets that 150 151 may be suitable for improvement of germination in oilseed rape crops.

## Seed germination speed is a key contributor to germination variability in *B. napus*winter accessions.

378

379 Rapid and uniform seed germination is key for seedling establishment and high crop 380 yield. Variability in seed germination has been reported previously of B. napus but a framework to analyze such differences has not been yet fully established. In order to 381 define this framework, we performed a detailed analysis of the germination kinetics of 382 six winter-type B. napus accessions (Winter OilSeed Rape, WOSR) selected by their 383 384 variability in terms of germination. Germination in the selected WOSR accessions started 385 as earlier as 12 hours after imbibition (hai) although the first germination event (the radicle breaking through the testa) was observed predominantly at ~24 hai (Figures 1A, 386 **B**). In fact, we observed that most of the variability in the germination traits measured in 387 388 these WOSR accessions arises at early time points. Thus, 50% of germination (T50) in 389 these varieties was observed in a period spanning from 27 hai (C129) to 42 hai (C032) while final GRs (GR at 72 hai, end of germination for WOSR) varied from 93% (C129) 390 391 to 83% (C110) (Figure 1A). In most of the WOSR accessions, cotyledons were visible at 392 36 hai, fully developed at 48 hai and the greening of cotyledons was observed at 72 hai (Figure 1B). Although these results define a common developmental pathway during 393 394 germination for all the WOSR accessions analyzed, significant deviations from mean GRs 395 were observed between 24 and 48 hai (Figure 1A). Specifically, at 36 hai, some 396 accessions (C129, C124 and C033) were reaching 70% GR whereas other lines (C032 and C110) were still close to just 40% GR (Figure 1A), suggesting a difference between 397 accessions in germination kinetics rather than in their final GRs. In order to find key 398 germination traits responsible for this variability, we first categorized the accessions 399 based on their germination kinetics by hierarchical clustering. This analysis separated the 400 accessions into 3 clusters (Figure 1C). We observed that BnaEXPA2, one of the main targets 829 830 of the RGL2-NAC25 complex, was expressed as AtEXPA2 and that both BnaRGL2 and 831 BnaNAC25 proteins are able to interact (Figure 9). SPT is another negative regulator of seed germination and cotyledon expansion that has been shown to interact with RGL2 in 832 yeast (Penfield et al., 2005; Gallego-Bartolome et al., 2010). Yeast cells carrying AD-833 834 BnaRGL2 with BD-BnaSPT constructs also show growth in selective media indicating a similar interaction than in Arabidopsis. All these results indicate that BnaRGL2 835 836 interactions with key regulators of germination are conserved in B. napus.

837 838 PIL5 is another negative regulator of seed germination integrating light and hormone signals (Oh et al., 2004, 2006, 2009). When PIL5 protein is not degraded by light, their 839 regulatory properties depend on several interactions with other proteins and gene 840 841 promoters. Moreover, PIL5 and ABI3 collaboratively activate the expression of SOM by 842 directly binding to and interacting with each other at the SOM promoter (Park et al., 843 2011). In turn, SOM negatively regulates seed germination by modifying the expression levels of ABA/GA metabolic genes (Kim et al., 2008). The kinetics of these genes 844 845 (Figure 8 and Supplementary Figure 3) are similar to those observed for Arabidopsis genes (Parcy et al., 1994; Piskurewicz et al., 2008; Park et al., 2011; Hsieh et al., 2012) 846 847 suggesting that this important regulatory network is conserved in *B. napus*. Indeed, the 848 result from our Y2H experiment indicates that BnaPIL5 (BD-BnaPIL5) and BnaABI3 (AD-BnaABI3) proteins are able to interact strongly in this system (Figure 9). Contrary 849 to PIL5, HFR1 is a positive regulator of light-induced seed germination able to directly 850 interact with and sequester PIL5 to prevent it from binding to its target genes (Shi et al., 851 852 2013). In addition, this interaction promotes reciprocal co-degradation of PIL5 and HFR1 853 (Xu et al., 2017). This way light-induced germination promotes increased abundance of HFR1, thus enhancing PIL5 degradation and leading to increased GA levels (Oh et al., 854 2006; Xu et al., 2017). As observed in Figure 9, BnaPIL5 and BnaHFR1 are able to 855 interact strongly since yeast cells carrying the BD-BnaPIL5 and AD-BnaHFR1 constructs 856 have enhanced growth in the presence of 3-AT above control levels and as strongly as the 857 BnaPIL5-BnaABI3 interaction. 858

## 865 DISCUSSION

867 Seed germination is a major component of seed vigour affecting plant survival and crop yield. Our analyses of a panel of B.napus accessions showing robust differences in seed 868 869 germination have determined that the speed of this process is responsible for germination 870 variability. Briefly, despite our accessions were very similar in terms of seed viability (understood as the germination potential of the seed, final germination rates), major 871 differences in seed germination were observed in T50 values, spanning 15 h between the 872 fastest (C129) and slowest (C032). In other words, when considering 36 hai as the average 873 T50, a 30% GR difference between extreme accessions was observed. Although seedling 874 establishment is largely dependent on seed viability, our results indicate that high seed 875 viability does not always correlate with high germination speed in B. napus. Given that 876 877 germination speed could have greater impact on seedling survival and crop yield when 878 environmental conditions are not favorable, this trait will be a valuable parameter to estimate and to improve seedling establishment in all environmental conditions affecting 879 880 this crop in the field. In fact, farmers require vigorous seeds that ensure the reliable and 881 successful establishment of their crops under environments subjected to changing 882 environmental cues (Finch-Savage and Bassel, 2016). Thus, seeds performing well under 883 optimal conditions are also more likely to perform better under stress (Bettey et al., 2000; 884 Foolad et al., 2007). It has been shown that B.napus seed germination can be substantially 885 improved (Hatzig et al., 2015) and our results suggest that using the genetic variability to 886 increase germination speed is a promising approach to achieve it. Moreover, our results 887 have confirmed that oilseed rape seeds germinate faster than Arabidopsis seeds, in 888 agreement with previously published data (Gu et al., 2019; Li et al., 2005; Kubala et al., 2015; Luo et al., 2019; Hatzig et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2016). This acceleration of 889 germination in B. napus could be reflecting the enhanced pressure to germinate that crop 890 891 seeds have to face on agricultural challenging environments, again pointing to this trait 892 as an interesting target to increase B. napus seed performance in the field. However, 893 testing this hypothesis is not easy given the existing differences in seed size between 894 Arabidopsis and oilseed rape and the absence of *B. napus* wild-type accessions. 895

896 Seed reserves such as oils, proteins and carbohydrates are key for metabolism activation during seed imbibition along the germination process, and serve to support early seedling 897 growth (Weitbrecht et al., 2011; Nonogaki, 2014; Paszkiewicz et al., 2017). Our 898 metabolic profiles agree with other seed germination profiles in other plants and crops, 899 where at later stages of germination there is also an increase in sugars (fructose and 900 901 glucose, and maltose) and minor carbohydrate metabolism (raffinose, sorbitol and xylose) 902 related with the increased necessity of energy during the germination process as well as 903 compounds associated with cell wall metabolism (myo-inositol and galactinol) (Howell 904 et al., 2009; Gu et al., 2016). During seed germination, carbon skeletons of oil reserves 905 are utilized to produced sugars to feed the energy metabolism machinery (glycolysis, 906 Kreb's cycle, oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial electron transport) thanks to 907 the Glyoxylate cycle. Interestingly, PCA analysis of carbohydrates evidenced that the 908 high germination speed C129 accession has significant lower levels of malate at all-time 909 points analyzed (Figure 2C). Malate is an important metabolite that can be oxidized to 910 oxaloacetate, a precursor of gluconeogenesis, and is produced by the Glyoxylate cycle 911 (glyoxysomes) as well as by the Kreb's cycle in the mitochondria (Eastmond and Graham, 912 2001). One explanation for our result would be that C129 is able to use malate faster than 913 other accessions resulting in lower basal levels of the metabolite. Consequently, this 914 accession would be more efficient generating energy required for germination that, in turn, will be accelerated. Although our analyses on amino acid levels yielded results 915 916 similar to those obtained for Arabidopsis (Figure 2B; Fait et al., 2006), a peak of aspartate

The plant Asp-family pathway leads to the synthesis of the essential amino acids lysine, threonine, methionine 919 920 and isoleucine and, for this reason, has been of interest for plant breeders (Jander and 921 Joshi, 2009; Galili, 2011). Besides their importance for human food quality, these amino acids are also well-known donor metabolites that feeds the TCA cycle (Weitbrecht et al., 922 2011). Thus, higher levels of aspartate could also be related to extra carbon being 923 available for germination and early seedling development. Both results suggest that 924 malate and aspartate would be good markers to monitor germination efficiency in this 925 926 crop.

ABA accumulates in dry seeds during seed and embryo development but endogenous 928 929 ABA content decreased significantly within 6-24 h after the onset of imbibition in Arabidopsis and rice (Ali-Rachedi et al., 2004; Ye et al., 2012). Consistently, ABA levels 930 931 progressively diminished over time in all accessions although steady state levels of ABA 932 in dry seeds varied between them. Thus, we found that among the accessions studied, rapid-germinating C129 has lower levels of ABA in dry seeds and at early time points. 933 However, our results combining germination kinetics of C129, C033 and C032 and 934 935 clustering analyses indicate that ABA content does not seem to be a major contributor to 936 germination variability on B. napus seeds. Moreover, RNA levels of key genes involved 937 in ABA metabolism are similar between the accessions along the sampled germination 938 times resembling the pattern observed for their orthologs in Arabidopsis (Supplementary 939 Figure S3). However, it seems that is the ABA/GA balance and the sensitivity to these 940 hormones that play a central role in the control of germination (Finch-Savage and Footitt, 2017). Bioactive GA accumulates in the embryo just before radicle protrusion (Kucera et 941 942 al., 2005; Ogawa et al., 2003). We detected GA<sub>4</sub> (bioactive) in the C129 and C033 943 accessions and found that its accumulation is delayed in the C032 accession (selected for 944 its slow germination speed). In accordance with these results, we found that the kinetics of two genes encoding key enzymes for early steps of the GA biosynthetic pathway 945 946 (BnaGA1 and BnaKO1) are delayed in the C032 accession in comparison to C129 and 947 C033 (Figure 8). Taking all these data together, the ABA/GA balance could explain some of the differences observed in germination speed between accessions. Thus, C129 has low 948 949 ABA and medium GA levels, C033 has more ABA but higher GA levels and C032 has 950 high ABA (similar to C033) but delayed accumulation and low levels of GA. In addition, it has been proposed that IAA acts as an enhancer of ABA in germination (Liu et al., 951 2007; Nguyen et al., 2016). Interestingly, we detected high levels of IAA at early time 952 953 points in C032 and C033 varieties (Supplementary Figure S2) that could be accounting 954 for slowing down germination speed. Moreover, the higher IAA-Asp levels observed in the C033 accession compared to C032, suggest that the former would be able to overcome 955 956 the inhibitory effects of IAA by activation of IAA degradation pathways to ensure germination (Ljung, 2013). 957

Unexpectedly, we also found significant differences in GA<sub>8</sub> content, being higher in C129 959 960 accession compared with C033 and C032. Although GA<sub>4</sub> is likely to be the major endogenous active GA in Arabidopsis 965 (Talón et al., 1990; Derkx et al., 1994; Hedden and Kamiya, 1997; Ogawa et al., 2003), our results suggest that GA1 may play a key role in WOSR seed germination. 979 Transcriptomic analyses are extremely useful to uncover the basic biological process 980 underlying any trait. Correspondence analysis between DEGs and WOSR varieties along the germination process and their projection in a 2D space highlighted that changes in the 981 982 transcriptomes corresponding to each accession were very similar (89%; Figure 6C). In 983 fact, most of the RNA kinetics (RNA-seq or/and qPCR) of genes related to ABA and GA 984 metabolism and signaling, including key regulators of the process, had similar patterns of gene expression between accessions. In the same way, important protein-protein 985 986 interactions known to control germination by integrating environmental and hormone 987 signals in other species (Gallego-Bartolome et al., 2010; Park et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2016; 988 Xu et al., 2017; Sánchez-Montesino et al., 2019), were found to be conserved when using 989 B.napus ortholog proteins (Figure 9). All together, these results reinforce the idea that 990 the basic molecular mechanisms governing germination are the same in the different accessions. However, some transcriptomic differences were located mainly at 12 hai 991 where C129, C033 and C032 did not cluster together along transcription progress 992 993 associated with seed germination. Indeed, several genes were found to have different 994 kinetics between the accessions that could contribute to their distinct germination speed 995 (Figure 8). Most of these differences fits with an early activation of germination related 996 gene networks, mainly related with hormonal pathways, in the rapid-speed germination 997 variety C129 and a delayed response of slow-speed C032 (Figure 8 and Supplementary 998 Figure S3). Furthermore, some of these genes could contribute directly to some of the seed germination traits differences in WOSR. Thus, we found that some genes previously 999 associated to seed germination, BnaTPC1 and BnaGDSL1, showed differential 1000 expression kinetics between our accessions (Supplementary Figure 3; Hatzig et al., 1001 2018; Ding et al., 2019). BnaGDSL1 is highly induced during seed germination and is 1002 phylogenetically very close to BnaLIP1. The Arabidopsis orthologue gene of BnaLIP1, 1003 is a marker gene for GA-induced expression during seed germination in the embryo 1004 1005 epidermis positively affecting seed germination speed (Rombolá-Caldentey et al., 2014). Seed germination requires GA signaling in the epidermis mediated by DELLA and HD-1006 ZIP IV proteins that control the expression of genes involved in growth of the embryonic 1007 axis (Rombolá-Caldentey et al., 2014). One of the target genes of this regulatory complex 1008 is EXPA8 (cluster 3; Supplementary Figure 3), an important player for cell wall 1009 remodeling during growth and a convergence point for several signaling pathways (de 1010 Lucas et al., 2008; Feng et al., 2008; Stavang et al., 2009; Bai et al., 2012; Gallego-1011 Bartolomé et al., 2012; Park et al., 2013). Interestingly, this gene was found highly 1012 expressed in C129 compared to C032, and C033, supporting again a GA-related 1013 mechanism underlying germination speed in these varieties. We have seen correlation 1014 1015 between elongation speed of the radicle (ES) and germination speed parameters. Taking 1016 into account that root elongation is mediated by cell expansion, it is intriguing to think that a GA mediated cell expansion could be taking part in this germination response. ## 1303 Contribution to the Field Statement

Farmers require vigorous seeds that ensure the reliable and successful establishment of 1304 1305 their crops under changing environmental conditions. Seed germination is a complex trait likely to be controlled by several genes that influence crop establishment and yield. Due 1306 to this complexity, the study of this trait in crops has been mainly focused on 1307 1308 physiological and genetic aspects. Therefore, we use integrated approaches combining different omics in order to have a more comprehensive understanding of the regulation of 1309 this process. We have defined a framework of metabolic, hormonal and transcriptional 1310 1311 changes that take place during seed germination in oilseed rape (OSR), one of the world's most important sources of high-quality vegetable oil for human nutrition and vegetable 1312 protein diet for livestock. Moreover, we have identified particular metabolic, hormonal 1313 1314 and transcriptional changes that correlate with the observed variability in germination kinetics between different WOSR accessions. These molecular signatures could be used 1315 as benchmarks to evaluate germination performance under different environmental 1316 conditions, as well as putative targets to be used in biotechnological and breeding 1317 1318 programs to improve this trait. In summary, the information gathered in these extensive 1319 analyses would be of enormous interest for plant biologist and breeders working in this process not only in B. napus but also in other crops. 1320
