### Summary

Three subtypes of C<sub>4</sub> photosynthesis exist (NADP-ME, NAD-ME and PEPCK), each known to be beneficial under specific environmental conditions. However, the influence of photosynthetic subtype on transcriptomic plasticity, as well as the genes underpinning this variability, remain largely unknown. Here, we exposed six  $C_4$  grass species spanning all three  $C_4$  subtypes to two controlled environmental stresses: low light (200 µmol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) and glacial CO<sub>2</sub> (sub-ambient; 180ppm). We identify a susceptibility within NADP-ME species to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>. Notably, although growth responses could be tied to  $C_4$  subtype, biochemical and transcriptomic responses to glacial CO<sub>2</sub> were species specific. Nevertheless, we were able to identify subtype-specific subsets of significantly differentially expressed transcripts which link resource acquisition and allocation to susceptibility to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>. Here, low light affected each species comparably with no clear subtype response. Despite transcriptomic responses to low light being largely species specific, numerous functional similarities were noted, hence, while transcriptomic regulation has diverged, functionality remains relatively conserved. Additionally, PEPCK species exhibited heightened regulation of transcripts related to metabolism in response to both stresses, likely tied to its  $\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{A}}$  metabolic pathway. These results highlight the influence that both species and subtype can have on plant responses to abiotic stress, building on our mechanistic understanding of acclimation within C<sub>4</sub> grasses and highlighting avenues for future crop improvements.

# Introduction

Grasses currently account for a large proportion of the staple food produced in both developed and developing countries (FAO, 2019). A subset of grasses utilise  $C_4$  photosynthesis, an adaptation where a carbon concentrating mechanism (CCM) evolved to reduce wasteful oxygenation by Rubisco, the enzyme responsible for the first step of the Calvin Benson cycle (Hatch, 1987). The  $C_4$  CCM functions by transiently

fixing carbon dioxide (CO<sub>2</sub>) into a 4-carbon acid within the mesophyll cells via Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC), before diffusion into adjacent bundle sheath cells. Here, the 4-carbon acid is decarboxylated to increase the concentration of CO<sub>2</sub> around Rubisco. Under ambient conditions the CCM allows C<sub>4</sub> plants to have higher assimilation, water use efficiency (WUE) and nitrogen use efficiency in comparison to C<sub>3</sub> counterparts (Ward *et al.*, 1999; Pinto *et al.*, 2011; Pinto *et al.*, 2014). Due to these traits, even though C<sub>4</sub> species represent only 3% of plant species, they account for a large proportion (~25%) of today's total plant productivity (Still *et al.*, 2003). Currently, the most produced C<sub>4</sub> crop is *Zea mays* (Maize) (FAO, 2019), but numerous others are utilised across the world including *Sorghum bicolor* (Sorghum), *Panicum miliaceum* (common millet) and *Saccharum officinarum* (Sugarcane). Their uses range from food for human consumption, to fodder, through to biofuel production.

The driver of C<sub>4</sub> evolution is thought to be a sharp decline in CO<sub>2</sub> concentrations during the Oligocene epoch, 24-35 million years ago (Sage, 2004; Christin *et al.*, 2008), which has since led to C<sub>4</sub> photosynthesis evolving independently over 60 times (Sage *et al.*, 2011). Within these evolutionary lineages three subtypes of C<sub>4</sub> photosynthesis have evolved (Hatch *et al.*, 1975), each known by the primary decarboxylating enzyme used to release CO<sub>2</sub> inside the bundle sheath. These are: NADP-malic enzyme (NADP-ME), NAD-malic enzyme (NAD-ME), and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK). Extensive study has now characterised the under-pinning genetics and biochemistry of each subtype's CCM, identifying substantial variation across the subtypes (Ashton *et al.*, 1990; Agostino *et al.*, 1996; Christin *et al.*, 2013a; Weissmann *et al.*, 2016; Watson-Lazowski *et al.*, 2018). It is thought the subtypes evolved due to selective environmental pressures and ecological niches such as temperature and water availability (Vogel *et al.*, 1986; Hattersley, 1992), as well as pre-existing leaf traits in C<sub>3</sub> ancestors (Christin *et al.*, 2013b). Therefore, elucidating the variation across these subtypes could hold key information regarding adaptive strategies to changing climates.

Two environmental variables that we expect to elicit subtype-specific responses are  $CO_2$  and light. Reducing  $CO_2$  supply is expected to exact a direct stress on photosynthesis (in particularly the CCM) of  $C_4$  species (Ripley *et al.*, 2013; Pinto *et al.*, 2014; Taylor *et al.*, 2018). In regards to light, the  $C_4$  subtypes are known to exhibit differences in their photosynthetic quantum yields (Ehleringer & Pearcy, 1983), energy requirements (Wang *et al.*, 2014), and CCM efficiencies (Kromdijk *et al.*, 2008; Ubierna *et al.*, 2013; Bellasio & Griffiths, 2014b; Bellasio & Griffiths, 2014a; Sonawane *et al.*, 2018). Utilising these conditions, amongst others, some  $C_4$  subtype specific characteristics have already been reported (Carmo-Silva *et al.*, 2007; Ghannoum *et al.*, 2010); with evidence suggesting PEPCK species have a more efficient Rubisco (Sharwood *et al.*, 2016a) and higher nitrogen use efficiency (Pinto *et al.*, 2016), NAD-ME species are more

suited to arid environments (Ghannoum *et al.*, 2002; Carmo-Silva *et al.*, 2009), and NADP-ME species are less compromised by low light (Sonawane *et al.*, 2018). However, the impact that subtype has on transcriptomic plasticity and which genetic mechanisms underpin these variations is still not fully understood.

Here, we took six C<sub>4</sub> grass species and exposed them to either low light (200  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) or glacial CO<sub>2</sub> (sub-ambient; 180 ppm), relative to control conditions (1000  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> light and 400ppm CO<sub>2</sub>), and investigated the influence of C<sub>4</sub> subtype on acclimation. The species span three independent origins of C<sub>4</sub> photosynthesis and all three subtypes; *Zea mays* (NADP-ME), *Setaria italica* (NADP-ME), *Panicum miliaceum* (NAD-ME), *Leptochloa fusca* (NAD-ME), *Megathyrsus maximus* (PEPCK) and *Chloris gayana* (PEPCK). *L. fusca* and *C. gayana* both originate from the Chloridoideae subfamily while the rest originate from the Panicoideae subfamily. Further, *Z. mays* resides in the Androponneae tribe, an independent origin of NADP-ME photosynthesis from *S. italica* within the Panicoideae subfamily (Brutnell *et al.*, 2010). Physiological and biochemical analyses were undertaken to assess the response of each species to the environmental stimuli. RNA sequencing was then employed to elucidate transcriptomic plasticity during abiotic stress, providing insight into the impact of C<sub>4</sub> subtype on transcriptomic plasticity. We hypothesise that exposure to the environmental stimuli will elicit both phenomic and transcriptomic subtype-specific responses, which together, will elucidate subtype-specific characteristics.

### Results

### Phenotypic and physiological acclimations

Responses to glacial CO<sub>2</sub> and low light revealed a range of subtype and species-specific acclimations (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S1). Only NADP-ME species (*Z. mays* and *S. italica*) exhibited a significant reduction in above ground biomass (AGB) (36% and 67% respectively;  $P \le 0.01$ ) and total leaf area (LA) (31% and 70% respectively;  $P \le 0.05$ ) in response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>. This was highlighted as subtype-specific, with only the NADP-ME subtype exhibiting a significant decrease in AGB and LA ( $P \le 0.05$ ) in response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>. Although PEPCK species showed an overall decrease in AGB in response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>, variability was high and hence not significant ( $P \ge 0.27$ ). AGB and LA decreased in response to low light for all species and hence within all subtypes ( $P \le 0.05$ ). However, NADP-ME species were least affected by low light, showing the lowest percentage change in AGB (73% and 87% decrease in *S. italica* and *Z. mays* respectively), while NAD-ME species (*P. milliaceum* and *L. fusca*) exhibited the highest ( $\ge 94\%$  decreases).

Below ground biomass followed the same trends as above ground biomass across species and treatments while specific leaf area exhibited species specific acclimations (Supplementary Table S1).

Gas exchange measurements showed all species had significantly reduced assimilation (A) ( $\geq$  -9%; P  $\leq$  0.05) at glacial CO<sub>2</sub> and to a greater extent low light ( $\geq$  -52%; P  $\leq$  0.01). Stomatal conductance (gs) significantly increased ( $\geq$  21%; P  $\leq$  0.05) at glacial CO<sub>2</sub> and significantly decreased ( $\geq$  -56%; P  $\leq$  0.05) at low light for all species. We also note that NADP-ME species exhibited the largest increases in gs ( $\geq$  74%) when compared to other species ( $\leq$  68%) in response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>. CO<sub>2</sub> saturated assimilation rate (A<sub>max</sub>) was significantly up-regulated only in the Chloridoideae species (Supplementary Table S1) at glacial CO<sub>2</sub> ( $\geq$  19%; P  $\leq$  0.05). A<sub>max</sub> was significantly down-regulated in all species at low light ( $\geq$  -12%; P  $\leq$  0.01), however, we note NADP-ME species exhibited the lowest decreases ( $\leq$  -16%).

# Enzyme Assays of Key C<sub>4</sub> Proteins

Enzyme activity assays were carried out for enzymes representing key steps of the C<sub>4</sub> pathway (Fig. 2a-d). PEPC activity was measured for most species, however, as insufficient soluble PEPC protein could be extracted, PEPC activity was not considered for *L. fusca* and *C. gayana*. Notably, *Z. mays* was the only species which showed a significant PEPC response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>, increasing PEPC activity by 44% (P  $\leq$ 0.01). All species measured decreased PEPC activity at low light ( $\geq$  -60%; P  $\leq$  0.05). High decarboxylase activity was consistent with subtype classification for each species. Additional decarboxylase activity of NADP-ME species was as expected, with *Z. mays* exhibiting PEPCK activity and *S. italica* not. Although NAD-ME and PEPCK species are thought to contain activity of two decarboxylases (NAD-ME and PEPCK), here only one NAD-ME species (*L. fusca*) showed considerable PEPCK activity and one PEPCK species (*M. maximus*) showed considerable NAD-ME activity.

Changes in decarboxylase activity mainly reflected species specific acclimations. For NADP-ME, *Z. mays* showed a significant decrease in activity in response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub> (-19%; P  $\leq$  0.01), while *S. italica* showed no response. Both *Z. mays* and *S. italica* significantly decreased NADP-ME activity at low light (-39% and - 34% respectively; P  $\leq$  0.05). NAD-ME activity was significantly increased in both *L. fusca* and *M. maximus* at glacial CO<sub>2</sub> (53% and 28% respectively; P  $\leq$  0.05), while *P. miliaceum* showed no response. NAD-ME activity in *R. maximus* decreased (-32%; P  $\leq$  0.05). No significant changes in PEPCK activity were identified for *C. gayana*, *Z. mays* or *L. fusca*, while *M. maximus* decreased PEPCK activity at low light (-33%; P  $\leq$  0.05).

Rubisco content was assayed using [<sup>14</sup>C] CABP (Supplementary Figure S1). Species largely showed no response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>, with a significant increase only being noted in *L. fusca* (55%;  $P \le 0.05$ ). In response to low light, again, species largely showed no response, with only *S. italica* and *L. fusca* significantly downregulating rubisco content ( $\ge$  -38%;  $P \le 0.05$ ).

### Transcript abundance within the C<sub>4</sub> Cycle

Transcript expression of C<sub>4</sub> specific genes (annotated based on published literature) exhibited a similar pattern to that of the enzyme activity, and changes were largely species specific or conserved across all species, with few genes exhibiting subtype species changes (Fig. 2e-f). In response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>, *L. fusca* showed increased expression in almost all genes related specifically to NAD-ME photosynthesis, while increases in NAD-ME specific transporters were noted for *P. miliaceum* (DTC, DiC1 and MPC2a; Fig. 2e). *S.italica* showed minimal changes in expression, while *Z. mays* increased expression in several transcripts, especially aspartate amino transferase (AspAT3 and AspAT5; Fig. 2e). Within PEPCK species, both showed a trend of downregulation of C<sub>4</sub> cycle transcripts at glacial CO<sub>2</sub>, although select transcripts associated with NAD-ME photosynthesis were up-regulated (DTC, AspAT1 and NAD-ME2; Fig. 2e). Low light caused a downregulation for the majority of C<sub>4</sub> transcripts across all species (Fig. 2f). However, within *M. maximus* several transcripts were up-regulated (AspAT3, NAD-ME2, MPC2a and PiC2), and downregulation in the remaining C<sub>4</sub> transcripts was reduced compared to all other species (Fig. 2f). Correlations between enzyme activity and expression were calculated (Supplementary Figure S2). The decarboxylases showed good relationships between activity and expression in all conditions (r ≥ 0.65), whereas PEPC showed a disconnected relationship between the two measures at glacial CO<sub>2</sub> and low light (r ≤ 0.16).

### Transcriptome responses to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>

The overlap of significantly differentially expressed (DE) transcripts (FDR  $\leq$  0.05) in response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub> was calculated and visualised using UpSetR (Conway *et al.*, 2017), and large variations between species were apparent (Fig. 3a-b). The lowest number of DE transcripts was 37 (*C. gayana*) and the highest 1,628 (*M. maximus*) with no subtype or subfamily basis for number of DE transcripts, suggesting species driven responses. The lists of DE transcripts can be found in Supplementary Data S1.

Small sub-sets of DE transcripts were identified as subtype specific (Fig. 3c), which were functionally characterised and then grouped into categories based on published literature. These transcripts appear to be linked with the subtype-specific reductions in growth, suggesting fine tuning of the transcriptomic responses related specifically to  $C_4$  subtype. Evidence suggests all five DE transcripts conserved within the

NADP-ME subtype play, or are likely to play, a role related to growth and development (Fig. 3c). Of these, the two DE transcripts which were up-regulated have a reported role in histidine (histidine biosynthesis aminotransferase; HPA1) or arginine synthesis (venosa 3; VEN3) (Ingle, 2011; Mollá-Morales *et al.*, 2011), while one of the down-regulated DE transcripts has been shown to be involved in growth regulation (hookless 1; HLS1) (Li *et al.*, 2004). One of the down-regulated DE transcripts associated with the NAD-ME subtype was  $\beta$  carbonic anhydrase 1 ( $\beta$ CA1), a gene known to have a key role stomatal aperture control and CO<sub>2</sub> sensing (Hu *et al.*, 2010; Kolbe *et al.*, 2018). There was a higher number of DE transcripts related to the PEPCK subtype (12), falling across a wider range of categories. The largest selection of DE transcripts within PEPCK species were related to metabolism, more specifically C<sub>4</sub> and sugar/starch metabolism, suggesting heightened metabolic regulation within the PEPCK subtype. Additionally, a senescence-associated subtilisin protease (SASP) was DE within PEPCK species, a gene which has been linked with stomatal aperture control and drought tolerance (Wang *et al.*, 2018).

Gene ontology (GO) over-representation analysis was used on each list of DE transcripts to interrogate functional characteristics of the DE transcripts. No GO categories were over represented in all species, furthering the species-specific nature of the response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>. There were, however, three categories that were PEPCK-specific related to carbohydrate metabolic process and response to chemical stimulus. This furthers the idea of heightened metabolic regulation within the PEPCK subtype. A full list of the over-represented GO categories can be found in Supplementary Data S1.

### Transcriptomic response to low light

The numbers of DE transcripts (FDR  $\leq$  0.05) in response to light was higher than those identified in response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>, with only *M. maximus* exhibiting fewer DE transcripts at low light. Again, the overlap of DE transcripts was calculated and visualised using UpSetR (Fig. 4a-b). Species was again the main driver of genetic response, although a large number of DE transcripts were identified only within Chloridoideae species, highlighting an influence of subfamily in the response to low light.

Only two DE transcripts were conserved across all species, one of which was a signal peptide peptidase (SppA) known to affect acclimation to variable light intensities (Wetzel *et al.*, 2009). The second encodes starch branching enzyme 2.2 (SBE2.2) (Wychowski *et al.*, 2017), likely related to the reduced production of sugars under low light. Again, sub-sets of subtype specific transcripts were identified indicating a proportion of acclimation to low light is tied to subtype, but due to the high number of such transcripts (164), individual functional annotations could not be manually curated. Instead, GO enrichment was utilised to suggest possible functional characteristics. GO categories that were over expressed in all

species, a specific subtype or all species except a specific subtype were extracted (Fig. 5). Unlike the response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>, categories related to abiotic stress, carbohydrate metabolism, the photosynthetic membrane, and the chloroplast thylakoid were found to be significantly over-represented across all species. This suggests even though overlap of specific transcripts was low, there is a conserved functional response to low light.

Several over-represented GO categories were also identified as subtype specific. The PEPCK subtype had distinct patterns of over-representation within categories related to metabolic processes (within the up-regulated DE transcripts), as well as a lack of over-representation within categories related to catabolic processes (within the down-regulated DE transcripts; Fig. 5). This furthers the idea of heightened metabolic regulation within PEPCK species. There was also evidence of over-representation of oxidoreductase activity in NADP-ME species and response to starvation in NAD-ME species within the down-regulated DE transcripts (Fig. 5). A full list of the over-represented GO categories can be found in Supplementary Data S1.

### Discussion

Here we investigated the response of six  $C_4$  species to both glacial  $CO_2$  and low light to examine the influence of  $C_4$  subtype on acclimation to abiotic stress. Here on, we discuss the identified phenomic acclimations before discussing the transcriptomic responses, both in general and in relation to the phenotypes identified.

### Reductions in growth suggest the NADP-ME subtype has a susceptibility to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>

Numerous  $C_4$  crop species housed within the Andropogoneae and Paniceae tribes utilise the NADP-ME pathway, making it the most economically important  $C_4$  subtype within grasses. Here, a significant decrease in biomass and leaf area in response to glacial  $CO_2$  was identified only within the NADP-ME subtype (Fig. 1a-b). However, gas exchange showed NADP-ME species acclimated in a similar fashion to all other species, decreasing photosynthesis (A) and increasing stomatal conductance (gs; Fig. 1c-d). It has previously been argued that elevated stomatal conductance has a part to play in the heightened negative response to glacial  $CO_2$  of NADP-ME species due to the inter-play of  $CO_2$  and water (Pinto *et al.*, 2014). Here, we again found NADP-ME species had the largest increases in stomatal conductance (Fig. 1d). Variation in stomatal conductance at glacial  $CO_2$  is thought to be related to glacial  $CO_2$  acting as a water stress on  $C_4$  plants (Cunniff *et al.*, 2017). Plants here were well watered, however, high stomatal conductance can still expose plants to increased risk of hydraulic failure (Osborne & Sack, 2012; Taylor *et al.*, 2018) hence its influence cannot be ruled out.

To investigate the influence of glacial CO<sub>2</sub> on the C<sub>4</sub> cycle, biochemical assays of key C<sub>4</sub> enzymes were interrogated. *Z. mays* increased PEPC activity (Fig. 2a), a response likely aimed at increasing CO<sub>2</sub> delivery to the bundle sheath to maintain photosynthetic rates at glacial CO<sub>2</sub>. An antagonistic decrease in NADP-ME activity was also identified (Fig. 2b) which would reduce the CO<sub>2</sub> being released, and hence leakage out of the bundle sheath. Both responses can be seen in terms of optimising CCM efficiency under limited CO<sub>2</sub> conditions (Caemmerer *et al.*, 2014). Substantial PEPCK activity was noted for *Z. mays*, but no change was identified in response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub> (Fig. 2d). However, transcripts related to the aspartate shuttle were up-regulated (Fig. 2e), which could lead to an increased flux through the PEPCK decarboxylase if a greater amount of aspartate is synthesised from oxaloacetate. This could represent plasticity between primary and secondary decarboxylases, an idea which has previously been suggested under environmental stress (Bellasio & Griffiths, 2014a; Wang *et al.*, 2014). These acclimations would represent an interesting solution to reduced CO<sub>2</sub> availability, however, *S. italica* showed almost no change in PEPC and NADP-ME activity, and limited change in C<sub>4</sub> transcript expression. This suggests these metrics are likely independent of the susceptibility of the NADP-ME species to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>, and the additional plasticity within the C<sub>4</sub> cycle of *Z. mays* cannot compensate for the reduced CO<sub>2</sub> supply.

Although these species cover two independent origins of NADP-ME photosynthesis within the Panicoideae subfamily (Brutnell *et al.*, 2010), it is worth highlighting it is unknown whether all NADP-ME grasses will exhibit the same susceptibility to glacial  $CO_2$ . Further to this, NADP-ME species from distant taxa, such as those within the eudicots, could well have a varied susceptibility to glacial  $CO_2$ . However, the conserved nature of the susceptibility here, as well as the susceptibility previously noted (Pinto *et al.*, 2014), provides weight to the conclusion that NADP-ME grasses have a heightened susceptibility to glacial  $CO_2$  when compared to the other  $C_4$  subtypes.

### Low light negatively affects all subtypes

Light intensity is a key driver of photosynthesis, and varying light can modify photosynthetic capacity (Martinez & Fridley, 2018). Recent work has provided mutants that have enhanced photosynthesis in environments where light levels fluctuate (Kromdijk *et al.*, 2016), highlighting its importance in the quest to improve crop yields. Here, low light negatively affected all species, showing significant decreases in biomass, leaf area, A, gs and A<sub>max</sub> (Fig. 1, Supplementary Figure S3). This conserved response was expected as low light exhibits a strong stress on photosynthesis. Biochemical measurements showed low light

reduced PEPC activity in all species measured (Fig. 2a), but decarboxylase activity was less straightforward. NADP-ME activity was significantly decreased at low light, whereas NAD-ME and PEPCK showed a more species-specific response and was only down-regulated in *M. maximus* (Fig. 2c-d). Similar trends were true for the transcript expression of genes related to the C<sub>4</sub> cycle. The majority of transcripts were down-regulated in all species; however, several transcripts (PiC2, MPC2a, NAD-ME2, AspAT3 and PEPCK1) were up-regulated in *M. maximus* and *P. miliaceum* in a species-specific manner (Fig. 2f).

It has previously been shown that low light has a heightened effect on the NAD-ME subtype when compared to the NADP-ME subtype (Sonawane *et al.*, 2018), and evidence of this was noted here. NAD-ME species showed the greatest reduction in biomass and A<sub>max</sub> at low light, while NADP-ME species showed the lowest decreases in both metrics. PEPCK species laid in between NAD-ME and NADP-ME species for both metrics. Although these subtype-specific responses were masked here by an overwhelming conserved negative response to low light, it is worth noting that these characteristics remained true within this study.

Species is the major driver of transcriptomic response to glacial  $CO_2$  and low light, but a conserved functional response to low light is apparent

An overwhelming species-specific response was apparent across the transcript expression profiles in response to both glacial  $CO_2$  and low light. Large numbers of significantly differentially expressed (DE) transcripts were species-specific, combined with vastly different degrees of response (based on numbers of DE transcripts) across species (Fig. 3-4).

A consistent transcriptomic response across species was expected in response to low light due to consistent exposure to fluctuating light levels over evolutionary time and the conserved nature of the phenotypic and physiological results, yet it appeared mainly species-specific. Further to this, only two DE transcripts were conserved in all species in response to low light (Fig. 4). Both transcripts were down-regulated, one of which was annotated as a SppA, a gene involved in acclimation to light (Wetzel *et al.*, 2009). It is thought that this gene is involved in light-dependent degradation of antenna and photosystem II complexes (Lensch *et al.*, 2001), and its conserved response here suggests it likely plays a key role in regulating the light harvesting machinery in C<sub>4</sub> grasses. The second was annotated as SBE2.2, a gene involved in formation of starch granules (Wychowski *et al.*, 2017). The gene likely correlates with a reduced formation of starch under low light due to the reduced availability of sugars (due to reduced photosynthetic rates).

Although responses to low light were largely species-specific at the transcript level, there was evidence of convergence within the functionality of the DE transcripts. Key GO categories including response to abiotic stress, carbohydrate metabolic processes and changes in the chloroplast thylakoid membrane were overrepresented within the DE transcripts of all species (Fig. 5). Acclimation within these GO categories was largely expected and is likely essential for survival under low light (Bailey *et al.*, 2001; Bellasio & Griffiths, 2014a). These results suggest that even though transcriptomic regulation in response to low light has diverged over time, the functional characteristics of the response remain and are conserved.

A species-specific response to glacial  $CO_2$  may be expected, as plants have not experienced these  $CO_2$  concentrations for millions of years. However, as glacial  $CO_2$  can elicit a pseudo-drought condition, we expected to see some evidence of conserved genetic acclimation either across all species or within each subtype. No conserved transcripts or GO categories were identified across all species in response to glacial  $CO_2$ , but some subtype-specific transcripts were noted. These transcripts are of interest due to the subtype-specific nature of the phenotypic response; hence, we discuss their implications in the following section.

# Subtype-specific transcriptomic responses shed light on susceptibility to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>

Although species was the major driver of transcript expression, the susceptibility of NADP-ME species to glacial CO<sub>2</sub> remains apparent, and small but possibly influential groups of DE transcripts were shown to acclimate in a subtype-specific manner (Fig. 3b). Five transcripts were DE in only NADP-ME species in response to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>, all of which have literature suggesting a link with cellular development based on orthologous annotation to *A. thaliana*. HLS1 has been shown to influence sugar and auxin signalling (Ohto *et al.*, 2006), while HPA1 and VEN3 are involved in amino acid biosynthesis pathways (histidine and arginine respectively) (Ingle, 2011; Mollá-Morales *et al.*, 2011). FKB16-4, is part of the FK506-binding protein gene family (Gollan *et al.*, 2012), a family of genes known to affect plant growth through auxin signalling and interactions with sugar sensors (Gollan *et al.*, 2012). Although this particular gene has yet to be described using mutants, due to the nature of the family of genes it could well play a role in plant growth and so is an interesting target for future investigation. These results suggest differential transcriptomic regulation of growth and development occurring within NADP-ME species, but the question that still remains is why?

One possible explanation is a redistribution of resources within NADP-ME species. Arginine has been previously linked to a low CO<sub>2</sub> response, possibly acting towards an increased sink for photorespired

nitrogen (Blume *et al.*, 2019), while histidine proteins are involved in the sensing of abiotic stress (Singh *et al.*, 2015). As the NADP-ME subtype is known to contain the lowest amount of leaf nitrogen (Ghannoum *et al.*, 2005), it is possible less is available for processes such as photorespiration. Hence, the upregulation of these amino acids could be necessary to mitigate the negative effects of reduced  $CO_2$  concentrations, thus limiting resources available for growth. This could elicit - or be elicited by - changes in transcript abundance for genes related to development and signalling, such as those identified here.

Another possibility lies within the DE transcripts identified within the NAD-ME and PEPCK subtypes. We identified two subtype-specific DE transcripts known to be linked to stomatal aperture control and water use efficiency. One of these (conserved only in the NAD-ME species) was  $\beta$ CA1, a gene known to sense CO<sub>2</sub> and influence stomatal aperture in both C<sub>3</sub> (Hu *et al.*, 2010) and C<sub>4</sub> plants (Kolbe *et al.*, 2018). The other (conserved only in the PEPCK species), SASP, also plays a role in stomatal aperture control with mutants exhibiting increased drought tolerance (Wang *et al.*, 2018). Mutants of  $\beta$ CA1 previously highlighted *Z. mays'* sensitivity to vapour pressure deficit (VPD) over intracellular CO<sub>2</sub> concentrations (Ci), and further, unoptimized water use efficiency (WUE) (Kolbe *et al.*, 2018). Variation in aperture control ties in with the increased conductance noted within NADP-ME species both previously (Pinto *et al.*, 2014) and here. Therefore, we suggest varied transcriptomic regulation of stomatal aperture, as well as variable sensitivity to Ci and VPD, could be linked to the variations seen in conductance across the C<sub>4</sub> subtypes, and hence susceptibility to glacial CO<sub>2</sub>.

### Subtype-specific transcriptomic responses to low light

Although the phenotypic and physiological responses to low light were conserved across species, we did identify evidence of subtype-specific transcriptomic responses (Fig. 5). Again, PEPCK species showed increased plasticity within metabolic processes. As it occurred under both conditions, we suggest there is increased regulation within the metabolism of PEPCK species, which is likely linked to the PEPCK pathway and provides interesting insight into the flexibility of the subtype. The characteristics of PEPCK decarboxylation within the cytosol and the possible requirement for additional decarboxylation pathways (Wang *et al.*, 2014) provides a greater number of cross points with various cellular metabolic processes including glycolysis, possibly with down/upstream effects on other metabolic processes, hence regulation may be intertwined with the PEPCK carbon concentrating mechanism.

An increased enrichment in oxidoreductase activity was noted within the down-regulated DE transcripts in NADP-ME species only. Oxidoreductase activity is often linked with the cyclic electron flow (CEF) around photosystem I (Takabayashi *et al.*, 2005), and NADP-ME species have been reported to utilise the highest amount of CEF (Hernández-Prieto *et al.*, 2019). Plasticity within CEF could have implications in the ability to acclimate to low light, and the subtype specific nature of this plasticity could have implications in the lower susceptibility of NADP-ME species to shade that has previously been reported (Sonawane *et al.*, 2018). Within NAD-ME species an increased enrichment in categories related to starvation was noted within the down-regulated DE transcripts. This was unexpected as NAD-ME species have been reported to have a higher susceptibility to low light (as well as data here reflecting this) (Sonawane *et al.*, 2018). We suggest this is linked to curtailed growth in NAD-ME species under low light, limiting the starvation response. These subtype specific signals within the transcriptomic response again highlight fine tuning that is tied to subtype.

### Conclusion

Utilising six  $C_4$  species, covering three independent origins of  $C_4$  photosynthesis and all three subtypes, we sought to elucidate the extent to which  $C_4$  subtype can influence phenomic and transcriptomic responses to abiotic stress. Only NADP-ME species were susceptible to glacial  $CO_2$ , whereas low light was uniformly detrimental to all subtypes. Species was the major driver of transcriptomic response to both glacial  $CO_2$ and low light. There was, however, evidence of a conserved functional response to low light. Sub-sets of differentially expressed transcripts were conserved within specific subtypes in response to both abiotic stresses suggesting subtype does play finer role in shaping transcriptomic response. Further to this, within these subsets we identify transcripts related to allocation (changes in amino acid biosynthesis) and acquisition (stomatal aperture control) of resources and hypothesise their role in the susceptibility of NADP-ME species to glacial  $CO_2$ . We identified heightened plasticity within transcripts related to metabolism within PEPCK species which we suggest is linked to the characteristics of PEPCK decarboxylation. Although limited to two species per subtype, these results highlight the influence subtype can have on acclimation within  $C_4$  grasses, as well as highlighting avenues which can be explored in attempts to improve  $C_4$  crop productivity.

### **Materials and Methods**

### Growth Conditions

Six grass species were grown in three controlled environment chambers (BioChambers walk-in growth cabinets) at the Hawkesbury Institute for the Environment, Western Sydney University; *Zea mays* (NADP-ME), *Setaria italica* (NADP-ME), *Panicum miliaceum* (NAD-ME), *Leptochloa fusca* (NAD-ME), *Megathyrsus* 

maximus (PEPCK) and Chloris gayana (PEPCK). Seeds were potted in 4 litre pots (n= 18) containing seed raising and cutting mix (Scotts, Australia) with commercial slow release plant fertiliser (Flowers, Fruit and Vegies, Hortico) and commercial liquid fertilizer (General Purpose, Thrive Professional) added as recommended by the manufacturer. Seeds were germinated in a control chamber at a CO<sub>2</sub> concentration of 400 ppm and light intensity of 1000  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> at plant height. Once seedlings were established (between 4-10 days depending on the species) seedlings were thinned and then equally split between the control chamber and two other chambers set at either 180 ppm of CO<sub>2</sub> and 1000 µmol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> of light (subambient  $CO_2$ ; referred to in this paper as glacial  $CO_2$  due to the  $CO_2$  concentration being in-line with those observed at the peak of the last glacial period (Sigman & Boyle, 2000)) or 400 ppm of  $CO_2$  and 200  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> of light (low light). Light levels were controlled through changing the intensity of the halogen lights within the chamber and CO<sub>2</sub> levels were controlled via a Li-COR LI-820 CO<sub>2</sub> gas analyser with an inbuilt CO<sub>2</sub> scrub. Each chamber temperature was set to 28°C (day) and 22°C (night) with 14-hour days. Plants were initially distributed via a block design and then randomised every 4 days both within and across blocks. Conditions and plants were switched between chambers periodically throughout growth to reduce any chamber effect. Long term acclimations were assessed as this has previously been shown to efficiently separate the  $C_4$  subtypes within these conditions (Pinto *et al.*, 2014; Sonawane *et al.*, 2018).

# Gas exchange

Gas exchange measurements (n=4) were taken using a Li-COR 6400XT portable photosynthesis system 26-30 days after establishment (between 10:00 and 14:00) on each young fully expanded leaf. Measurements of assimilation and conductance were made at growth CO<sub>2</sub> concentration (180 or 400 ppm) and light intensity (200 or 1000  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>), with a leaf temperature of 28°C. Before each measurement, the leaf was allowed to stabilize for 5-10 minutes until it reached a steady state of CO<sub>2</sub> uptake. The response of CO<sub>2</sub> assimilation rate to step increases of intracellular CO<sub>2</sub> (A/Ci) was measured on the same leaf at a light intensity of 1000  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> and a leaf temperature of 28°C (Supplementary Figure S3). 1000  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> was used for all A/Ci measurements to give a standardised light condition across curves which was relative to the growing conditions. From each A/Ci curve maximal assimilation (A<sub>max</sub>) was derived from the plateau of the curve.

### Harvest

At 28-32 days after establishment plants underwent a full harvest (n=4). Eight leaf discs of a known size (0.5-1 cm<sup>2</sup>) were harvested between 10:00 and 14:00 from the same leaf as used for the gas exchange measurements (no later than two days after said measurements), snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and

stored at -80°C until ready for enzyme and RNA extractions. Discs were cut from the central region of each leaf to avoid tip to base variation, avoiding the leaf midrib. The day after this a full harvest commenced which included: Above ground biomass (g) - all material above soil level was harvested, dried at 70°C for two days then weighed. Total leaf area (cm<sup>2</sup>) – all leaves were harvested and run through a leaf area meter (LI-COR LI-3100C) to calculate total leaf area. Specific leaf area (g/cm<sup>2</sup>) – leaf discs of a known area were dried at 70°C for two days then weighed. The ratio of area to weight was then calculated by dividing the oven dried weight (g) by the area of the leaf disc (cm<sup>2</sup>). The harvest order was randomised across treatments to avoid harvest time becoming a confounding variable.

# Enzyme activity and Content

Soluble leaf protein was extracted from several of the harvested leaf discs in ice cold mortar and pestles using buffers as described in Sharwood *et al.* (2016b) (for PEPC, NADP-ME and PEPCK) and Setién *et al.* (2014) (for NAD-ME), with the addition of 1% Glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.1% Triton (Chem-Supply) to aid in cell lysis (n=3-4). Each disc was extracted immediately after removal from liquid N<sub>2</sub> in 1ml of extraction buffer.

Samples were pulse centrifuged at room temperature and then 10-15µl (depending on enzyme assay) of soluble protein was added to assay buffers as described in Sharwood *et al.* (2016b) for PEPC, NADP-ME and PEPCK, or Setién *et al.* (2014) for NAD-ME. 15mM aspartic acid was also included in the PEPCK assay to remove any excess PEPC background activity. After the addition of the soluble protein, all reactions were left for up to one minute in the light spectrometer to identify any background (which was removed from the final activity). Activators were then added as described in Sharwood *et al.* (2016b) or Setién *et al.* (2014) and activity measured at 25°C using an NADH-coupled enzyme assay where the rate of NADH oxidation or reduction is monitored at 340 nm using a diode array spectrophotometer (Agilent 8453). Assays were performed in 1 ml cuvettes with a total volume of 0.5ml.

Rubisco content was estimated (n=3) using [<sup>14</sup>C] CABP (2-C-carboxyarabinitol 1,5-bisphosphate) binding as described in Sharwood *et al.* (2008). Rubisco was extracted using the same protein extraction protocol as for NADP-ME and PEPC, and 100 $\mu$ l of the soluble protein was used to assay rubisco content.

# **RNA-Sequencing**

Two of the remaining leaf discs were ground into a fine powder using a TissueLyser (Quiagen) with -80°C frozen clamps in a 2mL tube with the aid of metal ball bearings (n=3). Total RNA was extracted using PureZol (BioRad) following the method described in Sharwood *et al.* (2011). Aliquots of RNA were treated

with DNase I (AMBION) following the manufacturer's protocols before library preparation using a TruSeq Stranded RNA HT Kit for Plants (Illumina).

Strand specific RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) was carried out on the extracted RNA using an Illumina Hi-Seq 2500 machine with 125 base pair paired-end sequencing at the Western Sydney University NGS Facility. Raw data can be found on GenBank under BioProject ascension PRJNA508442. Between 25 and 35 million reads were returned per sample. Raw reads were processed using Trimmomatic (Bolger et al., 2014) and Blue (Greenfield et al., 2014) to both trim adapters and reduce sequencing errors. For non-model species, reads were assembled via de novo assembly through Trinity (Haas et al., 2013). This gave a set of nonredundant contiguous sequences (contigs) for each species to be used as a reference transcriptome. Summary statistics for each Trinity produced transcriptome can be found in Supplementary Table S2. For the model species Z. mays and S. italica, coding sequences predicted from published genomes were utilised (Schnable et al., 2009; Bennetzen et al., 2012). Reads were aligned to each transcriptome using the quasi-align mode within Salmon (Patro et al., 2017). This returned a relative transcript abundance for each transcript (transcripts per million; TPM) for each species. Transcripts were then hierarchically clustered based on expression and shared reads using Corset (Davidson & Oshlack, 2014) to produce clusters of reads ready for statistical testing. A summary of the mapping and clustering results can be found in Supplementary Table S2. DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014) was then used to identify significantly differentially expressed (DE) transcripts (with raw counts of each cluster from Corset as the input) with a minimum cut-off of 10 counts in at least three replicates.

### Annotation to the Z. mays Proteome and Functional Annotation

All contigs were annotated to the *Z. mays* proteome so inter-species comparisons could be made. Known *S. italica* proteins were annotated via BLASTp (McGinnis & Madden, 2004) to the *Z. mays* proteome. Candidate coding regions for transcriptomes assembled via *de novo* assembly were identified using Transdecoder (Haas *et al.*, 2013), and then all candidates were annotated to the *Z. mays* proteome via BLASTp. Once annotations were retrieved the *aggregate* function within R was used to identify the consensus annotation for each transcript and this was used as the annotation for each. Due to the issues of working with non-model organisms we had to ensure automated assemblies and annotations were correct. Therefore for select transcripts, we performed manual adjustments (i.e. curation) using multiple sequence alignments and phylogenetic analyses as implemented within Geneious (Kearse *et al.*, 2012). Orthologues within *Arabidopsis thaliana* were also identified for significantly differentially expressed

# Gene Ontology Enrichment

To assess functional enrichment of the DE transcripts, over-representation within gene ontology (GO) categories was assessed using agriGO v2.0 (Tian *et al.*, 2017). Singular enrichment analysis was used on each list of DE transcripts (up and down-regulated for each species) using the locus ID v3.30 (Gramene Release 50) as a reference for GO representation, a Fisher's statistical test, a Yekutieli adjustment for multiple testing, and an adjusted P-value cut off  $\leq$  0.05.

# Statistics

To assess the effect of glacial CO<sub>2</sub> and low light on each species a one-way ANOVA was utilised within R (R CoreTeam, 2013). To assess the effect on each C<sub>4</sub> subtype a linear mixed model was used, again within R, similar to Sonawane *et al.* (2018). Briefly, both subtype and treatment were set as fixed effects and species was nested inside subtype as a random effect and ran using the *lmer* function. The model was run separately for each subtype. The *Anova* function was then used within R to assign P-values to the output of the linear mixed model. Before either test was run, each variable was tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilks test and homogeneity of variance using Levene's test, then if necessary, transformed using the *log* function in R. If there was a significant interaction in either test ( $P \le 0.05$ ), a Tukey post-hoc test was used to indicate which groups differed significantly.
