# **Summary**

 Brassinosteroids (BRs) and abscisic acid (ABA) are plant hormones that antagonistically regulate many aspects of plant growth and development, however the mechanisms that regulate the crosstalk of these two hormones are still not well understood. BRs regulate plant growth and development by activating BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT 1 (BZR1) family transcription factors. Here we show that the crosstalk between BRs and ABA signaling is partially mediated by BZR1 regulated gene expression. *bzr1-1D* is a dominant mutant with enhanced BR signaling, our results showed that *bzr1-1D* mutant is less sensitive to ABA-inhibited primary root growth. By RNA sequencing, a subset of BZR1 regulated ABA-responsive root genes were identified. Of these genes, the expression of a major ABA signaling component *ABA INSENSITIVE 5* (*ABI5*) was found to be suppressed by BR and by BZR1. Additional evidences showed that BZR1 could bind strongly with several G-box *cis*-elements in the promoter of *ABI5*, suppress the expression of *ABI5*, and make plants less sensitive to ABA. Our study demonstrated that *ABI5* is a direct target gene of BZR1, and modulation the expression of *ABI5* by BZR1 plays important roles in regulating the crosstalk between the BR and ABA signaling pathways.

## **Introduction**

During their lifespan, plants are constantly challenged by biotic and abiotic stresses in their environment. These stresses are integrated into intrinsic hormone signaling networks that regulate plant growth and development. Production of the plant hormone abscisic acid (ABA) increases quickly under stressful conditions. The binding of ABA to members of the PYRABACTIN RESISTANCE1 (PYR1)/PYR1-LIKE (PYL)/REGULATORY COMPONENTS OF ABA RECEPTOR (RCAR) family of soluble receptors promotes the formation of a complex comprised of ABA-bound PYR/PYLs and PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 2C (PP2C). This breaks the inhibition of SNF1-RELATED PROTEIN KINASE 2s (SnRK2s) by PP2C. The activated SnRK2s are then able to phosphorylate and activate downstream ABA RESPONSE ELEMENT-BINDING FACTORs (ABFs); this sequence of events regulates gene expression and helps plants adapt to unfavorable environments (Lee and Luan 2012). Several studies have shown that additional plant hormones, including auxin, ethylene, cytokinin, gibberellins, and brassinosteroids (BRs), coordinate with ABA to regulate lateral root development, seed germination, cotyledon greening, root growth, and stomatal closure (Beaudoin et al. 2000; Brady et al. 2003; Debeaujon and Koornneef 2000; Guan et al. 2014; Rock and Sun 2005; Steber and McCourt 2001); however, the molecular mechanisms underlying the crosstalk among these hormonal signaling pathways are still not well understood.

As growth-promoting plant hormones, BRs regulate a broad range of developmental processes. It has been known for many years that BRs and ABA antagonistically regulate certain plant responses. For example, mutants deficient in BR biosynthesis or signaling are hypersensitive to ABA-inhibited primary root growth, seed germination, and stomatal closure, while plants showing enhanced BR signaling activity show increased resistance to ABA (Cai et al. 2014; Ryu et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2009). BR signaling is initiated by hormone binding to the membrane receptor BRASSINOSTEROID INSENSITIVE 1 (BRI1) and its co-receptor BRI1 ASSOCIATED RECEPTOR KINASE 1 (BAK1) (Li and Chory 1997; Santiago et al. 2013; Sun et al. 2013). This binding stimulates BRI1, which then activates BR SIGNALING

KINASE 1 (BSK1) and *bri1*-SUPPRESSOR 1 (BSU1), possibly by sequential phosphorylation (Kim et al. 2009, 2011; Tang et al. 2008). BSU1 then dephosphorylates and inactivates BRASSINOSTEROID INSENSITIVE 2 (BIN2) (Kim et al. 2009), allowing BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT 1 (BZR1)/*bri1*-EMS- SUPPRESSOR 1 (BES1) family transcription factors to be dephosphorylated by PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 2A (PP2A) and altering the expression of BR-responsive genes (He et al. 2005; Tang et al. 2011; Yin et al. 2005).

Recently, it was discovered that BIN2 can phosphorylate SnRK2 kinases, and that this phosphorylation is critical for SnRK2 kinase activation (Cai et al. 2014). By mass spectrometry, Cai et al. (2014) identified a number of BIN2 phosphorylation sites on SnRK2.3. Among these sites, replacing Thr180 with Ala not only greatly reduced the *in vitro* phosphorylation of SnRK2.3 by BIN2, it also abolished the ability of SnRK2.3 to phosphorylate its substrate, demonstrating that SnRK2.3 phosphorylation at Thr180 plays an important role in the BIN2-mediated activation of ABA signaling. Meanwhile, Hu and Yu (2014) found that BIN2 could also regulate ABA signaling by directly phosphorylating another major ABA signaling component, ABA INSENSITIVE 5 (ABI5). The phosphorylation of ABI5 by BIN2 stabilizes ABI5 protein and is required for proper ABA responses during seed germination. ABI5 is a critical mediator of the crosstalk between the ABA and BR signaling pathways. Ryu et al. (2014) demonstrated that BES1 attenuates the expression of *ABI5* via a TOPLESS (TPL)/HISTONE DEACETYLASE 19 (HDA19)/ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE 3 (ABI3) regulatory module. They showed that BES1 forms a transcriptional repressor complex with TPL/HDA19 that induces the histone deacetylation of *ABI3* chromatin; this suppresses the production of ABI3, which promotes *ABI5* expression. Because BES1 is a substrate of BIN2, the phosphorylation of BES1 by BIN2 prevents BES1 from binding to DNA (Vert and Chory 2006). The interplay of BIN2, BES1, ABI5, and SnRK2s in regulating ABA signaling suggests that BR and ABA signaling crosstalk is complicated and occurs at multiple levels.

To expand our knowledge of the crosstalk between the BR and ABA signaling pathways and its effect on plant growth regulation, we examined ABA-inhibited primary root growth in various BR signaling-deficient and gain-of-function mutants. Our results support that plant responses to ABA are negatively regulated by BR signaling. Through RNA sequencing, we identified subsets of ABA-controlled genes in root tissue that are regulated by BIN2 or BZR1. Revealing the roles of these genes in ABA signaling will further our understanding of the crosstalk between ABA- and BR-regulated plant responses. Meanwhile, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) followed by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) and electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) demonstrated that BZR1 suppresses *ABI5* expression by binding directly to G-box *cis*-elements in the *ABI5* promoter, and that this contributes to the negative regulation of plant ABA responses by BZR1. Given the high level of identity between the G-boxes in the *ABI5* promoter and an ABA-responsive element (ABRE) found in many ABA-regulated genes, G-box binding by BZR1 provides new insight into the crosstalk between BR- and ABA-regulated gene expression.

# **Materials and Methods**

#### **Plant materials and root growth assay**

The *Arabidopsis bri1-301*, *bsk3bsk4bsk5bsk6* quadruple knockout (*bsk3456*), *bin2-1*, *bzr1-1D*, *abi1-1C*, *snrk2.2/2.3* double mutant, and *pyr12458* pentuple mutants were produced in the Columbia (Col) background, while *bsu1-D* and *bin2bil1bil2* were produced in the Wassilewskija background. To assay primary root growth inhibition in the presence of ABA, seeds were surface-sterilized using 75% ethanol and 0.1% Triton X-100 for 9 min, followed by three quick rinses with 100% ethanol. The seeds were sown on agar plates containing 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) basal salts (PhytoTechnology Laboratories, Overland Park, KS), 1% sucrose, and 0.3% Phytagel (PhytoTechnology Laboratories). The pH of the medium was adjusted to 5.7 with KOH before autoclaving. The seeds were stratified at 4°C for 6 days, and then grown in a growth chamber (Percival Scientific, Perry, IA) at 22°C for 4 days under long-day (16 h of light/8 h of dark) conditions. The seedlings were then transferred to fresh 1/2 MS agar plates supplied with or without ABA (Solarbio Science and Technology Co.,

Ltd., Beijing, China). The positions of the transferred plant root tips were marked using a permanent marker, and the plants were allowed to continue growing under the same conditions for 4 days before taking photos for root length measurement. The lengths of the newly grown primary roots on the transferred plates were measured using Image J. Relative root growth was calculated by comparing seedlings grown on ABA-containing medium with those grown on ABA-free medium. The root assays were repeated at least three times (15–20 plants per experiment) with similar results; representative data from one repetition are shown in the figures.

#### **RNA-Seq library preparation and sequencing**

Seedlings were grown vertically on 1/2 MS plates in a growth chamber (Percival Scientific) for 7 days and then treated with or without 100 μM ABA for 3 h. Segments of the roots (about 1 cm from the tip) were then excised and collected for RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and the concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA). mRNA sequencing libraries were constructed with barcodes using a TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Six barcoded libraries were pooled together and sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq2000 sequencing system. The sequence reads were mapped to the *Arabidopsis* genome using TopHat software. Differential gene expression was analyzed using Cufflink software. Differentially expressed genes were defined by a 1.5-fold expression difference and a P-value <0.05.

#### **Real-time qRT-PCR analysis**

Total RNA was extracted from 7-day-old *Arabidopsis* roots treated with or without ABA using Trizol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Approximately 700 ng of total RNA were used for first-strand cDNA synthesis with M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Takara Bio Inc., Otsu, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed following a standard protocol on an ABI PRISM 7500 Real Time machine using the SYBR Premix Ex TaqTM system (Takara Bio Inc.). Gene specific primer sets used are: 5'-ttcgcggtgttctactgcc-3' and 5'-cggaaccgcgtagacgg-3' for *RD22*; 5'-atggcgtcttaccagaaccgt-3' and 5'-ccagatccggagcggtgaagc-3' for *RAB18*;

5'-gtccaaagttactgatcccac-3' and 5'-cttcatatcaaaatcatgact-3' for *RD29A*; 5'-ggcgggcaaagcgag-3' and 5'-tgcccgtaagcagtaacagatc-3' for *RD29B*; 5'-gctggcaaagctgaggagaa-3' and 5'-ttcccgcctgttgtgctc-3' for *KIN1*; 5'-tttatggtgctatagatcaca-3' and 5'-gaatgtcctgatgggtgtaaac-3' for *P5CS1*; 5'-gtggtttacgacaagaacaaggt-3' and 5'-ctatcacgacgacttcatctgtttc-3' for *NCEB3*; 5'-ggagaatgataaagaaccgagaatcag-3' and 5'-catgatccttgcctgctttcg-3' for *AREB1*; 5'-cgaggacgaagaagcaatacag-3' and 5'-tccaccatttcagcctgtttt-3' for *AREB2*; 5'-cagtggagaaagtagtggagagaag-3' and 5'-actctcaaaatactgttgcttcctc-3' for *ABI5*; 5'-ttctcgctccagtaatgggatccga-3' and 5'-gttctccacaaccgcttggtcgact-3' for *PP2A*. The expression level of different target genes was normalized by *PP2A.* The expression level of different target genes was normalized against that of *PP2A*. The relative expression level of each gene is presented as a ratio to the expression level in wild-type plants grown in the absence of ABA. The average ratio and standard error (SE) were calculated based on the values obtained from at least three biological repeats.

# **ChIP assay**

ChIP was performed according to Gendrel et al. (2005) with a slight modification. In brief, *pBZR1::BZR1-myc* transgenic or wild-type control plants were grown side by side in 1/2 MS liquid medium for 7 days and processed in parallel to minimize sample-to-sample variation. The seedlings were treated with 100 nM brassinolide for 1 h, frozen, and then ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. The powder was dissolved in buffer M1 (10 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1 M hexylene glycol, and proteinase inhibitor cocktail), and formaldehyde was added to the mixture to a final concentration of 1% (v/v). All subsequent steps were performed at 4°C. Crosslinking was performed on ice for 10 min; the reaction was stopped by adding glycine (final concentration, 0.125 M) and letting the tube stand for 5 min. The solution was filtered through two layers of Miracloth (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA) and centrifuged at 10,000 x *g* for 10 min. The pellet was washed three times with buffer M2 (10 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1 M hexylene glycol, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.5% Triton X-100, and proteinase inhibitor cocktail) and once with buffer M3 (10 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and proteinase inhibitor cocktail) by pipetting for 1 min. Following each wash, the pellet was collected by centrifugation at 10,000 x *g* for 10 min. After washing, the pellet was resuspended and sonicated in nuclei lysis buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 20 mM EDTA, 400 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, and proteinase inhibitor cocktail). Following centrifugation at 10,000 x *g* for 10 min, the supernatant was saved for immunoprecipitation with anti-Myc antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). The ChIP-enriched DNA was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR using the 7500 Real Time PCR system and SYBR Premix Ex TaqTM (Takara Bio Inc.). Primers sets used for qRT-PCR are: 5'-tgacatcgtcttctttgctgcttct-3' and 5'-tgccttcttgagctttaactcttcc-3' for *CNX5*; 5'-atgggaaaagggtgggctcacg-3' and 5'-gtctacgggtttgactgtccagttcg-3' for *DWF4*; 5'-taggtcgctggttcgattc-3' and 5'- gattaatttgattgatcaatactgattact-3' for *ABI5* promoter fragment P1; 5'-tccaaatttcacgttcttattatcttatatatg-3' and 5'-atggaatgctaacttagacaggtc-3' for *ABI5* promoter fragment P2

#### **Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assays**

EMSAs were performed using recombinant MBP-BZR1 protein purified from *Escherichia coli* and biotin-labeled double-stranded oligonucleotide probes containing G-box or E-box sequences from the *ABI5* promoter. Probe binding was performed using a LightShiftTM EMSA Optimization and Control Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts). The reactions were incubated on ice for 20 min and then loaded onto a 6% native polyacrylamide gel in TBE buffer (45 mM Tris, 45 mM boric acid, and 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.3). The gel was transferred to a HybondTM -N <sup>+</sup> membrane (GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, United Kingdom) and crosslinked at 120 mJ/cm<sup>2</sup> for 45–60 s using a UV crosslinker. The biotin-labeled probes were detected using a Chemiluminescent Nucleic Acid Detection Module Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific).

## **Results**

#### **BZR1 mediates BR-inhibited ABA signaling in** *Arabidopsis*

To gain insight into the mechanism of crosstalk between BR and ABA signaling, a number of BR signaling mutants were tested for their response to exogenously applied ABA. As shown in Fig. 1a and Fig. S1, compared with wild-type, those mutants with reduced BR signaling (*bri1-301*, *bri1-116*, *bsk3456* quadruple knockout, and *bin2-1*) showed increased sensitivity to ABA-inhibited primary root growth as young seedlings. In contrast, those mutants with enhanced BR signaling (*bsu1-1D*, *bin2bil1bil2* triple knockout, and *bzr1-1D*) showed reduced sensitivity to ABA treatment in the same root growth assay (Fig. 1b). In comparison, the ABA insensitivity was observed stronger in the *bin2bil1bil2* triple knockout mutant, but at a similar level in the *bzr1-1D* and *bsu1-1D* mutants, suggesting that BIN2 plays a major role in mediating the crosstalk between BR and ABA signaling.

Because it was previously reported that *bzr1-1D* mutant plants is hypersensitivity to ABA-inhibited root growth (Rodrigues et al. 2009; Ryu et al. 2014), to clarify the discrepancy, *mx3* transgenic plants, in which a *pBZR1::bzr1-1D-CFP* expression vector was transformed into wild-type Col plants (Huang et al. 2012), were used for ABA sensitivity test. Similar to *bzr1-1D* mutant plants, the *mx3* transgenic plants showed reduced sensitivity to ABA-inhibited root growth (Fig. 1c).

As a transcription factor, BZR1 functions downstream of BIN2 in the BR signaling pathway. To test whether BZR1-mediated gene expression plays an important role in mediating the crosstalk between ABA and BR signaling, *bzr1-1D/bin2-1* double mutant and transgenic *bin2-1* mutant plants expressing a *pBZR1::bzr1-1D-CFP* vector (*mi1/bin2-1*) were examined for root elongation in the presence of ABA. Both the *bzr1-1D* mutation and *pBZR1::bzr1-1D-CFP* transgene were able to significantly rescue the hypersensitive phenotype of the *bin2-1* mutant in terms of ABA-inhibited primary root growth (Fig. 2a and 2b). Similar results were also obtained in ABA-inhibited seed germination assay (Fig. 2c). Taken together, our results suggest that the inhibitory effect of BRs on ABA signaling is partially mediated by BZR1-regulated gene expression.

#### **RNA-Seq supports BIN2 as an important regulator of ABA signaling**

The hypersensitivity of the *bin2-1* mutant and insensitivity of the *bin2bil1bil2* triple mutant to ABA treatment suggested that BIN2 is important for the crosstalk between BR and ABA signaling in *Arabidopsis*. However, our analysis of *bzr1-1D*, *bin2-1*, and *bzr1-1D/bin2-1* mutant plants revealed that BZR1 might also participate in regulating BR-inhibited ABA responses in roots. To gain insight into the BIN2- and BZR1-mediated crosstalk between BR and ABA signaling, RNA-Seq was used to analyze the effect of the *bin2-1* and *bzr1-1D* mutations on ABA-induced gene expression changes in root tissue. Using a 1.5-fold change as the cut-off value, RNA-Seq analysis identified 3783 genes whose expression level was affected by 3 h of treatment with 100 μM ABA in wild-type roots, and 7355 genes whose expression was altered in *bin2-1* mutant roots compared with ABA-untreated wild-type plants (Table. S1). Among these genes, 768 (31.43%) genes that were up-regulated by ABA were also up-regulated in the *bin2-1* mutant, and 715 (53.36%) genes that were down-regulated by ABA were also down-regulated in the *bin2-1* mutant (Fig. 3a). Overall, 1483 out of 3783 (39.20%) ABA-regulated genes in root tissue showed similar patterns of regulation in the *bin2-1* mutant, supporting the hypothesis that BIN2 is an important regulator of ABA signaling.

To validate our RNA-Seq data, qRT-PCR was performed on nine genes (*RD22*, *RAB18*, *RD29A*, *RD29B*, *KIN1*, *P5CS1*, *NCED3*, *AREB1*, and *AREB2*) that were previously reported to be ABA-regulated marker genes (Fujita et al. 2005, 2009; Fujii et al. 2007, 2009; Gonzalez-Guzman et al. 2012). Consistent with our RNA-Seq results, all of the selected genes were up-regulated by ABA treatment in root tissue; similar to our sequencing result (Table. S1), only the expression of *NCED3*, *RD22*, and *P5CS1* was found to be up-regulated significantly in the *bin2-1* mutant (Fig. 3b and c). Together, these data indicate that our RNA-Seq data are reliable.

It has been reported that BIN2 enhances ABA signaling via the direct phosphorylation and activation of SnRK2s (Cai et al. 2014). To investigate whether BIN2 and SnRK2s regulate plant responses to ABA by regulating the expression of similar groups of genes, we compared our list of ABA- and *bin2-1*-regulated genes with a previously reported list of *snrk2.2/2.3/2.6*-dependent ABA-regulated genes (Gonzalez-Guzman et al. 2012). Of 1223 SnRK2s-regulated genes listed in the online supportive materials from Gonzalez-Guzman et al. (2012), 788 (64.4%) were also up-regulated by ABA in root tissue according to our list. This overlap rate is reasonably high if we consider that different tissues were used in the experiments. In contrast, 228 (18.6%) SnRK2s-regulated genes were both up-regulated by ABA and the *bin2-1* mutation (Fig. 3a and Table S1). Notably, only 10 out of 715 (1.39%) ABA- and *bin2-1*-down-regulated genes in root tissue were found to be directly regulated by *SnRK2.2/2.3/2.6.*

Given the importance of BIN2 in activating SnRK2s and regulating ABA signaling, the low rate of overlap between the *bin2-1* mutant- and SnRK2s-regulated ABA-responsive genes is interesting. Because the RNA used for sequencing was extracted from *bin2-1* mutant plants grown in the absence of ABA, it is possible that modulation of the ABA signaling pathway by BIN2 requires the activation of SnRK2s by ABA. To test this hypothesis, we crossed *bin2-1* mutant plants with various ABA signaling mutants to generate *pyr12458/bin2-1* hextuple, *abi1-1c/bin2-1* double, and *snrk2.2/2.3/bin2-1* triple mutant plants. Root growth assays showed that the simultaneous knock out of *PYR1*, *PYL2*, *PYL4*, *PYL5*, and *PYL8* could only partially recover the ABA hypersensitivity of the *bin2-1* mutant, possibly due to the functional redundancy of additional ABA receptors in root tissue. Meanwhile, the *abi1-1c/bin2-1* double and *snrk2.2/2.3/bin2-1* triple mutants were insensitive to ABA-inhibited root growth to an extent that was similar to that in the *abi1-1c* and *snrk2.2/snrk2.3* mutants (Fig. 3d and 3e). Taken together, these results suggested that the regulation of plant ABA responses by BIN2 requires the ABA-initiated inactivation of PP2C by PYR.

#### **The reduced ABA sensitivity of the** *bzr1-1D* **mutant is partially mediated by ABI5**

RNA-Seq also found that 3336 root genes were differentially regulated between the *bzr1-1D/bin2-1* double mutant and *bin2-1* mutant (Fig. 4a and 4b). Among these genes, *DWF4*, *CPD*, and *BR6OX2*, well-known targets of BZR1 that are feedback down-regulated by BRs, were found to be down-regulated in *bzr1-1D/bin2-1* mutant plants (Table. S1). Of these *bzr1-1D*-regulated genes, 1603 (48.05%) genes were found to be oppositely regulated in the *bin2-1* mutant. Compared with the amount of overlap between the *bin2-1* and ABA-regulated transcriptomic profiles (39.2%), only 678 out of 3783 (17.94%) ABA-regulated genes in root tissue were opposite regulated by *bzr1-1D*. This finding is consistent with the results of our root growth assays and supports the notion that BIN2 plays a major role in regulating crosstalk between the BR and ABA signaling pathways. In total, 407 out of 678 (60.03%) *bzr1-1D*-regulated ABA responding genes were found to be oppositely regulated by ABA treatment or the *bin2-1* mutation, suggesting that these genes contribute to the reduced ABA sensitivity caused by the *bzr1-1D* mutation in the *bin2-1* mutant (Fig. 4b and 4c). In comparison, 86 ABA- and SnRK2s-regulated genes (7%) were found to be regulated by the *bzr1-1D* mutant, suggesting that BZR1-regulated responses to ABA are not induced directly through SnRK2s-mediated ABA signaling.

Among the genes that were oppositely regulated by the *bzr1-1D* mutation, the ABA signaling-related gene *ABI5* was chosen for further study. qRT-PCR confirmed our RNA-Seq results and showed the expression of *ABI5* was indeed up-regulated by ABA treatment and the *bin2-1* mutation, but down-regulated in the *bzr1-1D* and *bzr1-1D/bin2-1* mutants (Fig. 4d and 4e). Consistently, the *ABI5* transcripts level in wild-type seedlings is decreased by BR

treatment (Fig. 4f). Furthermore, we crossed a *35S::ABI5* transgenic plant with *bzr1-1D* mutant and analyzed the ABA response of *35S::ABI5*/*bzr1-1D* double homozygous mutant plants. The root growth assay revealed that *ABI5* over-expression suppressed the ABA-insensitive phenotype of *bzr1-1D* (Fig. 4g). Taken together, our results suggest that BZR1-suppressed *ABI5* expression contributes to the ABA insensitivity of *bzr1-1D* mutant plants.

# *ABI5* **is a direct target of BZR1**

It has been reported that the BR-inhibited expression of *ABI5* is mediated by another ABA signaling component, ABI3. ABI3 up-regulates the expression of *ABI5*, but the expression of *ABI3* is suppressed by a BES1-TPL/HDA19 co-suppressor complex (Ryu et al. 2014). However, when analyze our root transcriptomic profiles, we found that the expression of *ABI3* was not significantly altered under any of the conditions we tested (Table. S1).

To determine how BZR1 regulates the expression of *ABI5*, we examined the *ABI5* promoter and found four G-box (CACGTG) and three E-box (CANNTG) regulatory *cis*-elements located ~800 bp upstream of the *ABI5* 5'-untranslated region. As it has been reported that G-box and E-box elements are highly enriched in BZR1-targeted genes, and given that the G-box contains two inverted repeats of the BZR1-targeted BR response element (BRRE, CGTG<sup>T</sup> /CG) core sequence, CGTG (Sun et al. 2010), we examined whether *ABI5* is a direct target of BZR1. A ChIP assay was first carried out using *pBZR1::BZR1-myc* transgenic plants. qPCR indicated that, besides the enrichment of the well-known BZR1-targeted *DWF4* promoter sequence, two G-box- and E-box-containing fragments from the *ABI5* promoter were significantly enriched, by 2.5 and 3.5 fold, in the *pBZR1::BZR1-myc* samples compared to the wild-type control (Fig. 5a). As expected, no enrichment of the *CNX5* promoter sequence was detected when *pBZR1::BZR1-myc* was immunoprecipitated (negative control).

There are four G-box and three E-box *cis*-elements in the two BZR1-binding fragments from the *ABI5* promoter. To investigate whether these G-box and/or E-box *cis*-elements contribute to the binding of BZR1 with the *ABI5* promoter sequence, DNA probes containing one or two E-box or G-box sequences were synthesized and tested for binding with recombinant MBP-BZR1 by EMSAs. *DWF4a*, a previously reported BZR1-binding probe, was used as a positive control (He et al. 2005). MBP-BZR1 was able to bind to all of the E-box- and G-box-containing DNA probes. However, the binding of MBP-BZR1 with the G-box-containing probes was much stronger than that with the E-box-containing probes (Fig. 5b and 5c). The binding of MBP-BZR1 to the G-box-containing probes was G-box-dependent, and could be competed out by unlabeled probe, but not by mutant probes in which the G-box was replaced with AAAAAA (Fig. 5b and d).

To investigate whether the binding of BZR1 protein with G-box *cis*-elements of *ABI5*  promoter contribute to BZR1 suppressed *ABI5* expression, we introduced mutations that will abolish BZR1 binding into the G-box *cis*-elements using an *ABI5* promoter-luciferase reporter gene (*mABI5::Luc*), which containing 1814 bp *ABI5* promoter sequence, and examined its effect on gene expression in transiently transformed protoplasts. Protoplast transformed with *mABI5::Luc* has higher luciferase activity than wild-type *ABI5::Luc*, suggesting that the BZR1 binding G-box *cis*-elements is required for suppression of *ABI5* expression *in vivo* (Fig 6). When four G-box *cis*-elements in *mABI5::Luc* reporter gene were all mutagenized, overexpression of BZR1 could reduce the expression of *ABI5::Luc* but not *mABI5::Luc* reporter genes (Fig. 6), which indicated that the binding with G-box *cis*-elements is indeed contribute to the suppression of *ABI5* expression by BZR1.

# **Discussion**

# **BIN2 plays a major role in mediating BR and ABA signaling crosstalk**

In this study, using loss-of-function and gain-of-function BR signaling mutants, we demonstrated that under the same growth conditions all of the BR signaling-inactive mutants were hypersensitive to ABA-inhibited root elongation, while the constitutive BR signaling mutants were partially insensitive to ABA-inhibited root elongation. These findings are consistent with previous results showing that BR signaling regulates ABA signaling in an antagonistic fashion. Based on the responses of the mutants to ABA treatment, we conclude

that BIN2 and BZR1 family transcription factors may both mediate the crosstalk between BR and ABA signaling. Compared to the wild-type, the degree of ABA insensitivity was seen much higher in the *bin2bil1bil2* triple mutant than in *bzr1-1D*, suggesting that BIN2 plays a major role in mediating the crosstalk between BR and ABA signaling. This hypothesis is further supported by our RNA-Seq results, which showed that a greater number (39.2%) of ABA-regulated genes in root tissue were also regulated in the *bin2-1* mutant compared with the number (17.94%) regulated in the *bzr1-1D* mutant.

Recently, Cai et al. (2014) reported that the BIN2-mediated phosphorylation of SnRK2.3 at Thr<sup>180</sup> is crucial for the activation of SnRK2.3. As SnRK2.3 is a key protein kinase that activates ABA signaling, it was demonstrated that the phosphorylation and activation of SnRK2s kinases is an important factor in the modulation of ABA signaling by BIN2. Here, we found that more than one-third of the ABA-regulated genes in root tissue showed similar expression pattern changes in *bin2-1* mutant plants grown on agar plates without ABA; this could explain the hypersensitivity of *bin2-1* to ABA treatment. Meanwhile, we found little overlap between *bin2-1* mutant- and SnRK2s-regulated ABA-responsive genes. We also found that additional *abi1-1c* or *snrk2.2/2.3* mutations in a *bin2-1* mutant background rendered the mutants nearly insensitive to ABA-inhibited root growth, indicating that ABI1 and SnRK2s function downstream of BIN2 in regulating plant ABA responses. Together, these results suggest a model of ABA signaling activation in which, upon ABA accumulation, ABA/PYRs bind ABI1 family PP2Cs, releasing SnRK2s from inhibition and allowing the activation of SnRK2s by BIN2 phosphorylation.

# **Crosstalk between the BR and ABA signaling pathways is partially mediated by BZR1**

Despite the important role of BIN2 in regulating ABA signaling, it was recently discovered that the antagonistic effect of BR signaling on plant ABA responses is partially mediated at the transcriptional level. Ryu et al. (2014) reported that BES1, a transcription factor and component of the BR signaling pathway, forms a transcriptional repressor complex with TPL and HDA19 that enables the epigenetic silencing of an ABA signaling component, *ABI3*, via histone deacetylation. The silencing of *ABI3* attenuates the expression of another ABA signaling component, *ABI5*, and makes the plant less sensitive to ABA treatment. As BES1 is a direct substrate of BIN2 in the BR signaling pathway, this finding suggests that BIN2-mediated ABA signal activation could occur partly through the direct regulation of downstream transcription factors. In support of this hypothesis, Hu and Yu (2014) discovered that BIN2 directly phosphorylates and stabilizes ABI5, allowing plants to respond to ABA during seed germination.

Here, we report that BZR1, a BES1 homolog and transcription factor in the BR signaling pathway, is also involved in regulating BR-suppressed ABA responses. Similar to the gain-of-function mutant *bes1-D*, we found that the gain-of-function mutant *bzr1-1D* was partially insensitive to ABA-inhibited root growth when compared with wild-type. This observation was further supported by the finding that transgenic plants expressing *pBZR1::bzr1-1D-CFP* were partially insensitive to ABA treatment, and that the *bzr1-1D* mutation could partially recover the hypersensitivity of the *bin2-1* mutant to ABA-inhibited root growth. However, there are conflicting reports showing that the *bzr1-1D* mutant is hypersensitive to ABA treatment (Rodrigues et al. 2009; Ryu et al. 2014). A similar discrepancy has been reported for the *bes1-D* mutant (Cai et al. 2014; Ryu et al. 2014). BZR1 and BES1, which exhibit 88% protein sequence identity, regulate BR signaling through similar mechanisms. Although the phenotypes of *bzr1-1D* and *bes1-D* mutant plants are not identical, both mutants were caused by the same mutation in which a Pro residue was changed to a Leu residue at the same site. In general, *bes1-D* and *bzr1-1D* mutants should show similar ABA-insensitive phenotypes. Because *bes1-D* and *bzr1-1D* are ethyl methanesulfonate-generated mutants, one possible explanation is that additional mutations exist in *bes1-D* and *bzr1-1D* that make the plants hypersensitive to ABA treatment. Indeed, the *bzr1-1D* mutant used in this study has been back-crossed with wild-type Col for multiple generations.

RNA-Seq analysis showed that only a small group of ABA-regulated genes in root tissue (407 out of 3783; 10.76%) were oppositely regulated between the *bzr1-1D*/*bin2-1* and *bin2-1* mutants. This is consistent with the observation that *bzr1-1D* could only partially recover the hypersensitivity of the *bin2-1* mutant to ABA-inhibited root growth. Among these genes, *ABI5* was found to be up-regulated by ABA treatment and in the *bin2-1* mutant, but down-regulated in the *bzr1-1D*, *bzr1-1D/bin2-1* mutants and by BR treatment. Genetic studies have shown that *ABI5* over-expression suppressed the ABA-insensitive phenotype of *bzr1-1D*. Together with the findings of Ryu et al. (2014), these results suggest that modulating the expression of *ABI5* contributes to the partial insensitivity of *bes1-D* and *bzr1-1D* plants to ABA treatment. Additionally, we found that BZR1 could bind directly to the *ABI5* promoter, suggesting that BZR1 regulates the expression of *ABI5* without the help of the TPL-HDA19-ABI3 module.

It has been demonstrated that the BZR1/BES1-mediated transcription network is critical for BR-regulated plant growth and development. In general, BZR1 and BES1 bind to BRRE (CGTC<sup>T</sup> /CG) and E-box (CANNTG) *cis*-elements to regulate gene expression (He et al. 2005; Yin et al. 2005). An analysis of BZR1 target genes revealed that BRREs are enriched in the promoters of genes that are repressed by BRs while E-boxes are enriched in the promoters of BR-induced genes (Sun et al. 2010). In addition to BRREs and E-boxes, G-box was also found to be enriched in the promoters of BR-repressed genes (Sun et al. 2010). Recently, it was discovered that BZR1 can interact with PIF4 and bind to G-box to regulate cell elongation (Oh et al. 2012). Using EMSAs, we demonstrated that BZR1 could bind directly with several G-box-containing sequences in the *ABI5* promoter. In contrast, the binding of BZR1 with the E-boxes in the *ABI5* promoter was much weaker. Together, our results suggest that BRs regulate the expression of *ABI5*, and possibly other G-box-containing genes, directly via BZR1 and/or BES1.

In conclusion, in this study we obtained genetic and transcriptomic evidence indicating that BRs antagonize plant ABA responses via two different mechanisms through BIN2 and BZR1. As the BIN2-mediateed phosphorylation of BZR1 inhibited BZR1-regulated gene expression, BZR1- and BIN2-mediated crosstalk between BR and ABA signaling should be highly interactive. Similar to BES1, BZR1 may desensitize plants to ABA by attenuating the expression of *ABI5*. Interestingly, BZR1 exhibited strong affinity for G-boxes; the CACGTG G-box sequence shares significant identity with an ABA-responsive *cis*-element ( C /TACGTGGC) that is present in a number of ABA-regulated genes. This ABRE contains an ACGT core, which is recognized by plant bZIP proteins, including ABFs, which regulate ABA signaling (Foster et al. 1994; Choi et al. 2000). Future experiment should be performed to test whether BZR1 could compete with bZIP transcription factors to regulate plant ABA responses.
