#### **ABSTRACT**

*De novo* shoot organogenesis (DNSO) is a post-embryonic development program that has been widely exploited by plant biotechnology. DNSO is a hormonally regulated process, where auxin and cytokinin (CK) coordinate suites of genes encoding transcription factors, general transcription factors, and RNA metabolism machinery genes. Here we report that silencing *Arabidopsis thaliana* carboxyl-terminal domain (CTD) phosphatase-like 4 (*CPL4RNAi*), which increases phosphorylation level of RNA polymerase II (pol II) CTD, altered lateral root development and DNSO efficiency of the host plants. Under the standard growth condition, *CPL4RNAi* lines produced no or few lateral roots. When induced by high concentration of auxin, *CPL4RNAi* lines failed to produce focused auxin maxima at the meristem of lateral root primordia, and produced fasciated lateral roots. By contrast, root explants of *CPL4RNAi* lines were highly competent for DNSO. Efficient DNSO of *CPL4RNAi* lines was observed even under 10 times less CK required for the wild-type explants. Transcriptome analysis showed *CPL4RNAi* but not wild-type explants expressed high levels of shoot meristem related genes even during priming on media with a high auxin/CK ratio, and during subsequent shoot induction with a lower auxin/CK ratio. On the other hand, *CPL4RNAi* enhanced the inhibitory phenotype of the *shoot redifferentiation defective2-1* mutation, which affected snRNA biogenesis and formation of the auxin gradient. These results indicate that CPL4 functions in multiple regulatory pathways that positively and negatively affect DNSO.

#### INTRODUCTION

Plants have a unique potential to regenerate the whole body from a single somatic cell (Vasil and Hildebrandt, 1965, Vasil and Hilderbr.Ac, 1965). This trait has been explored in horticultural and agricultural applications and biotechnologies such as asexual propagation, tissue culture and transformation (Newell, 2000, Butt *et al.*, 2015). The regeneration process involves *de novo* shoot organogenesis (DNSO), which requires coordinated and balanced actions of the phytohormones auxin and cytokinin (CK) (Skoog, 1950, Skoog, 1957). The process of DNSO is, in principle, an exogenously-induced post-embryonic development program, which, in nature, governs formations of new organs from apical meristems, as well as de novo formation and development of lateral meristems (Perianez-Rodriguez *et al.* 2014). Genes expressed during DNSO are also central to natural shoot meristem establishment and organization, therefore, findings relating to DNSO are applicable for understanding plant meristem functions.

DNSO is typically induced by two-step incubation on media with different auxinic ratios (Christianson and Warnick, 1983, Che *et al.*, 2007). First, excised plant tissues (explants) are incubated for 3-5 days on a medium containing both auxin and CK with a high auxin/CK ratio (Callus Induction Medium; CIM). This process confers explants transient competence to induce *de novo* shoots. Subsequent incubation of the pre-incubated explants on higher and lower auxin/CK ratio would lead to induction of root and shoot, respectively, whereas prolonged incubation of explants on CIM promotes a formation of callus, a mass of proliferating multipotent cells (Christianson and Warnick, 1983). Callus formation and lateral root primordia (LRP) development are auxin dependent processes (Sugimoto *et al.*, 2010), whereas CK induces DNSO from callus by activating genes essential for development and maintenance of the shoot apical meristem (SAM) (Che *et al.*, 2007). *De novo* shoots originate from LRP expressing the NAC (No Apical Meristem/Arabidopsis thaliana activating factor/Cup-shaped cotyledon 2) transcription factor *CUP-SHAPED COTYLEDON 2* (CUC2), whose expression is induced during CIM-pre-incubation (Gordon *et al.*, 2007). Subsequent incubation on CK-rich shoot induction medium (SIM) activates other transcription factors such as APETALA2/Ethylene Responsive Factor (AP2/ERF) family *ENHANCER OF SHOOT REGENERATION 1* (*ESR1*) and *ESR2*, which also activate expression of *CUC1* (Banno *et al*., 2001; Matsuo *et al.*, 2009). The earlier induction of these transcription factors on SIM results in activation of essential homeobox transcription factors *SHOOT MERISTEMLESS* (*STM*) and *WUSCHEL* (*WUS*), leading to *de novo* shoot meristem initiation from the LRP/calli, and eventually DNSO (Cary *et al.*, 2002).

*De novo* organogenesis from root explants initiates from pericycle cells, which are arrested at G2 phase (Beeckman *et al.*, 2001). Activation of the cell cycle is a key step to initiate LRP development, callus formation, and subsequent shoot organogenesis (Himanen *et al.*, 2002, Xu *et al.*, 2012). The cell cycle progression is regulated through coordinated expression and action of cyclindependent kinases (CDKs) and their activators, i.e., cyclins (Morgan, 1995, Francis, 2007). For example, the expression of Arabidopsis Cyclin A (CYCA2;1, CYCA3;1) and Cyclin B (CYCB1;1) was associated with G1-S phase and mitotic division (G2-M transition), respectively (Hemerly *et al.*, 1992, Niebel *et al.*, 1996, Himanen *et al.*, 2002, Menges *et al.*, 2005). Consistently, the highest kinase activities of CDKA1 were observed at G1-S and G2-M phases, and CDKB1;1 activity reached a maxima at G1-S (Joubes *et al.*, 2000). D-type cyclins including CYCD3;1 were responsive to CK and were rate limiting factors for G1-S phase transition and CK response; Constitutive overexpression of cyclin D bypassed CK requirement for shoot organogenesis (Riou-Khamlichi *et al.*, 1999, Dewitte *et al.*, 2003, Menges *et al.*, 2006, Dewitte *et al.*, 2007). The importance of cell cycle activation was reinforced recently because it was shown to be essential for clearing a repressive epigenetic mark from the WUS promoter so that subsequent high CK treatment could induce WUS expression on SIM (Zhang *et al.* 2017).

Factors related to general transcription, splicing, and RNA metabolism machinery are also involved in the CK sensitivity and DNSO process. Mutants in two *CYTOKININ-HYPERSENSITIVE* (*CKH1* and *CKH2*) genes were isolated from a screening for rapid callus growth and greening on a low-CK media containing only 0.025 mg/l kinetin (Kubo and Kakimoto, 2000). *CKH1* encodes a TATA-binding protein complex associated factor 12B (TAF12B) homolog, which is a part of general transcription factor TFIID (Kubo *et al.*, 2011). The c*kh1* mutants showed a CK hypersensitive phenotype in calli without affecting the expression pattern of primary CK-responsive genes (Kubo *et al.*, 2011). *CKH2/PICKLE* encodes a SWItch/Sucrose Non-Fermentable (SWI/SNF) chromatin remodeling factor, which promotes histone H3 K27 trimethylation mark (Zhang *et al.* 2012), and functions synergistically with *CKH1* (Furuta *et al.*, 2011). *SHOOT REDIFFERENTIATION DEFECTIVE 2* (*SRD2*) is an essential gene for *in vitro* shoot regeneration from root explants (Yasutani *et al.*, 1994), and encodes an homolog of the SNAP50 subunit in the snRNA activator protein complex (SNAPc), suggesting that the proper production of snRNA is essential for dedifferentiation and regeneration of tissues (Ohtani and Sugiyama, 2005, Ohtani *et al.*, 2015). Similarly, a screening for defects in adventitious root formation from shoot explants identified genes involved in pre-rRNA processing (*ROOT INITIATION DEFECTIVE*, *RID2* and *RID3*) and spliceosome activity (*RID1*, a DEAH-box helicase homologous to yeast Prp22) (Sugiyama, 2003, Ohtani *et al.*, 2013).

We previously showed that CPL4 was a phosphatase specific to the RNA polymerase II Cterminal domain (pol II CTD) (Fukudome *et al.*, 2014), and was involved in co-transcriptional regulation of snRNA biogenesis in Arabidopsis (Fukudome *et al.* 2017). In eukaryotes, specific CTD phosphorylation profiles produced during transcriptional initiation, elongation and termination recruit proteins required for transcription elongation and co-transcriptional RNA modifications, such as capping, splicing and termination and 3'-end processing (Hajheidari et al. 2013). Some CTD kinases, such as CDKD and CDKF can act as CDK-activating kinases (Hajheidari *et al*. 2012), and may be involved in CTD phosphorylation as well as cell cycle regulation during DNSO, whereas the roles of CTD phosphatases in DNSO are yet to be established. In this study, we characterized role of a CTD phosphatase in DNSO using *CPL4* knockdown lines (*CPL4RNAi*), in which the CPL4 expression level is reduced to approximately one-tenth of the wild type (Fukudome *et al.* 2014). *CPL4RNAi* lines were compromised in LRP development without affecting auxin responsive gene expression. While *CPL4RNAi* did not affect callus formation from shoots and root explants, DNSO from *CPL4RNAi* root explants was greatly enhanced, which could be attributed to an increased CK sensitivity of *CPL4RNAi*. By contrast, *CPL4RNAi* enhanced the DNSO inhibition phenotype of the *srd2-1* mutant. Transcriptome and targeted gene expression analysis revealed that *CPL4RNAi* root explants overexpress multiple components involved in DNSO, including DNA replication, cell cycle regulation, and key transcription factors for SAM regulation, which pointed to CPL4 function in cell cycle regulation and the downstream epigenetic control of the CK-response. These results indicated that CPL4 was involved in both positive and negative regulatory pathways for DNSO, and the genetic background determined which pathway dominated the phenotypic outcomes.

## **RESULTS**

#### **Knockdown of CPL4 impairs lateral root formation**

The root of *CPL4RNAi* seedlings produced unique growth characteristics. Their primary roots grew slower than the wild-type roots and had no lateral roots (Figure 1a). The root auxin response of *CPL4RNAi* was strongly compromised compared to wild-type. While the growth of wild-type (Col *gl1*) and *CPL4RNAi* primary roots were similarly inhibited by Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) (Figure 1b), lateral root induction by IAA was observed only in wild-type roots even with 100 nM IAA treatment (Figure 1a, c). To determine if *CPL4RNAi* had altered auxin distribution and/or signaling, expression of the *DR5::GUS* reporter gene was monitored. The root tips of both wild-type and *CPL4RNAi* seedlings showed comparable levels of *DR5::GUS* expression with or without IAA-treatment, consistent with the observations that the auxin sensitivity of the *CPL4RNAi* primary root growth was similar to the wild type. (Figure 1d). Interestingly, the *DR5::GUS*-positive lateral root primordia (LRP) were observed not only in the wild-type but also in the *CPL4RNAi* roots, albeit at a lower frequency (Figure 1e, f). These results indicated that *CPL4RNAi* inhibited lateral root development after the initiation of LRPs, i.e., *DR5::GUS-*positive spots on the primary root.

Under the standard growth condition, the *CPL4RNAi* seedlings were able to produce LRP but not normal lateral roots until the very late stage of growth. The LR development process in *CPL4RNAi* was evaluated using a semi-synchronous lateral root induction assay (Figure 2). In this assay, root explants without the root tip were placed on the root induction medium (RIM) containing a high concentration of auxin (Indole-3-butyric acid; IBA, 0.5 mg/L). Under this condition, the *CPL4RNAi* root explants could produce LRPs, however they had distinctive developmental defects. At early stage (48 hours post transfer (hpt)), the *CPL4RNAi* LRPs corresponding to stage IV (Malamy and Benfey, 1997) were similar to those of the wild type (Figure 2a). However, many *CPL4RNAi* LRPs became fasciated and showed disorganized cell orientation at 72 hpt while the wild-type LRPs established vasculaturelike organization in the center (Figure 2a). Because a similar observation had been reported for *srd2- 1* LRP*,* a temperature-sensitive allele of *SRD2* containing an amino acid replacement (Ohtani *et al.*, 2010), was used to compare LRP in *CPL4RNAi* and *srd2-1. CPL4RNAi* LRP became significantly wider than the wild type, and were similar to *srd2-1* LRPs produced at the restrictive temperature (Figure 2c). At 96 hpt, established and elongating lateral roots were observed on the wild-type root explants, whereas the *CPL4RNAi* LRPs were not fully established yet (Figure 2a). In rare cases, fasciated *CPL4RNAi* LRPs with a split meristem were observed at 8-day post transfer (Figure 2b). The *DR5::GUS* reporter expression profile showed that *CPL4RNAi* could establish auxin localization at the tip; however, the area showing auxin maxima was larger than that of the wild type, and a weak GUS activity was observed throughout the emerging LR (Figure 2d). This suggests that *CPL4RNAi* was compromised in establishing a proper auxin gradient during lateral root development, resulting in fasciated LRPs.

#### **CPL4 enhances hypocotyl elongation in dark**

 Hypocotyl elongation is another auxin-regulated process (Nagpal *et al.*, 2005, Oh *et al.*, 2014). Light-grown *CPL4RNAi* seedlings did not show a significant difference in hypocotyl length; however, dark-grown *CPL4RNAi* seedlings were significantly shorter than the wild-type seedlings (Figure S1). By contrast, when CPL4 was overexpressed using the EL2 promoter (CaMV 35S promoter with 2x enhancer) (Fukudome *et al.,* 2014*)*, seedlings produced slightly longer hypocotyls in the dark. Sucrose content in the media did not affect the phenotype, suggesting that the compromised hypocotyl elongation of *CPL4RNAi* seedlings was not due to carbohydrate starvation (Figure S1c). This was consistent with an alteration in auxin response in *CPL4RNAi*; however, the impact of *CPL4* on this process was less prominent than that on the root phenotype.

## *De novo* **shoot organogenesis (DNSO) from root explants is enhanced in** *CPL4RNAi*

 The phenotype of *CPL4RNAi*, such as reduced seedling lateral root development, abnormal auxin-induced lateral root formations, and alteration in snRNA metabolism (Fukudome et al., 2017) are similar to the symptoms observed with the *srd2-1* mutant, which shows strong inhibition of *in vitro* cell proliferation and shoot organogenesis (Yasutani *et al.*, 1994, Ohtani and Sugiyama, 2005, Fukudome *et al.*, 2017). Therefore, we tested *CPL4RNAi* for hallmark phenotypes related to *in vitro* organogenesis. Callus formation was not affected in *CPL4RNAi* (Figure S2). To induce DNSO, the explants were pre-treated with CIM for 5 days, and shoot organogenesis was then monitored on shoot induction medium containing 0.15 mg/L IAA and various concentrations of CK (Figure 3). Surprisingly, DNSO from root explants exhibited marked differences between the wild type and *CPL4RNAi*. The *CPL4RNAi* root explants formed shoots much more vigorously than wild type both under the standard and the low-CK condition (0.5 mg/L and 0.05 mg/L 2iP, respectively). No shoot formation was observed without 2iP in either genotype (Figure 3a-b). In the case of the hypocotyl explants, the degree of callus greening and DNSO in *CPL4RNAi* in the low-CK condition were comparable to the wild type (Figure S3a-b). However, unlike the root explants, the hypocotyl explants of *CPL4RNAi* did not show as much DNSO as the wild type under the standard-CK condition (Figure S3a-b). This indicated that *CPL4RNAi* hypocotyls could sense CK, but the CK response was partially impaired at the standard CK concentration. By contrast, the *CPL4RNAi* specifically enhanced DNSO capability from *CPL4RNAi* roots was elevated apparently by enhancing responsiveness to CK; however, exogenous CK was still necessary.

## **DNSO enhancement in** *CPL4RNAi* **depends on** *SRD2*

Despite the fact that *CPL4RNAi* and *srd2-1* share a similar LRP development pattern, *CPL4RNAi* promoted DNSO from root explants rather than inhibiting it as in *srd2-1*. To assess how *CPL4* and *SRD2* genetically interact during DNSO, *CPL4RNAi srd2-1* lines were prepared and subjected to DNSO assay (Figure S4). At the permissive temperature, DNSO was less vigorous in *CPL4RNAi srd2-1* than the parent *CPL4RNAi*, but not completely abolished (Figure S4a). The DNSO of *CPL4RNAi srd2-1* was strongly impaired at the restrictive temperature. Not only did *CPL4RNAi srd2-1* produce no shoots on the SIM containing 0.5 mg/L 2iP, but also the calli did not proliferate from the root explants (Figure S4b). This indicated that *CPL4* and *SRD2* might synergistically function at an early stage of DNSO, likely at the level of induction of organogenic calli. Because *CPL4RNAi* can induce transcriptional switch from snRNA to mRNA (Fukudome, et al. 2017), it might exacerbate the snRNA deficiency of *srd2-1* when these genotypes were combined.

## *CPL4RNAi* **does not substantially alter endogenous auxin levels**

 To assess if the DNSO phenotype of *CPL4RNAi* was caused by changes in endogenous auxin levels, we quantified endogenous auxin in wild-type and *CPL4RNAi* lines using liquid chromatographytandem mass spectrometry (Figure S5). We observed a slight increase of IAA concentration in *CPL4RNAi*, but the differences were relatively small and not highly significant, particularly in root tissue. Considering that the auxin concentrations used in DNSO are >100 times than endogenous auxin concentration, we concluded that *CPL4RNAi* did not change endogenous IAA concentrations to a biologically significant level. This was consistent with the fact that we did not detect a constitutive increase of auxin marker (*DR5-GUS*) expression (Figure 1) nor of *Aux/IAA* family genes (Fukudome *et al.* 2014).

#### **Genes synergistically upregulated by** *CPL4RNAi* **and CK during early DNSO**

 The results described above established that *CPL4RNAi* root explants are capable of low-CK DNSO. Because DNSO is a highly integrated developmental process, it is not immediately obvious which aspect of DNSO CPL4 influences. To dissect molecular pathways that facilitated low-CK DNSO in *CPL4RNAi*, transcriptome analysis using RNA-seq was performed on root tissues undergoing the low-CK DNSO (Figure S6a). Analyses were conducted with samples from four conditions, namely, W(- ), wild type on SIM without 2iP; W(+), wild type on SIM with low CK (0.05 mg/L 2iP); C4(-); *CPL4RNAi* on SIM without 2iP; C4(+), *CPL4RNAi* on SIM with low CK (0.05 mg/L 2iP). To compare different genotypes and treatments by the same standard, differentially expressed transcripts relative to W(-) were identified for each sample (Figure S6b, Data S1). Similarly, binary comparisons were made between *CPL4RNAi* with or without CK, and *CPL4RNAi* and wild type treated with CK, however, these comparisons did not reveal new classes of genes. Because only the C4(+) root explants were capable of DNSO (Figure 3), the 2,838 genes showing significantly higher expression at least by 1.5-fold in C4(+) than in W(-), which was the most inclusive set of differentially expressed genes, were primarily investigated to identify pathways contributing to the DNSO capability (Figure S6b). The 2,838 genes are hereafter referred to as "C4(+)UT", C4(+)-Upregulated Transcripts. Notably, the level of *SRD2* transcripts did not change by genotype or treatment (Data S1).

 We classified the 2,838 C4(+)UTs based on their expression profiles, namely, up-regulated by *CPL4RNAi* (class II and IV), CK (2iP) (class III and IV) or by synergistic effects of *CPL4RNAi* and CK (class I) (Figure 4, Data S2). Class I contained 778 (527 + 251) C4(+)UTs without overlapping with C4(-)UTs or W(+)UTs, indicating that their up-regulation required both *CPL4RNAi* and CK. As shown in a heat map (Figure 4b-e). The overall expression pattern of class I genes was more similar to that of class II genes than to those of class III or class IV, albeit less intense. Indeed, 527 out of 778 class I genes are weakly (below 1.5 fold threshold) but significantly up-regulated in C4(-)/W(-) comparison, and therefore can be considered as a subclass of class II. These 527 genes were termed "class Ia". Class II genes (1,594) were up-regulated in *CPL4RNAi* regardless of CK (Figure 4a). Similarly, class III genes (132) were up-regulated also in W(+), and hence were CK-responsive. Class IV genes (334) overlapped with both C4(-)UTs and W(+)UTs, indicating that these genes could be up-regulated by either *CPL4RNAi* or CK.

 Each class of C4(+)UTs contains known DNSO-related genes (Figure 5). Among class I/Ia genes were 2 negative regulators of auxin response (IAA1 and IAA12) and 1 CK receptor protein (CRE1) (Inoue *et al.*, 2001), suggesting *CPL4RNAi* was more effective in perceiving/propagating signals from decreased auxin/CK ratio upon transfer to low-CK SIM (Figure 5a and 5 b). Class II contained the largest number of genes in C4(+)UTs. Intriguingly, essential SAM regulator genes such as *WUSCHEL* (*WUS*), *SHOOT MERISTEMLESS* (*STM*) and *CUP-SHAPED COTYLEDON* (*CUC*) were grouped in class II, indicating that their expression in *CPL4RNAi* roots was not dependent on CK in SIM (Figure 5c). To identify other CK-independent DNSO-related genes/pathways in *CPL4RNAi*, we conducted GO overrepresentation analysis of the 1,594 class II genes (Table S1). GOs related to DNA replication, cell cycle regulation, cyclin-dependent protein kinase activity and chromosomal part were overrepresented in class II genes, indicating that after CIM pre-activation the *CPL4RNAi* roots were actively going through cell cycles (Table S1, Data S3). Notably, an overexpressed cyclin D, CYCD3;1, was a rate limiting factor in CK response, and its overexpression resulted in ectopic cell division (Riou-Khamlichi *et al.*, 1999, Dewitte *et al.*, 2007). These observations indicated that cells in *CPL4RNAi*

## *CPL4RNAi* **roots activate DNSO-regulator genes during CIM pre-incubation**

Because SAM regulators were expressed in *CPL4RNAi* both with and without CK in SIM, the timing of expression for the selected SAM regulator genes was examined by RT-qPCR analysis (Figure 6). Interestingly, *WUS* and *STM* expressions were observed in *CPL4RNAi* roots on CIM at a level similar to those after the low-CK SIM incubation, suggesting that the CIM-pre-incubation alone is enough for *WUS* and *STM* activation in *CPL4RNAi*. CIM-pre-incubation did not strongly induce *ESR1* and *CUC1*, but *CYCB1;1* and early DNSO marker *CUC2* were strongly induced in CIM-pre-incubated *CPL4RNAi*, suggesting that *CPL4RNAi* promote cell division and acquisition of DNSO competence during CIM-preincubation. On the other hand, the *ARR5* expression level in *CPL4RNAi* was similar to that in wild-type after CIM-pre-incubation or on SIM, confirming that the CK response itself is not hyper-induced by *CPL4RNAi*. Upon transfer to low-CK SIM, *ESR1* expression was induced in both genotypes (class III), but higher in *CPL4RNAi*. Notably, expression of *CUC1* was detected only in *CPL4RNAi* on low-CK SIM.

## **DISCUSSION**

Recent studies have identified the importance of basic transcriptional machinery and RNA metabolisms in DNSO in Arabidopsis (Furuta *et al.*, 2011, Kubo *et al.*, 2011)*.* One of such regulators was *SRD2*, an activator of snRNA transcription. Because *CPL4RNAi* lines showed an unusual snRNA biogenesis (Fukudome, *et al*. 2017) and a poor lateral root development phenotype similar to *srd2-1*, we investigated DNSO potential of *CPL4RNAi* lines. Similar to *srd2-1, CPL4RNAi* explants were compromised in auxin-induced lateral root formation, which was associated with an abnormal auxin distribution at the LRP. Surprisingly, unlike wild type and *srd2-1, CPL4RNAi* promoted DNSO from root explants even on the medium containing a concentration of CK 10 times lower than the standard condition. Yet, combining *CPL4RNAi* with *srd2-1* substantially enhanced the inhibitory effect of *srd2-1* on DNSO, suggesting there were multiple pathways, namely, SRD2-dependent and SRD2 independent pathways, in which CPL4 regulated DNSO. This phenotype is unlikely due to changes in endogenous auxin and cytokinin levels in *CPL4RNAi* based on the direct measurement data of IAA (Figure S5) as well as lack of differential expression of auxin and cytokinin markers in previous microarray and RNA-seq experiments (Fukudome, *et al*. 2014, Fukudome, *et al*. 2017).

The impacts of *CPL4RNAi* on various organogenesis-related phenotypes are likely bidirectional (Fig. 7). In the SRD2-dependent pathway, both *CPL4RNAi* and *srd2-1* compromised snRNA biogenesis (Fukudome, *et al*. 2017, Ohtani and Sugiyama 2005). However, because the effect of *CPL4RNAi* by itself on the total snRNA level was negligible under the standard condition (Fukudome, *et al*. 2017), the inhibitory effects might become significant only in limited processes, such as the formation of the auxin gradient at the developing LRPs (Ohtani 2017, Ohtani *et al.* 2015). When combined with *srd2-1* mutation, however, the *CPL4RNAi srd2-1* calli were no longer able to proliferate at restrictive temperature (Fig. S4), suggesting the level of functional snRNA pool might have dropped below the threshold to sustaining the cell proliferation (Ohtani, *et al*. 2015). On the other hand, promotion of DNSO by *CPL4RNAi* is likely independent of the specific function of the SRD2-snRNA pathway. Upregulation of cell-cycle and proliferation-related transcripts indicated a highly proliferative state of *CPL4RNAi* during the early stage of DNSO (Fig. 4 and 5). A contribution of auxin gradient in this process is less likely due to the high concentration of external auxin as well as the type of auxin used (2,4-D), which was less mobile via the polar transport mechanism (Sugawara *et al.* 2015). Zhang *et al.* (2017) showed that the cell division-dependent clearance of the repressive epigenetic mark (H3K27m3) was required for the *WUS* promoter to become responsive to CK signals, which is mediated by B-type ARRs (Meng *et al.*, 2017; Wang *et al.*, 2017; Zhang *et al.*, 2017; Zubo *et al.*, 2017). Expression of *WUS* in *CPL4RNAi* during CIM incubation suggested that the highly proliferative state of *CPL4RNAi* was sufficient to make the *WUS* promoter an open state and responsive to low-level CK in CIM, thus promoting early commitment to DNSO (Gordon *et al.*, 2007). This model is consistent with the CK-hypersensitive DNSO phenotype of the loss of function mutant of *CKH2/PICKLE,* which promotes introduction of H3K27m3 marks (Zhang, *et al*. 2012), and with our observation that DNSO of *CPL4RNAi* explants were not totally independent of CK. However, other mechanisms should not be excluded at this point.

High CK-sensitivity of *CPL4RNAi* DNSO provided some insights in the hormonal regulation of DNSO. Here, it is important to note that the CK concentration in low-CK SIM (0.05mg/L 2iP) was comparable to 0.05 mg/L kinetin in CIM, making the level of CK nearly constant throughout the treatment, and the main difference for CIM/low-CK SIM was >10-fold decrease of auxin concentration (2.0 mg/L 2,4-D to 0.15 mg/L indole-3-acetic acid). Indeed, many CK-inducible DNSOregulatory genes were already upregulated in *CPL4RNAi* during pretreatment on CIM (Fig. 6), suggesting that an epigenetic regulatory mechanism similar to that described for the *WUS* promoter might operate widely for key DNSO regulators even though we cannot exclude the possibilities that CPL4 directly represses some DNSO regulators. Our transcript analyses showed that the levels of Btype ARRs (activators of CK signaling) did not change in *CPL4RNAi*, however, the transcript levels were increased for CRE1 (a major receptor of CK), and HD-ZIP III (homeodomain leucine zipper III) transcription factors PHAVOLUTA (ATHB9)/ATHB8, which could dimerize with B-type ARR and ESR1 in transcriptional activation of the target genes (Chandler *et al.* 2007b, Zhang, *et al*. 2017). This could induce *WUS* expression in both CIM and low-CK SIM, which then could repress the expression of A-type ARR (repressor of CK signaling) and minimize the negative feedback in CK signaling (Leibfried *et al.* 2005). Interestingly, expression levels of *ESR1* and *CUC1* were low and not significantly affected by *CPL4RNAi* while on CIM, but were significantly induced in low-CK SIM, separating expression profile of *ESR1* and *CUC1* from other DNSO regulator genes. Because CK levels were constant in CIM and low-CK SIM, *ESR1* and *CUC1* were likely repressed in CIM by the high auxin concentration even under the active CK signals in *CPL4RNAi*. Both *ESR1* and *CUC1* were regulated by auxin response factor 5 (ARF5) (Cole *et al.* 2009, Kamiuchi *et al.* 2014), and the *ESR1* promoter contained auxin responsive elements (Cole, *et al*. 2009). Because ESR1 regulates CUC1 expression (Matsuo *et al.* 2009), repressor ARFs targeting the *ESR1* promoter could be responsible for this regulation.

 The mechanism by which *CPL4RNAi* affects the plant cell cycle is not clear, however, in animals, the CPL4 ortholog TFIIF-INTERACTING CTD PHOSPHATASE 1(FCP1) dephosphorylated an Mphase promoting factor, and downregulation of FCP1 stabilized cyclin B, which in wild type was degraded during the exit from the mitosis (Visconti *et al.* 2012). Considering overexpression of numerous cell cycle related transcripts including CYCB;1 in *CPL4RNAi*, the effect of *CPL4RNAi* was not likely limited to a specific phase. Instead, *CPL4RNAi* might promote the overall cell cycle progression, resulting in the clearance of H3K27me3 at the promoter of *WUS* and perhaps other DNSO regulators. The progression of the cell cycle is regulated by a cascade of phosphorylation/dephosphorylation reactions, in which multiple targets for FCP1 have been identified in humans (Della Monica *et al.* 2015, Hegarat *et al.* 2014, Visconti *et al.* 2015, Visconti, *et al*. 2012). This implies that CPL4 and potentially other CPLs (Feng *et al.* 2010, Jin *et al.* 2011, Koiwa 2006, Koiwa *et al.* 2002, Koiwa *et al.* 2004, Ueda *et al.* 2008) have more targets than just pol II CTD and could be directly involved in phosphoregulation of the cell cycle progression in plants.

#### **EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES**

#### **Plant materials and growth condition**

 Unless otherwise stated, Arabidopsis seeds were germinated and vertically grown on ½ MS media containing 1% sucrose and 1.5% agar (referred to as the basal media throughout this study), at 25 <sup>o</sup> C under long-day condition. For hypocotyl growth measurements, seedlings were germinated and horizontally grown on the basal media with 0.7% agar. *CPL4RNAi* and CPL4 overexpression transgenic lines are previously described (Bang *et al.*, 2006, Fukudome *et al.*, 2014). The *srd2-1* mutant and *CPL4RNAi srd2-1* lines were described in (Fukudome *et al.*, 2017). *DR5::GUS* construct was introduced into *CPL4RNAi* line #6 by crossing homozygous parental lines.

#### **Auxin treatment and GUS staining**

 Seeds were germinated on cellophane membranes placed on the basal media. The seedlings were transferred to a new basal media containing 0 or 100 nM IAA and grown at the same condition for the indicated period. For GUS staining, the tissues were first fixed in ice-cold 90% acetone for 15 min. Then, the fixed tissues were washed once with 100 mM potassium phosphate (pH 7.0). GUS staining solution (100 mM potassium phosphate pH7.0, 5 mM K3 ferrocyanide, 0.5 mM K4 ferrocyanide, 0.1% Triton X-100 and 2 mM X-Gluc) was added to the washed tissues and incubated for 6 hours to overnight. GUS stained tissues were observed under a dissecting microscope or subjected to clearing for Differential Interference Contrast (DIC) imaging.

#### **Semi-synchronous lateral root induction and tissue clearing for DIC imaging**

 Semi-synchronous lateral root induction was performed essentially as previously described (Ohtani *et al.*, 2010). Shoot portions including cotyledons and root apices were removed from 4-d old vertically grown seedlings. The remaining tissues were cultured on root induction medium (RIM; 1x MS medium containing 1xB5 vitamin, 0.2 g/L KH2PO4, 0.1 g/L myo-inositol and 0.5 mg/l indole-3 butyric acid; IBA). Prior to microscopic observation, explants were cleared following a method previously described with a slight modification (Malamy and Benfey, 1997). Explants were dipped in solution 1 (0.24N HCl and 20% methanol), vacuum infiltrated for 5 min and incubated at 57 <sup>o</sup> C for 15 min. After removal of solution 1, solution 2 (w/v 7% NaOH and 60% ethanol; optional 7% hydroxylamine-HCl is omitted) was added and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The explants were then rehydrated in 40%, 20%, 10% and 5% ethanol solutions. Each rehydration step was 10 min (15 min for 5% ethanol) at room temperature. Finally, the explants were incubated in 25% glycerol at room temperature for 15 min. The cleared samples were mounted in 50% glycerol on glass plate and observed under DIC objectives using Olympus BX51 microscope.

#### **Callus induction and in vitro shoot regeneration**

 Explants for callus induction and shoot regeneration were prepared from 10 to 15 day-old seedlings. Root explants of approximately 5 mm in length were excised from a primary root segment 5 mm away from the root tip. Therefore, one hypocotyl and one root explant each could be prepared from a seedling. Callus formation was induced by incubating explants on callus induction medium (CIM; 1x MS medium containing 1x B5 vitamins, 0.2 g/L KH2PO4, 0.1 g/L myo-inositol and 2.0 mg/L 2,4-Dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D), 0.05 mg/L kinetin and 0.9% agar) for the indicated amount of time (Doelling and Pikaard, 1993). For *in vitro* shoot regeneration, the explants were pre-incubated on the CIM for 4-6 days, then transferred to and incubated on shoot induction medium (SIM) containing 1x MS medium containing 1xB5 vitamins, 0.2 g/L KH2PO4, 0.1 g/L myo-inositol and 0.15 mg/L indole-3-acetic acid, 0 – 0.5 mg/L 2-isopentenyladenine (2iP) and 0.9% agar. All incubation was done under long-day conditions with 25-35 µmol m-2 s-1 PPFD at 22 °C or 28 °C.

#### **Transcriptome analysis of roots incubated on SIM by RNA-seq**

 The transcriptome analysis experimental design is depicted in Figure S6. Wild type and *CPL4RNAi* line #7 seeds were sown and germinated on the basal media. Aerial portions of ten-day old seedlings were removed, and the root segments were subjected to CIM-pre-incubation for 5-day. Then, the root tissues were transferred to modified SIM containing 1/10 of standard CK (0.05 mg/L 2iP) or none (0 mg/L 2iP and incubated for 72 hours. Subsequently the root tissues were harvested, flash-frozen with liquid nitrogen and stored for RNA extraction. Total RNA including short and small RNAs were extracted from the frozen tissues by miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat. #217004). After quality check on agarose gel electrophoresis and nanodrop measurements, for each of twelve samples (2 genotypes x 2 conditions x independent biological triplicates), 50 µl (50 ng/µl) of total RNA in RNase-free water was submitted to the Genomics and Bioinformatics Service facility in Texas A&M University (TxGen). The TruSeq-Stranded RiboZero library preparation and Illumina-HiSeq 2500v4 High Output run (125-paired end) were performed by the TxGen facility. The raw sequence read files were produced in fastq format.

#### **Bioinformatic analysis**

The fastq files were loaded to the Galaxy public server (https://usegalaxy.org/) for bioinformatics analysis. For each sample, adapter-free read files in fastq format were produced by the facility. After quality-control by Fast QC, the processed reads were mapped with Tophat (v2.0.14) against the reference assembly Ensembl TAIR10 from Illumina iGenome, using the default parameters except the minimum and the maximum intron lengths as 60 and 6,000, respectively. Differential gene expression analysis (Figure 4, 5, S6b) was done by running Cuffdiff (v2.2.1.3). *CPL4* (AT5G58003) was mistakenly detected as a class II gene due to the detection of reads derived from the RNAi construct, and therefore, was manually removed from the class II list. Fragments per Kilobase Per Million (FPKM) values, fold-change values and statistics were summarized in Data S1. For GO overrepresentation analysis (Table S1, Data S3), a list of 1,594 class II genes was uploaded to PANTHER13.0 (http://pantherdb.org/) (Mi *et al*., 2017) and analyzed using Statistical overrepresentation test (release 20170413) with default settings (p<0.05, after Bonferronni correction for multiple testing). The list was tested for GO biological process complete, GO molecular function complete and GO cellular component complete (GO Ontology database Released 2017-10- 23). Then, GOs with fold-enrichment (number of observed genes/number of expected genes) higher than 3 were selected as highly and significantly overrepresented GOs among class II genes. The GOs and corresponding class II genes were summarized in Table S1 and Data S3, respectively.

## **RT-PCR and qPCR**

 RNA extraction, reverse transcription and qPCR were performed as described in (Fukudome *et al*., 2014). Primers used are listed in Table S2.

#### **IAA analysis**

 For IAA analyses, 5 biological replicates of 69-88 roots and hypocotyls were collected for each line. Samples were spiked with a 13C6-labeled IAA standard and extracted as described previously (Yao and Finlayson, 2015). IAA was separated using a Waters HSS C18 column (1.8 μM, 2.1 x 100 mm) on a Waters Aquity UPLC system using a gradient of 85% 10 mM formic acid in water: 15% acetonitrile to 10% 10 mM formic acid in water: 90% acetonitrile at 0.35 mL min-1, over 9 min. IAA in the UPLC eluent were quantified using a Waters TQD detector with an ESI probe and mass filters operated in negative MRM mode. Source parameters were: desolvation N2 at 900 mL min-1, desolvation temp 450<sup>o</sup> C, cone flow 90 mL min-1 N2, 3.1 kV capillary voltage, 23 V cone voltage, Ar collision gas at 0.15 mL min-1. IAA was quantified using a collision energy of 10 V and precursor and product ions 174.15, 130.05 and 180.15.10, 136.05 m/z were monitored for the endogenous hormone and internal standard, respectively.

**Figure 7. A model for CPL4 functions in DNSO from root explants.** Gene symbols in red indicate transcript upregulation in *CPL4RNAi*. Red arrows indicate processes possibly activated in *CPL4RNAi*. CPL4 likely affect DNSO in 2 pathways. In the SRD2-dependent pathway, CPL4 promotes proper termination/3'-processing of snRNAs that are important for formation of the auxin

gradient. Combining *CPL4RNAi* and *srd2-1* can cause severe defects in snRNA production and growth arrest. CPL4 likely functions in regulation of the cell cycle, and *CPL4RNAi* deregulate and over-activate the cell cycle at high auxin concentrations. Cell division promotes removal of histone H3 K27m3 marks at WUS and possibly other DNSO regulator promoters, and sensitizes the WUS promoter to activation by the low-levels of CK present in CIM and low-CK SIM. CK signaling in *CPL4RNAi* is also sensitized perhaps by higher expression of the HD-ZIP III transcription factors PHV/AtHB8, which can dimerize with, and activate, B-type ARRs to promote ESR1 expression, and PHV can dimerize with the resulting ESR1 protein as well. However, specific expression of *ESR1* and its target *CUC1* in *CPL4RNAI* on low-CK SIM indicated that *ESR1* expression is repressed by high concentrations of auxin in CIM. On low-CK SIM, *ESR1* can fully express in *CPL4RNAi* and dimerize with HD-ZIP IIIs to induce expression of *CUC1* and other target genes. Concerted actions of *STM, WUS, ESR1, CUC1, CUC2* induce DNSO.
