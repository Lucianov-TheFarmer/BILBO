# ABSTRACT

Chard (*Beta vulgaris* ssp. *vulgaris* var. *cicla*) is a member of one of four different cultigroups of beets. While the genome of sugar beet, the most prominent beet crop, has been studied extensively, molecular data on other beet cultivars is scant. Here, we present a genome assembly of chard, a vegetable crop grown for its fleshy leaves. We report a *de novo* genome assembly of 604 Mbp, slightly larger than sugar beet assemblies presented so far. About 57 % of the assembly was annotated as repetitive sequence, of which LTR retrotransposons were the most abundant. Based on the presence of conserved genes, the chard assembly was estimated to be at least 96 % complete regarding its gene space. We predicted 34,521 genes of which 27,582 genes were supported by evidence from transcriptomic sequencing reads, and 5503 of the evidence-supported genes had multiple isoforms. We compared the chard gene set with gene sets from sugar beet and two wild beets (i.e. *Beta vulgaris* sp. *maritima* and *Beta patula*) to find orthology relationships and identified genome-wide syntenic regions between chard and sugar beet. Lastly, we determined genomic variants that distinguish sugar beet and chard. Assessing the variation distribution along the chard chromosomes, we found extensive haplotype sharing between the two cultivars. In summary, our work provides a foundation for the molecular analysis of *Beta vulgaris* cultigroups as a basis for chard genomics and to unravel the domestication history of beet crops.

# 1. Introduction

Four different types of cultivated beets exist which have been assigned to individual cultivar groups, i.e. to the sugar beet group, fodder beet group, garden beet group and, lastly, the leaf beet group, respectively (Lange et al., 1999). Chard (*Beta vulgaris* ssp. *vulgaris* var. *cicla*) is a member of the latter. The leaf beet group comprises all beet crops which are primarily grown for their leaves, often associated with thickened leaf midribs or stems. Contrasting the remaining three cultivar groups, leaf beets lack a swollen taproot. Today, leaf beet varieties comprise spinach beet and chard, also referred to as Swiss chard. Chard occurs in many colorful varieties, adding to its popularity as a vegetable and salad.

The molecular analysis of beet genomes has so far mainly focused onto sugar beet. A reference genome assembly has been published based on a double-haploid sugar beet breeding line (Dohm et al., 2014), and assembled genomes from further five sugar beet genotypes are publicly available (Dohm et al., 2014; Funk et al., 2018). Molecular data from the other cultivar groups are scant. In the case of chard, just a few sequences are available in GenBank. Recently, genomic sequencing data from eight different chard accessions (Galewski and McGrath, 2020) were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA), the central repository from data generated by massively parallel sequencing.

The domestication history of beets is not yet fully resolved (McGrath et al., 2011; Galewski and McGrath, 2020). It is not clear whether domestication of beet crops goes back to a single attempt of taking the sea beet (*Beta maritima* ssp. *maritima*), the ancestor of all beet crops, into cultivation. However, leaf beets are the oldest group of cultivated beets and have been grown since antiquity (McGrath et al., 2011).

The possibly complex history of beet crop domestication cannot be elucidated solely based on the available sugar beet genome sequences. Likewise, the molecular basis of the phenotypic differences that exist between leaf beets and other cultivars can not be addressed from the exclusive analysis of sugar beet data. #### **2. Results and discussion**

#### *2.1. Chard genome assembly*

Swiss chard is one of several varieties of the *Beta vulgaris* group. Contrasting other beet varieties as fodder beet, table beet, and sugar beet, chard does not develop a fleshy taproot but is cultivated for its leaves which are used as vegetable. Thus, based on its phenotype, chard is quite distinct from all other beet cultivars. Of the cultivated beet varieties, only the sugar beet genome is well studied. In order to provide a foundation to the molecular characterisation of chard, we sequenced the genome of the chard variety "White Silver". On the Illumina sequencing platform we prepared sequencing data of one genomic paired-end library with a peak insert size of 700 bp, as well as data from three different mate-pair libraries with span sizes of 4.5 kbp, 6.5 kbp and 9 kbp, respectively (Suppl Fig. 1). Using the soapdenovo software, a genome draft of 604 Mbp was assembled consisting of 62,136 sequences (length at least 500 bp). The scaffold N50 size was 107 kbp and the largest scaffold was 945 kbp (Table 1). The chard genome draft is slightly larger than currently available genome assemblies from sugar beet (range: 487 Mbp to 569 Mbp). We evaluated the completeness of the chard genome draft using the Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) (Simao ˜ [et al., 2015\)](#page-9-0) approach. Of 956 plant-specific core genes (BUSCO groups), 96 % were found within the chard genome assembly and we conclude that intervals of the chard genome which encompass coding sequences are well represented within the assembly. The number of duplicated BUSCO groups was 34 % in both chard and sugar beet (332 duplicated BUSCO groups in chard and 334 duplicated BUSCO groups in sugar beet). Considering that the diploid status of the sugar beet genome is well established, we conclude that the genome of Swiss chard M4021 is diploid as well. The higher number of fragmented BUSCOs in chard (38) than in sugar beet (11) probably reflects the higher degree of fragmentation of the chard assembly. There were 33 BUSCO groups (3.4 %) reported as missing in chard. In the sugar beet assembly RefBeet-1.2 ([Dohm et al., 2014](#page-8-0)) 23 missed BUSCO groups were recorded, and 18 groups were missing in sugarbeet as well as in chard. We have previously shown that shared missed BUSCOs in beets represented genes of higher divergence which probably have evolved rapidly in Caryophyllales and, in consequence, are not detected during the alignment step of BUSCO when using default parameters [\(Rodríguez Del](#page-9-0)  [Río et al., 2019](#page-9-0)). Hence, the reported level of completeness regarding the assembled gene space in the chard genome assembly of 96 % is a conservative estimate and the true level of completeness is likely to be higher.

# *2.2. Repetitive sequences in the chard genome*

Many plant genomes contain a large number of repetitive sequences and a large proportion of the genome consists of repetitive DNA. For sugar beet, a repeat content of 63 % was estimated by DNA reassociation kinetics ([Flavell et al., 1974\)](#page-9-0). We generated repeat catalogs of chard and sugar beet based on their assemblies to assess the amount of repetitive sequence in the chard genome. With the two repeat catalogs we masked repeats in the chard genome using sugar beet repeats first and subsequently using the chard repeats. In total, 57.4 % of the chard genome was reported as repetitive, of which 53.1 % was masked by repeats from the sugar beet catalog and additional 4.3 % was masked by chard repeats. We tested the same approach on the sugar beet assembly RefBeet-1.2 and found similar numbers, i.e. a total of 56.4 % of repeats (52.8 % using the sugar beet repeat catalog, additional 3.6 % using the chard repeat catalog). We would expect that the chard assembly showed a lower repeat proportion since it was calculated based on short Illumina reads in contrast to the sugar beet assembly which was mainly based on Roche 454 reads that are a bit longer. Using an Illumina-only assembly of sugar beet of the same genotype (RefBv) [\(Dohm et al., 2014\)](#page-8-0) we even found a slightly higher repetitive proportion of 59.0 % (55.1 % based on sugar beet repeats, additional 3.9 % based on chard repeats). We conclude that the short-read assembly of chard is as comprehensive as the assembly of sugar beet regarding repetitive sequence, although RefBeet-1.2 has a much higher N50 size of 2 Mbp compared to the chard assembly with an N50 of 108 kbp. Furthermore, the chard assembly seems to contain a small fraction of repeats that are not detected when using a sugar beet repeat catalog. In the sugar beet reference genome assembly RefBeet-1.2, repetitive regions may have been collapsed. However, this is difficult to assess by mapping of Illumina sequencing data, due to ambiguous mapping positions of reads derived from repeats in the genome.

Of the classified repeats in the chard genome, long terminal repeat retrotransposons (LTR-RTs) were the largest group (masking 13.2 % of the genome) followed by long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs, 4.4 %) and DNA elements (3.0 %) [\(Fig. 1\)](#page-2-0). Only a small fraction of short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs) were detected (0.01 %). In sugar beet, 0.65 % of the genome (3.73 Mbp of 567 Mbp) had been classified as SINEs after manual curation of predicted repeats [\(Dohm et al., 2014](#page-8-0)). According to [Schwichtenberg et al. \(2016\)](#page-9-0) SINEs are poorly investigated in plants, and we assume that many of them were assigned to the category of unclassified repeats in the current study on chard.

Due to the importance of the "copy and paste" LTR-RTs [\(Feschotte](#page-9-0)  [et al., 2002](#page-9-0)) we analysed these elements in more detail to obtain a non-redundant LTR-RT catalog (which consisted of 1519 LTR-RTs) and a genome-wide annotation of LTR-RTs in the chard genome assembly. A total of 23 % of the chard assembly was masked by this catalog, and further classification into superfamilies revealed 11 % as Copia elements, 9% as Gypsy elements, and 3% of unknown superfamily assignment. We could detect 528 fully intact LTR-RTs in the chard genome assembly.

The two flanking LTR sequences that enclose the retrotransposon are identical at the time when integration into the genome occurs. After integration, these sequences gather mutations, and we can assume that the number of accumulated mutations is proportional to the time that has passed since insertion of the LTR-RT into the genome. Based on LTR sequence comparisons we determined their divergence and inferred the insertion time of these elements [\(Fig. 2](#page-2-0)). The results suggest that most of the intact full length LTR-RTs had been inserted at recent times during the past 500,000 years. The focus on full-length elements introduces a bias, because the more time passes, the less likely the chance that LTR-RTs remain intact. Thus, old integration events will be less frequently observed. Peaks in the distribution of insertion times can be considered as remnants of past waves of integration of particular classes of LTR-RTs.

Such a wave of Copia element integration, for instance, seems to have occurred approximately 250,000 years ago. The majority of recent full length LTR-RTs were found to be from the Copia family indicating a higher activity of this family compared to other families.

In order to draw a comparison between the LTR-RT elements encompassed within the genomes of chard and its closest relatives, we identified full length LTR-RTs in the genome assemblies of sugar beet ([Dohm et al., 2014](#page-8-0)) and sea beet ([Rodríguez Del Río et al., 2019](#page-9-0)). For sugar beet and sea beet, 988 and 775 full length LTR-RT elements could be identified, respectively. This number was higher than in chard (528 full-length elements), and also the number of non-redundant LTR-RT elements was higher in sugar beet (1862) and in sea beet (1828) compared to chard (1519) which may reflect the fragmentation of the chard assembly indicated by its lower N50 size. To assess the number of shared families of LTR-RT elements, the non-redundant LTR elements (i. e. one representative for each family) used for annotation were aligned against each other. Elements were considered as shared if they were at least 80 % identical and shared at least 80 % of their length. Chard and sugar beet shared approximately 530 non-redundant LTR-RTs. Surprisingly, following these criteria, no shared element could be found with sea beet, neither for sugar beet nor for chard. After lowering the required length to 65 %, only one shared element between sugar beet and sea beet could be discovered, and only when lowering the requirement further to 50 %, two elements shared between chard and sea beet were found. This remarkable difference in the content of LTR-RTs is hard to explain by differences in the assembly quality since both chard and sea beet were short-read assemblies. We can either assume that the cultivated forms have developed rapidly after domestication similar to previous findings of mutation accumulation in cultivated rice [\(Lu et al., 2006](#page-9-0)) or we may assume that the sequenced sea beet, derived from a locality in Denmark ([Rodríguez Del Río et al.,](#page-9-0)  [2019\)](#page-9-0), was not part of the sea beet population where the cultivated forms were derived from, and sea beet populations show considerable divergence. Genetic variation within sea beets related to geographical clusters have been described in previous studies ([Leys et al., 2014](#page-9-0); [Andrello et al., 2016\)](#page-8-0).

Two gene loci that were known for repeat insertions were analysed. Genes may be altered in their function upon insertion of mobile elements ([Hirsch and Springer, 2017](#page-9-0)). An example of a loss-of-function mutation is the rhizomania resistance gene *Rz2*. It has been shown that rhizomania-susceptible sugar beets carry a transposon insertion at this locus whereas some sea beets have the intact gene ([Cap](#page-8-0)[istrano-Gossmann et al., 2017](#page-8-0)). Chard also shows susceptibility to rhizomania caused by the *Beet necrotic yellow vein virus* (BNYVV), initiating symptoms like foliage discoloration, yellow veining of the leaf midrib, stunted growth, or wilting ([European and Mediterranean Plant Protec](#page-9-0)[tion Organization, 2006](#page-9-0)).

We aligned the sequence of *Rz2* from sea beet against the chard assembly and found two matches separated by 9 kbp in cha\_scaffold1166 (Fig. 3). This scaffold was syntenic to Bvchr3\_un.sca001 of sugar beet (RefBeet-1.2) where the *Rz2* locus had been analysed in comparison to sea beet and *Beta patula* previously ([Rodríguez Del Río et al., 2019](#page-9-0)). Closer inspection revealed that the disruption of the *Rz2* sequence was located at the same positions in sugar beet, chard, and *B. patula*, respectively, whereby one end of the disruption showed the exact same base positions in all three genomes, the other end was the same in sugar beet and *B. patula* but was shifted by 100 base positions in chard due to an assembly gap. This suggests that the same insertion event is observed in the two cultitypes and in *B. patula*, probably inherited from a common wild ancestor.

Beet crops use a 'master switch' for flowering time regulation at the bolting locus *B* containing the *BvBTC1* gene as the responsible gene for flower development ([Pin et al., 2012\)](#page-9-0). This gene is present both in sea beet and in sugar beet, however, different allelic versions of this gene exist, resulting in different life cycles. While most sea beets are annual plants sugar beet requires vernalization for the formation of the inflorescence, turning the originally annual lifestyle into a biannual one. The molecular reason for this different behavior turned out to be a repeat-rich insertion of 28 kbp in the promoter region of the *BvBTC1*  gene as well as 11 altered nucleotide positions in the gene sequence that were found in biennial sugar beets and affected the expression of *BvBTC1* [\(Pin et al., 2012;](#page-9-0) Hoft ¨ [et al., 2017](#page-9-0)). Chard shows a biannual lifestyle as well and flower formation starts in the second year of growth after vernalization ([Bartsch et al., 2001\)](#page-8-0). We aligned the *BvBTC1*  sequence of sugar beet (Bv2\_045920\_gycn of BeetSet-2) against the chard assembly and found as matching gene cha\_g15839 in scaffold cha\_scaffold4538 [\(Fig. 4\)](#page-4-0). The sequences differed by only one single nucleotide deletion indicating that the biennial version of the gene was present in chard. Furthermore, we found repetitive sequence upstream of the chard gene suggesting a similar genomic structure as in the sugar beet locus. However, a region of about 9 kbp (between positions 21k and 30k in the chard scaffold of which a stretch of 1.8 kbp was an N-gap between positions 22k and 24k) starting about 3 kbp upstream of the chard gene could not be aligned to the corresponding region in the sugar beet scaffold (Bvchr2\_un.sca04 of RefBeet-1.2). The region beyond 30k of the chard scaffold (i.e. about 12 kbp upstream of the chard gene) matched the sugar beet sequence well. It seems that the inserted 28 kbp sequence of sugar beet is only partially present in chard or that incorrect assemblies of the repetitive regions led to kbp-long differences.

Taken together, we have analysed repeats in the chard genome to assess the repetitive content of the genome and, in more detail, the LTR-RT abundance. In addition, we have analyzed two regions of the genome that are typical for cultivated *B. vulgaris* and involve repeat insertions.

## *2.3. Chard gene set*

We established a gene set of chard using the AUGUSTUS pipeline ([Stanke et al., 2008](#page-9-0)) with evidence of transcription by mRNA-seq read-pairs derived from chard leaves and roots generated in this study and mRNA-seq reads from sugar beet generated previously [\(Dohm et al.,](#page-8-0)  [2014;](#page-8-0) [Minoche et al., 2015](#page-9-0)).

In total, we predicted 34,521 genes and 36,413 transcripts in the chard genome. By excluding models without expression evidence, the number of genes and transcripts was reduced to 27,582 and 29,463, respectively. The numbers are comparable to sugar beet where 26,409 genes with cDNA evidence had been predicted with a similar approach ([Minoche et al., 2015](#page-9-0)). Over 90 % of the gene models were associated with a single transcript. For 1614 genes, several isoforms were predicted per gene. The majority of these genes had two isoforms (1396 genes) and the maximum number of isoforms was six (one gene). For the subsequent analysis only primary transcripts were considered, i.e. the transcript showing the highest fraction of features being supported by mRNA-seq evidence. Around 35 % (11,927) of the genes had at least one transcript that was supported by 100 % evidence and 85 % of them contained both a start and a stop codon. Additionally, 45 % of genes were supported by at least 1% evidence, resulting in approximately 80 % of transcripts supported by expression evidence. Most of these transcripts contained a start and a stop codon (75 %). In the sugar beet gene set, around 91 % of evidence-supported genes contained a start and a stop codon [\(Dohm et al., 2014\)](#page-8-0). The percentage of transcripts lacking a start or a stop codon was highest in the group of transcripts without evidence. Around 70 % of genes with 100 % evidence containing a start and stop codon were composed of more than one exon. In multi-exon genes, 83 % of the start codons were located in the first exon, and 95 % of the stop codons were located in the terminal exon. However, instances of complex gene structures were observed which featured multi-exonic untranslated regions (UTRs) either preceding the translation start or following the translational stop signal, respectively.

In genes with predicted start and stop codons, the average sizes of predicted mature mRNA sequences, coding sequences (CDS), and coding exons in genes with 100 % evidence support (mRNA: 1551 bp, CDS: 1121 bp, coding exon: 419 bp) and at least 1% support (mRNA: 1512 bp, CDS: 1185 bp, exon: 349 bp) did not show much difference.

The average number of coding exons in multi-exon transcripts in chard was 6.2 for transcripts with 100 % evidence. For sugar beet, an average of 4.9 exons per gene had been determined [\(Dohm et al., 2014](#page-8-0)). The mean CDS size in multi-exon chard transcripts was 1283 bp with a mean size of 269 bp per coding exon. In sugar beet the mean CDS length was 1159 bp, thus, the CDS size was similar in both cultitypes. Transcripts lacking evidence of expression consisted of only 2.9 exons on

#### *2.4. Detection of differentially spliced genes in the chard genome*

*De novo* gene prediction necessitates the reduction of noise in order to obtain as many correct gene structures as possible. The strategies that employ noise reduction lead to a decrease in alternative splicing events that get reported. However, once gene structures are known, transcriptome data obtained by high-throughput sequencing lend themselves to re-evaluate the possible existences of transcript isoforms. The program SplAdder [\(Kahles et al., 2016\)](#page-9-0) was implemented towards this goal: it takes as input mRNA-seq data, and, after alignment against the sequenced genome, assesses the presence of alternatively spliced transcripts by integration with previously annotated gene models. The initial gene prediction identified alternative splicing for 1614 evidence-supported genes (6 %) in chard. Using the alignment program STAR [\(Dobin et al., 2013\)](#page-8-0) which allows splice-aware alignment of transcriptomic reads against a genomic reference, we mapped chard mRNA-seq data to the chard genome sequence and analysed the alignments using SplAdder. At the highest level of confidence 7432 occurrences of alternative splicing were identified in 5503 genes, i.e. within 20 % of all 27,582 evidence-supported genes of chard. Most frequently observed were 3′ events (45.2 %) and 5′ events (20.5 %), whereby isoform-specific sequence changes only affected the respective UTR sequences, and ORFs remained unaltered. Exon skipping (15.7 %) and intron retention (16.8 %) were also common. Only a few instances of multiple exon skips (1.2 %) and mutually exclusive exons (0.6 %) were discovered. In the case of exon skipping, only 200 of 892 events were located within genes previously predicted by AUGUSTUS to undergo alternative splicing, and the remainder of 692 exon skips were novel. In summary, about 4300 events of alternative splicing in chard were identified within genes that had previously been associated with only a single isoform. Considering that the mRNA-seq data used for isoform prediction were derived from leaves and roots of chard only, splicing events specific to tissues not included in this study will certainly have been missed. Thus, the landscape of alternative splicing in chard is probably even more extensive than outlined herein.

#### *2.5. Gene orthology in chard, sugar beet, and two wild beet species*

Genomes evolve in complex and dynamic ways so that gene family expansion and gene loss can quickly obscure the evolutionary history of a gene. Sequence comparison between the sets of proteins encoded within two genomes can help to ascertain the evolutionary relationship of individual genes. Also, the gene repertoire of cultivated varieties may differ from their wild relatives and comparison of their genes may lead to novel insights regarding crop domestication, disease susceptibility, or pathogen resistance. Using the program OrthoFinder [\(Emms and Kelly,](#page-9-0)  [2015\)](#page-9-0), we performed an all-versus-all comparison of gene sets from chard, the reference genotype of sugar beet ([Dohm et al., 2014\)](#page-8-0), and two wild beet species ([Rodríguez Del Río et al., 2019\)](#page-9-0). As input we used protein sequences (sequences shorter than 10 amino acids were removed) predicted from evidence-supported genes, i.e. 27,513 chard genes, 26,385 sugar beet genes, 27,617 genes from sea beet (*Beta vulgaris*  ssp. *maritima*), and 25,068 genes from *Beta patula*. These gene sets were initially grouped by OrthoFinder into 20,378 orthogroups containing 86,758 genes. By definition, an orthogroup contains at least two protein sequences from at least one species. We found 17,075 orthogroups containing genes from sugar beet and chard of which 15,836 orthogroups contained genes from all four species and 392 orthogroups contained only sugar beet and chard genes (i.e. no wild beet genes). Wild beet genes only were found in 507 orthogroups. There were 14,603 orthogroups with 1:1 relationships between sugar beet and chard, i.e. containing a single gene each from sugar beet and chard (ignoring wild beets), and 12,746 orthogroups showing 1:1:1:1 relationships between all four taxa, i.e. containing exactly one gene from each taxon. We also found orthogroups that contained genes at different proportions per taxon or that were taxon-specific. However, we have previously shown that the raw Orthofinder output is less trustworthy for unbalanced orthogroups ([Rodríguez Del Río et al., 2019\)](#page-9-0).

The orthogroups were used as starting point for detecting and visualizing synteny between chard and sugar beet. From the orthogroups we extracted 1:1 orthologous gene pairs and collected their positions within scaffolds of the chard and sugar beet genome assemblies, respectively. Scaffolds were kept if at least five of their genes had orthologs within a scaffold of the other genome, otherwise the scaffold and its genes was removed from the analysis. The filtered set of scaffolds and genes was visualized separately for each sugar beet scaffold that shows synteny to at least one chard scaffold ([Fig. 5\)](#page-5-0). The filtered genes were also used to re-establish orthogroups resulting in 16,050 synteny-supported orthogroups containing 58,193 genes of sugar beet, chard, and wild

#### *2.6. Extensive sharing of haplotypes between chard and sugar beet*

The genetic variation between different breeding lines of sugar beet has been explored previously; accessions differed in sequence comparisons based on read mapping by 1.0 million variants on average [\(Dohm](#page-8-0)  [et al., 2014](#page-8-0)). However, little information exists regarding the genetic relatedness of chard and sugar beet. We used the Illumina paired-end

sequencing data prepared for *de novo* assembly of the chard genome and mapped them against the sugar beet reference genome to detect single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and short insertions and deletions (indels). The variant allele frequency (VAF) distribution were dominated by homozygous variants suggesting that the chard genotype M4021 is highly inbred (78 % of the variants had a VAF *>* 90 %, [Fig. 6](#page-6-0)). Counting high-confidence homozygous variants only, we identified 741, 979 SNVs and 61,454 indel mutations. The numbers are comparable to the variation detected between different double-haploid sugar beet genotypes [\(Dohm et al., 2014](#page-8-0)), which differed on average by 1 million (range: 0.88–1.2 million) homozygous variant positions relative to the sugar beet reference genome sequence RefBeet-1.2 based on read mapping of Illumina paired-end sequences. #### **3. Conclusions**

In this work, we present the first genome assembly of chard, a member of the species *Beta vulgaris* that is affiliated with a cultigroup other than sugar beet. Despite pronounced phenotypical differences between chard and sugar beet, we find that the two genomes are very closely related. Sugar beet and chard encode for a similar number of genes, and 53 % of the chard genes were recognised as 1:1 orthologs with sugar beet. The extensive sharing of haplotypes between chard and sugar beet is unexpected and points towards a scenario whereby both types of cultivars originated from the same founding population which comprised just a small number of plants. As a resource for future studies, we provide a genome assembly of chard based on short-read data, repeat analysis, chard gene set, orthology relationships between the gene sets of chard, sugar beet, and wild beets, a genome-wide collection of syntenic scaffolds between chard and sugar beet, and genomic variants of chard compared to all nine sugar beet chromosomes. It should be noted that short-read genome assemblies are fragmented and cannot cover all features of a genome. Nevertheless, our work paves the way towards a molecular understanding of the phenotypic breadth observed in today's beet cultigroups as well as their domestication history.

#### *4.2. Preparation of sequencing libraries and Illumina sequencing*

One microgram of genomic DNA was sheared to a peak fragment length of 700 bp using a Covaris M220 Focused Ultrasonicator (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA). The genomic library was prepared using the NEB Next sequencing library preparation kit NEB#E6000S (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). After ligation of TruSeq adapters (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to the DNA sample, size selection was performed on a 2% agarose gel in 1 x TAE buffer. The gel slice containing DNA of the desired size range was removed from the gel using disposable gel

excision tips, followed by recovery of the encompassed DNA using a QIAgen gel extraction kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The recovered library was thereafter amplified using 12 PCR cycles. The library quality was checked on a DNA 1000 chip on the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyser. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencing instrument in "Rapid Run" mode using a 2 × 101 cycle paired-end sequencing protocol. On two flowcell lanes, a total of 294,084,356 raw read-pairs were obtained. Additionally, three mate-pair libraries were prepared using the Nextera mate-pair sample preparation kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Library size selection was performed on 0.8 % agarose in 1 x TAE buffer. Size ranges of 4.5 kbp, 6.5 kbp, and 9 kbp, respectively, were excised from the gel and converted into separate libraries by circularisation, digestion of linear DNA and shearing of the circularised DNA using a Covaris instrument. Junction fragments were enriched on streptavidin M-280 Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by processing of the samples using library preparation reagents as above using indexed adapters. The library with span size of 4.5 kbp was amplified using 10 PCR cycles, the remaining two libraries were amplified with 15 cycles of PCR. Amplified mate-pair libraries underwent another round of size selection on a 2% agarose gel, recovering library inserts in the 400–700 bp size range. Finally, the quality of the libraries was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyser. Mate-pair sequencing was performed with a pool of the indexed libraries in a single flow cell lane on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 instrument using a 2 × 50 cycle protocol. For each mate-pair library, we obtained between 61 million and 71 million raw read-pairs. Sequencing of mRNA-seq libraries was performed in paired-end mode on the Illumina GA IIx sequencer, and 23.6 million pairs (leaf sample) and 40.9 million pairs (root sample) were obtained, respectively.

#### *4.3. Genome assembly*

Genomic paired-end data were quality-filtered as described [\(Dohm](#page-8-0)  [et al., 2014](#page-8-0)). A high-quality subset of the filtered sequencing data, i.e. 189 million read-pairs corresponding to 50-fold coverage of the chard genome assuming an expected genome size of 758 Mbp ([Arumugana](#page-8-0)[than and Earle, 1991](#page-8-0)) were used to calculate a pre-assembly based on single reads using SOAPdenovo v1.05 ([Luo et al., 2012\)](#page-9-0). To this pre-assembly, mate-pairs were mapped using BWA ([Li and Durbin,](#page-9-0)  [2009\)](#page-9-0) in order to estimate the true span size of the mate-pair libraries. Assemblies were calculated with SOAPdenovo v1.05 (SOAPdenovo-63mer, -K 49, pair\_num\_cutoff = 3, map\_len = 32) followed by gap filling using GapCloser v1.12. Mate-pairs were only used for scaffolding. Assembly metrics were determined using custom Perl scripts, reporting assembled sequences *>* = 500 nt and using Quast v3.2 ([Gur](#page-9-0)[evich et al., 2013](#page-9-0)). Genome completeness was assessed using BUSCO v1.1b1 (Simao ˜ [et al., 2015\)](#page-9-0).

#### *4.4. Repeat analysis*

The programs BuildDatabase and RepeatModeler v2.0.1 (<http://www.repeatmasker.org>, accessed 2019) were applied on the chard genome assembly of this work as well as on the sugar beet genome assembly RefBeet-1.2 ([Dohm et al., 2014](#page-8-0)) for generating *de novo* repeat libraries (parameter -engine ncbi). The sugar beet library was used for masking the chard assembly using RepeatMasker v4.0.9 **(**[http://www.](http://www.repeatmasker.org)  [repeatmasker.org,](http://www.repeatmasker.org) accessed 2019) (parameters -engine ncbi -gff -pa 15), and the masked assembly was again masked using the chard library.

The independent LTR-RT analysis was performed by running gt suffixerator (parameters -tis -suf -lcp -des -ssp -sds -dna) for generating the enhanced suffix array ([Abouelhoda et al., 2004\)](#page-8-0) and gt ltrharvest ([Ellinghaus et al., 2008\)](#page-9-0) for *de novo* LTR-RT identification (default parameters) in the chard genome assembly, both tools are part of GenomeTools v1.6.1 [\(Gremme et al., 2013](#page-9-0)). The identified LTR-RTs were used for classification and annotation by LTR\_retriever v2.8.7 ([Ou and Jiang, 2018](#page-9-0)) (default parameters). Intact LTR-RTs were further used to estimate the insertion age of the LTR-RTs with a previously described method [\(SanMiguel et al., 1998](#page-9-0)) considering the divergence between the two flanking LTR segments and the mutation rate. In our analysis the mutation rate of *A. thaliana* 1.5 × 10<sup>−</sup> 8 as previously proposed by others was used ([Koch et al., 2000](#page-9-0); [Weber et al., 2013](#page-9-0)).

The same LTR-RT annotation approach was applied on the genome assemblies of sugar beet and sea beet. Shared elements of the nonredundant LTR-RT library (LTR\_retriever output) were searched using blastn v2.9.0 [\(Altschul et al., 1990\)](#page-8-0). Elements were considered as shared if they showed a sequence identity of at least 80 % and shared at least 80 % of their length.

Based on synteny information we identified the chard scaffold cha\_scaffold1166 that corresponds to the sugar beet scaffold containing the *Rz2* locus (Bvchr3\_un.sca001). We matched the mRNA sequence of the intact *Rz2* gene from *B. v. maritima* (mar\_g9573) against the chard scaffold cha\_scaffold1166 using blastn. The insertion position was compared between chard, sugar beet, and *B. patula* by comparing the sequences of the blastn output (qseq and sseq in the -outfmt option). Similarly, we identified the *B* locus in chard by matching the *BvBTC1*  gene (Bv2\_045920\_gycn) onto the corresponding chard scaffold cha\_scaffold4538 using blastn. The Integrative Genomics Viewer ([Robinson](#page-9-0)  [et al., 2011\)](#page-9-0) was used for graphical display.

#### *4.5. Gene prediction*

Gene prediction was performed using the AUGUSTUS pipeline ([Stanke et al., 2008](#page-9-0)). Evidence of transcription was provided by mapping mRNA-seq read-pairs derived from chard leaves and roots generated in this study and mRNA-seq reads from sugar beet generated previously against the chard genome assembly ([Dohm et al., 2014](#page-8-0); [Minoche et al., 2015](#page-9-0)). The AUGUSTUS software combines *in silico* gene prediction with assessing the reliability of gene models based on transcript data, so that evidence support is reported in a range between 0%– 100% for each gene model. Full support (100 %) indicates that all important gene features, like splice sites, transcription start, and polyadenylation sites, are confirmed by experimentally determined cDNA sequences. Such gene models have the highest probability of being correct. On the other hand, predictions with 0 % evidence should be treated with caution, however, they can still represent genuine genes with a restricted pattern of expression that makes it hard to collect transcription evidence for them. A repeat hint file was created using RepeatMasker open-4.0.6 (parameters -lib -gff -no\_is -norna -nolow) based on a library containing a repeat catalog of transposons and retrotransposons in sugar beet and spinach ([Minoche et al., 2015\)](#page-9-0). Crossmatch was used as search engine for sequence comparison. To create the intronic and exonic hint files from chard and sugar beet mRNA-seq data, recommendations by the authors of the AUGUSTUS pipeline [\(Stanke](#page-9-0)  [et al., 2008\)](#page-9-0) were followed. The Perl script RNA-seq-noise-reduction.pl ([Minoche et al., 2015\)](#page-9-0) was used to reduce noise within mRNA-seq data so that exon-intron boundaries were more clearly recognisable which had been shown to increase the output of correct gene models ([Minoche et al., 2015](#page-9-0)). As a drawback of the reduction of intronic noise, genuine but rare transcript isoforms may fail to get reported. The hint files were concatenated and the obtained file extrinsic.cfg was used during gene prediction. AUGUSTUS v2.7 was run on the chard genome using parameters optimised for gene prediction in Caryophyllales ([Minoche et al., 2015](#page-9-0)) (parameters -species = beta\_vulgaris -hintsfile -extrinsicCfgFile = extrinsic.cfg -UTR = on -alternatives-from-evidence = true -allow\_hinted\_splicesites = atac).

#### *4.6. Identification of alternatively spliced transcripts*

Transcript isoforms to chard genes were identified using SplAdder ([Kahles et al., 2016\)](#page-9-0). This program takes RNA-Seq alignment results in BAM format and an annotation file in GFF format as input to identify alternative splicing events. Chard mRNA-seq data were aligned against <span id="page-8-0"></span>the chard genome assembly using the splice-aware alignment program STAR v2.4.2a (Dobin et al., 2013). Individual alignment files were merged using samtools v1.3 ([Li, 2011](#page-9-0)) and used as input for SplAdder together with a GFF file containing the exon coordinates of chard genes with *>* = 1 % of evidence support. SplAdder was run with the parameters -a gff -b bam -ignore\_mismatches = y -v y -c 3 -M merge\_graphs -T y -V n -n 100 -P y -p n -parallel = 10 -t exon\_skip,intron\_retention,alt\_3prime,alt\_5prime, mutex\_exons,mult\_exon skip -o.

#### *4.7. Orthology assessment and synteny visualization*

Translated sequences of the gene sets of chard (this work), sugar beet (Dohm et al., 2014), sea beet, and *B. patula* [\(Rodríguez Del Río et al.,](#page-9-0)  [2019\)](#page-9-0) were used as input for OrthoFinder v1.1.5 (default parameters) ([Emms and Kelly, 2015](#page-9-0)). The input sequences were supported by at least 1 % transcription evidence, had a minimum length of ten amino acids, and each gene was represented by only one peptide sequence. The primary transcript as assigned by AUGUSTUS was selected, i.e. the transcript showing the highest fraction of features being supported by mRNA-seq evidence. The OrthoFinder output was analysed and prepared for synteny visualization with custom scripts written in bash and Perl 5. The Perl script GeneConnect.pl [\(Rodríguez Del Río et al., 2019\)](#page-9-0) was used for generating genome-wide synteny pictures.

#### *4.8. Variant identification*

Variants were identified after mapping of Illumina paired-end reads from chard against the sugar beet reference genome assembly RefBeet-1.2 (Dohm et al., 2014) using bwa v0.6.1 ([Li and Durbin, 2009\)](#page-9-0) with setting -n 3 and samtools v0.1.18 [\(Li, 2011](#page-9-0)). Samtools mpileup was run with settings -q 30 -uf, and the output was analysed with custom scripts written in bash and Perl. For the variant allele frequency (VAF) plot variants were required to have a VAF of at least 10 % and to be supported by at least one forward and one reverse mapping read. High-confidence homozygous variants were required to be supported by more than ten and less than 200 reads, to have an average read mapping quality greater 30, a VAF of at least 90 %, only one alternative allele, and support by a least five reads mapping to either strand. For the variant distribution plot, the number of high-confidence homozygous variants was summarized in intervals of 2 kbp shifted by 1 kbp along RefBeet-1.2 scaffolds.

### **Significance statement**

Chard (*Beta vulgaris* ssp. *vulgaris* var. *cicla*) is a vegetable crop grown

for its leaves but little is know about its genome so far. We have generated a genome sequence of chard and performed genome-wide gene prediction that allowed us to conduct comparisons to the wellstudied sister taxon sugar beet and to the wild beets *Beta patula* as well as *Beta vulgaris* ssp. *maritima*, the ancestor of all beet crops. The study sheds light onto genomic features of chard and provides a foundation for further molecular studies of beet cultigroups.
