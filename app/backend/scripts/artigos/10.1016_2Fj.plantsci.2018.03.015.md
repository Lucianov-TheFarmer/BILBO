#### **Highlights:**

- PEPCase activity was positive correlation with protein content but negative with oil content in cottonseed.
- RNA interference of *GhPEPC2* increased oil content and reduced protein content in cottonseed.
- In transgenic lines, up-regulated genes were mainly involved in lipid synthesis pathways, while down-regulated ones were almost contained in amino acid metabolism.
- RNA interference of *GhPEPC*2 enhanced salt tolerance of cotton seedlings.

#### ABSTRACT

Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPCase) mainly produces oxaloacetic acid for tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Here we reported that *GhPEPC2* silencing with *PEPC2*-RNAi vector could regulate oil and protein accumulation in cottonseeds. In *GhPEPC2* transgenic plants, PEPCase activities in immature embryos were significantly reduced, and the oil content in seed kernel was increased 7.3 percentages, whereas total proteins decreased 5.65 percentages. Compared to wild type, agronomical traits of transgenic plant were obviously unaffected. Furthermore, gene expression profile of *GhPEPC2* transgenic seeds were investigated using RNA-seq, most lipid synthesis related genes were up-regulated, but amino acid metabolic related genes were down-regulated. In addition, the *GhPEPC2* transgenic cotton seedlings were stressed using sodium salts at seedling stage, and the salt tolerance was significantly enhanced. Our observations of *GhPEPC2* in cotton would shade light on understanding the regulation of oil content, protein accumulation and salt tolerance enhancement in other plants.

#### 1. Introduction

Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPCase; EC 4.1.1.31) catalyzes the irreversible carboxylation of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) to yield oxaloacetate (OAA) and Pi [1]. PEPCase is widely present in plants, green algae and cyanobacteria, most archaea and non-photosynthetic bacteria [2]. PEPCs in plants belong to a small multigene family with several plant-type PEPCs, along with at least one distantly related bacterial-type PEPC [3]. The plant type *PEPC* genes encode 105-110 kDa polypeptides that have a conserved N-terminal serine-phosphorylation domain [4]. PEPC located at the core of plant C-metabolism which catalyzed the irreversible  $\beta$ -carboxylation of phosphoenolpyruvate in the presence of HCO<sup>-3</sup> and Mg<sup>2+</sup> to form oxaloacetate and inorganic phosphate [5]. PEPCase appears to participate in diverse physiological and cellular processes, and the functions of PEPCase have been extensively studied for the role of assimilating atmospheric CO<sub>2</sub> in C<sub>4</sub> and crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) plants [5, 6]. Besides the fundamental role of PEPCase in photosynthesis, this

enzyme has many other roles including sustaining carbon-nitrogen interactions [7], fruit ripening [8], seed formation and germination [9], and helps plants to acclimatize to abiotic stresses [10, 11].

Approaches on enhancing the expression of oil related genes to increase oil content had been well studied [12-14]. Researches also explored the PEPCs taken part in regulating carbon flux distribution. The activity of PEPCase highly correlated to the contents of protein and lipid in soybean seed [15]. Based on previous researches, a hypothesis, "substrate competition", insisted that the lipid and protein composed the main storage materials in seed, which were derived from the intermediate product of glycolysis, and exists as "substrate competition" between lipid and protein metabolism [13, 16]. The activities of PEPCase and acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACCase) determined the metabolism efficiency of protein and lipids, respectively. Studies on increased oil accumulation by overexpression the subunits of ACCase have been reported in Arabidopsis thaliana [17], Nicotiana tabacum [18] and Gossypium hirsutum [14]. It was reported that the carbon flowing into the lipids metabolism could be promoted via inhibiting the activity of PEPCase. Down-regulating the expression of pepc2 could increase oil content by 14% to 28% in Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, reflecting the negative correlation between pepc2 and oil content [19]. The TAG level can be increased by 20% through knockdown the expression of C. reinhardtii PEPC1 gene, whereas decreased by 37% under over-expression of PEPC1 [20]. At the same time, down-regulation of AhPEPC1 gene increased the lipid content by 5.7% to 10.3% in peanuts (Arachis hypogaea) [21]. It was reported that down-regulated the expression of GhPEPC1 could increase the oil accumulation in Upland cotton [22]. Those work indicated that the lipid content can be changed by regulating the expression of *PEPC* gene in oil crops.

The first full length cDNA of *PEPC* (*GhPEPC*1, GenBank: AF008939.1) in cotton was cloned from developing fiber with RACE method [23]. Another *GhPEPC*2 (GenBank: EU032328.1) in cotton

was isolated from *G hirsutum* cv. Zhongmian No. 35 with RACE-PCR [24]. The molecular basis of *GhPEPC*1 and *GhPEPC*2 genes in cotton fiber had been characterized. The fiber rapid elongation phase requires high activity of PEPC, probably through the expression of the *GhPEPC*1 and *GhPEPC*2 genes [25]. Recently, the utilization of *GhPEPC*1 interference on regulating lipid metabolism has been reported [22]. Dramatically, the release of cotton genomes sequences including the genome data of diploid cotton species *G. raimondii* [26, 27] and *G arboreum* [28], as well as allotetraploid cotton species of *G hirsutum* [29, 30] and *G barbadense* [31, 32], provide accurate and comprehensive references for cotton omics study. Some transcriptomic analysis on cottonseed with RNA-seq were carried out [33-35], for improving the networks of oil biosynthesis metabolism in cotton..

To explore the molecular basis for the influence on down-regulated *GhPEPC2*, RNA silencing vector of the conserved fragment of *GhPEPC2* was constructed and transformed into cotton cultivar Xu244. Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-Seq) on cotton embryos of 10, 20 and 30 days post anthesis (DPA) in the transgenic lines and wild-type (WT) was performed, and genes related to lipid and amino acid metabolism in cottonseed was identified. Furthermore, seed germination test and seedling root scanning investigation under salt stress were carried out. The correlation between oil content and salt tolerance was estimated in cotton seedlings.

#### 2. Materials and methods

#### 2.1. Plant materials and sampling

Upland cotton (*G. hirsutum* L.) cultivars 'Xu 244' and 'Coker 201' were grown under normal conditions in the experimental field of China Agricultural University. With desired agronomic

characters of Xu244 was chosen as the receptor for transformation. The tissues including ovary, anther, sepal, petal, ovule, root, stem and leaves, were sampled from 'Coker 201', following promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for DNA and RNA extraction.

#### 2.2. GhPEPC genes identification and expression analysis

The annotation and coding sequence data of the *G hirsutum* genome [30] were downloaded from CottonGen website [36]. *GhPEPC* family genes were identified with phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase family domain (IPR021135), Lys active site (IPR018129) and His active site (IPR033129) on InterProScan website (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/). The online tool of ExPASy was used to predict the theoretical molecular weight (MW) of the GhPEPC proteins. Multiple sequences alignment of *GhPEPC* was performed using clustalW, then unrooted phylogenetic tree with neighbor joining method was constructed with MEGA6. Bootstrap tests with 1000 replications were carried out to evaluate the statistical reliability [37].

To explore *GhPEPC* genes expression in cotton tissues, the public RNA-seq datasets were downloaded from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA: PRJNA248163). The gene expression level was estimated by fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) value with TopHat and Cufflinks.

#### 2.3. DNA, RNA isolation and cDNA reverse transcription

Genomic DNA was extracted from leaves of cotton according to Paterson et al. [38] and treated with RNaseA to eliminate the presence of RNA. Total RNA extraction was carried out using cetyltrimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) method [39]. The reverse transcription of cDNA was carried out with PrimeScript<sup>TM</sup> RT reagent Kit (TAKARA, Tokyo, Japan) following the manufacturer's protocol.

#### 2.4. RNAi vector construction and transgenic plants identification

Total RNA of immature embryos of Coker 201 at 20 DPA was extracted and used for cDNA synthesis. The 601-bp conserved fragment corresponding to 1773-2373 nt of *GhPEPC2* (GenBank Accession No.: EU032328.1) was amplified from the cDNA with primers of P601F and P601R (Supplementary Table S1). The sense *GhPEPC2* 601-bp fragment was amplified by primers P601SF and P601SR (with *XhoI* and *EcoRI* restriction sites) and the anti-sense *GhPEPC2* 601-bp fragment was amplified by primers P601AF and P601AR (with *CaII* and *BamHI* restriction sites), and the amplicons were cloned into intermediate vector of pKANNIBAL [40]. The recombination RNAi region with inverted repeat sequences was driven by the promoter of  $\alpha$ -global protein (AGP) B gene, forming the pKANNIBAL $\alpha$ -601S+601A vector. Then PEPC601-RNAi expression cassette was ligated into *NotI* restricted binary vector of pART27 [41], named as *PEPC2*-RNAi. Xu244 works as recipient for *PEPC2*-RNAi transformation using pollen tube pathway method [42]. The neomycin phosphotransferase gene (*NPTII*) driven by the Nos promoter (Nos P) confers resistance to kanamycin, served as a selective marker for transgenic plants.

The putative transgenic materials were detected preliminary by kanamycin (5 g/L), then confirmed with PCR using primer pairs P1F and P1R (Supplementary Table S1).  $T_1$  generation of *GhPEPC2* transgenic plants with high oil content in cottonseed were chosen for copy number validation with southern blot.

#### 2.5. Semi-quantitative RT-PCR and quantitative real-time PCR analysis

For semi-quantitative RT-PCR (semi-qRT-PCR) analysis, cDNA samples were normalized using a pair of primers (P2F and P2R, Supplementary Table S1) designed for *G. hirsutum* actin (*GhACT*1, GenBank Accession No.: AY305723.1 [39]). semi-qRT-PCR was performed using normalized cDNA as template and the gene-specific primers P3F and P3R (Supplementary Table S1), under non-saturating PCR

conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5min; 22 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s; 60 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 1 min; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The obtained fragments were migrated together with a molecular marker in a 0.8% agarose gel, visualized under UV light and recorded.

The quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis of all cDNA samples was carried out using SYBR Premix EX Taq<sup>TM</sup> kit (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan) following the manufacturer's protocol. qRT-PCR reactions with triplet technique replicates were performed with ABI 7500 Real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City CA, USA). The qRT-PCR for *GhPEPC2* and other oil related genes was conducted using the following 25  $\mu$ L reaction mixture: 12.5  $\mu$ L real-time PCR master mix, 2  $\mu$ L first-strand cDNA sample, 1  $\mu$ L gene-specific forward and reverse primers (Supplementary Table S1), and 9.5  $\mu$ L nuclease-free water. The amplification parameters were as follows: 95 °C for 10 min, 1 cycle; 95 °C 5 s, 61 °C 34 s, 40 cycles; 95 °C 15 s, 61 °C 60 s, 95 °C 5 s, 40 cycles. The quantification of PCR products was conducted using the 2<sup>-ΔΔCT</sup> method. *GhUBQ7* (GenBank Accession No.: DQ116441) was used as the internal control.

#### 2.6. Lipid, sugar and protein content detection and phenotypic investigation of transgenic cotton

Seeds of the T<sub>2</sub> transgenic lines with single copy of insert (line L2) were harvested and grown in field. The T<sub>3</sub> transgenic plants were used for lipid, sugar and protein content determination (LK009-1, LK017-2, LK017-5, LK017-6 and LK090-3). Total lipid (crude lipid) content in mature cotton seeds was quantified using Soxhlet extraction method [43], the total proteins (crude proteins) in seeds were extracted from mature seeds following Kjeldahl Method [44]. Soluble sugars were quantified following authrone reagent method [45].

Phenotypic traits of  $T_3$  transgenic lines, including plant height, height of primary branch, branch number, boll number and boll weight were investigated in the field. Some quality traits such as fiber

length, upper half mean length, uniformity index, micronaire value and elongation rate were quantified as well.

#### 2.7. RNA sequencing and functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs

Total RNA of T<sub>5</sub> transgenic embryo of LM009 and LM010 (from T<sub>3</sub> lines LK009-1 and LK017-5) and the WT at developing stages 10 DPA, 20 DPA and 30 DPA were isolated. A total of 100 ng purified mRNA was selected for cDNA library construction using NEB Next® Ultra<sup>TM</sup> RNA library prep kit (NEB, Ipswich MA, America), following the manufacturer protocol, then applied for 150 base paired-end sequencing on Illumina Hiseq<sup>TM</sup> 2500 platform. The data was cleaned with FASTX-Toolkit (http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx\_toolkit/). The clean data were deposited in NCBI SRA section with BioProject No. PRJNA419446.

The genome data sets of Upland cotton (70,478 genes) [30] were downloaded from CottonGen website [36]. The clean reads of each sample were aligned to the reference genome data by TopHat (Version 2.0.1) (http://tophat.cbcb.umd.edu/), and reads for each gene were counted by htseq-count software [46]. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with R package DESeq [47] at the threshold of false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01 and fold change > 2. Gene expression level was determined by FPKM value with Cufflinks (Version 2.2.1). Gene ontology (GO) was performed on WEGO website (http://wego.genomics.org.cn/), kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was enriched using ccNET website [48].

#### 2.8. Germination test and root architecture analysis of cotton seedlings under salt stress

Seed germination testing, root architecture observation and seed RNA sequencing of  $T_5$  transgenic lines LM009 and LM010 and the control were performed. Seeds of  $T_5$  transgenic lines LM009 and

LM010 and wild-type were germinated under 5% (mass concentration) NaCl stress and normal condition by filtration paper cylinder method at room temperature (25 °C). Germination rate was scored at three days after sowing, bud length was measured and ten fresh buds were weighted at seven days after sowing.

The hydroponics cotton seedlings of T<sub>5</sub> transgenic lines (LM009 and LM010) and WT, were used for root architecture analysis. Cotton seeds were germinated in sands till the cotyledons fully expanded, seedlings were transplanted for hydroponics in Hoagland solution. After four days renewal cultivation, seedlings were treated with 150 mM NaCl and normal nutrient condition for eight days, then the roots were scanned using Epson Scanner V800 (Seiko Epson Corp., Tokyo, Japan) at 600 dpi. Then scanned images were quantized by WinRHIZO (Version 4.0b, Regent Instruments Inc., Quebec, Canada) [49]. The total root length, total root volume, total root surface area and the number of root tips were measured, and plant height and fresh weight were scored as well. Especially, malondialdehyde (MDA) content in seedling leaves of the transgenic lines and WT after salt stress treatment for eight days was determined with triplicates by thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction [50].

#### 3. Results

#### 3.1. PEPCase activity in cottonseed was negatively correlated with oil content

To check the relationship among PEPCase activity and the protein and lipid contents in cotton seeds, the seeds of 13 cotton cultivars with different protein and lipid contents were employed for further analysis. The variations range from 31.26% to 45.36% on protein accumulation, and from 24.65% to 39.96% on lipid content in cottonseeds. The correlation formula between protein content (X) and lipid

content (Y) of the 13 cultivars was expressed as y=-0.34x+33.45, with  $R^2=0.90$  (Fig. 1A). PEPCase activity (Y) was positively correlated to protein contents (X) (y=2.11x-1.30,  $R^2=0.85$ ) (Fig. 1B), whereas PEPCase activity (Y) showed negative correlation to lipid contents (X) (y=-1.91x+141.02,  $R^2=0.82$ ) (Fig. 1C), suggesting that the PEPCase activity was crucial to protein and lipid setting in plant seeds. The result indicated that the protein and lipid contents could be modified via regulating PEPCase activity in cottonseeds, and the approach of increased seed oil content by down-regulating of *PEPC* is anticipating.

3.2. Genome-wide identification of GhPEPC homologs and the expression patterns in Upland cotton

*GhPEPC* homologs were identified in Upland cotton. A total of 17 *GhPEPC* family genes were annotated in Upland cotton, and could be divided into three groups according to PEPC annotated in Arabidopsis: PEPC1 (At1g53310), PEPC3 (At3g14940) and PEPC4 (At1g68750) (Supplementary Table S2). The typical *PEPC* genes encode 100-118 kDa polypeptides [3, 4], contains the phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase family domain (IPR021135), Lys active site (IPR018129) and His active site (IPR033129) [51, 52]. Based on this threshold, ten *GhPEPC* genes were detected in Upland cotton (Supplementary Table S2). Phylogenetic analysis using the *GhPEPC* coding sequences, the cloned *GhPEPC* genes of *GhPEPC*1 (GenBank: AF008939.1) and *GhPEPC*2 (GenBank: EU032328.1), and the 4 types of Arabidopsis *PEPC* genes (ATPEPC1, ATPEPC2, ATPEPC3 and ATPEPC4) acting as outgroup (Fig. 2A) showed that *GhPEPC*1 and *GhPEPC*2 belonged to two distant branches. Furthermore, *GhPEPC*2 was vested in the class of PEPC3, *Gh\_A01G0390* and *Gh\_D01G2339* had closer relationship with *GhPEPC2*. *GhPEPC*1 was assigned to the class of PEPC1, sequence of *Gh\_A09G0010* was mostly similar with that of *GhPEPC*1 (Fig. 2A).

Based on public RNA-Seq data of multiple cotton tissues, the expression patterns of GhPEPC

genes in Upland cotton could draw a global view. Expression level of *GhPEPC*1 (Gh\_A09G0010) was elevated in leaves and 10 DPA fiber, but decreasing in developing ovules. Another isoform of *GhPEPC*1 (Gh\_D12G0808) was not detectable expression in any Upland cotton tissues. The homologous of *GhPEPC*2 (Gh\_A01G0390 and Gh\_D01G2339) was more abundant in petal and root. Here, we highlight that the two homologous genes (Gh\_A08G2074 and Gh\_D08G2655) of *GhPEPC*2 were highly expressed in developing fiber and ovules (Fig. 2B), suggesting that *GhPEPC*2 might play more important role than *GhPEPC*1 in cottonseed. Since RNA interference of *GhPEPC*1 could regulate fatty acids biosynthesis [22], the regulating *GhPEPC*2 expression could generate greater effect than *GhPEPC*1 in seed oil accumulation.

#### 3.3. Down-regulation of GhPEPC2 decreased PEPCase activity

To view the effects of *GhPEPC* on cottonseed oil accumulation, the conserved 601 bp segment of *GhPEPC2* (GenBank Accession No.: EU032328.1) was selected for RNA interference approach analysis. Semi-quantitative RT-PCR result showed that *GhPEPC2* was expressed in vegetative and reproductive tissues (Supplementary Fig. S1A), indicating *GhPEPC2* was involved in basal metabolism of cotton. Future more, *GhPEPC2* exhibited higher expression in anther, petal and ovule than in other tissues (Supplementary Fig. S1A), in accordance with the trends of public RNA-Seq data (Fig. 2). To avoid the negative effects on cotton growth and development, RNAi vector driven by AGP promoter, seed-specific expressed, was constructed and transformed into cotton (Supplementary Fig. S1B). The activity of AGP promoter was verified by Gus transient expression staining on embryonic callus (Supplementary Fig. S1C). *GhPEPC2* in transgenic plants was identified with PCR, and the copy numbers of *GhPEPC2* was validated ranging from one to four (Supplementary Fig. S2). The T<sub>3</sub> lines LK009-1, LK017-2, LK017-5, LK017-6 and LK090-3, derived from line L2 (T<sub>1</sub>) containing a single

insert, were chosen for further investigation.

The expression patterns of *GhPEPC*2 in WT and *PEPC*2-RNAi transgenic lines (T<sub>3</sub>) were detected by semi-quantitative RT-PCR and qRT-PCR. Compared with WT, *GhPEPC*2 in root, leaf and stem of transgenic lines showed implicit expression difference, but specific down-regulation in immature seed of transgenic line LK017-5 (Supplementary Fig. S3A). In contrast to WT plants, *GhPEPC*2 expression in immature seeds (20 DPA) of transgenic lines LK009-1, LK017-2, LK017-5, LK017-6 and LK090-3 were all down-regulated (Supplementary Fig. S3B), indicating *GhPEPC*2 expression in transgenic plants was suppressed. At the same time, PEPCase activity in immature embryos (20 DPA) of both WT and transgenic lines were measured as well, it displayed that PEPCase activity in immature embryo was reduced significantly by 37%, 33%, 40%, 39% and 41% of transgenic lines LK009-1, LK017-2, LK017-5, LK017-6 and LK090-3, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3C), suggesting that the silencing construct has taken effect on *GhPEPC* expression inhibiting and consequently reduced PEPCase activity.

Furthermore, the expression profiles of cottonseed at developmental stages 10 DPA, 20 DPA and 30 DPA of two transgenic lines (LM009 and LM01) and the control (WT) were generated using RNA-seq, resulting in a total of 36 Gb RNA-Seq data, about 4 Gb data for each sample. High quality data was scored and sent for the further analysis (Supplementary Table S3). All clean reads were aligned to Upland cotton genome [30], with 90% mapped rates and 80% concordant pair alignment rates (Supplementary Table S4). *Gh\_A08G2074* and *Gh\_D08G2655*, highly expressed *GhPEPC* genes, displayed decreasing expression during cottonseed development (Supplementary Fig. S4). Although PEPC3 class genes were abundant in cottonseed (Supplementary Fig. S5), PEPC1 and PEPC4 ones were barely expressed in the transgenic and WT lines (Supplementary Fig. S5). Genes in PEPC3 class

were distinctly down-regulated in developing cottonseed at 20 DPA transgenic plants (Supplementary Fig. S5A). No obvious expression difference of genes in PEPC1 and PEPC4 classes was detected between the transgenic lines and the WT (Supplementary Fig. S5B, C). Consequently, nucleotide sequences of PEPC3 class genes were highly conserved, showing more than 90% sequence identify to the 601 bp fragment used for RNAi approach in this study (Supplementary Fig. S6), but *GhPEPC* genes in PEPC1 and PEPC4 classes exhibited distant branches (Fig. 2A).

#### 3.4. Silencing GhPEPC2 increased seed oil content

Since lipid, protein and sugar were the three major components in mature cottonseeds, these three main components were detected in transgenic lines and WT plants. Compared with the WT, in mature seeds of transgenic lines, oil content was significantly increased by 28.7%, total protein was decreased by 12.7% and soluble sugar was no obvious difference (Fig. 3), indicating that silencing *GhPEPC*2 in seed has taken effects. Furthermore, yield traits of transgenic lines LK009-1, LK017-2, LK017-5, LK017-6 and LK090-3 and the WT were investigated (Supplementary Table S5). Compared with the WT, the seed index was higher (Supplementary Fig. S7B, D), the lint index and lint percentages were lower in most transgenic lines (Supplementary Fig. S7A, C). For other quality traits, no difference has significant happened between the WT and transgenic lines, indicating *PEPC*2-RNA silencing did not impact the fiber quality of cotton (Supplementary Table S5).

Subsequently, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in developing cottonseed were identified between the transgenic lines and WT. A total of 362 genes were significantly up-regulated and 1181 genes were down-regulated at 20 DPA (Fig. 4). Obviously, the number of DEGs at 20 DPA was higher than at 10 DPA and 30 DPA. GO enrichment analysis indicated that up-regulated DEGs in the transgenic lines at 20 DPA and 30 DPA enriched in lipid related categories, such as seed oil-body

biogenesis (GO:0010344), lipid storage (GO:0019915), lipid localization (GO:0010876) and lipid particle (GO:0005811), etc. (Supplementary Table S6). Moreover, some lipid metabolism pathways, including alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (ko00592), fatty acid biosynthesis (ko00061) et al, were significant enrichment in transgenic lines at 20 DPA (Supplementary Table S7), indicating that down-regulation of *GhPEPC2* in cottonseed enhanced lipid metabolism. But such trends of the up-regulated DEGs did not occur in cottonseed on 30 DPA.

Especially, gene for the four subunits of pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDHC), subunit biotin carboxylase (BC, At5g35360) of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACCase, EC: 6.4.1.2) expressed much higher in transgenic lines than in WT at 10 DPA and 20 DPA (Supplementary Fig. S8). While *BCCP*1 and *BCCP*2 for subunits biotin carboxyl carrier protein (BCCP) of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACCase, EC: 6.4.1.2) were up-regulated steady in transgenic lines at 10 DPA, 20 DPA and 30 DPA (Supplementary Fig. S8). In addition, genes for ketoacyl-ACP reductase (KAR, EC: 1.1.1.100), hydroxyacyl-ACP dehydrase (HAD, EC: 4.2.1.17) and enoyl-ACP reductase (ENR, EC: 1.3.1.9) were more abundant in transgenic lines, suggesting fatty acid carbon chain elongation process were enhanced with *PEPC2*-RNA silencing (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Furthermore, genes encoding GPDHP (At5g40610), an isoforms of NAD-dependent glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GPDH, EC: 1.1.1.8) was higher expressed with more than 2-fold changes in transgenic line seeds at 10 DPA, 20 DPA and 30 DPA. Genes encoding eight isoforms of glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (GPAT, EC: 2.3.1.15) were detected in cottonseeds, but only gene for isoform *GPAT9* of GPAT was up-regulated in transgenic lines (Fig. 5B, Fig. 6). Genes for 1-acylglycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase 2 (*LPAAT2*, EC: 2.3.1.51) in transgenic lines were enhanced expression at 30 DPA (Fig. 5C, Fig. 6). Phosphatidate phosphatase 2 (*LPP2*, EC: 3.1.3.4) was higher

expressed than other LPPs, but no expression difference was observed between transgenic and the WT cottonseed (Fig. 5D, Fig. 6). Acyl-CoA: diacylglycerol acyltransferase (DGAT, EC: 2.3.1.20) and phospholipid: diacylglycerol acyltransferase (PDAT, EC: 2.3.1.118) catalyze DAG to TAG. In transgenic lines, PDAT2 was up-regulated by 2.26-fold at 20 DPA and 1.58-fold at 30 DPA (Fig. 5E. Fig. 6), DGAT3 was down-regulated at 10 DPA and 20 DPA, but up-regulated on 30 DPA (Fig. 5F, Fig. 6). LPCAT1 (1-acylglycerol-3-phosphocholine acyltransferase) and PDCT (Phosphatidylcholine: diacylglycerol cholinephosphotransferase) were up-regulated expression at 20 DPA and 30 DPA in transgenic lines (Fig. 5G, 5H, Fig. 6). Oil body was composed of a core of TAG surrounded by a monolayer of phospholipids and proteins, such as steroleosins, caleosins and oleosins and caleosins. Genes encoding HSD1 (At5g50600), CLO1 (At4g26740), OLE1 (At4g25140), OLE2 (At5g40420) and OLE4 (At3g01570) for oil body were higher expressed at 20 DPA and 30 DPA in transgenic cottonseed (Fig. 5I-L), suggesting that silencing of GhPEPC2 in cottonseed enhanced oil accumulation. Expression level of twelve oil related genes on 20 DPA were randomly selected for verification by qRT-PCR approach (Fig. 7), gene expression changes between the transgenic lines and WT were highly consistent with the FPKM value (Supplementary Fig. S9).

#### 3.5. Interference of GhPEPC2 weakened amino acid metabolism

To explore the influence of *GhPEPC2* silencing in cottonseed, GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were carried out on the down-regulated DEGs in transgenic lines. Genes mainly involved in the categories of respiratory chain complex IV assembly (GO:0008535), asparagine metabolic process (GO:0006528), asparagine biosynthetic process (GO:0006529) were significant enrichment at 20 DPA. Those ones were obviously deepened in the categories of peptidase activity (GO:0070011), cysteine-type endopeptidase activity (GO:0004197), serine-type peptidase activity (GO:0008236) and

serine hydrolase activity (GO:0017171) at 30 DPA (Supplementary Table S6). KEGG enrichment analysis on those down-regulated DEGs suggested that amino acid metabolism related pathways were significant enrichment at 20 DPA (Supplementary Table S9), mostly including alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism (ko00250), tyrosine metabolism (ko00350) and arginine biosynthesis (ko00220), etc. However, no significant enrichment pathways of the down-regulated DEGs were observed at 30 DPA. Furthermore, the total protein content in cottonseed of transgenic line and WT was measured, it suggested the total protein content was evidently decreased from 44.36% in WT to 38.71% in transgenic line LK017-5 (Fig. 3).

As we know, PEPCase utilizes pyruvate for the synthesis of oxaloacetate acid (OAA), OAA was subsequently catalyzed to produce citrate by citrate synthase (CISY2, EC: 2.3.3.1, At3g58750), citrate was then used for isocitrate synthesis by aconitase (ACO, EC: 4.2.1.3, At4g35830). The influence of *GhPEPC2* silencing on PEPCase was worth tracing. In transgenic lines, genes for CISY2 and ACO were down-regulated in developing cottonseed (Supplementary Fig. S10), those for fumarase (FUR, At2g47510) and malate dehydrogenase (MDHP, EC: 1.1.1.37, At3g47520) were decreased expression level in cottonseed as well (Supplementary Fig. S10). These results indicated that amino acid metabolism and TCA cycle was weakened under the *GhPEPC2* silencing in developing cottonseed (Fig. 3).

#### 3.6. PEPC2-RNAi enhanced salt tolerance of cotton seedlings

In order to unearth the potential of oil content to salt tolerance in cotton, the transgenic lines LM009 and LM010 ( $T_5$ ) were WT was hydroponic cultured under the normal (control) and 150 mM NaCl conditions. After salt stress for eight days, certain growth suppressing reaction occurred on two-week old transgenic seedlings (Fig. 8A), but serious inhibition happened on WT seedlings. Under normal

condition, no significant obvious difference was observed between *PEPC*2-RNAi transgenic seedlings and WT (Fig. 8A). Consequently, plant height and fresh weight of both transgenic and WT seedlings under salt stress were measured, and the above traits of transgenic lines were significantly higher than that of WT. Under normal condition, only plant height of LM009 was higher than that of WT (Fig. 8C-D).

Subsequently, root architecture of *PEPC2*-RNAi transgenic and WT seedlings under salt stress and normal conditions was investigated. The root architecture of both transgenic lines and WT showed no obvious difference under normal condition, but root development inhibition in WT line was severer than that in *PEPC2*-RNAi transgenic lines under salt stress (Fig. 8B). For root length, total root volume, total root surface area, and number of root tips, the transgenic seedlings displayed significant larger parts than WT (Fig. 8E-H). Since the concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) in root reflects the injuring, the MDA content in *PEPC2*-RNAi transgenic lines and WT under salt stress was detected. The MDA content in transgenic seedlings was distinctly lower than that in WT (Supplementary Fig. S11), suggesting that *PEPC2*-RNAi transgenic lines was evaluated as well. No obvious difference on germinability of *PEPC2*-RNAi transgenic lines and WT was displayed, both under normal and salt stress conditions, but the bud length and bud fresh weight of *PEPC2*-RNAi transgenic lines was distinctly increased under salt stress (Supplementary Fig. S12), indicating that the salt tolerance of *PEPC2*-RNAi transgenic seedlings was enhanced.

#### 4. Discussion

Vegetable oil is an important edible product and valuable industrial feed stocks, especially as a resource

of biodiesel [53]. The demand of vegetable oil has been increased dramatically with the growth of global economy by exhausting petroleum resources [54]. Cotton (*Gossypium* spp) is one of the most important cash and oil crops in the world, cotton seed oil is widely used as costly bioenergy source (food and biodiesel). Utilizing genes encoding enzymes and transcription factors regulating carbon metabolism, FA and TAG synthesis to improve oil production have been documented [12, 14, 55]. In this study, *PEPC*-RNAi has been probed into the inhibiting amino acid metabolism, increasing oil content in cottonseed and enhancing salt tolerant of cotton seedling.

As we know, PEPCase activity is obviously positive correlated to protein content, and negative to lipid content in soybean seed [15]. It is interesting to note that similar trend has been observed in cottonseed as well (Fig. 1), indicating that PEPCase activity could regulate the oil content in cotton. *GhPEPC1* was high expressed in cotton leaves, but *GhPEPC2* was more abundant in developing cottonseed (Supplementary Fig. S5, S6). Down-regulation of *GhPEPC1* could yield more oil in cottonseed [22], *PEPC* down-regulation in *Brassica napus* [16], *C. reinhardtii* [19, 20] and *Arachis hypogaea* [21] also enhanced oil content. Here, suppression of *GhPEPC2* by RNAi could increase oil content and decrease protein content in cottonseed (Fig. 3). It potentially suggested that protein biosynthesis was blocked by *GhPEPC2* silencing, and more precursors derived from glycolysis were served for TAG biosynthesis, leading to more oil accumulation in cottonseed. Our results provide further proof for the hypothesis of 'substrate competition' [13, 16].

RNA-Seq analysis demonstrated that genes involved in lipid metabolism were up-regulated (Supplementary Table S6, S7), while genes related to amino acid metabolism were down-regulated in *PEPC2*-RNAi transgenic lines (Supplementary Tables S6, S9). In transgenic lines, genes encoding isoforms of PDHC and ACCase complex for fatty acid synthesis were up-regulated at10 DPA and 20

DPA (Fig. 6, Supplementary Fig. S8), *KAR*, *HAD* and *ENR* involved in carbon chain elongation cycle were more abundant. Likewise, *GPDH*, *GPAT*9, *PDAT*2, *LPCAT* and *PDCT* for TAG biosynthesis were up-regulated expression at 20 DPA and 30 DPA (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). However, genes participating in the key step of TCA cycle were down-regulated in *PEPC2*-RNAi lines (Supplementary Fig. S10). Similar depression of OAA content was also observed in *GhPEPC*1 RNAi transgenic plants [22].

Furthermore, PEPC2-RNAi can enhance both oil yield in seeds and salt tolerant of seedlings (Fig. 8). Root length, total root volume, total root surface area, and number of root tips were obviously enlarged in PEPC2-RNAi transgenic lines (Fig. 8E-H). Under the salt stress, root architecture structure was changed distinctively, overexpression of GhDof1 in Upland cotton also could increase oil content and enhance salt tolerance as well [56]. In this study, genes involved in fatty acid synthesis and TAG biosynthesis pathway were up-regulated in *PEPC2*-RNAi transgenic lines (Fig. 6), which will supply more precusor substance for phospholipid signaling and phosphatidic acid (PA) synthesis [57]. PA is the main component in membrane lipid, PA and diacylglycerol formation could increase plant tolerance for osmotic stress [58, 59]. Meanwhile, it has demonstrated that the increasing of polyunsaturated fatty acids could boost the mobility of membrane lipids, thus enhance plant salt stresses [60]. In this study, plant height, total root volume and total root surface area were also increased in LM009 transgenic line under normal condition. The cause maybe that seed index was improved in transgenic lines (Supplementary Fig. S4), which was also observed in GhPEPC1-RNAi transgenic plants [22]. MDA was responsible for the production of membrane lipid peroxidation, which has been widely recognized as an index for measuring the oxidative damage degree [61]. In this study, MDA content in PEPC2-RNAi transgenic seedlings were significantly reduced under salt stress (Supplementary Fig. S11), suggesting that PEPC2-RNAi reduced oxidative damage and improved salt tolerance at seedling

stage.

Finally, *GhPEPC*<sup>2</sup> was employed for RNAi approach in Upland cotton, transgenic plants with increased oil content and no negative-effects in agronomical traits were obtained. RNA-Seq approach revealed the molecular basis of *GhPEPC*<sup>2</sup> interfering on developing cottonseed. In the meantime, salt tolerance was enhanced in transgenic seedlings. Hence, the strategy of regulating seed oil content via *PEPC* interfering provides the promising opportunity for high oil and abiotic stress study in other crops.
