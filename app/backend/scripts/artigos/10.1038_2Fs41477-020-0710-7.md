# DREAM complex suppresses DNA methylation maintenance genes and precludes DNA hypermethylation

Yong-Qiang Ning<sup>1,2,5</sup>, Na Liu<sup>1,3,5</sup>, Ke-Ke Lan<sup>1</sup>, Yin-Na Su<sup>1</sup>, Lin Li<sup>1</sup>, She Chen<sup>1,4</sup> and Xin-Jian He<sup>1,4</sup>

The DNA methyltransferases MET1 and CMT3 are known to be responsible for maintenance of DNA methylation at symmetric CG and CHG sites, respectively, in *Arabidopsis thaliana*. However, it is unknown how the expression of methyltransferase genes is regulated in different cell states and whether change in expression affects DNA methylation at the whole-genome level. Using a reverse genetic screen, we identified TCX5, a tesmin/TSO1-like CXC domain-containing protein, and demonstrated that it is a transcriptional repressor of genes required for maintenance of DNA methylation, which include *MET1*, *CMT3*, *DDM1*, *KYP* and *VIMs*. TCX5 functions redundantly with its paralogue TCX6 in repressing the expression of these genes. In the *tcx5 tcx6* double mutant, expression of these genes is markedly increased, thereby leading to markedly increased DNA methylation at CHG sites and, to a lesser extent, at CG sites at the whole-genome level. Furthermore, our whole-genome DNA methylation analysis indicated that the CG and CHG methylation level is lower in differentiated quiescent cells than in dividing cells in the wild type but is comparable in the *tcx5/6* mutant, suggesting that TCX5/6 are required for maintenance of the difference in DNA methylation between the two cell types. We identified TCX5/6-containing multi-subunit complexes, which are known as DREAM in other eukaryotes, and demonstrated that the *Arabidopsis* DREAM components function as a whole to preclude DNA hypermethylation. Given that the DREAM complexes are conserved from plants to animals, the preclusion of DNA hypermethylation by DREAM complexes may represent a conserved mechanism in eukaryotes.

NA methylation at cytosine is a conserved epigenetic mark that is required for genome stability, transposon silencing and the regulation of gene expression in eukaryotes<sup>1</sup>. In Arabidopsis thaliana, DNA methylation is enriched in pericentromeric heterochromatin and, to a lesser extent, in chromosome arms<sup>2</sup>. DNA methylation at symmetric CG sites is sustained by the DNMT1-like maintenance DNA methyltransferase MET1 after DNA replication<sup>3,4</sup>. In mammals, an SRA domain-containing methylcytosine-binding protein, UHRF1, targets DNMT1 to hemi-methylated DNA and is thereby involved in maintenance of DNA methylation after replication<sup>5</sup>. The Arabidopsis UHRF1-like proteins VIM1, VIM2 and VIM3 have a conserved role in maintaining DNA methylation<sup>6-8</sup>. DNA methylation at symmetric CHG (H is A, T or C) sites is exclusively maintained by the plant-specific DNA methyltransferase CMT3 (ref. <sup>4</sup>). The Su(var)3-9-like histone methyltransferases KYP/SUVH4, SUVH5 and SUVH6 mediate the repressive histone modification H3K9me2 and cooperate with CMT3 to facilitate the formation of a feed-forward loop between CHG methylation and histone H3K9 dimethylation<sup>9,10</sup>. The DNA methyltransferase DRM2 is guided through an RNA-directed DNA methylation pathway to mediate de novo DNA methylation at CHH sites and, to a lesser extent, at CG and CHG sites<sup>11-13</sup>.

DNA methylation can be actively reduced by a base excision repair pathway that is initiated by a family of 5-methylcytosine glycosylases<sup>14-16</sup>. The 5-methylcytosine glycosylases ROS1, DML2 and DML3 are ubiquitously expressed and mediate active DNA demethylation at transposable elements adjacent to protein-coding genes, thereby preventing DNA methylation and transcriptional silencing of nearby genes<sup>17,18</sup>. The 5-methylcytosine glycosylase

DME is specifically expressed in both the central cell and vegetative cell to mediate DNA demethylation, which reinforces transposon methylation in female and male gametes, respectively, and thereby enhances transposon silencing and genome stability across generations<sup>14,19,20</sup>. Although DME-dependent DNA demethylation occurs in the maternal genome but not in the paternal genome of the endosperm, the DNA methylation levels of both maternal and paternal genomes are lower in the endosperm than in the embryo<sup>21,22</sup>, suggesting that a DME-independent mechanism may be responsible for DNA demethylation of the endosperm. DNA demethylation can also occur passively when the expression of methylation maintenance genes such as *MET1* and *CMT3* is reduced<sup>23</sup>. However, it is largely unknown how the expression of these genes is reduced and whether this reduction is responsible for genome-wide DNA demethylation.

Genes encoding maintenance DNA methyltransferases in plants are highly expressed in dividing cells but not in differentiated quiescent cells<sup>24,25</sup>, suggesting that maintenance of DNA methylation is coordinated with the rate of cell division and thereby ensures that hemi-methylated DNA can be completely methylated after DNA replication during cell division. It is well known that the expression of hundreds of cell cycle genes is periodically regulated during cell division<sup>26</sup>. The retinoblastoma (RB)-E2F pathway is responsible for regulating the expression of cell cycle genes. In mammals, RB-like proteins interact with DP1, E2Fs and MuvB core components, thereby forming a multi-subunit complex which is now termed the DREAM (DP, RB-like protein, E2F and MuvB core) complex<sup>27,28</sup>. The MuvB core consists of LIN9, LIN37, LIN52, LIN54 and RBBP4 (ref. <sup>29</sup>). The DREAM complex functions as a transcriptional repressor

In *Arabidopsis*, partial orthologues of DREAM complex subunits were shown to have a conserved role in regulating the expression of cell cycle genes and thereby to affect growth and development[30,](#page-13-23)[31](#page-13-24). Considering that maintenance of DNA methylation occurs shortly after DNA replication during cell division, it remains to be determined whether the expression of genes required for maintenance of DNA methylation is regulated by the DREAM complex. If the latter inference is correct, it will be important to determine whether the DREAM complex helps regulate DNA methylation at the whole-genome level.

In the current study we performed a reverse genetic screen to identify new regulators of DNA demethylation, and this led to the identification of TCX5, a tesmin/TSO1-like CXC domain-containing transcription factor[32](#page-13-25). Our subsequent experiments demonstrated that TCX5 and its close homologue, TCX6, function redundantly to preclude DNA hypermethylation not only at transposons but also at gene flanking regions, thereby suppressing transcriptional silencing. TCX5 and TCX6 (TCX5/6) bind to the promoters of cell cycle genes, as well as to the promoters of genes required for maintenance of DNA methylation, and thereby repress the expression of these genes. In contrast, they do not affect the expression of genes involved in RNA-directed DNA methylation. We demonstrated that TCX5/6 preclude DNA hypermethylation in differentiated quiescent cells and, to a lesser extent, in dividing cells, by repressing the expression of methylation maintenance genes. We also identified an integral *Arabidopsis* DREAM complex and demonstrated that TCX5/6 function as subunits of the complex in regulation of DNA demethylation. The study reveals how maintenance of DNA methylation is coordinated with cell division in different tissues, and demonstrates that the DREAM complex is responsible for the preclusion of genomic DNA hypermethylation by reducing the expression of methylation maintenance genes.

### **Results**

**Identification of TCX5/6 as components required for DNA demethylation and anti-silencing.** To identify new regulators required for DNA demethylation, we collected available transfer DNA mutants and performed a reverse genetic screen to identify those with increased DNA methylation. The collection includes mutants of chromatin-related proteins, RNA metabolism-related proteins and transcriptional regulators associated with chromatin-related proteins (Supplementary Dataset 1). We cleaved the gDNA of mutants with the methylation-specific DNA restriction enzyme McrBC and then performed PCR to screen for mutants showing increased DNA methylation at the genomic locus *DT539* (*AT3G22770*) that are targeted by the active DNA demethylase ROS1 (ref. [33\)](#page-13-26). We found that the DNA methylation level of this locus is increased in the mutants *tcx5* and *ros1* (Fig. [1a](#page-2-0)). Moreover, the DNA methylation levels of two other ROS1-target genomic loci were also increased in the *tcx5* mutant, suggesting that TCX5 mediates DNA demethylation (Fig. [1a\)](#page-2-0). To confirm the function of TCX5 in DNA demethylation, we carried out genetic crossing to introduce the *tcx5* mutation into plants harbouring a constitutive *CaMV 35S* promoter-driven luciferase transgene (*35S-LUC*), which was previously termed *YJ*[34.](#page-13-27) Expression of the *35S-LUC* transgene was lower in the *tcx5* mutant than in the sibling wild type (WT) (Fig. [1b,c](#page-2-0)). Reduced expression of the *35S-LUC* transgene in the *tcx5* mutant was accompanied by increased DNA methylation of the *35S* promoter (Fig. [1d](#page-2-0)), suggesting that TCX5 is required for DNA demethylation and anti-silencing of the *35S-LUC* transgene.

TCX5 is a tesmin/TSO1-like CXC domain-containing transcription factor closely related to its homologue TCX6. We created a *tcx6* mutation (1-base pair (bp) deletion in the first exon of *TCX6*) by clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR)–Cas9 in the *tcx5* mutant background, and thereby generated a *tcx5/tcx6* (*tcx5/6*) double mutant (Supplementary Fig. 1). A *tcx6* single mutant was generated by genetic crossing between the *tcx5/6* double mutant and WT. We then determined how DNA methylation and transcriptional silencing are affected by the *tcx6* and *tcx5/6* mutations. The DNA methylation levels of the three ROS1-target loci shown above were either slightly increased or not increased in the *tcx6* single mutant, but the DNA methylation levels were substantially higher in the *tcx5/6* double mutant than in the *tcx5* single mutant (Fig. [1a](#page-2-0)). Expression of the *35S-LUC* transgene was not notably reduced in the *tcx6* single mutant but was markedly lower in the *tcx5/6* double mutant than in the *tcx5* single mutant (Fig. [1b,c\)](#page-2-0). Consistent with reduction in *35S-LUC* expression, the DNA methylation level of the *35S* promoter was increased in both the *tcx5* single mutant and the *tcx5/6* double mutant but not in the *tcx6* single mutant (Fig. [1d\)](#page-2-0). We also tested the effect of *tcx5/6* on the expression of previously characterized methylated genomic loci including *solo LTR*, *AtMU1* and *AtGP1* (ref. [35\)](#page-13-28), and found that the expression levels of these loci were lower in the *tcx5/6* mutant than in WT (Fig. [1e\)](#page-2-0). These results suggest that TCX5/6 may function redundantly in DNA demethylation and anti-silencing, not only in the transgene but also in endogenous genomic loci.

**TCX5/6 prevent excessive cell proliferation to maintain limited organ size.** The *tcx5* and *tcx6* single mutants have no obvious developmental phenotypes compared to WT, whereas the *tcx5/6* double-mutant plants produced larger organs including cotyledons, rosette leaves, flowers and seeds (Fig. [2a–d](#page-3-0), Supplementary Fig. 2 and Extended Data Fig. 1). Moreover, *tcx5/6* mutant plants had down-turned cotyledons and a weak, late-flowering phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 3). These phenotypes were restored by co-transforming *TCX5* and *TCX6* into the *tcx5/6* mutant (Extended Data Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3), confirming that the *tcx5/6* mutation is responsible for the phenotypes. To determine how cotyledon size is increased in the *tcx5/6* mutant, we examined the cotyledon cell microscopically and found that the cotyledon cell was smaller in the *tcx5/6* mutant than in WT even though the cotyledon was larger in the *tcx5/6* mutant (Fig. [2e\)](#page-3-0), suggesting that the increased size of the cotyledon in the *tcx5/6* mutant was caused by an increase in cell number. Therefore, TCX5/6 may be responsible for inhibition of cell proliferation. The cessation of cell proliferation is frequently accompanied by endoreduplication in mature rosette leaves[36.](#page-13-29) To determine whether *tcx5/6* affects endoreduplication, we compared the DNA content of nuclei from mature rosette leaves in the *tcx5/6* mutant and WT by flow cytometry. The results indicated that the numbers of nuclei with 8C and 16C (C values refer to the copy number of each chromosome) DNA content were notably reduced in the *tcx5/6* mutant relative to WT (Fig. [2f\)](#page-3-0), whereas the numbers of nuclei with 2C and 4C DNA content were either weakly increased or not affected (Fig. [2f](#page-3-0)), indicating that increased cell proliferation in the *tcx5/6* mutant is accompanied by reduced DNA content. Given that endoreduplication can be activated by the cessation of cell proliferation in mature rosette leaves[36,](#page-13-29) we predict that reduced DNA content is caused by excessive cell proliferation in the *tcx5/6* mutant. These results suggest that TCX5/6 are responsible for maintaining limited organ size, by prevention of excessive cell proliferation.

**Other DREAM components repress the expression of methylation maintenance genes.** To investigate whether DREAM components function as a whole to repress the expression of methylation maintenance genes, we tested their expression in DREAM complex mutants *aly2/3*, *lin37a/b* and *myb3r1* by quantitative PCR with reverse transcription (RT–qPCR), which indicated that expression of some of these genes can also be increased in *aly2/3*, *lin37a/b* and *myb3r1* (Extended Data Fig. 6), supporting the notion that DREAM components function as a whole to repress transcription of these genes and thereby preclude DNA hypermethylation. However, increase in expression of these genes was generally weaker in *aly2/3*, *lin37a/b* and *myb3r1* than in *tcx5/6* (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Extended Data Fig. 6). The weaker effect of *lin37a/b* suggests that LIN37A/B may function as accessory subunits of the DREAM complex, which is supported by the finding that the *lin37a/b* mutant has no severe developmental defects (Supplementary Fig. 8). Because both MYB3R1 and ALY2/3 have additional homologues in *Arabidopsis*, the weaker effect of *myb3r1* and *aly2/3* may have resulted from functional redundancy with their homologues.

The results shown above indicated that TCX5/6 can bind to the transcription start site of both cell cycle-related genes and methylation maintenance genes (Fig. [4f](#page-6-0), Extended Data Figs. 4 and 5 and Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). If TCX5/6 function as subunits of the DREAM complex to repress the expression of these genes, other DREAM components are presumed also to bind to the transcription start site of these genes. ### **Discussion**

It is well known that DNA methylation is faithfully sustained by maintenance DNA methyltransferases after DNA replication[5](#page-13-1) . Although the expression of maintenance DNA methyltransferase genes is markedly higher in tissues containing dividing cells than in differentiated tissues[24,](#page-13-17)[25](#page-13-18), genome-wide DNA methylation levels do not substantially differ between tissue types[48,](#page-13-40)[49](#page-13-41), suggesting that some mechanisms are required to maintain DNA methylation at a stable level in the cells of differentiated and non-differentiated tissues. The conserved DREAM complex in metazoans is responsible for initiating a quiescent state of cells by repressing the transcription of cell cycle gene[s26](#page-13-19). We identified the DREAM complex in *Arabidopsis* and demonstrated that the complex represses the expression of cell cycle genes, confirming the conserved function of the DREAM complex in regulation of DNA replication and cell division. More importantly, we demonstrated that the DREAM complex represses the expression of genes required for maintenance of DNA methylation and thereby precludes DNA hypermethylation at the whole-genome level. In the *tcx5/6* mutant, DNA methylation was increased at thousands of genomic loci, suggesting that increased expression of methylation maintenance genes resulting from disruption of the DREAM complex is responsible for DNA hypermethylation. DNA hypermethylation in the *tcx5/6* mutant may have been caused by enhanced efficiency in maintenance of DNA methylation at hemi-methylated symmetric cytosine sites. In particular, maintenance of CHG methylation is not as efficient as that of CG methylation in *Arabidopsis*[50](#page-13-42), which is consistent with the finding that CHG methylation is increased to a greater extent than CG methylation.

Our whole-genome DNA methylation analysis indicated that a substantial subset of CG and CHG hyper-DMRs identified in the *tcx5/6* mutant showed a notable (>twofold) increase in CG and CHG methylation (Dataset 8, Fig. [5d](#page-7-0) and Extended Data Fig. 7), suggesting that it is unlikely that DNA hypermethylation is entirely the result of enhanced efficiency in maintenance of DNA methylation at hemi-methylated symmetric cytosine sites. Recent studies reported that the maintenance DNA methyltransferase, DNMT1, is involved not only in maintenance of DNA methylation but also in de novo methylation in human[s51,](#page-13-43)[52](#page-13-44). A previous study showed that CMT3 can bind to both hemi-methylated and unmethylated DN[A53,](#page-13-45) supporting the notion that CMT3 can mediate methylation at unmethylated cytosine sites, which is termed de novo DNA methylation. Although DNA hypermethylation in the *tcx5/6* mutant predominantly occurs in pre-existing methylated regions, both methylated and unmethylated sites of these regions can be hypermethylated (Fig. [5d](#page-7-0) and Extended Data Fig. 7), suggesting that the *tcx5/6* mutation can not only enhance the efficiency of maintenance of DNA methylation but also mediate de novo DNA methylation. Given that de novo methylation occurs at symmetric CG and CHG sites, whose methylation can be maintained during cell division, we predict that de novo methylation caused by disruption of the DREAM complex can be inherited across generations. Our study may provide a plausible interpretation for the generation of hypermethylated epi-alleles, which may be the result of reduced activity of the DREAM complex under certain environmental conditions.

DNA methylation is dynamically regulated during development, fruit ripening, stress responses, genomic imprinting and other processes in plants[54–](#page-13-46)[57.](#page-13-47) A base excision-dependent active DNA demethylation pathway initiated by a family of 5-methylcytosine glycosylases is thought to play a critical role in the dynamic regulation of DNA methylatio[n58.](#page-13-48) These 5-methylcytosine glycosylases mediate DNA demethylation predominantly at gene flanking regions and short TEs in euchromatin region[s17](#page-13-10),[18](#page-13-11)[,20](#page-13-13). A series of regulatory factors facilitate the function of 5-methylcytosine glycosylases in DNA demethylatio[n33](#page-13-26),[58,](#page-13-48)[59](#page-13-49). Unlike active DNA demethylation mechanisms, TCX5/6 mediate DNA demethylation by suppressing the expression of genes required for maintenance of DNA methylation including *MET1*, *DDM1*, and *CMT3* and *VIM1/2/3*. The genes required for maintenance of DNA methylation function as major methylation regulators at the whole-genome level[4,](#page-13-0)[40](#page-13-33), whereas the RNA-directed DNA methylation pathway is mainly responsible for DNA methylation at gene flanking regions and short TEs dispersed in euchromatic chromosome arms[12,](#page-13-50)[13](#page-13-7). Our study indicated that, in the *tcx5/6* mutant, DNA methylation is increased at the whole-genome level. Therefore, it reveals an important DNA methylation regulator that is probably required for DNA methylation dynamics. Furthermore, we demonstrate that TCX5/6 can repress the expression of the major active DNA demethylase gene *ROS1* by precluding hypermethylation of the DNA methylation sensor in the *ROS1* promoter region, thus facilitating the formation of a feedback loop for the regulation of DNA methylation. DNA demethylation is involved in the dynamic regulation of DNA methylation during diverse biological processes.

We demonstrate that TCX5/6 can preclude DNA hypermethylation in both quiescent and dividing cells, but that this effect is stronger in the former. As determined by large-scale transcriptome analysi[s25,](#page-13-18) the expression patterns of TCX5 and TCX6 are constitutive and are not exclusive of dividing cells. Therefore, the different activities of TCX5/6 are most likely to be regulated at a post-transcriptional level rather than at a transcriptional level. In mammalian cells, phosphorylation of Rb-like proteins by cyclin-dependent kinases facilitates disassembly of the DREAM complex, resulting in transcriptional activation of cell cycle genes in dividing cell[s60](#page-13-51). In *Arabidopsis*, the Rb-like protein RBR1 also contains phosphorylation sites even though the regulatory role of RBR1 phosphorylation requires further investigation[31.](#page-13-24) We predict that a cell cycle-dependent mechanism may regulate the expression of both cell cycle genes and methylation maintenance genes, thereby differentially regulating DNA methylation in different cell states. In future, it will be interesting to investigate whether and how the regulation is involved in DNA methylation dynamics during important biological processes.

TCX5/6 can preclude DNA hypermethylation not only at TEs but also at methylated promoters. In the *Arabidopsis* genome, promoter methylation occurs in hundreds of genes enriched in pericentromeric heterochromatin regions[2](#page-12-1) . The 5-methylcytosine glycosylases are responsible for prevention of DNA methylation and transcriptional silencing through an active DNA demethylation pathwa[y58](#page-13-48). However, the active DNA demethylation pathway mainly mediates DNA demethylation at genomic loci targeted by RNA-directed DNA methylation and which are distributed in chromosome arms rather than in pericentromeric heterochromatin region[s18](#page-13-11). Our results indicated that the average methylation level of methylated promoters is markedly increased in the *tcx5/6* mutant, suggesting that TCX5/6 are required for preclusion of DNA hypermethylation of methylated promoters at the whole-genome level. Furthermore, we demonstrated that preclusion of promoter hypermethylation by TCX5/6 is important for gene expression. The methylated DNA-binding protein complex SUVH-SDJ was recently shown specifically to activate the expression of genes with promoter methylation[61–](#page-13-52)[63.](#page-14-0) Because SUVH-SDJ prevents transcriptional silencing at only a small subset of genes with promoter methylation, the suppression of promoter hypermethylation is still important for maintaining the expression of these genes. Agricultutal crops have much larger genomes than *Arabidopsis* and contain more methylated TEs that are close to genes, and DNA methylation is therefore more important in crops in the regulation of gene expression[56](#page-13-53)[,64.](#page-14-1) We predict that the preclusion of promoter hypermethylation by the DREAM complex may play a more important role in these crops than in *Arabidopsis*.

The 5-methylcytosine glycosylase DME is known to be a major component responsible for active DNA demethylation and genomic imprinting in the *Arabidopsis* endosper[m20–](#page-13-13)[22.](#page-13-15) Although passive DNA demethylation via DNA replication is thought to be required for DNA demethylation in the endosperm, the mechanism is largely unknown. The Rb-related protein RBR1 was shown to interact with MSI1 to suppress the expression of *MET1* in the female central cell and thereby to mediate gene imprinting in the endosper[m65.](#page-14-2) However, it is not known whether RBR1 and MSI1 mediate DNA demethylation in the endosperm. Given that RBR1 and MSI1 can function as subunits of the DREAM complex, we predict that DREAM complex components may function as a whole to suppress the expression of *MET1* in the endosperm. More importantly, we demonstrated that the DREAM complex is required for preclusion of DNA hypermethylation at the whole-genome level. It is possible that the DREAM complex may suppress the expression of methylation maintenance genes and thereby mediate genome-wide DNA demethylation in the endosperm. In humans, disruption of DREAM complex components and overexpression of the maintenance DNA methyltrasferase DNMT1 are frequently associated with carcinogenesis and cancer cell survival[26,](#page-13-19)[66](#page-14-3). Therefore, the preclusion of DNA hypermethylation by DREAM complexes may be conserved from plants to mammals.

### **Methods**

**Plant materials and growth conditions.** Te T-DNA insertion mutants obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center included *tcx5* (SALK\_144605), *aly1* (SALK\_114476), *aly2* (SALK\_082641), *aly3* (SALK\_125138), *lin37a* (SALK\_057175), *lin37b* (SALK\_103139), *myb3r1* (SALK\_018482), *cmt3-12* (SALK\_148381) and *ros1* (SALK\_045303[\)67](#page-14-4). Te *ddm1-2* mutant and *YJ* luciferase reporter transgenic plants were previously described[34,](#page-13-27)[68.](#page-14-5) Te mutant *tcx5/tcx6* was generated by deletion of a base in the frst exon of *TCX6* in the *tcx5* mutant background with the CRISPR–Cas9 construct *pHEE401E*[69](#page-14-6). DNA oligonucleotides used for the production of a gRNA are shown in Dataset 19. Te *tcx5/6* double mutant was crossed with WT to obtain a *tcx6* single mutant in their F2 group. All of the mutants used in this study were in the Columbia (Col-0) background. Te primers used for genotyping of mutants are listed in Dataset 19. Seedlings were grown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium plates at 22°C under long-day (16h light/8h dark) conditions, and were then transplanted into soil and grown under the same conditions. For 3xFlag or 3xMyc transgenic plants, native promoter-driven *TCX5*, *TCX6*, *ALY3*, *LIN37B*, *RBR1 and MSI1* genomic sequences were cloned into pCAMBIA1305 vectors with their C-terminal tagged by 3xFlag or 3xMyc. For *35S-TCX5-Myc*, full-length TCX5 complementary DNA was cloned between the 35S promoter sequence and 3xMyc in the modifed pCAMBIA1300 vector. For co-expression of *TCX6-Myc*, the *TCX6* genomic sequence was introduced into the pRI909 vector with the 5′-terminal 3xMyc tag. Te primers used in the amplifcation of these genes are listed in Dataset 19. All constructs were transformed into *Agrobacterium* strain GV3101 and then introduced into *Arabidopsis* by the foral-dip method. Te T1 seedlings were grown on MS medium supplemented with 30μgml–1 hygromycin for pCAMBIA1300 and pCAMBIA1305 or 150μgml–1 kanamycin for pRI909, and resistant seedlings were grown for further analysis.

**Affinity purification and mass spectrometry.** For affinity purification, a mix of 2 g of floral tissues and 2 g of 2-week-old seedlings was collected for isolation of protein extracts, which were obtained as previously described[70](#page-14-7). Extracts were incubated with 100μl of anti-Flag M2 (no. A2220; Sigma-Aldrich) in lysis buffer at 4 °C for 2h. Agarose-bound proteins were precipitated by centrifugation, washed four times and eluted with Flag (no. F4799; Sigma-Aldrich). For mass spectrometry, the eluted proteins obtained from affinity purification were run on a 10% sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide electrophoresis gel and visualized by silver staining (PROTSIL1; Sigma-Aldrich). Proteins were extracted from the gel and analysed by tandem mass spectrometry as previously describe[d70](#page-14-7). The obtained peptide sequence data were searched in the International Protein Index database of *Arabidopsis* on the Mascot server (Matrix Science).

**Y2H assay.** Y2H assays were used to test interaction of the subunits of the DREAM complex. Both full-length and a series of truncated cDNA sequences of DREAM component genes were cloned into vectors pGADT7 and pGBKT7. The primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Dataset 19. Interactions between target proteins were assessed by yeast mating according to the Match-Maker gold Y2H system (Clontech) user manual, with minor modification. In brief, concentrated cultures of both bait and prey proteins in strains AH109 and Y187 strains, respectively, were grown overnight, mixed and mated for 18h before being spotted on double-drop-out (SD/-Leu/-Trp) and triple-drop-out (SD/-Leu/-Trp/-His) selective-medium agar plates for screening of positive clones. A 3mM solution of 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole was added to the triple-drop-out medium to inhibit background growth.

**Flow cytometry.** Flow cytometry was performed according to a previously described method, with minor modification[71](#page-14-8). Twenty pieces of the third and fourth rosette leaves from 4-week-old plants were chopped with a razor blade in 1–2ml of mG buffer (45mM MgCl2, 20mM MOPS, 30mM sodium citrate, 0.2% (v/v) Triton X-100, 1% (w/v) PVP-40, 10mM Na2EDTA, 20μlml–1 β-mercaptoethanol pH 7.0). The lysate was passed through a 40-μm cell strainer (no. 352340; BD Falcon) and incubated at 4°C for 5min. Following centrifugation, nuclei were suspended in 100μl of mG buffer and 150μl of staining solution (50μgml–1 propidium iodide, 50μgml–1 RNase A) and incubated at 4°C for 10min in the dark. After repeat filtering of nuclei, 10,000 were analysed for each sample by BD FACSAria III.

**Phenotypic analyses.** To measure the cotyledon size, cotyledons from 2-week-old plants were attached to a glass microscope slide and photographed with a stereomicroscope (Leica M125). Image J software was used to analyse the photographs and measure the size of organs and tissues. To measure cell area, cotyledons were incubated in 50% methanol and 10% acetic acid at 4 °C for 12h and subsequently stained with modified pseudo-Schiff propidium iodide. Cotyledons were then cleared overnight in chloral hydrate solution (8 g of chloral hydrate, 11ml of H2O, 1ml of glycerol) and imaged using a Zeiss LSM800 microscope. To measure the size of rosette leaves, the first to seventh leaves of 4-week-old plants were mounted on glass microscope slides and photographed. The petals were also attached to glass microscope slides for photography. Seeds were harvested from mature plants and photographed using a Nikon SMZ645 microscope. For weighing of seeds, 100 were measured using an electronic analytical balance.

**Luminescence imaging.** Seven-day-old seedlings grown on MS plates were evenly sprayed with 2.5mgml–1 luciferin (Nano light, NF-FF-FA) solution and placed in the dark for 15min. Luminescence imaging was performed using a CCD camera (Princeton Instruments) and WinView32 software.

**RNA-seq and data analysis.** For genome-wide expression analysis, total RNA was isolated with TRIzol reagent (no. 15596018; Invitrogen) from 12-day-old seedlings and from the shoot apical meristem and distal part of the third and fourth mature leaves of 26-day-old plants. Sequencing libraries were built by Novogene and sequenced with an Illumina HiSeqXTen instrument using a paired-end scheme (no. PE150). After tremoval of adaptor sequences, reads were mapped to the *Arabidopsis* genome (TAIR10) using the programme TopHat v.2.0.6 (ref. [72\)](#page-14-9). Genes with a statistically significant (>twofold) change in expression (FDR<0.05 or <0.01 as determined by glmFit in edgeR) were considered as differentially expressed. The results shown in the study are from two independent biological replicates.

**Analyses of RNA transcripts by RT–PCR.** Total RNA was isolated from 2-week-old seedlings grown on MS medium. RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using 5×All-In-One RT MasterMix (no. G492; ABM) and subjected to RT–qPCR using a Kapa SYBR Fast quantitative PCR (qPCR) master mix (no. KM4101; Kapa Biosystems). Expression of each gene tested in this study was normalized to that of Actin2. The primers used for qPCR are listed in Dataset 19.

**ChIP.** A 1.5-g sample of 12-day-old seedlings was harvested, and chromatin immunoprecipitation assays were performed as described previousl[y63.](#page-14-0) Anti-FLAG M2 Magnetic Beads (no. M8823; Sigma-Aldrich) or Anti-c-Myc Agarose Affinity Gel (no. A7470; Sigma-Aldrich) was used for TCX5-3xFlag, ALY3-3xFlag, MSI1- 3xFlag and TCX5-3xMyc ChIP assays. For TCX6-Myc and LIN37B-Myc ChIP assays, chromatin was incubated with anti-Myc (no. ab32; Abcam) antibody and Dynabeads ProteinG (no. 10003D; Invitrogen). For qPCR, 0.5μl of ChIP DNA was used and the primers are listed in Dataset 19. Enrichment of TCX5, TCX6, ALY3, MSI1 and LIN37B on genes tested in this study was normalized to that on the TA3 locus. For ChIP–seq, sequencing libraries were built by Novogene and sequenced with an Illumina HiSeqXTen instrument using a paired-end scheme (no. PE150). Two independent ChIP–seq experiments were performed. ChIP–seq analysis was performed as previously described[73.](#page-14-10)

### **DNA methylation assay.** Genomic DNA was extracted by

cetyltrimethylammonium bromide from 12-day-old seedlings and from the shoot apical meristem and distal part of the third and fourth mature leaves of 26-day-old plants, and was purified by phenol: chloroform:isoamyl alcohol, 25:24:1. DNA in the supernatant was precipitated with isopropanol. DNA methylation was tested by methylation-sensitive enzyme digestion followed by PCR (chop–PCR) and bisulfite sequencing. For chop–PCR, gDNA was cleaved with the methyl-specific endonuclease McrBC (no. M0272; NEB) that cleaves DNA containing methylcytosine. The same amount of gDNA, without cleavage of McrBC, was used as a control. Relative DNA methylation levels were determined by PCR. The primers used are listed in Dataset 19. For bisulfite sequencing, gDNA was sent to Novogene for library building and sequencing. For data analysis, unique bisulfite sequencing reads were mapped to the TAIR10 reference genome using Bismark, allowing one mismatch. The methylation level of each cytosine site was represented by the number of reads reporting C divided by the total number of reads reporting both C and T[74.](#page-14-11) Gene annotations were downloaded from The Arabidopsis Information Resource. DNA methylation of annotated protein-coding genes and TEs was calculated. DNA methylation at CG, CHG and CHH sites was evaluated separately. Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing was independently repeated, and the repeats showed similar results.
