# **Apple russeting as seen through the RNA‑seq lens: strong alterations in the exocarp cell wall**

**Sylvain Legay · Gea Guerriero · Amélie Deleruelle · Marc Lateur · Danièle Evers · Christelle M. André · Jean‑Francois Hausman**

Received: 2 September 2014 / Accepted: 23 February 2015 © Springer Science+Business Media Dordrecht 2015

**Abstract** Russeting, a commercially important defect in the exocarp of apple (*Malus* × *domestica*), is mainly characterized by the accumulation of suberin on the inner part of the cell wall of the outer epidermal cell layers. However, knowledge on the underlying genetic components triggering this trait remains sketchy. Bulk transcriptomic profiling was performed on the exocarps of three russeted and three waxy apple varieties. This experimental design was chosen to lower the impact of genotype on the obtained results. Validation by qPCR was carried out on representative genes and additional varieties. Gene ontology enrichment revealed a repression of lignin and cuticle biosynthesis genes in russeted exocarps, concomitantly with an enhanced expression of suberin deposition, stress responsive, primary sensing, NAC and MYB-family transcription factors, and specific triterpene biosynthetic genes. Notably, a strong correlation (R<sup>2</sup> <sup>=</sup> 0.976) between the expression of a MYB93-like transcription factor and key suberin biosynthetic genes was found. ## **Introduction**

Apple is one of the most important fruit crops in the world with more than 76 million tons produced worldwide in 2012 (FAOSTAT data). Since the 60s, traits such as yield, biotic stress tolerance, fruit shape, taste or storage properties have been improved. Despite the rich diversity of apple varieties, consumer's choice clearly favors waxy and glossy apples, restricting apple commercial germplasm to a majority of waxy skin varieties. Some old heritage varieties such as 'Reinette Parmentier', 'St Edmund Pippin' or 'Patte de loup' may, however, present a contrasting phenotype, displaying a russeted (rough and brown) skin. Waxy apples can also be affected by russeting, which, in this case, is perceived as a major defect by consumers and leads to quality and price downgrading, considerably reducing the profitability of fruit production (Curry [2009\)](#page-17-0). Despite significant improvements in apple commercial germplasm, this nondesirable phenotype can appear in a large number of glossy commercial varieties, including well-studied ones such as 'Golden Delicious' and 'Cox Orange Pippin'. To date, few treatments, including phytohormones and fungicides, are able to limit its occurrence (Gildemacher et al. [2006](#page-17-1); Barabdoozi and Talaie [2009](#page-17-2)).

Russeting occurrence has been linked to lacks and defects in the cuticular and epidermal layers (Watanabe [1969](#page-19-0); Skene [1982;](#page-19-1) Khanal et al. [2013](#page-18-0)). Cuticle is a lipophilic barrier composed of many different components, such as cutin and waxes, which covers the epidermal layer, limits gas exchange or water loss, and protects plants from environmental injuries (Suh et al. [2005\)](#page-19-2).

A broad range of environmental factors that cause epidermal and cuticular defects have been identified. Among them, pathogen attacks, non-optimal environmental conditions, such as heavy rainfalls, temperature stress, humidity, or the application of plant protection agents are the most predominant cause of russeting (Faust and Shear [1972](#page-17-3); Skene [1982](#page-19-1); Khanal et al. [2013](#page-18-0)). As a representative example, extended exposure to a humid environment results in a thinner or complete absence of the cuticle. Apples are particularly sensitive to russeting during early stages of fruit development, at which time extreme tangential tensions of the epidermis occur because of the high pressure exerted on the skin by the growing fruit, which consequently results in microscopic cracks in the cuticle (Walter [1967;](#page-19-3) Watanabe [1969;](#page-19-0) Faust and Shear [1972](#page-17-3); Skene [1982](#page-19-1); Khanal et al. [2013\)](#page-18-0).

Apple russeting is an accumulation of suberin on the inner side of the cell wall where it plays a role as global barrier between plants and the environment, thus limiting water, solute, gas, and ion exchanges, as well as decreasing pathogen susceptibility (Sousa et al. [2012\)](#page-19-4). Its deposition has been mainly investigated in *Arabidopsis thaliana* roots (Beisson et al. [2007;](#page-17-4) Franke and Schreiber [2007](#page-17-5); Li and Beisson [2009](#page-18-1); Beisson et al. [2012;](#page-17-6) Kosma et al. [2014](#page-18-2)) and the tuber skin of potato (*Solanum tuberosum* L.) (Lulai and Corsini [1998](#page-18-3); Graca and Pereira [2000b](#page-18-4); Serra et al. [2009b,](#page-19-5) [2010](#page-19-6); Boher et al. [2013\)](#page-17-7). In *Arabidopsis*, suberin deposited in Casparian strips controls the apoplastic transport of water and minerals (Zimmermann et al. [2000;](#page-19-7) Franke et al. [2005](#page-17-8); Baxter et al. [2009](#page-17-9)), while reduced suberin deposition, observed in a fatty acid hydroxylase mutant, increased root permeability (Hofer et al. [2008\)](#page-18-5). Conversely, two mutants with increased amounts of suberin in the roots exhibited reduced levels of Mn, Zn and Ca in their shoots (Ranathunge and Schreiber [2011\)](#page-18-6).

Suberin is a ubiquitous insoluble biopolyester composed of two poly-phenolic and one poly-aliphatic domain (SPPD, for Suberin Poly-Phenolic Domain, and SPAD, for Suberin Poly-Aliphatic Domain, respectively; Bernards [2002\)](#page-17-10). The SPAD is composed of cross-linked saturated and substituted hydroxy acids or α,ω-diacid monomers, esterified with glycerol and ferulic acid (Graca and Pereira [2000a](#page-17-11); Franke et al. [2005](#page-17-8); Graca and Santos [2007](#page-18-7)), while SPPD displays a ligninlike structure with a higher hydroxycinnamic acid content (Bernards [2002\)](#page-17-10). Despite commercial and scientific interest, knowledge of the genetic components responsible for suberin synthesis in apple remains limited. Available data on the suberization process mainly originate from model plants. In *A. thaliana*, a number of gene families involved in suberin synthesis have been described (Ranathunge et al. [2011](#page-18-8); Beisson et al. [2012](#page-17-6)), providing an initial, precursory overview of the complexity of the suberin biosynthetic process. The SPAD monomers are synthesized in plastids and the endoplasmic reticulum. C16–C18 acyl precursors are elongated by the fatty acid elongation complex and activated by fatty acid long chain acyl-CoA synthetases. Cytochrome P450 proteins, belonging to the CYP86 and CYP94 subfamilies, are involved in the multiple modifications of C16 and C18 acyl precursors resulting in α,ω-dicarboxylic acid and hydroxyacyls precursors. The resulting suberin precursors are then esterified with glycerol by a glycerol phosphate acyltransferase (Moire et al. [1999;](#page-18-9) Beisson et al. [2007](#page-17-4); Li et al. [2007;](#page-18-10) Hofer et al. [2008](#page-18-5)), or reduced by a fatty acid reductase and conjugated with ferulic acid (Molina et al. [2009\)](#page-18-11). The SPPD anchored in the cell wall is considered as a hydroxycinnamic acid polymer comparable to lignin (Pollard et al. [2008\)](#page-18-12). Suberin monomers may be exported out of the cell by ABC transporters and lipid transfer proteins, but few genes have been identified so far (Panikashvili et al. [2010](#page-18-13)). How these SPPD and SPAD are finally polymerized is not known, although the existence of a polyester synthase function has been proposed (Soler et al. [2007\)](#page-19-8). Recent studies suggest that some oxidizing enzymes, such as peroxidases, are involved in the polymerization of the SPPD, whereas lipase/esterase may play a role in the final step of the SPAD synthesis (Beisson et al. [2012\)](#page-17-6).

Few studies related to the regulation of suberin deposition are available. Plant growth regulators influence suberin synthesis-related genes. The application of gibberellins can reduce russeting in 'Golden Delicious' (Barabdoozi and Talaie [2009\)](#page-17-2). In potato tubers, feruloyl transferase expression is enhanced by wounding and abscisic acid, whereas a repression was observed in response to salicylic acid (Boher et al. [2013](#page-17-7)). Finally, in *A. thaliana* roots, AtMYB41 has been recently identified to trigger the expression of a large number of suberin synthesis genes under abiotic stress condition (Kosma et al. [2014\)](#page-18-2).

To identify genes and pathways associated with apple russeting, gene expression profiling using RNA sequencing (Illumina) was performed. In order to draw a consistent picture of the gene expression profiles specific to both russeted and waxy apples and at the same time to highlight and interpret the mechanism leading to the russeted phenotype, a bulk RNA-sequencing was performed on the exocarp of a group of three distinct fully-russeted apple varieties ('Patte de loup', 'Reinette Parmentier', 'St Edmund's Pippin') and a second one including 3 fully waxy varieties ('Gala', 'CRAW/Ma/AF42', and 'CRAW/Ma/AG94'). This specific design was chosen to decrease the effect of genotype on the obtained results and consequently to gain a more specific overview of the suberization process occurring in apples during the development of russeting.

#### **Materials and methods**

#### Plant material

For the preparation of the RNA-sequencing library, 6 apple varieties presenting 3 fully-waxy ('CRAW/Ma/AF42', 'CRAW/ma/AG94', 'Gala') and 3 fully-russeted phenotypes ('Patte de loup', 'Reinette Parmentier', 'St Edmund's Pippin') were harvested in October 2012 at commercial maturity (150 days after full bloom) in the orchards of the Walloon Agronomic Research Centre (CRA-W, Gembloux, Belgium). qPCR experiments were performed using additional apple fruit, sampled in October 2013, including the waxy cultivar 'CRAW/Ma/AG94', the russeted 'Patte de loup', 'Reinette du Canada Gris', 'Court Pendu Gris' and semi-russeted apple 'Cox Orange Pippin' varieties, the latter being collected separately in two different locations (CRA-W and a Luxemburgish commercial orchard). The phenotypes, pedigree and origins of each variety can be found on the Online Resource 1. For each variety, six fruit were sampled and each fruit was split into quarters. A 5 mm (large) longitudinal slice was peeled with scalpels from each quarter, taking care to remove the remaining flesh from the exocarp. For the semi-russeted variety 'Cox Orange Pippin', waxy and russeted exocarpic areas were carefully excised from six fruit. The resulting exocarp samples were directly flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, roughly ground to homogenize the sample and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction.

#### RNA extraction

Samples (250 mg) were ground to a fine powder with mortar and pestle in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using an adapted CTAB buffer extraction protocol (Gasic et al. [2004](#page-17-12)). Total RNAs were cleaned and treated with DNase I using the RNeasy plant mini kit (QIAGEN, Leusden, The Netherlands), following the manufacturer's guidelines. Total RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 assay (Agilent Technologies, Diegem, Belgium) and a 2100 Bioanalyzer with quality parameters adapted to plant RNA profiles (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Samples, with RINs (RNA integrity number) lower than 8, were excluded from the experiment. RNA purity was assessed measuring the absorbance at 230, 260 and 280 nm using a Nanodrop ND1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo scientific, Villebon-sur-Yvette, France). Total RNA was quantified using a Qubit RNA assay kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

## Library preparation and sequencing

Libraries were prepared from 3 µg total RNA using the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation v2 kit according to the manufacturer's guidelines (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). After mRNA purification, cDNA synthesis, shearing, Illumina adapters/barcodes ligation and 15-cycles of PCR amplification, libraries were controlled using the Agilent 2100 bioanalyser and the DNA-1000 kit. Libraries were quantified using the Library Quantification Kit—Illumina/ROX Low (Kapa Biosystems, Boston, MA, USA) on a Viia7 96-well real-time-qPCR instrument (Life Technologies). The pooled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq using four consecutive runs (Illumina MiSeq reagent V2 and V3) to generate 76 base-pairs paired-end reads. Raw sequences have been deposited at the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus website (GEO, [http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo,](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) accession number: GSE61040).

#### Data analysis

MiSeq generated FASTQ files were imported in CLC genomics workbench v6.5 discarding reads with poor quality (<Q30). For each library, read duplicates (23– 32 %) were removed from each sample independently using the duplicate read removal plugin, Illumina adapter residues were then trimmed using the TruSeq adaptor sequence and finally filtered (~2 % removed) using the following criteria: low sequence quality <0.01, maximum of two ambiguous nucleotides, read length between 35 and 80 nucleotides. The remaining reads were mapped to the predicted apple transcriptome (Velasco et al. [2010\)](#page-19-9) with annotations obtained from Phytozome v.10 beta (<http://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov>) with the following criteria: mapped reads having multiple hits lower than 5, 90 % identity and 50 % of coverage with the reference. Expression values were calculated using the RPKM (Reads per kilobase transcript per million reads) method (Mortazavi et al. [2008](#page-18-14)). Genes with no mapped reads, with no specifically mapped reads and/or with a RPKM value lower than 0.1 in at least one of the libraries were removed from the dataset.

Differentially expressed genes between the russeted exocarp group ('Patte de loup', 'Reinette Parmentier', 'St Edmund's Pippin') and the waxy group ('CRAW/Ma/ AF42', 'CRAW/ma/AG94', 'Gala') were statistically determined using a Baggerley's 'on proportions' weighted test (Baggerly et al. [2003\)](#page-17-13), a false discovery rate (Benjamini– Hotchberg correction) was set at 0.2 and a fold-change cut-off value at −1 > log2 ratio (Russet group/Waxy group) >1. The Gene ontology enrichment analysis was performed from a subset of genes (−2 > log2 ratio (Russet group/ Waxy group) >2, FDR <0.2) in Cytoscape (v3.1.0) with the ClueGO v2.1.1 and CluePedia v1.1.1 plugins (Bindea et al. [2009](#page-17-14), [2013](#page-17-15)) to highlight significantly regulated biological processes (Benjamini–Hotchberg corrected *p* value <0.05) following these criteria: gene ontology from level 3 to level 10, kappa score set at 0.9. A suberin-related functional association network was built using STRING [\(http://string](http://string-db.org)[db.org](http://string-db.org)) from the TAIR code obtained from the significantly expressed gene, which were more expressed in the russeted exocarps.

#### QPCR validation

Gene expression results were validated on a subset of 8 representative genes using qPCR. Reverse transcription was carried out using the M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (RNase H-), the Murine RNase Inhibitor (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and random hexamers (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, NM, USA) following the manufacturers' guidelines. qPCR primers were designed using the Primer3 software [\(http://frodo.wi.mit.edu/](http://frodo.wi.mit.edu/)) with the following criteria: primer size between 18 and 25 base pairs, GC content between 30 and 70 %, amplicon size from 80 to 150 base pairs, primer annealing temperatures in the 57–61 °C range. Matching primer sets were checked using NetPrimer [\(http://www.premierbiosoft.com/net](http://www.premierbiosoft.com/netprimer/index.html)[primer/index.html](http://www.premierbiosoft.com/netprimer/index.html)) for unexpected secondary structures. In order to test the specificity of the primers, a BLAST search against the *M.* × *domestica* genome sequence was carried out. qPCR runs were carried out, using technical triplicates and 3–4 biological replicates (Online Resource 3), on a Viia7 384-well real-time PCR instrument (Life Technologies) with Takyon SYBR Green low ROX (Eurogentec) following the manufacturer's guidelines. Experiments were carried out following the MIQE guidelines (Bustin et al. [2009](#page-17-16)) and a melting curve was performed at the end of all the runs to assess the specificity of the primers. Primers information and melting curves are available on the Online Resource 2. The relative expression of a gene of interest and the Pearson correlation between them was calculated using the Biogazelle Qbase+ v2.5 software (Hellemans et al. [2007\)](#page-18-15), taking into account multiple reference gene normalization and specific PCR efficiencies. For the present experiment, the GeNorm+ analysis described the MdEF1 and MdActin gene couple as the most suitable for optimal normalization of the data (Vandesompele et al. [2002](#page-19-10)). A hierarchical clustering of the results was performed using the Cluster 3.0 software with un-centered correlation and complete linkage metrics. The resulting heatmap was built using Treeview. Statistical analysis of the log2 transformed normalized relative quantities was performed using SPSS 19.0. Normal distribution was assessed and a general linear mixed model using a Tukey Post-Hoc test was applied (α = 0.05). Results of the Post-Hoc test are displayed on the Online resource 3 using different letters indicating significantly different data points.

#### **Results and discussion**

## Sequencing and mapping

The direct interest of our study is to draw a picture of the suberization process in apple. Whole gene expression profiling using RNA-seq is the method of choice to identify genes linked to a trait of interest. For this purpose, 6 cDNA libraries were built from mRNA extracted from the skin of 6 apple varieties with either a russet or waxy phenotype. A total of 156,914,224 76-base pairs-sequence reads, ranging from 17 to 36 million reads per library, were generated from four consecutive MiSeq runs (Table [1\)](#page-4-0). About 69–73 % of the reads were mapped to the reference transcriptome with a consistent part (18–19 %) matching to multiple contigs with a large majority of reads mapped to two contigs (Table [1](#page-4-0)). This non negligible part of multi-hit mapped reads may be probably due to the specific structure of the *Malus* × *domestica* Borkh. genome which results from a genome-wide duplication of a *Pyreae* ancestor (Velasco et al. [2010](#page-19-9)). Indeed, the chromosomes 3, 5, 9 and 13 have a high nucleotide sequence identity with the chromosomes 11, 10, 17 and 16 respectively and many shorter segments from other chromosomes also present high identities between each other. QPCR validation (Fig. [1](#page-4-1)) was performed on eight suberin-related genes and displayed a strong correlation between both methods (y <sup>=</sup> 1.0038x <sup>+</sup> 0.4639, R<sup>2</sup> <sup>=</sup> 0.9941).

## Overview of the differentially expressed genes between russeted (RAE) and waxy apple exocarp (WAE)

In order to highlight genes involved in the russet trait and to minimize noise due to genotypic specificities of each cultivar, a Baggerley's "on proportion" statistical test was performed on gene expression results obtained between russeted and waxy varieties. A total of 1261 genes were differentially regulated between russet and waxy apple exocarps. The first interesting key result was related to the number of up-regulated genes, which was nearly three times higher than the number of down-regulated genes. The same trend has been already described in *Quercus suber* (cork tree) where the authors compared gene expression profiles of the phellem with wood tissues (Soler et al. [2007\)](#page-19-8) and when comparing *Pyrus pyrifolia* green and russeted pericarp (Wang et al. [2014b\)](#page-19-11). Given this distribution of the significantly regulated genes, russeting occurrence can be considered an additive trait.

As mentioned above, comparison between RAE and WAE resulted in a large number of differentially expressed genes belonging to the lipid metabolism (Figs. [2,](#page-5-0) [3\)](#page-6-0). Indeed, the whole primary fatty acid elongase (FAE) complex, from acetyl-CoA carboxylase to NADH-dependent enoyl-acyl carrier protein (ACP) reductase-like gene (Fig. [3](#page-6-0), Online Resource 4), was less expressed in the RAE suggesting a lower C16-ACP synthesis rate compared to the WAE. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment performed on the highly significant genes using Cluepedia/ClueGO plugins (*p* value <0.05, FDR <0.2 correction, −2 < log2 (Russeted/waxy) <2) confirmed this trend showing a majority of down regulated biological processes being involved in the alkanes, unsaturated and long-chain fatty acid, phosphatidylglycerol biosynthetic process, cutin and wax metabolism, as well as cuticle development (Fig. [4\)](#page-7-0).

Other genes belonging to the following biological processes cuticle development, cutin and wax biosynthetic process, such as the 3-ketoacyl CoA synthetase (KCS) 1, 3, 6, and 10, are less expressed in RAE (Table [2](#page-8-0); Figs. [4,](#page-7-0) [5\)](#page-13-0). KCS genes, which encode very-long-chain fatty acid condensing enzymes, have been shown to play a crucial role in the synthesis of epicuticular wax (Serra et al. [2009a\)](#page-19-13) and were also less expressed in russeted sand pear pericarps (Wang et al. [2014a](#page-19-14), [b\)](#page-19-11). The wax ester synthase/acyl-coenzyme A: diacylglycerolacyltransferase (WSD1) gene, which catalyzes the final step of the wax esters synthesis (Li et al. [2008\)](#page-18-16), is also less expressed in russeted skin (Table [2;](#page-8-0) Fig. [5](#page-13-0)). Following the same trend, RNA-seq and qPCR data showed that the glycerol-3-phosphate acyl transferase 6-like gene (GPAT6-like) was also less expressed in RAE compared to the WAE (Table [2;](#page-8-0) Fig. [5](#page-13-0)). GPATs, which are responsible for the linkage of acyls to glycerol-3-phosphate, are the key regulators of both cutin and suberin synthesis (Fig. [4](#page-7-0)). Eight GPAT isoforms with diverse functions have been identified in *A. thaliana* with localized expression and isoform-specific functional characterization (Beisson et al. [2012](#page-17-6)). AtGPAT6 is a crucial enzyme in the synthesis of palmitate-based cutin monomers in flowers (Li-Beisson et al. [2009\)](#page-18-17), suggesting that russeted apple fruits have a lower cuticle deposition. Among the large number of differentially expressed transporters, two ABCG11 transporters (Table [2\)](#page-8-0), which are implicated in the excretion of the cuticular (cutin and wax) monomers through the epidermal cell (McFarlane et al. [2010](#page-18-18)), were also down-regulated in RAE. These observations are in accordance with the literature arguing that the thickness and the structure of the cuticle play a central role in the appearance of russeting (Faust and Shear [1972](#page-17-3); Skene [1982](#page-19-1); Khanal et al. [2013\)](#page-18-0). Since the cuticle is a critical protective barrier against environmental stresses (Beisson et al. [2012\)](#page-17-6), the decreased expression of cuticle biosynthetic genes and the resulting weakening of the exocarp may trigger additional defense responses such as the deposition of the suberized layer. MacMillan et al. [2010](#page-18-21)). Two xyloglucan endotransglycosylases (XETs) were also found to be up-regulated. Xyloglucan is one of the most abundant hemicelluloses in primary plant walls and is cross-linked with cellulose microfribrils. XETs are considered as the major regulator of cell wall expansion and are able to cleave and elongate xyloglucan (Bourquin et al. [2002](#page-17-17)). Another up-regulated gene involved in cell wall modification is the expansin EXLB3-like protein, which is a non-enzymatic protein able to rapidly disrupt hydrogen bonds between cellulose and hemicellulose and thus to participate in cell wall expansion allowing the rapid increase of cell growth (Cosgrove [2005;](#page-17-18) Sampedro and Cosgrove [2005](#page-19-15)). Pectin is another major component of primary walls and middle lamellas. Pectin methylesterase (PME) is usually associated with cell wall modifications, in response to biotic and abiotic stimuli, by catalyzing the de-esterification of pectin with the resulting severing of the bonds with cellulose (Pelloux et al. [2007\)](#page-18-22). A large number of PMEs were found up-regulated in RAE (Table [2](#page-8-0)), together with a gene involved in the synthesis of UDP-glucuronate (GAE1), which is the major precursor of pectin (Molhoj et al. [2004](#page-18-23)).

Taken altogether, differentially expressed genes related to the cell wall followed a common trend suggesting that enhanced cell wall loosening occurred in RAE compared to WAE. The whole cell wall polysaccharide matrix composed of cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin might be impacted by the differential expression of the above-mentioned genes, thereby modifying the relative abundance of the different polysaccharides and severing the interchain bonds connecting them. Peroxidases might be involved in a wall loosening mechanism (Passardi et al. [2004](#page-18-24)) and in this respect it is tempting to speculate that they could contribute to the differences observed between RAE and WAE. However it should be noted that in our results only very few peroxidases showed significant differences in expression levels, therefore further studies are needed to validate this hypothesis.

During apple fruit growth, the increase of fruit size is mainly limited by the expansion of the epidermis. The sigmoidal increase of fruit size triggers extreme biaxial tangential tensions and these mechanical stresses lead, in certain cases, to tissue disruption and microcracks in the primary fruit skin i.e. cuticle, epidermis and hypodermis (Watanabe [1969;](#page-19-0) Skene [1982](#page-19-1); Curry [2009;](#page-17-0) Khanal et al. [2013](#page-18-0)). The development of fleshy fruit is characterized by stages of significant expansion which must necessarily be accompanied by deep modifications of the cell walls. The expansion phase experienced by the growing fruit implies an active synthesis and deposition of cuticular membrane constituents which contribute to the overall strength of the skin surface. Hence, it is clear that a fine cross-talk exists and is activated during fleshy fruit development and expansion and delicate regulatory mechanisms must coordinate these crucial events. It is already described that critical cell wall-related factors control the onset of cracking susceptibility in fruit: the activities of wall-modifying enzymes, for instance β-galactosidases, can have important effects in cracking tolerance (Balbontin et al. [2014\)](#page-17-19). Overexpression of a pectin methylesterase inhibitor (PMEI5) in *A. thaliana* triggers dramatic changes in stems, which appear curly and display altered biomechanical properties and failure of organ separation (Müller et al. [2013\)](#page-18-25). Notably these overexpressing lines also show defects associated with an altered cuticle development: the cuticle indeed appears discontinuous (Müller et al. [2013\)](#page-18-25).

Strong differential expression of the suberin deposition-related genes in the russeted apples

As expected, the RAE tissues displayed several strongly expressed key genes related to suberin synthesis and deposition compared to the waxy group (Figs. [4](#page-7-0), [6](#page-14-0); Table [2](#page-8-0)). Comparison of RAE to WAE revealed differences in the expression of genes of the phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathway (Fig. [4](#page-7-0)). This family of secondary metabolites, which includes ferulic acid, p-coumaric acid and caffeic acid, are partly involved in determining the structure of both suberin SPAD and SPPD (Pollard et al. [2008](#page-18-12); Beisson et al. [2012](#page-17-6)). Two cinnamate-4-hydroxylases (C4H), a caffeoyl CoA-O-methyltransferase (CCoAOMT) and a caffeate-O-methyltransferase (COMT) were strongly expressed, suggesting that the hydroxycinnamic acid and especially ferulic acid synthesis was enhanced in RAE. A feruloy-CoA-acyl-transferase was also found among the large number of differentially regulated BAHD family acyl transferases (Table [2\)](#page-8-0). This gene has been already described in *A. thaliana* and *S. tuberosum* to be a key player of the ω-hydroxyacid-alkyl-ferulate and carboxyalkyl-ferulate synthesis, which are suberin monomers found in the suberin aliphatic domain (Gou et al. [2009](#page-17-20); Molina et al. [2009](#page-18-11); Boher et al. [2013](#page-17-7)). Conversely, some monolignol synthesis genes, such as a cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR) and cinnamoyl alcohol dehydrogenases (CAD4 and 9-like genes) were less expressed in russeted apples (Table [2](#page-8-0)). In russeted Sand Pear, a repression of the the CCR and CAD genes was also observed and may contribute to the russet pigmentation of the exocarp (Wang et al. [2014a](#page-19-14), [b](#page-19-11)). Previous experiments also showed a reduced activity and expression of the CCR and CAD in the suberized tissues with concomitant decreased lignin precursor content (Bernards et al. [2000;](#page-17-21) Bernards [2002](#page-17-10)). Indeed, the SPPD shares a similar structure and localization in the cell wall with lignin, but displays a contrasting composition. As previously mentioned, the amount of monolignols in the SPPD is lower than in lignin, whereas hydroxycinnamic acids are more abundant (Bernards et al. [1995;](#page-17-22) Bernards[,2002\)](#page-17-10). This decreased expression of lignin precursor's synthesis genes in the SPPD may further favor the cell wall loosening and expansion as suggested earlier (Moura et al. [2010](#page-18-26)).

The SPPD assembly is still not fully understood. However, in *S. tuberosum*, *S. lycopersicum* and *A. thaliana*, hypotheses involving the participation of oxidizing enzymes such as oxidase/peroxidase couples and/or laccases, have been proposed (Quiroga et al. [2000;](#page-18-27) Bernards and Razem [2001;](#page-17-23) Soler et al. [2007](#page-19-8); Pollard et al. [2008](#page-18-12)). Based on the data from the present study these two hypotheses seem to be conceivable. Increased expression of amine oxidase and peroxidase genes, including a thioredoxin peroxidase (TPX1)-like gene, was found in RAE (Table [2,](#page-8-0) Online Resource 4). In tomato, expression of the TPX1 cell wall basic peroxidase is enhanced in lignified/

suberized roots, but the involvement of other peroxidases is also suspected (Quiroga et al. [2000](#page-18-27)). Additionally, a higher expression of several laccase genes was noted in the russeted tissues (LAC14- and LAC15-like, Table [2\)](#page-8-0). Although laccases seem to be involved in lignin metabolism in the secondary cell wall, their role is not clearly understood. The LAC15 gene has been linked with the synthesis of lignin in seeds and roots during elongation (Liang et al. [2006](#page-18-28)), but the involvement of laccases in suberin synthesis is so far only suspected (Soler et al. [2007;](#page-19-8) Pollard et al. [2008\)](#page-18-12). Nevertheless, the similar structure of lignin and the SPAD, together with the enhanced expression of laccase genes observed in the russeted apple tissues, as well as in the cork of *Q. suber* (Soler et al. [2007\)](#page-19-8) and the russeted exocarp of *Pyrus pyrifolia* (Wang et al. [2014a\)](#page-19-14), lead to speculate about the implication of laccases in suberin metabolism.

Gene ontology enrichment and the qPCR analysis highlight the importance of a group of genes implicated in the synthesis of SPAD monomers (Table [2](#page-8-0); Figs. [4,](#page-7-0) [5](#page-13-0)). The most differentially expressed genes included the well-known CYP86A1 and B1, which encode fatty acid ω-hydroxylases and a long-chain fatty alcohol dehydrogenase (FAD) gene. These gene code for proteins involved in the early synthesis steps of ω-hydroxyacid and α,ωdicarboxylic acid suberin monomer precursors (Benveniste et al. [1998](#page-17-24); Kurdyukov et al. [2006;](#page-18-29) Compagnon et al. [2009](#page-17-25); Hofer et al. [2008](#page-18-5); Pinot and Beisson [2011](#page-18-30)). In contrast to the behavior of the cutin-related GPAT6, the GPAT5 like is an alternative isoform of the GPAT enzyme family, which was also strongly expressed in the RAE (Figs. [4,](#page-7-0) [5](#page-13-0)). In *A. thaliana* roots, this enzyme is considered as the key regulator of the suberin monomer synthesis, linking ω-hydroxyacid and α,ω-dicarboxylic CoA precursors to glycerol (Beisson et al. [2007\)](#page-17-4). To date, it is not clearly understood how acyls are supplied for the synthesis of these monomers (Pollard et al. [2008](#page-18-12)). In the present experiment, despite the lower expression of the primary fatty acid synthesis genes (FAE complex) observed in the RAE, a significant number of glycolysis-related genes, including many pyruvate kinase genes, were significantly more expressed, reflecting a potential remobilization of sugar for acetyl-CoA synthesis (Fig. [3](#page-6-0), Online Resource 4). Further functional analyses have to be performed to unravel the involvement of these transporters in the suberin deposition process. Pentacyclic triterpenes are particularly interesting for their pharmacological properties including anti-inflammatory, antitumor, and anti-HIV activities. They belong to a large family of 30-carbon terpenes that are embedded in the leaf, fruit skin cuticle and tree bark (Watanabe [1969;](#page-19-0) Brendolise et al. [2011;](#page-17-27) Andre et al. [2013\)](#page-17-28). In the apple skin, three major families of pentacyclic triterpenes are highly abundant: ursane-, oleane- and lupane-series (Andre et al. [2012\)](#page-17-29). The respective precursors of these triterpene families, α-amyrin, β-amyrin and lupeol, are produced by cyclization of the 2,3-oxidosqualene precursor by multifunctional oxido-squalene cyclases (OSCs). Remarkably, waxy apples have a higher content of ursolic (ursane) and oleanic acid (oleane), whereas russeted apples have a higher betulinic acid (lupane) content, as well as some specific triterpene-caffeate conjugates (Andre et al. [2013](#page-17-28)). However, only a few genes involved in the triterpene synthesis have been identified so far. In the present data (Table [2](#page-8-0)), many OSC genes were differentially regulated. Only one OSC gene sharing more than 99 % identity with the MdOSC1 gene, was less expressed in the RAE (Brendolise et al. [2011](#page-17-27)). MdOSC1 is responsible for the synthesis of both α- and β-amyrin with a 5:1 ratio, confirming the lower amount of ursolic and oleanic acid generally found in russeted apple (Brendolise et al. [2011](#page-17-27)). Five OSC genes, which were strongly expressed in the russeted tissues, might be implicated in lupeol synthesis, but the genes responsible for this biosynthetic step have not yet been identified. Therefore, further functional analyses needs to be performed to validate this hypothesis. Gene(s) implicated in the oxygenation of the α-amyrin, β-amyrin and lupeol have not yet been identified in apple, but a CYP716A family gene (Table [2\)](#page-8-0), highly homologous to the triterpene synthesis multifunctional oxidase identified in *M. truncatula*, was strongly down-regulated in the RAE (Fukushima et al. [2011](#page-17-30)). Interestingly, the concomitant higher amount of both lupeol and betulinic acid observed in the 'Merton Russet' variety compared to waxy ones such as 'Royal Gala' might be explained by a similar regulation of the CYP716A gene (Andre et al. [2013](#page-17-28)). Finally, the recent observation of the betulinic acid-3-trans caffeate conjugate in the 'Merton Russet' variety and earlier in *Q. suber*, suggests that shared enzymes with the suberin synthesis pathway might result in the synthesis of this compounds. As an example, some hydroxycinnamoyl transferase such as the feruloyl-CoA acyl transferase candidate genes found in the present experiment might be involved in this reaction.

Stress responses signaling cascades and transcription factors were strongly enhanced in russeted apple

The lower expression of cuticle synthesis genes may result in micro-cracks in the cuticle observed in many studies. As the cuticle plays a critical role as a hydrophobic barrier against the environment and limits water loss or gas exchange, we hypothesize that the relative lack of efficient protection trigger a boosted stress response signaling cascades and transcription factors expression in russeted apple. This postulate is substantiated by the activation in RAE of a large set of genes related to the stress response machinery, like genes related to the signal transduction, stress, hormone metabolism or RNA processing (Fig. [2\)](#page-5-0). Transmembrane pattern recognition receptors (PRR), such as PR5K, lectin, LRR-motif and wall-associated kinases (WAK1) are highly expressed, which is usually observed during biotic and to a lower extent abiotic stress exposure. A similar expression pattern was also observed in *Pyrus pyrifolia*, where a large number of disease resistance response proteins were more expressed in the russeted pericarps (Wang et al. [2014a\)](#page-19-14). Expression of many TIR-NBS-LRR disease proteins (intracellular PRR) is also increased (Table [2,](#page-8-0) Online Resource 4). PRR proteins are involved in the recognition and transduction of biotic stress signals, i.e. microbial and damage stimuli, and trigger defense responses and cell apoptosis through the hypersensitive response mechanism (Belkhadir et al. [2004](#page-17-31); Tor et al. [2009\)](#page-19-16). The increased expression of the wall associated kinase WAK1, which is implicated in cell wall loosening and expansion, and the cell wall-related expression pattern discussed above, support the hypothesis that an enhanced cell wall expansion and loosening occur in RAE.

The downstream phytohormonal regulation is slightly different between the two apple groups and although the primary sensing implicated some biotic stress-like responsive genes, only a few genes related to salicylic and jasmonic acid degradation were differentially expressed, suggesting that the supposed "stress state" of russeted apple is more particular and may not involve the common wounding and biotic stress response. Conversely, a number of genes implicated in auxin and ethylene signaling pathways are more expressed in russeted apple (Table [2,](#page-8-0) Online Resource 4). To our knowledge, the involvement of auxin regulatory pathways in the suberization process has not been investigated and ethylene seems to have no effect on wound-induced suberization in *S. tuberosum* (Lulai and Suttle [2004\)](#page-18-33). But, due to the major roles of auxin and ethylene in fruit development, growth and ripening and their strong implication in the present data, some involvement in the russeted phenotype may be expected. Indeed, specific senescence-related gene 1 (SRG1)-like ACC oxidase and flavonol synthase genes displayed an increased expression, whereas other non-SRG1 ACC oxidase isoforms were less expressed (Online Resource 4). Multiple auxin-induced growth regulators and an IAA-amino acid conjugate hydrolase (ILR) were also found. Recent studies demonstrate the tight crosstalk between these two phytohormones, which are able to regulate each other i.e. accumulation of auxin and ethylene down-regulation, through flavonol accumulation and auxin efflux (Lewis et al. [2011](#page-18-34)). This is in accordance with the decreased expression of genes classified in the auxin efflux and basipetal transport GO process (Fig. [4](#page-7-0)). In apple fruit, increased auxin content is able to trigger cell expansion (Devoghalaere et al. [2012\)](#page-17-32). As previously discussed, the present data suggests that an accumulation of auxin, through the ILR gene and the lower efflux, might occur and lead to enhanced cell expansion in RAE.

A large number of transcription factors were differentially expressed, including members of families such as the bHLH, AP2/EREBP, C2H2, NAC-domain and R2R3-MYB and MYB-related (Table [2,](#page-8-0) Online Resource 4). Although some of these families are suspected to be involved in the regulation of the suberization process, to date few candidates have been identified *in planta* but none in apple. A significant number of transcription factors belonging to the NAC-domain family such as NAC038, 058, 073, 075, were strongly expressed in RAE (Table [2](#page-8-0)). NAC family transcription factors participate in the regulation of a broad spectrum of biological processes in plants. In Rice, OsNAC075 is responsive to abiotic stress such as salt and cold stress (Jung et al. [2011\)](#page-18-35). Interestingly, NAC058 displayed a slight interaction (confidence = 0.534) association with the CYP86A1 gene, but no clear evidence of the involvement of NAC transcription factor has been found yet (Fig. [6](#page-14-0)).

The R2R3-MYB transcription factor family is implicated in many biological processes such as metabolism, plant development or defense and displayed the most significant differences (Table [2,](#page-8-0) Online Resource 4). In the present study MYB93, MYB67, MYB68, MYB52, MYB5 and MYB102-like transcription factors were strongly expressed in russeted apple, whereas MYB94 and MYB42 were less expressed (Table [2](#page-8-0)). MYB52 and MYB42 were classified in the GO "regulation of the secondary cell wall biogenesis", together with ANAC073/SND2, which is also up-regulated in russeted apple skin (Fig. [4\)](#page-7-0). Their functions are not fully understood, but they are part of the second regulatory layer of secondary cell wall biogenesis, which is driven by the master SND1 regulatory gene, and involved in the regulation of secondary cell wall thickening (Zhong et al. [2008](#page-19-17)). Recent investigations showed that MYB52, which is co-expressed with SND2, may be a repressor of the lignin biosynthesis and secondary cell wall formation (Cassan-Wang et al. [2013\)](#page-17-33). SND2 regulates cellulose, xylan and mannan synthesis as well as lignin polymerization (laccase), but is not involved in the regulation of monolignol synthesis (Hussey et al. [2011\)](#page-18-36). In maize, MYB42 is responsive to wounding and represses the COMT and to a lower extent the C4H, 4CL and CAD6 genes, which lead to a decreased lignin deposition (Fornale et al. [2006\)](#page-17-34). In the present study, the expression of MYB42 seemed to be associated with the RAE (Table [2,](#page-8-0) Fig. [5\)](#page-13-0) and a concomitant increase in the expression of COMT and C4H genes was observed (Table [2](#page-8-0)), while the CAD4 and CAD9 remained less expressed suggesting that additional regulatory processes occur. The stronger expression of the cell wall loosening-related genes observed in the present study might be linked to the increased expression of MYB102, which enhances the expression of expansins, XETs or pectin esterases in response to wounding in *A. thaliana* (De Vos et al. [2006\)](#page-17-35). As these R2R3-MYB transcription factors play a role in secondary cell wall modification, we can speculate that they might also be involved in the suberization process. Moreover, the function of MYB93, MYB67 and MYB94 transcription factors, which displayed the largest differences in expression, is not known. Particular interest should be given to the MYB93 transcription factor, which displays a weak interaction with the CYP86A1 fatty acid ω-hydroxylase in the STRING analysis (Fig. [6](#page-14-0)). However, qPCR data (Fig. [5](#page-13-0)) shows a high Pearson correlation coefficient between MYB93 and GAPT5, CYP86A1, CYP86B1, with R<sup>2</sup> <sup>=</sup> 0.987, 0.976 and 0.976 respectively (*p* value <0.0001). In *Arabidopsis*, AtMYB93 is induced by auxin and acts as a repressor of lateral root development (Gibbs et al. 2014), but no studies related to the involvement of MYB93 in suberization are available. Further investigations have to be done to clarify whether these regulators play any role in the russeted phenotype.

## **Conclusion**

In plants, russeting occurs in response to various stimuli. However, in russeted and semi-russeted apple varieties, this phenotype can be observed without any direct external stress exposure. Previous studies showed that the tensions caused by the growing fruit on the periderm can lead to cracks in the cuticle, which in turn leads to suberin deposition. The present study provides a comprehensive picture of the causes, consequences and actors of russeting. A non-expected result is the differential expression of numerous cell wall-related genes, which suggests a stronger loosening of the cell wall in russeted apple skin concomitantly with a lower expression of cuticle deposition-related genes, i.e. genes involved in cutin and wax synthesis. The alterations of the peridermal composition and the weakening of the protective barrier due to the mechanical stress may explain the increased expression of numerous genes involved in stress responsive signaling and regulatory machinery, which probably initiates the synthesis and deposition of suberin as well as the synthesis of specific triterpenes. Taken altogether, these data confirm previous hypotheses, which postulate that suberin occurrence is tightly linked to the integrity of the cuticle layer. Additionally, this particular experimental design provides a large coverage of orthologous genes implicated in apple skin russeting and unveils new players related to suberin synthesis and its regulation.
