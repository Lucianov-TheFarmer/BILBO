## **Abstract**

 Senescence occurs in a programmed manner to dismantle the vegetative tissues and redirect nutrients towards metabolic pathways supporting reproductive success. External factors can trigger the senescence program as an adaptive strategy, indicating that this terminal program is controlled at different levels. It has been proposed that epigenetic factors accompany the reprogramming of the senescent genome; however, the mechanism and extent of this reprogramming remain unknown. Using bisulphite conversion followed by sequencing, we assessed changes in the methylome of senescent Arabidopsis (*Arabidopsis thaliana*) leaves induced by darkness, and monitored their effect on gene and transposable element (TE) expression with transcriptome sequencing. Upon dark-induced senescence, genes controlling chromatin silencing were collectively downregulated. As a consequence, the silencing of TEs was impaired, causing in particular young TEs to become preferentially reactivated. In parallel, heterochromatin at chromocenters was decondensed. Despite the disruption of the chromatin maintenance network, the global DNA methylation landscape remained highly stable, with localized changes mainly restricted to CHH methylation. Together, our data show that the terminal stage of plant life is accompanied by global changes in chromatin structure but only localized changes in DNA methylation, adding another example of the dynamics of DNA methylation during plant development.

## **Introduction**

 Senescence can be defined as a time-dependent, gradual decay of multiple biological functions (López-Otín *et al.*, 2013). The functional decline of certain metabolic pathways is a direct and irreversible consequence of the progressive accumulation of atrophies and cellular damage. In plants, senescence is a developmentally programmed transition in which the leaves are dismantled in a coordinated manner to recycle nutrients and remobilize them to the seed in order to ensure reproductive success (Schippers *et al.*, 2015; Himelblau & Amasino, 2001). For instance, in Arabidopsis (*Arabidopsis thaliana*), 80% of the seed nitrogen content is derived from chloroplast degradation (Girondé *et al.*, 2015). Chloroplasts are the first organelles to be dismantled, followed by the mitochondria and the nucleus, which remain intact until the late stages of senescence (Dodge, 1970; Butler, 1967). Another important function of leaf senescence is phosphate remobilization, which is accomplished by the degradation of organellar DNA and RNA, and cytosolic ribosomal RNA. The onset of senescence is tightly regulated to occur in an age-dependent manner. Nevertheless, external conditions that compromise the survival of the plant can trigger the senescence program as an adaptive strategy to secure reproductive success. As such, senescence in plants can be broadly categorized as either developmental or precocious (Schippers *et al.*, 2015). Dark-induced senescence, for instance, largely depends on both the intensity and wavelength of the perceived light, variations of which can trigger precocious senescence (Lers, 2007). Consistent with the role of leaves as photosynthetic factories, darkening of individual leaves leads to an accelerated senescence response. In contrast, exposure of whole plants to darkness delays the senescence program, suggesting that the light status of the whole plant determines the onset of the senescence program in individual leaves (Weaver & Amasino, 2001).

 The induction of senescence is accompanied by genome-wide transcriptional changes leading to the activation of senescence-associated genes (SAGs) (Aeong Oh *et al.*, 1997). Key players of this regulatory cascade are transcription factors such as NAC, WRKY, C2H2, AP2/EREBP, and MYB proteins [Reviewed in (Liebsch & Keech, 2016)]. Overexpression of those regulators of senescence is accompanied by a significant increase in the deposition of the activating histone marks H3K4me2 and H3K4me3 (Liu *et al.*, 2019; Brusslan *et al.*, 2012; Ay *et al.*, 2009), highlighting the importance of a concerted epigenetic regulation even in the very last stages of plant development. Concomitant to the transcriptional response and increased deposition of activating histone marks, the pericentromeric heterochromatin in the nuclei of senescent leaves becomes successively decondensed (Ay *et al.*, 2009). Pericentromeric heterochromatin is characterized by high levels of enrichment of the silencing histone mark H3K9me2, which in turn reinforces the deposition of DNA methylation (Law *et al.*, 2010; Cokus *et al.*, 2008), leading to a stable transcriptional silencing of this chromatin state (Sequeira-Mendes *et al.*, 2014). Consistently, the observed expansion of the senescent chromocenters is accompanied by an extended redistribution of the heterochromatic mark histone H3 lysine 9 dimethylation (H3K9me2) and H3 lysine 27 monomethylation (H3K27me1) (Ay *et al.*, 2009); however, whether senescence causes global changes in DNA methylation remains to be explored. Plant genomes can be methylated in three different sequence contexts: symmetric CG and CHG and asymmetric CHH, where H can be either A, T, or C (Law & Jacobsen, 2010). DNA methylation is in particular required to silence TEs and other repetitive sequences to prevent their uncontrolled transposition and thus, maintain genome integrity. *De novo* DNA methylation in the plant genome is established by two pathways targeting distinct regions of the genome: RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) mediates *de novo* methylation in all sequence contexts mainly in euchromatic regions, whereas the

 DDM1-dependent CMT2 methyltransferase mediates *de novo* methylation in heterochromatic regions from where the RdDM pathway is excluded (Zemach *et al.*, 2013). The canonical RdDM pathway is triggered by 24-nucleotide (nt) small RNAs produced by RNA POLYMERASE IV (Pol IV) (Herr *et al.*, 2005). Pol IV transcripts are converted into double stranded RNA by RNA-DEPENDENT RNA POLYMERASE 2 (RDR2) and cleaved into small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) by DICER-LIKE PROTEIN 3 (DCL3) (Herr *et al.*, 2005; Xie *et al.*, 2004). siRNAs are loaded into ARGONAUTE 4 (AGO4) or AGO6 to recognize the Pol V-transcript and trigger DNA methylation by recruiting DOMAINS REARRANGED METHYLTRANSFERASE 2 (DRM2) (Duan *et al.*, 2015; Zhong *et al.*, 2014; Eun *et al.*, 2011; Zilberman *et al.*, 2003). Furthermore, pre- existing histone modifications such as H3K9me2 can recruit Pol IV and reinforce silencing (Law *et al.*, 2013). Context-dependent dedicated mechanisms maintain DNA methylation: METHYLTRANSFERASE 1 (MET1) maintains CG methylation (Liu *et al.*, 2012; To *et al.*, 2011); CHROMOMETHYLASE 3 (CMT3) is the main methyltransferase maintaining CHG methylation (Cao & Jacobsen, 2002), and CHH methylation is maintained by DRM2 or CMT2, depending on the chromatin context (Liu *et al.*, 2014; Zemach *et al.*, 2013; Huettel *et al.*, 2006).

 Here, we report a severe transcriptional downregulation of a gene module in charge of maintaining the integrity of the chromatin during dark-induced senescence. The transcriptional changes are accompanied by decondensation of heterochromatin at chromocenters and disrupted expression of TEs. Despite the massive downregulation of genes involved in the homeostasis of DNA methylation, only localized changes in the senescent methylome were observed, preferentially located in the CHH context and co-occurring with transcriptional changes.

## **Results**

# **Chromocenter heterochromatin relaxation is a common nuclear phenotype of senescent leaves**

 Senescence can be induced in individually covered leaves (Weaver & Amasino, 2001), offering the possibility to investigate the senescing and non-senescing tissue derived from the same plant. This reduces confounding effects from developmental or systemic stress responses, highlighting those that are exclusive to the senescence response. Given the previously described de-condensation of the centromeric heterochromatin during the early stages of developmental leaf senescence (Ay *et al.*, 2009), we tested whether a similar reconfiguration of the chromatin would prevail when senescence was induced in individual leaves. We triggered senescence by individually covering mature leaves for five days and used non-senescent leaves from the same plant as control (Supplemental Fig. S1a). Onset of senescence was evident by yellowing of the covered leaves and significantly increased expression of senescent marker genes *SEN4*, *SAG12*, and *SGR1* in covered versus non-covered leaves as measured by RT-qPCR (Supplemental Fig. S1b-c). Staining of interphase nuclei with 4'-6-diamidino-2- phenylindole (DAPI) showed that the signal from the senescent chromocenters was less compact than the signal from the non-senescent ones (Fig. **1a**). Quantification of the relative heterochromatic fraction (RHF, defined as the fluorescence intensity of all DAPI- stained chromocenters relative to the fluorescence of the entire nucleus (Soppe *et al.*, 2002)) confirmed lower heterochromatin condensation in senescent (mean value ~25 %) than in non-senescent (mean value ~15 %) nuclei (Fig. **1b**). The same effect was observed by quantifying the relative chromocenter area (the proportion of DAPI-stained nuclear DNA retained in chromocenters (Ikeda *et al.*, 2017)) of senescent (mean value ~10%) versus non-senescent (mean value ~5%) nuclei (Supplemental Fig. S2). Nevertheless, the number of detectable chromocenters was the same in both conditions

 (Fig. **1b**), indicating that upon senescence there is an extensive heterochromatin relaxation of the chromocenters but there is no formation of additional conspicuous chromocenters. These results reveal that heterochromatin decondensation in chromocenters is a common nuclear phenotype of senescent nuclei, both, as a consequence of the normal developmental program and of dark-induced senescence.

## **Dark-induced senescence activates TEs**

 Transcriptome analysis of dark-induced senescent and control leaves revealed 3,939 upregulated (log2FoldChange > 1 and Bonferroni p.adj < 0.05, Supplemental Table S7) and 5,080 downregulated genes (log2FoldChange < -1 and Bonferroni p.adj < 0.05, Supplemental Table S8). Metabolism-related categories such as rRNA processing, starch biosynthetic processes, and regulation of DNA replication prevailed at the top of the gene-ontology enrichment of differentially expressed genes (Table **1**, Supplemental Fig S3.), consistent with previous transcriptome studies of developmental leaf senescence in Arabidopsis (Mishra *et al.*, 2018; Woo *et al.*, 2016; Breeze *et al.*, 2011). Strikingly, the ontology related to histone H3K9 methylation was enriched in our senescent transcriptome. From the 115 differentially expressed genes that were significantly enriched in this ontology (p-value < 0.01 in classic Kolmogorov-Smirnov test), 109 were downregulated in senescent leaves (Supplemental Table S9 and Fig. S3.). This transcriptional downregulation is consistent with previously reported changes in H3K9me2 deposition during developmental senescence (Ay *et al.*, 2009) that accompany the observed relaxation of chromocenters (Fig. **1**). Because H3K9me2 marks constitutive heterochromatin and is required for silencing of TEs and repetitive regions, we wondered whether the relaxed chromocenter heterochromatin and global downregulation of the H3K9me2-mediated silencing machinery would coincide with ectopic reactivation of TEs. From our senescent and non-senescent transcriptome data

 we were able to recover transcripts of 4,846 TEs out of the 31,189 TEs annotated in TAIR10. From those, 465 were defined as differentially expressed TEs (DET): 215 were upregulated (log2FoldChange > 1 and Bonferroni p.adj < 0.05, Supplemental Table S10) and 250 downregulated (log2FoldChange < 1 and Bonferroni p.adj < 0.05, Supplemental Table S11). Despite the decondensed chromocenters in the senescent samples, most deregulated TEs were not located in pericentromeric regions and belonged to DNA and RC/*Helitron* TE families (p-value < 0.05, Hypergeometric test) (Table **2**). Interestingly, even though most of the TEs for which we recovered transcripts are classified as ancient insertions (276 out of 465 DET, Table **2**) (Zhong *et al.*, 2012), the TEs that were most affected upon dark-induced senescence corresponded to young TEs, which were deregulated in both directions (Fig. **2a**). Together, our data reveal that dark-induced senescence causes activation of young, non-pericentromeric TEs belonging to DNA and *Helitron* families.

# **Dark-induced senescence triggers downregulation of histones and chromatin regulators**

 In addition to the deregulated genes affecting H3K9me2, we also found significant enrichment of genes involved in DNA methylation, small RNA transcriptional regulation, and Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2)-mediated silencing among the downregulated genes (log2Folchange < 1 and Bonferroni p.adj < 0.05) in the dark- induced senescence transcriptome (Table **3**). Using the retrieved significant ontology classes as proxy to reconstruct the enrichment network, we found a highly connected module formed by multiple nodes containing genes involved in the regulation of chromatin structure and maintenance during cell cycle (Fig. **2b,** Supplemental Table S12). Among the most highly connected genes were those encoding for canonical histones and histone variants (Supplemental Table S13). We found five genes encoding

 for different copies of the canonical histone H3.1 to be severely downregulated (log2FoldChange ranging from -2.2 to -5.5 and Bonferroni p.adj < 0.05). Similarly, the gene *AT2G28740* encoding for the canonical histone H4 was downregulated and highly connected in the chromatin structure network. Dimers of (H3-H4)2 are incorporated into chromatin by CAF-1 in a replication-dependent manner (Smith & Stillman, 1989). *FAS1* and *FAS2*, encoding for the largest and middle subunits of CAF1, were also downregulated (log2FoldChange = -3.19 and -2.82, respectively and Bonferroni p.adj < 0.05). Shutting down the components of the replication-dependent chromatin assembly machinery might be a consequence of the extended reprogramming of the leaf genome to progressively transition into an irreversible state of senescent cellular arrest. Consistently, we found joint downregulation of mini-chromosome maintenance (MCM) proteins MCM2-7 and the cyclin-dependent kinase CDC45, which are required to assemble the pre-replicative complex (pre-RC) during the G1 phase and to trigger the transition to replication (Aparicio *et al.*, 2006; Zou & Stillman, 1998). Also downregulated were the helicase ATRECQ4A and topoisomerases II and III (ATTOPII and AtTOP3alpha) that function in DNA replication during S phase in proliferative tissues [Reviewed in (Tuteja *et al.*, 2011)]. In line with the observed disruption of the chromocenters after dark-induced senescence, the centromere-specific histone variant CENH3 (log2FoldChange = -3.41 and Bonferroni p.adj < 6.7e-07 ) and the pericentromeric histone variant H2A.W.6 (Yelagandula *et al.*, 2014) (log2FoldChange = -5.06 and Bonferroni p.adj < 2.07e-109 ) were also dramatically downregulated. These results reveal that senescence is associated with a strong downregulation of histones, histone chaperones, and regulators of replication, which eventually might lead to the remobilization of TEs to facilitate fragmentation and recycling of the components of the chromatin.

# **The Arabidopsis DNA methylation landscape is robust to dark-induced senescence**

 Among the downregulated genes represented in the chromatin-enriched network (Fig. **2b**), we encountered members of both, the canonical *de novo* RdDM pathway and its complementary CMT2-directed methylation pathway (Supplemental Tables S8 and S12). We thus hypothesized that senescence induced changes in the methylation landscape, which could in turn explain the disrupted expression of TEs (Table **2,** Fig. **2a**). To test this hypothesis, we performed whole genome sequencing (WGS) on bisulphite-treated DNA (WGBS) extracted from the same mix of senescent and non- senescent leaves used for the RNA sequencing (Supplemental Fig. S1). Consistent with previous reports (Law & Jacobsen, 2010), about 21% of the recovered cytosines were detected as methylated in the control leaves and this status did not change in senescent conditions in any methylation context (Fig. **3a-b.**). Thus, the genome-wide level of DNA methylation is robust to dark-induced senescence. We tested the possibility that cytosine methylation became redistributed to regions that are normally not methylated by assessing the methylation status of genes and TEs in control and senescent conditions. Nevertheless, the profiles and levels of CG, CHG, and CHH methylation remained undisturbed upon senescence (Fig. **3c.**), revealing that the Arabidopsis methylome remains globally stable upon dark-induced senescence.

## **The CHH methylation context is the most affected in dark-induced senescence**

 We tested whether dark-induced senescence would cause localized DNA methylation changes in the form of Differentially Methylated Regions (DMRs). The methylation levels of all samples were highly correlated in all sequence contexts (Supplemental Fig. S4a-c), consistent with the previously noticed stability of the DNA methylome. Nevertheless, clustering of methylation levels revealed the existence of methylation

 differences that were strong enough to distinguish control and senescent samples (Supplemental Fig. S4d-f). Consistent with the observed global stability of the methylome (Fig. **3**), we detected only very few DMRs in CG and CHG sequence context (35 and 2, respectively, (Fig. **4a**) and 1,111 DMRs in CHH context, of which 890 were hypomethylated in the senescent samples (Fig. **4a**). The majority of the CHH DMRs lost about 25% of the methylation (Fig. **4b**). Most of the DMRs losing CG methylation were located in gene bodies, whereas CHH DMRs were equally detectable in promoters (2 Kb upstream of the transcriptional start site of protein coding genes), gene bodies, and TEs (Fig. **4c**). All of the TEs carrying CG DMRs were located outside of genes and promoters, whereas about 10% of TEs carrying CHH DMRs overlapped promoters and 3% overlapped genes (Fig. **4d**). LTR *Gypsy* elements were the most overrepresented TE family carrying DMRs from both CG and CHH, but the overrepresentation was only significant in the CHH context (Fig. **4e**). LTR *Gypsy* elements are mainly located in pericentromeric regions; thus, the observed methylation changes are consistent with the observed relaxation of the chromocenters in senescent samples (Fig. **1**). Nevertheless, only very few LTR/*Copia* TEs with CHH DMRs and none of those carrying CG DMR were significantly deregulated (Supplemental Fig. S5, Table S11). # **Drought stress and dark-induced senescence cause a similar transcriptional, but**

## **not epigenome response**

 Previous studies demonstrated that the Arabidopsis methylome remains stable upon abiotic stress such as drought (Ganguly *et al.*, 2017) and phosphate deprivation (Secco *et al.*, 2015). In contrast, biotic stress causes the formation of numerous DMRs, many of which are associated with differentially expressed genes (Dowen *et al.*, 2012). In order to address the specificity of the epigenomic and transcriptomic response to darkness- induced senescence, we compared the changes in the methylome and transcriptome upon darkness-induced senescence to those induced by biotic and abiotic stresses. A small fraction of CHH DMRs in response to senescence coincided with methylation changes induced upon *Pseudomonas syringae* infection (Supplemental Table S22)

 (Dowen *et al.*, 2012). However, none of these common DMRs were associated with DEGs induced by senescence. From the 49 DMRs induced by drought stress in Arabidopsis (Ganguly et al., 2017), none of them overlapped with DMRs promoted by dark-induced senescence. In contrast, from the 4,371 DEGs induced by drought, 2,717 were also DEGs in dark-induced senescence (p-value of overlap = 0) (Supplemental Fig. S8, Supplemental Tables S7 and S8). Importantly, those common DEGs were less likely to have a DMR in senescent samples than senescence-specific DEGs (Supplemental Table S23), consistent with the fact that there was a strong overlap in DEGs but not DMRs between both stress treatments. Out of the 428 genes contained in the epigenomic network downregulated in senescence (Fig. **2b**), 184 genes were also DEGs in drought stress. While this overlap is significant (p-value of overlap = 2.05 e -35 ), it is nevertheless only less than half of the genes in the network being similarly deregulated under drought and senescence, providing a possible explanation for the difference in the DNA methylation response under both stress treatments. Thus, the strong overlap of commonly deregulated genes was not accompanied by a common set of DMRs induced by drought and senescence, revealing that senescence-induced DMRs and associated transcriptional changes are more prominent in response to senescence than in response to biotic and abiotic stresses that have similar phenotypic consequences.

# **Discussion**

 In the present study, we report the dark-induced downregulation of a transcriptional network whose nodes contain genes that encode key players in the maintenance of chromatin integrity and DNA methylation. The chosen system to induce senescence by individually darkening mature Arabidopsis leaves was based on a previous study (Weaver & Amasino, 2001) and the same system was later used on several other studies (Law et al., 2018; Keech et al., 2007; van der Graaff et al., 2006; Pruzinska et al., 2005). At five days after individually covering the leaves, high expression of senescence markers was observed in the darkened leaves and this timepoint was therefore chosen in this study. Previous studies have described the dynamics of the Arabidopsis transcriptome upon developmental senescence (Mishra *et al.*, 2018; Woo *et al.*, 2016; Breeze *et al.*, 2011). Most of the enriched gene ontology classes related to metabolic processes such as chlorophyll degradation, perception and response to light and others, were also present at the top of the gene ontologies recovered from the differentially expressed genes upon dark-induced senescence. However, previous studies did not uncover the senescence-mediated effect on the chromatin-maintenance network. The genes with the highest connectivity encode for the canonical histones H3 and H4, respectively. Along with these, transcripts of FAS1 and FAS2, members of the chromatin assembly factor CAF1 (Loyola & Almouzni, 2004) were also downregulated. CAF1 loads the dimers H3-H4 to the newly transcribed DNA during S-phase (Smith & Stillman, 1989). Downregulation of the components of nucleosomes that are loaded in a replication-dependent manner, as well as its chaperon complex, strongly suggests that during senescence the genome is widely reprogrammed towards a stage of irreversible cellular arrest. Importantly, we also observed a strong downregulation of components of the RdDM and DDM1/CMT2 methylation pathway, consistent with the observation of

 decondensed chromocenters and loss of CHH methylation at LTR *Gypsy* TEs. Decondensation of the chromocenters has been previously observed in developmentally programmed senescence (Ay *et al.*, 2009), revealing that it is a common nuclear phenotype induced by senescence, possibly as the first step to facilitate fragmentation of the genome and recycling of phosphate (Dodge, 1970). Derepression of TEs could act as the trigger to break genome integrity. We found about ~15% of the TAIR10-annotated TEs were transcribed during dark-induced senescence. While most of the expressed TEs corresponded to ancient events of transposition as classified by (Zhong *et al.*, 2012), young TEs were preferentially reactivated, indicating that young TEs are more sensitive to the disruption of epigenetic silencing.

 Strikingly though, despite the strong downregulation of genes involved in DNA methylation, the overall methylation landscape remained highly stable. The observed changes were mainly in CHH context and highly localized. While not on a global scale, there were many DMRs associated with changes in gene expression. The changes of DNA methylation induced by dark-induced senescence were highly specific, since no significant overlap was found with DMRs induced by different biotic and abiotic stresses. DNA methylation is dynamically controlled in different tissues and throughout plant development. For instance, the endosperm of rice and Arabidopsis is globally hypomethylated compared to the embryo (Ibarra *et al.*, 2012; Zemach *et al.*, 2010; Gehring *et al.*, 2009; Hsieh *et al.*, 2009). During embryo development, CHH methylation increases genome-wide, but subsequently decreases again during germination, pointing to a crucial role of CHH methylation during seed dormancy (Bouyer *et al.*, 2017; Kawakatsu *et al.*, 2017; Lin *et al.*, 2017; Narsai *et al.*, 2017). Similarly, tomato fruit ripening is accompanied by changes in the methylome. The promoters of fruit-ripening genes that bear a binding site for RIPENING-INHIBITOR undergo active DNA demethylation, which correlates with the binding of RIPENING-INHIBITOR and

 transcriptional inhibition (Lang *et al.*, 2017; Zhong *et al.*, 2013). Thus, our data add another example of the dynamics of DNA methylation during the terminal stage of plant life, highlighting the importance of DNA methylation and the reprogramming of the epigenome for plant development.

# **Materials and Methods**

**Plant material and growth conditions** 

 The Arabidopsis (*Arabidopsis thaliana*) (L.) Heynh. wild-type accession Columbia (Col) was used in all experiments. Seeds were sown on 0.5× basal salts Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium (Duchefa, Haarlem, The Netherlands), stratified at 4°C for one day, and then transferred to growth chambers for germination at 20°C under short day (SD, 8 h light) photoperiods for ten days before being transferred to soil. Plants were grown for 42 days under short day (SD, 8 h light) photoperiods. On the 42nd day, rosette leaf numbers five and six were individually covered with a black cloth mitten to induce senescence. Tissue was collected on the 48th day at ZT (Zeitgeber time) = 2. For the bisulphite conversion and RNA sequencing experiments, non-senescent tissue corresponds to a mix of uncovered leaves from the same plants. For the cytogenetic analysis, the non-senescing leaves corresponded to uncovered leaves number five and six from different plants to avoid possible confounding effects due to leaf age.

**RNA isolation and RT-qPCR**

 RNA was extracted using the QIAGEN RNeasy kit. Reverse transcription was performed as described previously (Alexandre *et al.*, 2009) with minor modifications. DNA-free RNA was reverse-transcribed using a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Fermentas, Helsingborg, Sweden). Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed with gene-specific primers (Supplemental Table S1) using SYBR

 green (Fermentas) on an IQ5 multicolor Real time PCR cycler (BIO-RAD, PA, USA). RT-qPCR reactions were performed in triplicate, gene expression levels were normalized to a *PP2A* control gene (*AT1G69960*), and results were analysed as described (Simon, 2003).

## **Cytogenetic analyses**

 After induction of senescence, leaves were fixed in a 3:1 ethanol/acetic acid solution and stored at 4°C. Interphase nuclear spreads for DAPI staining were prepared according to (Fransz *et al.*, 2002). Briefly, leaves were rinsed in citrate buffer (10 mM sodium citrate, pH 4.5) and incubated in an enzyme mix (0.3% (w/v) pectolyase (Sigma), 0.3% (w/v) cytohelicase (Sepracor), and 0.3% (w/v) cellulase (Sigma) in citrate buffer for 2 h at 37°C. Digested leaves were softened in 100 µl 60% (v/v) acetic acid on a non-coated, ethanol cleaned, microscopic slide, spread at 45°C, fixed with ice-cold ethanol/acetic acid (3:1) solution, and dried. The dried preparations were mounted in Vectashield containing 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Vector laboratories) and used for nuclear size and chromocenter numbers measurements. Imaging was performed with a Leica DMI4000. For relative heterochromatin area and intensity quantification, manual measurements of grey-scale images were carried out with ImageJ. Relative heterochromatin area defined as the area of all DAPI-stained chromocenters relative to the area of the entire nucleus was calculated for each sample as the ratio of the sum of the chromocenter area to the area of the whole nucleus. RHF, defined as the fluorescence intensity of all DAPI-stained chromocenters relative to the fluorescence of the entire nucleus, was calculated for each sample as the ratio of the sum of chromocenter intensities to the intensity of the whole nucleus. At least 100 nuclei from each sample and replicate were measured. Significance corresponds to a p-value < 0.01 in a Wilcoxon test.

**RNA sequencing library preparation** 

 Libraries of two biological replicates of senescent and non-senescent transcriptomes were generated using DNA-free RNA with the TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit v2 (Illumina) according to the instructions of the manufacturer. Sequencing was performed on Illumina HiSeq2000 platform in 50-bp single-end mode. Read counts are reported in Supplemental Table S2. The biological replicates had a Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.98 and 0.84 for non-senescent and senescent conditions, respectively (Supplemental Fig. S7).

## **Bisulphite conversion and library preparation**

 Genomic DNA of two biological replicates of senescent and non-senescent samples was isolated using the MagJET Plant Genomic DNA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and bisulphite converted using the EpiJET Bisulfite Conversion Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA libraries were prepared using the EpiGnome kit (currently Illumina TruSeq).

## **Analysis of Cytosine-DNA methylation by bisulphite sequencing**

 Two biological replicates of each, senescent (S1 and S2) and non-senescent (NS1 and NS2) bisulphite converted DNA were sequenced in paired-end mode of 120 nucleotide long reads using Illumina HiSeq2000 platform. Quality control was performed with in- house scripts. Adapter trimming and removal of low complexity sequences was done using reaper (Davis *et al.*, 2013). Reads with total sequencing quality of less than 30 Phred or shorter than 10 nucleotides were discarded. Clean reads were mapped to the TAIR10 reference genome using Bismark (Krueger & Andrews, 2011), allowing at most one mismatch per 25-nucleotide seed. Forward and reverse reads were mapped independently. Optical duplicates were removed using the SAMTools function rmdup (Subgroup *et al.*, 2009). Read counts are reported in Supplemental Table S2. Conversion rate of cytosines were obtained using bismark\_methylation\_extractor. Cytosines in the plastid genome are not methylated, allowing us to estimate the

 efficiency of bisulphite conversion. The mean conversion rate was > 94% and the estimated mean false positive methylation rates were < 6% (Supplemental Table S3). Consistency of biological replicates was assessed by correlating the methylation status of 50 bp non-overlapping bins containing a minimum of three cytosines covered by at least one read in each replicate. While methylation values from all four libraries displayed a significant pair-wise correlation (Supplemental Fig. S4a-c), Ward's clustering of the correlation distances allowed us to separate the senescing methylation profiles from the non-senescing ones (Supplemental Fig. S4 d-f). To increase the power of the statistical test to detect differential methylation, Bismark mappings from the replicates were merged and optical duplicates removed. Cytosines that were covered by at least three reads were considered for the calculation of differentially methylated regions (DMRs). The Arabidopsis TAIR10 genome sequence was divided into non- overlapping windows of 50 bp. Because libraries S1 and NS2 were sequenced to a much higher depth than S2 and NS1 (Supplemental Table S2), to normalize the different contribution of reads per biological replicate, methylation values were calculated as the weighted methylation level (Schultz *et al.*, 2012) along bins containing at least three covered cytosines. To avoid calling DMRs with artificially small or large methylation differences due to low read coverage, the 10th percentile of bins with the lowest coverage was selected and among those, the bins with the 20% smallest and largest average methylation values were discarded. Bins with a q-value ≤ 0.01 (Fisher's exact test, Multiple Testing Correction by Storey's method (Storey, 2002)) were considered as significantly differentially methylated. Methylation changes are expressed as the absolute difference of the weighted methylation levels in senescent (S) vs non- senescent (NS) conditions. Bins located within 300 bp from each other were merged together to form the final set of DMRs (Supplemental Tables S4-S6).

**RNA sequencing data analysis** 

 Untrimmed reads were mapped to the TAIR10 Arabidopsis reference genome using STAR (v2.5.3.a, (Dobin *et al.*, 2012)). Expression counts were generated using the R function summarizeOverlaps from the package HTSeq in union mode on exons from the reference transcriptome AtRTD2 (Zhang *et al.*, 2017). Differential expression analyses were performed using the R package DESeq2 [v1.20.0, (Love *et al.*, 2014)]. Genes with absolute log2FoldChange ≥ 1 and FDR ≤ 0.05 were considered as differentially expressed. Gene ontology enrichment of all DEGs was done using TopGO package from R (version 2.34.0, (A & J, 2018)) and the OrgDb object org.At.tair.db version 2.1 (Carlson, 2019). Significance of enrichment corresponds to a p-value < 0.01 after a classic Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Gene ontology enrichment from only the downregulated genes were explored using gProfiler! (Peterson *et al.*, 2007) hierarchical, best-per-parent filtering. Network reconstruction of the most enriched gene ontologies was performed with the EnrichmentMap (Merico *et al.*, 2010) extension of Cytoscape [version 1.8.0\_162; (Shannon *et al.*, 2003)]. Similarly, differential expression analysis for TEs was performed by mapping untrimmed reads to the indexed fasta sequences of the 31,189 TAIR10 annotated TEs using bowtie2 (version 2.2.9; (Langmead & Salzberg, 2012)). The read count matrix along individual TEs was prepared with TEcount.py from the software package TETools (version 1.0.0-283c1ca, (Lerat *et al.*, 2016)) and imported into R to perform differential expression analysis using DESeq2 and the same threshold values as for gene transcripts.
