# Transcriptome responses in wheat roots to colonization by the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus Rhizophagus irregularis

Mengjiao Li<sup>1</sup> & Runze Wang<sup>1</sup> & Hui Tian<sup>1</sup> & Yajun Gao<sup>1</sup>

Received: 11 April 2018 /Accepted: 17 September 2018 # Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2018

# Abstract

The influence of arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) colonization on the expression of genes in the roots of wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) at the transcriptome level is largely unknown. A pot experiment was established to study the responses of the transcriptome profile in the roots of wheat to colonization by the AM fungus Rhizophagus irregularis using high through-put sequencing methods. The results indicated that the expression of 11,746 genes was regulated by AM colonization, and 64.7% of them were up-regulated genes. 1106 genes were only expressed in roots colonized by AM fungi, and 108 genes were only expressed in nonmycorrhizal roots. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were primarily distributed on the 2B, 3B, 2A, 2D, and 5B chromosomes of wheat. The DEGs (including both up- and down- regulated) mainly located on membranes, and functioned in nucleotide binding and transferase activity during cellular protein modification and biosynthetic processes. The data revealed that AM colonization up-regulated genes involved in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway and transcription factors which play vital roles in protecting plants from biotic or abiotic stresses. A number of key genes involved in molecular signal biosynthesis and recognition, epidermal cell colonization and arbuscule formation, carbon and nutrients exchange during AM symbiosis were found. All the ammonium transporter (AMT), iron-phytosiderophore transporter, boron, zinc, and magnesium transporter genes found in our study were up-regulated DEGs. One new AM-specific induced AMT and three new AM-specific induced nitrate transporter (NRT) genes were found in the roots of wheat colonized by AM fungi, even though a negative growth response of wheat to AM colonization occurred. The present study provided new information which is important for understanding the mechanisms behind the development and function of the symbiosis between wheat and AM fungi.

Keywords Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi . RNA-seq . Wheat . Transcriptome

# Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the most important food crops in the world and serves as the staple food source for about 30% of the human population (The International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium [2014](#page-11-0)). The roots of wheat can be colonized by arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi, although the extent of AM colonization varies depending on wheat varieties (Lehnert et al. [2017\)](#page-11-0), AM species (Duan et al. [2015](#page-10-0)) and environmental conditions (Talaat and Shawky [2014](#page-11-0)). The growth responses of wheat to AM colonization could range from positive to neutral to negative depending on the combination of wheat verities and AM species and environmental conditions (Duan et al. [2015](#page-10-0); Li et al. [2006;](#page-11-0) Talaat and Shawky [2011](#page-11-0)), but a meta-analysis indicated that colonization by AM fungi could improve nutrient uptake, growth, or yield of wheat based on 38 published field trials (Pellegrino et al. [2015\)](#page-11-0).

The influence of AM colonization on gene expression in wheat roots has been reported. Several AM-inducible phosphate-transporter genes only expressed in root cells containing arbuscules have been identified in wheat (Glassop et al. [2005;](#page-10-0) Sisaphaithong et al. [2012](#page-11-0)). Duan et al. ([2015\)](#page-10-0) studied the expression of four phosphate (Pi) transporter, five nitrate transporter (NRT), and three ammonium transporter (AMT)

genes in wheat roots associated with four different AM fungal species (Acaulospora delicata, Claroideoglomus etunicatum, Rhizophagus intraradices, and Funneliformis mosseae) and found that colonization by AM fungi regulated the expression of Pi transporter genes locally rather than systemically; inoculation with AM fungi either had no effect or could locally or systemically down-regulate expression of NRT or AMT genes, depending on the gene type and AM fungal species. Another study found the expression of the genes AMT1.1, AMT1.2, and NAR2.2 in the roots of wheat was not influenced by colonization by AM fungi, but the expression of NRT1.1 was up-regulated. Additionally, some drought stress-related genes were found down-regulated by AM colonization in this study (Fileccia et al. 2017). However, the responses of gene expression in wheat roots to AM colonization at the transcriptomic level, which is of great importance for understanding the mechanisms of wheat-AM symbiosis development, have not been reported so far although the sequencing of the whole genome of wheat is nearly completed (Shi and Ling 2018).

Different transcriptional profiles in mycorrhizal vs. nonmycorrhizal plants using microarrays or next-generation sequencing technology have been reported since the beginning of the twenty-first century, but mainly for model plants such as Medicago truncatula L., Lotus japonicus L., and rice (Oryza sativa L.) (Bonneau et al. 2013; Hohnjec et al. 2005; Handa et al. 2015). According to these studies, large numbers of genes are up-regulated or down-regulated in roots of AM plants vs. non-AM plants; however, the responses of transcriptome profiles in roots to colonization by AM fungi may differ among genetically distant plant species. For example, Sugimura and Saito (2017) compared the AM-responsive genes between two different hosts, tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) and L. japonicus L., during AM development, and the results indicated that only one quarter to one third of the AM-inducible genes in each plant species were coup-regulated in both species. The co-up-regulated genes mainly included those known to be essential genes for AM development and function. Previous transcriptomic studies have revealed that genes involved in some bioprocesses such as abiotic stimuli, defense, chromatin, and DNA metabolism, which are not directly involved in AM formation could also be regulated by AM colonization in roots of plants (Fiorilli et al. 2009; Hohnjec et al. 2005; Handa et al. 2015). Compared to diploid or tetraploid plants, wheat is a hexaploid plant and thus has a larger and more complex genome (Li et al. 2012). With 21 pairs of chromosomes, wheat is structurally an allopolyploid with three homoeologous sets of seven chromosomes in each of the A, B, and D subgenomes. The hexaploid wheat (AABBDD) is believed to have originated through one or more rare hybridization events between Aegilops tauschii (DD) and the tetraploid T. turgidum (AABB) (The International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium 2014). The sequencing and assembly of the entire wheat genome is very challenging due to the complexity of its genome structure, which delays the transcriptomic study of wheat compared with other crops such as rice and maize (*Zea mays* L.). During the past several years, many efforts were made on the sequencing of the wheat genome and a series of draft and nearcompleted genome assemblies for wheat have been generated in succession leading to an excellent basis for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) studies for wheat (The International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium 2018; Shi and Ling 2018). Thus, the present study aimed to investigate the responses of the transcriptome profile in the roots of wheat to AM colonization using high-throughput sequencing technology.

## Materials and methods

#### Plant materials and growth conditions

Seeds of wheat (T. aestivum L., Cultivar: Chinese spring) were surface-sterilized with 1% NaClO for 10 min, rinsed with deionized water, and then pre-germinated on moist filter paper in the dark at 25 °C for 24 h. The pre-germinated wheat seeds were planted into pots (20.5 cm in diameter and 14.5 cm high) filled with approximately 2.5 L of steam-sterilized soil-quartz sand mixture (1:3) (v/v). The soil was clay loam and was classified as a Eum-Orthic Anthrosol (FAO Soil Classification, Gong et al. 2003). Its basic fertility characteristics were as follows: nitrate 10.2 mg kg<sup>-1</sup>, ammonium 1.95 mg kg<sup>-1</sup>, available phosphorus (P) (Olsen-P) 7.35 mg kg<sup>-1</sup>, available potassium (K) 143.0 mg kg<sup>-1</sup>, and organic matter 8.55 g kg<sup>-1</sup>. Two treatments were established: (1) non-mycorrhizal treatment (NM), in which 100 g sterilized inoculum of an AM fungus Rhizophagus irregularis (BGC-HEB07D) was added in pots, and (2) AM inoculation treatment (AMI), in which pots were inoculated with the 100 g AM inoculum. To equalize starting microbial communities with the inoculated treatment, pots in the NM treatment were treated with 50 ml suspensions of 30 g of unsterilized AM inoculum filtrated through filter membrane (pore diameter  $10 \,\mu$ m). There were three replicates (one pot for each replicate) for each treatment. Twenty-five pre-germinated wheat seeds were planted into each pot, and plants were thinned to 15 plants for each pot 5 days after seeding. Wheat plants were cultured outdoors and watered when necessary.

#### Harvest

Wheat plants were harvested 6 weeks after seeding when roots could be fully colonized by AM fungi (Castillo et al. 2012). Shoots were cut, oven-dried at 75 °C for 72 h, and then weighed. Dry shoots were ground into a powder using a ball miller (MM400, RETSCH, Germany). Shoot powder was digested with  $H_2SO_4$ – $H_2O_2$  for total nitrogen (N), P, and K

concentration measurements. N and P concentrations were measured using naphthol blue colorimetric and phosphoric acid molybdenum antimony colorimetric methods, respectively, with a high-resolution digital colorimeter AutoAnalyzer 3 (AA3, SEAL Company, Germany); K concentration was measured using a flame spectrophotometer (Flame Photometer 410, Sherwood Company, England) (Bao [2008](#page-10-0)). Roots were collected from soils and washed with distilled water. All the roots collected from each pot were cut into small segments, mixed evenly and divided into two parts: one part was stored in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction, and the other part was stored at − 20 °C for quantifying AM colonization.

#### AM colonization measurement

About 0.5-g root segments were cleared in 10% (w/v) KOH at 90 °C in a water bath for 60 min. After cooling, the root samples were washed and stained with 0.05% (w/v) Trypan blue in lactoglycerol (1:1:1 lactic acid, glycerol and water) (Trouvelot et al. [1986\)](#page-12-0). Thirty root segments were mounted on slides in a polyvinyl alcohol–lactic acid–glycerol solution (Koske and Tessier [1983\)](#page-11-0) and examined at × 100–400 magnification under a Nikon YS100 microscope. The percentage of root colonization by AM fungi was calculated according to the method of Trouvelot et al. ([1986](#page-12-0)).

#### RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from approximately 100 mg of wheat root of three biological replicates of the two treatments using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration and quality of the total RNA were estimated using a NanoDrop Spectrometer (Thermo; USA) and verified using agarose gel electrophoresis and an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA). RNA samples were sent to Sagene Biotech. Co. Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) for sequencing. Six independent complementary DNA (cDNA) libraries corresponding to the three biological replicates for each treatment were constructed. cDNA library quality and fragment length were verified using an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer and real-time PCR. Fragment length distributions were within the expected ranges according to the CLC Sequence Viewer software, and the average read length was 150 bp. The resulting cDNA libraries were sequenced in a paired-end system (2 × 150 bp) with the Illumina HiSeq PE150 system. Samples were individually labeled for library construction and were simultaneously run in one lane.

#### Bioinformatics analyses

Sequences obtained for each of the six libraries were imported and individually processed using RSEM version 1.3.0 (Li and Dewey [2011\)](#page-11-0). Sequencing coverage was estimated according to the RSEM algorithm by dividing the total number of acquired sequence bases per condition by the number of reference bases. For quality filtering, a trimming step was performed on raw reads with the following conditions: (1) removing reads containing adapters; (2) removing reads containing more than 10% of unknown nucleotides; and (3) removing low-quality reads containing more than 50% of low-quality (Q-value ≤ 20) bases. High-quality reads were then mapped against the wheat reference transcriptome BEnsembl release 30 IWGSC1.0^ [\(http://archive.plants.ensembl.org/Triticum\\_](http://archive.plants.ensembl.org/Triticum_aestivum/Info/Index) [aestivum/Info/Index\)](http://archive.plants.ensembl.org/Triticum_aestivum/Info/Index) using the following mapping parameters: maximum read mismatch, 2; distance between mate-pair reads, 50 bp; and error of distance between matepair reads, ± 80 bp. To pick out RNA sequences belonging to R. irregularis, reads were also mapped against the genome of R. irregularis [\(https://genome.jgi.doe.gov/Rhiir2\\_1/Rhiir2\\_1.](https://genome.jgi.doe.gov/Rhiir2_1/Rhiir2_1.home.html) [home.html\)](https://genome.jgi.doe.gov/Rhiir2_1/Rhiir2_1.home.html). The reconstruction of transcripts was carried out with software Cufflinks, which, together with TopHat2, allows biologists to identify new genes (Trapnell et al. [2012](#page-12-0)). All of the reconstructed transcripts were aligned to the reference genome and were divided into 12 categories by using Cuffcompare. Gene abundances were quantified by the software RSEM, and gene expression levels were normalized by using the FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) method (Li and Dewey [2011](#page-11-0)). To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across treatments, the edgeR package [\(http://www.r-project.org/](http://www.r-project.org)) was used. We identified genes with a fold change ≥ 2 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 in a comparison as significant DEGs. To interpret the results, the DEGs were arranged into two classes: Bup-regulated DEG^, genes that were up-regulated in the AMI treatment in comparison to the expression in the NM treatment, and Bdown-regulated DEG^, genes that were downregulated in the AMI treatment in comparison to the expression in the NM treatment. A principal component analysis was performed on the DEGs of the three replicates from each condition using the R package gmodels [\(http://www.r-project.](http://www.r-project.org) [org/](http://www.r-project.org)). This analysis indicated that biological replicates from each condition grouped together and segregated from the other condition (data not shown).

# GO (Gene Ontology) and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) enrichment analysis

All DEGs were mapped to GO terms according to the GO database ([http://www.geneontology.org/](http://www.geneontology.org)), gene numbers were calculated for every term, and significantly enriched GO terms in DEGs compared to the genome background were defined by the hypergeometric test using Blast2GO software (Yu et al. [2012](#page-12-0)). A KEGG (a database resource for understanding high-level functions and utilities of the biological system) pathway (<http://www.genome.jp/kegg/>) enrichment analysis was conducted to identify significantly enriched metabolic or signal transduction pathways in DEGs compared with the whole genome background.

#### Validation by quantitative RT-PCR

Ten genes were selected to validate expression results from the RNA-seq experiment by quantitative RT-PCR (qPCR). The qPCR assays were carried out on the same RNA used for the RNA-seq procedure. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA with the GoScript Reverse Transcription System (Promega, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative RT-PCR reactions were performed in triplicate for each of the three biological replicates using an ABI StepOnePlus real-time PCR system (ABI, USA). PCR reactions were run in a total reaction volume of 20 μL, which included 10 μL of 2× qPCR Master Mix (Promega, USA), 0.5 μL of each primer (10 μM), and 1.0 μL of cDNA template. The PCR program included a preheating step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. Dissociation curves were performed at the end of each run. The glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene was used for normalization. The primers of all the genes were designed using Primer Premier Version 5.0 (Palo Alto, USA) (Table S1). The comparative threshold cycle method 2−ΔΔCt was used to analyze the relative gene expression (Schmittgen and Livak [2008](#page-11-0)).

#### Statistical analyses

A Shapiro-Wilk normality test and Bartlett's test were applied to check the normality and homoscedasticity of the data, respectively, using SAS 9.1 software (SAS Institute [1999\)](#page-11-0). The influence of AM inoculation on shoot dry weight, shoot N, P, and K content of wheat was analyzed by t test with SAS 9.1 software. Shoot N and P content data were log transformed to fit normality before t test analysis.

# Results

#### AM colonization and plant growth

Wheat roots in the AMI treatment were all colonized by AM fungi (Fig. S1), and roots in the NM treatment were not colonized by AM fungi. Shoot dry weight, shoot total N, P, and K contents in the AMI treatment were significantly lower than those in the NM treatment (Table S2).

## Number of DEGs

After trimming and applying quality filters, high-quality reads ranged from 42,503,286 to 49,846,770 reads for individually sequenced libraries, and more than 99% of the raw data were high-quality reads for most of the libraries (Table S3). More than 2/3 of the reads were each mapped to a single, specific locus of the reference transcriptome and were used for subsequent analysis, and a very low ratio of reads was multiplemapped paired reads. The wheat reference transcriptome that was used includes 114,264 genes, including both experimentally and theoretically described genes. In the present study, we found 87,794 and 89,736 genes expressed in the roots of wheat in the NM and AMI treatments, respectively.

A total number of 11,746 DEGs, including 7603 upregulated DEGs and 4143 down-regulated DEGs, were found in the roots of wheat in the AMI treatment (Supplement files 1–3). The up- or down-regulation of the genes by the AMI treatment was validated via qPCR on the ten selected genes (Table S4). Among the up-regulated DEGs, 1106 genes had a Bgene count^ value of zero in the NM treatment and were considered as AM-specific induced genes (Supplement file 2); among the down-regulated DEGs, the expression of 108 genes were totally silenced by AM colonization (Supplement file 3). The log2FC of the other 10,532 DEGs ranged from − 9.3 to 12.9. Most (75.6%) of the expression levels of DEGs were changed 2- to 8-fold (1 < |Log2 FC| < 3) by AM colonization, and as many as 577 DEGs changed more than 32-fold (5 < |Log2 FC|) compared to those in the roots in the NM treatment. The expression levels of 86 and 1 DEGs were greatly (> 1000-fold) up-regulated and down-regulated by the colonization of AM fungi, respectively (Fig. S2).

The DEGs covered all the seven homoeologous chromosome groups of the wheat genome, and mainly located on 2B, 3B, 2A, 2D, and 5B chromosomes (Fig. [1\)](#page-5-0). In each chromosome, 53.8–72.2% DEGs were up-regulated DEGs (Fig. [1](#page-5-0)(a)). A total number of 3014 (including 1919 up-regulated DEGs), 3463 (including 2411 up-regulated DEGs), and 3127 (including 1986 up-regulated DEGs) DEGs located on the A, B, and D subgenomes of wheat, respectively. The homoeologous group 2 had the highest number of DEGs (Fig. [1\)](#page-5-0). The AMspecific induced DEGs also covered all the seven chromosome groups, but the AM-specific down-regulated DEGs were missing from the 1B chromosome. The 2B and 3B chromosomes had the highest number of the AM-specific induced DEGs (Fig. [1\(](#page-5-0)e)).

#### GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of the DEGs

The DEGs were primarily enriched into 11, 13, and 6 GO terms from the aspects of molecular function, biological process, and cell component, respectively, although thousands of DEGs cannot be enriched into a known GO term (Table [1\)](#page-7-0). Most of the DEGs located on membranes and functioned in nucleotide binding and transferase activity during cellular protein modification and biosynthetic processes (Table [1\)](#page-7-0). The GO terms Bhydrolase activity^ and Bcellular protein modification process^ included the highest number of the AMspecific induced DEGs from the aspects of molecular function and biological process, respectively. The AM-specific downregulated DEGs mainly belonged to Bnucleotide binding,^ Bhydrolase activity,^ BN compound metabolic process,^ and Bnucleobase-containing compound metabolic process.^ Some AM-specific regulated DEGs were also enriched into several non-dominant GO terms such as Bcarbon-oxygen lyase activity,^ Bcation binding,^ and Bmulti-organism process^ (Table [1\)](#page-7-0). From the aspect of molecular function, the GO term Bnucleotide binding^ included the highest number of DEGs on most of the chromosomes, and Btransferase activity^ included the highest number of DEGs on the 4D, 5B, 5D, 6A, 6D chromosomes, and the whole homoeologous group 2 (Fig. [1\(](#page-5-0)b)). BHydrolase activity^ was also a dominant GO term on most of the chromosomes, and Bprotein binding^ was dominant GO term on the 1D, 4A, 4D chromosomes, and the whole homoeologous group 6 (Fig. [1](#page-5-0)(b)). From the aspect of biological process, the GO term Bcellular protein modification process^ included the highest number of DEGs on ten chromosomes, and Bbiosynthetic process^ included the highest number of DEGs on the 3B chromosome and the whole homoeologous groups 1 and 4. The GO term Btransport^ included the highest number of DEGs on the 3A, 6D, 7A, and 7B chromosomes (Fig. [1\(](#page-5-0)c)).

The KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that DEGs were mainly involved in Bphenylpropanoid biosynthesis,^ Bplantpathogen interaction,^ and Bbiosynthesis of amino acids,^ and the Bphenylpropanoid biosynthesis^ pathway had the highest number of DEGs (282 DEGs including 210 up-regulated DEGs) (Fig. [2\)](#page-8-0). The pathway Bphenylpropanoid biosynthesis^ included the highest number of DEGs on 12 chromosomes, and Bplant-pathogen interaction^ included the highest number of DEGs on the 5D, 6A, 6B, and the whole homoeologous group 7. BBiosynthesis of amino acids^ included the highest number of DEGs on the 4A, 4D, and 5A chromosomes. BProtein processing in endoplasmic reticulum^ included the highest number of DEGs on the 4B chromosome, and both of Bterpenoid backbone biosynthesis^ and Bcutin, suberine, and wax biosynthesis^ included the highest number of DEGs on the 6D chromosome (Fig. [1\(](#page-5-0)d)).

# The responses of the expression of transcription factor (TF) genes in wheat roots to AM colonization

The expression of TF genes belonging to 14 families in the roots of wheat was found regulated by AM colonization. Among the 14 families, WRKY and NAC had the highest number of up- and down-regulated DEGs, respectively. Most of the DEGs in the families WRKY, bHLH, MYB, AP2-EREBP, and PHD were up-regulated DEGs, but more down-regulated DEGs than up-regulated DEGs were found in the families NAC, MYB-related, MADS, and Trihelix. All the 30 DEGs belonging to GRAS and 2 DEGs belonging to E2F-DP were up-regulated DEGs, but all the DEGs belonging to bZIP, HB, and GRF families were down-regulated DEGs (Fig. [3\)](#page-8-0). Seven genes belonging to WRKY, nine belonging to GRAS, four belonging to Bhlh, three belonging to MYB, one belonging to NAC, and two belonging to AP2-EREBP only were expressed in the roots of wheat colonized by AM fungi.

# DEGs involved in key processes during AM symbiosis development

A total number of 656 genes that may be involved in key processes during AM symbiosis reported previously were found, and the expression of 507 genes was up-regulated by the colonization of R. irregularis (Table [2](#page-9-0)). The 656 DEGs were evenly distributed on the A (197), B (199), and D (183) genomes, and the 2B and 3B chromosomes had the highest number of DEGs (Fig. [1\(](#page-5-0)e)). DEGs involved in the colonization process mainly located on the 2B, 4B, and 2A chromosomes, and DEGs involved in carbon exchange during AM symbiosis were distributed evenly on most of the chromosomes. DEGs involved in inorganic nutrient transport mainly located on the 3B, 4B, 7D, and 2D chromosomes (Fig. [1\(](#page-5-0)e)).

The expression of three genes involved in flavonoids biosynthesis was specifically induced by AM colonization. All the 13 genes involved in strigolactones biosynthesis were upregulated DEGs. Ten genes belonging to LysM-domain gene family were up-regulated DEGs, and most of the genes belonging to CSSP components were also up-regulated DEGs. More than 200 genes that functioned during epidermal cell colonization and arbuscule formation were up-regulated DEGs, although there were also 55 such genes belonging to down-regulated DEGs. As many as 27 serine carboxypeptidase genes and 24 germin-like protein genes were only expressed in roots colonized by AM fungi. The expression of 123 and 64 genes involved in carbon exchange was upand down-regulated by AM colonization, respectively. Eleven ABC transporter genes were only expressed in AM roots, and all the 12 genes that encoded pyruvate kinase were up-regulated DEGs. Genes involved in N, P, K, boron (B), iron (Fe), sulfur (S), zinc (Zn), magnesium (Mg), and copper (Cu) were found up-regulated by AM colonization, and all the AMT, iron-phytosiderophore transporter, B, Zn, and Mg transporter genes were up-regulated DEGs. The downregulation by AM colonization of K transporter, NRT, sulfate, Pi, and Cu transporter genes was also found. An AMT gene, a K transporter gene, and 3 NRT genes were found only expressed in roots colonized by AM fungi (Table [2](#page-9-0)).

# Discussion

Negative growth responses of wheat plants to colonization by R. irregularis in shoot dry weight and N/P/K content were found in the present study (Table S2). Similar results were also found in some previous studies for wheat colonized by R. irregularis(Graham and Abbott [2000](#page-10-0); Li et al. [2005,](#page-11-0) [2006\)](#page-11-0). The growth responses of wheat to AM colonization can range from positive to neutral to negative depending on many factors such as the combinations of wheat verities and AM species (Duan et al. [2015\)](#page-10-0), soil fertility conditions (Li et al. [2006\)](#page-11-0), and plant density (Schroeder-Moreno and Janos [2008\)](#page-11-0). In our study, the plant density (477 plants m−<sup>2</sup> , similar to the plant density under the field conditions in north China (Li et al. [2016\)](#page-11-0)) may lead to increases in the overlap of root and AM hyphal P depletion zones in the growth medium, thereby diminishing the benefit derived from AM fungi. In addition, the low nutrient level in the growth medium we used may result in competition between plants and the AM fungus.

The expression of 11,746 genes in wheat roots was regulated by AM colonization, and this number is much higher than in some previous studies using microarrays or oligoarrays (Fiorilli et al. [2009;](#page-10-0) Guimil et al. [2005\)](#page-11-0). Handa et al. Information about the positions of the DEGs on chromosomes was missing from many previous transcriptomic studies of AM symbiosis, which may be due to the simple structure of the genomes of diploid (for most of the model plants) plants. Wheat has a complex genome derived by the hybridization of three subgenomes (A, B, and D) (Shi and Ling [2018\)](#page-11-0), and thus it is important to know the distribution of DEGs on chromosomes in detail. A similar number of DEGs were distributed on the A, B, and D genomes, and the 2B, 3B, and 2A chromosomes had the highest number of DEGs (Fig. [1\)](#page-5-0). This is consistent with the size of the chromosomes (Kersey et al. [2018](#page-11-0)); chromosomes which have a high number of encoded genes usually also have many DEGs. A recent study found 30 significant markers associated with root colonization, represented by six quantitative trait loci (QTLs) on chromosomes 3A, 4A, and 7A in the wheat genome via a genome-wide association study (GWAS) (Lehnert et al. [2017\)](#page-11-0). Nevertheless, only 94 wheat genotypes were used for the GWAS study and different conclusions may have arisen if additional wheat genotypes had been used because sample size can affect the outcome of GWAS (Klein [2007](#page-11-0)). A previous study using chromosome substitution lines which permit the examination of each individual chromosome and its influence on plant characteristics found that the 3B, 6B, and 7B chromosomes may play the most important roles in determining AM colonization (Hetrick et al. [1995](#page-11-0)). We found that DEGs involved in the colonization process mainly located on the 2B, 4B, and 2A chromosomes (Fig. [1\(](#page-5-0)e)). However, our results are not necessarily in conflict with the two previous studies because it is still unknown which genes are most important in determining AM colonization extent among the multiple genes that may be involved in AM colonization, and key genes that determine AM colonization extent are not necessarily located on the chromosomes that contain the highest number of DEGs.

According to the GO enrichment analysis, most of the DEGs were located on bio-membranes and functioned in nucleotide binding and transferase activity during cellular protein modification and biosynthetic processes (Table [1](#page-7-0)), which is similar to the dominant GO terms reported previously in M. truncatula, L. japonicus, and tomato colonized by AM fungi (Hohnjec et al. [2005;](#page-11-0) Handa et al. [2015;](#page-11-0) Fiorilli et al. [2009\)](#page-10-0). Thus, most of the DEGs found in our study were associated with AM colonization instead of nutrient deficiency stress for the AMI treatment (Table S2). In addition, gene expression that responded to N-starvation in wheat roots mainly belong to the GO terms Bcellular N compound metabolism,^ Bspermine biosynthesis,^ and Bcinnamic acid^ (Curci et al. [2017\)](#page-10-0) which are not dominant GO terms in our study. The expression of genes responded to Pi-starvation in wheat roots primarily belong to the GO terms Boxidationreduction^ and Bprotein phosphorylation^ (Oono et al. [2013\)](#page-11-0), and are also not dominant in our study.

[2016\)](#page-10-0), willow (Salix purpurea L.) (Aliferis et al. [2015](#page-10-0)) and tomato (Vos et al. [2013\)](#page-12-0). DEGs involved in N, P, K, Mg, S, B, Zn, Fe, and Cu transport were found, and all the ammonium, B, Fe, Zn, and Mg transporter genes were up-regulated by AM colonization (Table 2). Positive responses of wheat to AM colonization for some of these nutrients uptake have been reported (Pellegrino et al. [2015](#page-11-0)), but our study suggests that the expression of

<span id="page-10-0"></span>nutrient transporter genes can also be up-regulated even in the case of a negative response of wheat to AM colonization. This is consistent with the finding in Tian et al. (2017) that the expression of nutrient transporter genes was independent of nutrient transfer during AM symbiosis, and that the expression may have been regulated during the signal communication process between the host plants and AM fungi (Czaja et al. 2012). Interestingly, a K transporter, an AMT, and 3 NRT genes were found specifically induced by the AMI treatment (Table 2). The expression of K transporters could be regulated by AM colonization (Estrada et al. 2013); however, AM-specific induced K transporters located at the peri-arbuscular membrane (a hostderived membrane surrounding the symbiont arbuscule during symbiosis) have not been reported previously. AM-inducible AMT genes, specifically expressed in arbusculated cortical cells, have been cloned in several plant species including soybean (Glycine max), sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), and L. japonicus (Guether et al. 2009; Kobae et al. 2010; Koegel et al. 2013). However, no such NRT genes were reported previously in wheat. Although most N transferred by AM hyphae has been shown to be absorbed by the host plant as  $NH_4^+$ (Bücking and Kafle 2015; Hodge et al. 2010), our data support the hypothesis that NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> is also transferred by the AM-induced NRT at the peri-arbuscular membrane. Considering wheat plants in the AMI treatment may suffer from stress to a greater extent than in the NM treatment due to competition for nutrients between plants and the AM fungus, some up-regulated DEGs involved in nutrient uptake (especially NRT and Pi transporter genes), may also be caused by nutrient stress (Lejay et al. 1999; Miao et al. 2009). However, we conclude that the specific induced DEGs were caused by AM colonization because it is unlikely that these transporter genes would not have been expressed at all in wheat roots in the NM treatment which also was under nutrient deficiency conditions.
