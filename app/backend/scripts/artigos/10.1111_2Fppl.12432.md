Cotton is an economically important crop that provides both natural fiber and by-products such as oil and protein. Its global gene expression could provide insight into the biological processes underlying growth and development, which involve suites of genes expressed with temporal and spatial control by regulatory networks. Generally, the goal for cotton breeding is improvement of the fiber; thus, most previous research has focused on identifying genes specific to the fiber. However, seeds may also play an important role in fiber development. In this study, we constructed and systematically analyzed 21 strand-specific RNA-Seq libraries for *Gossypium hirsutum*, covering different tissues, organs and development stages, from which approximately 970 million reads were generated to provide a global view of gene expression during cotton development. The organ (tissue)-specific gene expression patterns were investigated, providing further insight into the dynamic programming associated with developmental processes and a way to study the coordination of development between fiber cells and ovules. Series of transcription factors and seed-specific genes have been identified as candidate genes that could elucidate key mechanisms and regulatory networks in nutrient accumulation during ovule development and in fiber development. This study reports comprehensive transcriptome dynamics at various stages of cotton development and will serve as a valuable genome-wide transcriptome resource for initial gene discovery and functional characterization of genes in cotton.

### **Introduction**

Cotton is the world's most common cash crop and provides the majority of natural textile fiber as well as being a major source of oil and protein (Sunilkumar et al. 2006). Cotton fibers are single-celled epidermal trichomes that differentiate from the unidirectional expansion of surface cells of the epidermal layer of the seed coat. The development of cotton fiber consists of four distinctive but overlapping developmental stages: fiber initiation, elongation, secondary cell wall thickening and fiber maturation by dehydration.

Initiating fiber cells usually emerge before or on the day of anthesis (0 dpa, days post-anthesis). Notably, early fiber development (initiation and elongation) has an important influence on fiber yield and quality and is also a good research model for cellulose synthesis, cell differentiation and polar elongation (Haigler et al. 2012).

Numerous factors regulating fiber development have been identified, and the structures and functions of some of these factors have been verified. The R2R3 MYB genes *GhMYB25* and *GhMYB25*-like have been shown to play key roles in the very early stages of fiber cell differentiation (Machado et al. 2009, Walford et al. 2011). The cotton *GaMYB2* is also reported to play an important role during plant trichome development (Wang et al. 2004). Furthermore, the homeodomain-leucine zipper (HD-ZIP) transcription factor *GhHOX3* can promote fiber cell elongation by GA signal (Shan et al. 2014), and very long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) can promote fiber cell elongation by activating ethylene synthesis (Qin et al. 2007). *GhFLA1* was reported to function in fiber initiation and elongation by affecting arabinogalactan-protein (AGP) composition and the integrity of the primary cell wall matrix (Huang et al. 2013). *WLIM1a* not only binds the actin cytoskeleton to bundle actin filaments, thereby facilitating elongation of fiber cells, but also functions as a transcription factor to activate phenylpropanoid biosynthesis to build up the secondary cell wall (Han et al. 2013). However, research in recent years has been largely focused on identifying genes present only in fibers, ignoring overall seed development, which may play an important role in fiber development (Ruan 2013).

Identification of the coordinated gene expression between seed and fiber development was an obstacle to further study. To date, several methods have been developed for identifying key genes involved in fiber development and functional validation, and next-generation sequencing technology has greatly accelerated this process. However, the majority of these studies have focused on mutants with fiber defects (fibreless/lintless mutants; Kim et al. 2013, Wang et al. 2010c), individuals with significant differences within genetic populations (isogenic lines; Fang et al. 2014), and the comparison of expression patterns between wild and domesticated cotton (Chaudhary et al. 2008, Yoo and Wendel 2014). The published transcriptome profiling in cotton is focused on limited numbers of tissues and organs; to our knowledge, the global view of transcriptome dynamics during cotton development remains to be discovered, and this could efficiently and accurately reveal the complex changes in transcription level and regulatory networks. Establishment of a gene expression atlas could cover different tissues, organs and developmental stages, facilitating the identification of suites of genes expressed in the same temporal and spatial patterns to achieve the specification of morphology and physiology of the tissue or organ systems. Thus, through defining subsets of expressed genes, this strategy provides a way to explore the mechanism of coordinated development between the fiber cell and the seed (ovule) and deepens our understanding of cotton development.

Gene expression atlases of *Arabidopsis* (Schmid et al. 2005), *Oryza sativa* (Wang et al. 2010a), *Glycine max* (Severin et al. 2010), *Medicago truncatula* (Benedito et al. 2008) and *Lotus japonicus* (Verdier et al. 2013) have been developed to better understand the transcriptional programs involved in specific developmental processes. RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) has recently emerged as a high-specificity method for quantifying transcriptome using the next-generation sequencing technology, and it presents several advantages over the microarray approach (Malone and Oliver 2011). These advantages include little background noise, no *a priori* information, a high level of reproducibility, relatively little variation and quantification of gene expression over a wide range (Wang et al. 2009). The release of *Gossypium raimondii* (Paterson et al. 2012, Wang et al. 2012) and *Gossypium arboreum* (Li et al. 2014) genome sequences also expanded the use of RNA-Seq. Furthermore, a draft genome of cultivated upland cotton (*Gossypium hirsutum*) has recently been completed (Li et al. 2015, Zhang et al. 2015).

In this study, we have developed a cotton gene expression atlas that provides a global view of gene expression covering 21 representative organs at different developmental stages, with special emphasis on seed development. Using the deoxyuridine triphosphate (dUTP) method of library construction for strand-specific RNA-Seq made it possible to provide more accurate expression profiles for genes derived from natural antisense transcripts. This large dataset will serve as a foundation for understanding the precise regulatory mechanisms underlying fiber and seed development in *Gossypium*.

### **Materials and methods**

# **Plant materials**

*G. hirsutum* (Elite Chinese cotton cultivar CRI 49) was cultivated in the field at Institute of Cotton Research of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences using standard procedures. In total, 21 samples were collected, covering various organs and developmental stages: (1) the seed germination stage: cotyledon (Y1-1), radicle (Y1-2), hypocotyl (Y1-3); (2) the three-leaf stage: new leaf (Y2-1), old leaf (Y2-2), cotyledon (Y2-3) and stem (Y2-4); (3) the maturation/senescence stages: new leaves (Y3-1), old leaves (Y3-2) and boll-shell 10 dpa (Y7-1); (4) the flower development stage: corolla (Y4-1), bracts (Y4-2), calyx (Y4-3), androecium & gynoecium (A&G) (Y4-4); (5) the seed/ovule development stage: ovule at 0 dpa (Y5-1), 10 dpa (Y5-2), 20 dpa (Y5-3), 30 dpa (Y5-4) and 40 dpa (Y5-5); and (6) the fiber-development stage: 10 dpa (Y6-2) and 20 dpa (Y6-3). Each type of sample was obtained from at least three individual plants, and individual samples were pooled during sample preparation.

The above samples were collected separately and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen before storage at −80∘C until subsequent RNA extraction. Total RNA for each sample was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing) according to the manufacturer's recommendations, and they were then treated with RNase-free DNase I to remove DNA contamination. The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA were assessed using the Agilent Technologies 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, Calif, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. The RNA samples with RNA integrity values greater than eight were used for the subsequent construction of RNA-Seq libraries.

### **Library construction and sequencing**

Transcriptome libraries covering 21 different development stages were constructed using dUTP method (Parkhomchuk et al. 2009). In brief, polyA+RNA was purified by the mRNA purification Dynabeads and first strand cDNA was generated using oligo (dT) primer. Then, dNTPs were removed and second-strand synthesis was performed using the dUTP instead of deoxythymidine triphosphate (dTTP). The incorporation of dUTP in second strand synthesis is important to keep the strand-specificity. Before library amplification, uracil-*N*-glycosylase was used for uridine digestion, which allows selective degradation of the second cDNA strand. By this principle, the transcript direction and strand-specific information was reserved. Then, the 21 individual RNA-Seq libraries were sequenced separately on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform with 2 × 125 base paired-end reads.

### **Reads mapping and transcriptome assembly**

The genome sequences were downloaded from the Cotton Genome Project (CGP) (Li et al. 2014). Using the spliced reads aligner TOPHAT-2.0.11 (Trapnell et al. 2009), we mapped 125 nt reads from 21 RNA-Seq libraries to the cotton genome with the following parameters: anchor length more than 8 nt for splice junction reads; the mean inner distance between paired ends was 50 bp; segment mismatches no more than 2. After running TOPHAT, transcriptome assembly to obtain high-quality transcript sequences was performed with CUFFLINKS v2.2.1 (Trapnell et al. 2012) using following parameters: −F 0.2; -I 500000; --min-intron-length 50; --frag-len-mean 200; --frag-len-std-dev 100. Then, the above assemblies were merged together using the Cuffmerge utility from the CUFFLINKS package to generate a uniform assembly for new transcripts. Open reading frames were identified using TransDecoder with the default parameters (Grabherr et al. 2011). New transcripts were annotated using UniProt databases and an NCBI BLAST 2.2.27+ homology search with an E-value of 10<sup>−</sup>6. In order to identify novel transcripts, unaligned RNA-seq reads due to the incomplete genome were assembled by the latest release of TRINITY v2.1.1 using default parameters (minimum assembled contig length 200) to obtain novel transcripts (Grabherr et al. 2011).

### **Functional enrichment analysis**

The Gene Ontology (GO) terms were assigned to assemble the sequences based on the UniProt databases. *P <*0.05 (hypergeometric test with Benjamini & Hochberg false discovery rate correction) was used as the cutoff for GO enrichment analysis with BINGO 3.0.2 (Maere et al. 2005).

## **Gene expression analysis**

For more accurate calculations of the expression levels, only reads with unique locations were retained. The expression level of each gene was normalized as the fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM), analogous to single-read RPKM, which can remove bias due to sequencing depth and gene length (Mortazavi et al. 2008). We also mapped all the reads to assembled transcript sequences to compare the mapping results using Bowtie2 2.2.3 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012). To remove the negative effect of low expression noise, genes with FPKM *>*3 in at least one library were considered to be expressed. Hierarchical clustering was performed using the 'hclust' function in R package. Differentially expressed genes were identified by fold change and the statistical package DEGseq with the MA-plot-based method in R software, version 3.0.3 (Wang et al. 2010b). The DEGseq package was employed to calculate the *P* value. Fold change was calculated by FPKM. Then, the threshold for identifying differentially expressed genes was set as a fold change of *>*1.5 of the normalized (FPKM) expression values and *P <*0.01. Concentric circles diagram was drawn using Circos to show the gene density of differentially expressed genes (Krzywinski et al. 2009). The Short Time-series Expression Miner (STEM) clustering algorithm was used to calculate significant clusters of co-expressed genes (Ernst and Bar-Joseph 2006). The expression patterns of the clusters were drawn using ggplot2 in R.

## **Quantitative PCR analysis**

To validate the expression profiles of selected genes, quantitative reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed using the 7500 Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems) as previously described (Bustin et al. 2009). Briefly, RNA was isolated from plants using and purified using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing). The cDNA was synthesized using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Takara, Dalian, China). The resulting cDNA was quantified by SYBR® Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Dalian, China). Two independent biological replicates and three technical replicates of each biological replicates for each tissue sample were analyzed in real-time PCR analysis. GhHIS3 was used as a loading control. The primer sequences used for real-time PCR analysis in this study are provided in Table S1, Supporting Information. The correlation coefficient between qRT-PCR and RNA-Seq were identified using the 'cor' function in R software.

# **Results and discussion**

### **Transcriptome sequencing and assembly**

To develop a comprehensive, global gene expression atlas during development in *G. hirsutum*, we collected 21 representative samples, including cotyledon, root, stem, leaf, flower, fiber and seed tissues at various developmental stages, germination, three-leaf, flowering, fiber, seed development and plant maturation/senescence. By expanding the number of samples, not using only the fiber, we can identify the functions of more genes, especially tissue-specific genes. RNA-Seq libraries for different development stages were constructed and sequenced using the Illumina high-throughput sequencing platform. The important improvement in this study was to maintain the strand specificity during library construction using the dUTP method, which made it possible to provide more accurate expression profiles for genes with natural antisense transcripts. Each RNA-Seq library was sequenced at a depth of at least 5 G bases, which could cover most of the genes in each sample and detect genes at low expression levels (Table S2). In total, the 21 RNA-Seq libraries ultimately generated 974 343 674 reads of at least 125 nt in length among different stages, which was sufficient to maximize the transcript diversity (Table S2). All of the original sequence reads are available at NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, http://www.ncbi.nlm.gov.geo/) under the accession number GSE70369. Because we included several libraries that have not been reported in previous publications, many new transcripts had not been annotated. In this study, we used genome-guided methods to assemble our paired-end RNA-Seq reads to include new transcripts in this study. Briefly, RNA-Seq reads were first mapped to *Gossypium arboreum*. Then, reads that mapped to multiple positions in the reference genome were excluded. In total, 584 514 103 unique mapped reads were obtained for the subsequent analysis.

Finally, transcriptome assembly was performed using CUFFLINK to reconstruct the transcript sequences for each library using the unique mapping results. Then, the above assembled sequences were merged into a uniform assembly using the Cuffmerge utility, yielding 56 754 transcripts from 29 541 unigenes (Fig. 1A). The sequences for the transcriptome assembly, chromosome position and the functional annotation could be found in Table S3. At the same time the relationship between predicted gene models and existing gene models were deposited in Table S4. The average length of the assembled transcripts was 2004 nt, and more than 80% transcripts longer than 1000 nt were generated (Fig. 1A). Owing to the uncompleted genome, approximately 30% of the reads could not map to the present assembled genome sequence. In order to identify novel transcripts, Trinity were employed to assemble all these unaligned reads into 53 586 contig sequences. The average length of these assembled sequences was only 316 bp (Fig. S1). The short *de novo* assemble length suggested that many assemble sequences was not full length and more deep sequence was needed due to lacking of corresponding genome sequences. All the new transcript sequences for the transcriptome assembly could be found in Table S5. These assembled sequences represented novel transcripts and could be used to investigate the function for new transcripts in future.

### **Organ (tissue)-specific gene expression during different stages**

Understanding uncharacterized organ (tissue)-specific genes can help explain their functions and the underlying regulatory control of the tissue or organ. In particular, promoters of tissue-specific genes may be employed for targeted manipulations of transgenes at certain developmental stages, which may avoid the negative effects resulting from constitutive expression of certain genes (Verdier et al. 2013). Multiple representative tissues or organs for each stage were collected and profiled to obtain comprehensive insight into the development of cotton. Genes that were preferentially expressed in the germination stage, the three-leaf stage, the maturation/senescence stage and the flower development stage were identified (Fig. 3). For the germination stage, 672, 1457 and 737 genes were preferentially expressed in the cotyledon, radicle and hypocotyl, respectively (Table 1). The information for the FPKM and *P* value for all the differential expressed genes could be found in Table S8. During germination, many small molecular compounds, such as ammonium, phosphate, amino acids, peptides and sugars obtained through dissimilation are transported to the radicle and embryo; thus, transport-related genes were activated and upregulated, as reported for other crops (Yu et al. 2014). These genes may also be involved in the metabolism of starch, proteins and lipids, providing essential energy for growth. The three-leaf stage showed over-representation of genes involved in DNA metabolism, gene expression, RNA metabolism and RNA processing, reflecting the regulation of transcription and post-transcription processes during this stage. The preferential expression of genes associated with translation, protein transport and structural components of ribosomes reflects greater protein synthesis activity during the three-leaf stage. #### **Characterization of fiber development-related genes**

During fiber development, 10 and 20 dpa are considered to be key stages, respectively representing the fiber cell elongation and the transition from primary cell wall growth to secondary cell wall growth (Yoo and Wendel 2014). Therefore, we focused on comparisons of the gene expression at these two key fiber development stages (10 and 20 dpa) with other stages to find differentially expressed fiber-related genes. In total, 341 genes exhibited upregulated expression (Fig. 4A) and 749 exhibited downregulated expression during fiber development (Fig. 4B).

After investigating the enrichment biological process terms, we found that 'signal transduction', 'protein localization and transport', 'protein complex assembly', 'actin filament based process' and 'hydrogen and proton transport' were enriched among the 341 upregulated genes (Fig. 5A). Upregulated genes also showed enrichment of 'GTP metabolic process' and 'ATP hydrolysis coupled proton transport'. This is consistent with the high requirement of plasmodesmatal activity, phytohormone biosynthesis, transporter activities and microtubule organization. Enrichment analysis at the level of molecular function showed that 'structural molecule activity', 'cytoskeletal protein binding', 'pyrophosphatase activity', 'GTP binding and GTPase actively', and 'signal sequence binding' were enriched among the upregulated genes (Fig. 5B). However, the GO terms (biological processes) 'regulation of transcription DNA-dependent', 'regulation of RNA metabolic process', and 'negative regulation of transcription' were enriched among the 749 downregulated genes (Fig. 5C). At the molecular function level, the GO terms 'binding', 'nucleic acid binding', 'zinc ion binding' and 'sequence-specific DNA binding' were enriched among the downregulated genes (Fig. 5D).

Although previous studies have been performed to identify transcript factors (TFs) controlling fiber development, genome-wide TFs dominantly expressed at fiber development have not been investigated. Several transcription factors were upregulated only at 10 dpa. These included three bZIP TFs (Cotton\_A\_06019, Cotton\_A\_23165 and Cotton\_A\_20105) and four transcription factors of other types: GATA (Cotton\_A\_11893), AP2 (Cotton\_A\_17105), HSF-type (Cotton\_A\_16493) and homeobox-associated leucine zipper (Cotton\_A\_07685). Four TFs were upregulated only at 20 dpa: two SRF-type (Cotton\_A\_04560 and Cotton\_A\_03985), one WRKY (Cotton\_A\_14073) and one AP2 (Cotton\_A\_41232). Interestingly, only two TFs (Cotton\_A\_18441) and histone-like TFs (CBF/NF-Y, Cotton\_A\_03200) were upregulated at both 10 and 20 dpa, during fiber development. The bZIP TF (Cotton\_A\_18441) encodes a protein that is related to a functionally characterized gene, ABI5, which is involved in ABA signaling during seed maturation and germination (Chen et al. 2014). ABA may be involved in secondary wall thickening of cotton fiber (Yang et al. 2001). It will be interesting to investigate whether these TFs contribute to the regulation of fiber development.

The fiber is part of the seed, and its development is tightly controlled by seed development. To investigate the global dynamic patterns during ovule and fiber development, we performed gene expression cluster analysis using the STEM algorithms. STEM algorithm analyses of the expressed genes generated 12 significant cluster patterns (*P <*0.001) based on the similarity of expression profiles, with numbers of genes in each cluster ranging from 330 to 4489 (Fig. 7A). The largest gene expression cluster 1 contained 4489 genes. Compared with the high expression level of ovules at 0 dpa, other stages showed decreased expression (Fig. 7B). This cluster comprised 15% of the total genes and suggested the existence of complicated reprogramming of regulatory networks at the early stage (0 dpa) of seed development, probably associated with fiber initiation. Meanwhile, cluster 9 comprised 485 genes showing increased expression at the later stage of seed development (30 and 40 dpa), consistent with the accumulation of nutrients such as protein and oil (Fig. 7C). Enrichment analysis of the most highly expressed genes at the molecular-functional level showed nutrient reservoir activity for synthesis of seed storage proteins such as vicilin (Cotton\_A\_16507, Cotton\_A\_16506, Cotton\_A\_13315), legumin A (Cotton\_A\_03222) and legumin B (Cotton\_A\_40775) with seed maturation. Triacylglycerol (TAG) is the major form of lipid stored in seeds. The glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (GPAT, Cotton\_A\_25468) and phospholipid-diacylglycerol acyltransferase (PDAT, Cotton\_A\_09547, Cotton\_A\_20000) were identified as predominantly expressed during later stages of seed development, showing a dramatically rising trend. In many oilseed crops, diacylglycerol acyltransferases (DGATs), which catalyze the final step of the Kennedy pathway, have been suggested to be important enzymes in seed storage lipid accumulation (Saha et al. 2006). However, compared with the expression level of DGAT (Cotton\_A\_10913, Cotton\_A\_10911), we speculated that PDAT activities may contribute more strongly to controlling the relative flux of acyl chains onto the sn-3 position of TAG in cottonseed. In the allopolyploid cotton, orthologous genes, such as those participating in fatty acid synthesis, are widely distributed in the genome. How to identify the functional differentiation of genes within such families is a core issue for biological research during the post-genomic era. The gene expression atlas developed in this study provides a way to address the problem of gene redundancy.

Notably, 911 genes in cluster 5 showed higher values at 10 dpa for both ovule development and fiber development compared with other stages (Fig. 7D), consistent with the fiber elongation stage, implying that these genes in ovules may also participate in fiber development. The very long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) have been demonstrated to promote fiber cell elongation by activating ethylene synthesis (Qin et al. 2007). The 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthases (KCS, Cotton\_A\_27018, Cotton\_A\_19287) encoding the first and rate-limiting step in VLCFA biosynthesis, are preferentially expressed in the ovule, with even higher expression in fiber. Enhancement of cellular communication and nutrient exchange between fibers and ovules is critical for fiber elongation (Zhu et al. 2003). The ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter *GhWBC1* (Cotton\_A\_36970) was also identified at a high level (Zhu et al. 2003), and we also found another eight ABC transporter genes (Cotton\_A\_23097, Cotton\_A\_02748, etc.) showing similar expression patterns during seed development, although these genes show differences in expression at other developmental stages. It will be interesting to systematically further identify the functions of these genes in the future. Moreover, these co-expressed genes included several putative transcription factors belonging to the GATA (Cotton\_A\_15023 and Cotton\_A\_11893), bZIP (Cotton\_A\_20105 and Cotton\_A\_06019), TCP (Cotton\_A\_27060), HSF-type (Cotton\_A\_16493), PLATZ (Cotton\_A\_00861), HD-ZIP (Cotton\_A\_07685) and AP2 domain (Cotton\_A\_11454) families.

The 388 identified genes in cluster 11 showed higher expression at 20 dpa during both ovule and fiber development than at other time points (Fig. 7E), consistent with the secondary wall biosynthesis. GO enrichment analysis of these genes indicated that 43 (21% of annotated genes) were membrane related, such as transmembrane transport for biological process or integral component of membrane for cellular component, suggesting a mechanism to promote the exchange of signaling molecules or nutrients between ovule and fiber. In addition, two WRKY TFs (Cotton\_A\_04044 and Cotton\_A\_25910) were identified. In summary, these candidate genes showing similar expression patterns during seed and fiber development might play important roles in the regulation of fiber yield and quality. Targeted regulation of these genes to enhance overall seed development, perhaps increasing seed number and size, may be an effective strategy for increasing fiber production (Ruan 2013).

Transcriptome profiling is essential for improving our understanding of development dynamics. We have generated an extensive expression atlas across a wide array of tissues and developmental stages of *G. hirsutum* using strand-specific RNA-Seq that provides more accurate information about gene structure and high-resolution gene expression. This dataset can also be used to identify promoters for use in transgenic studies for various purposes, such as high and constitutive expression, tissue/organ-specific expression at various levels and/or any defined spatiotemporal expression patterns. The construction of dynamic transcriptome libraries will serve as an essential transcriptome resource to facilitate the development analyses in *Gossypium* and accelerate cotton breeding programs.
