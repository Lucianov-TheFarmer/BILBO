## SUMMARY

RNA-based processes play key roles in the regulation of eukaryotic gene expression. This includes both the processing of pre-mRNAs into mature mRNAs ready for translation and RNAbased silencing processes, such as RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM). Polyadenylation of pre-mRNAs is one important step in their processing and is carried out by three functionally specialized canonical nuclear poly(A) polymerases in Arabidopsis thaliana. Null-mutations in one of these, termed *PAPS1*, result in a male gametophytic defect. Using a fluorescence-labelling strategy, we have characterized this defect in more detail using RNA and small-RNA sequencing. In addition to global defects in expression of pollen-differentiation genes, paps1 null mutant pollen show a strong overaccumulation of transposable-element transcripts, yet a depletion of 21- and particularly 24-nucleotide long short interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) targeting the corresponding transposable elements. Double-mutant analyses support a specific functional interaction between PAPS1 and components of the RdDM pathway, as evident from strong synergistic phenotypes in mutant combinations involving *paps1*, but not paps2 paps4 mutations. In particular, the double mutant of paps1 and rna-dependent rna *polymerase* 6 (*rdr*6) shows a synergistic developmental phenotype disrupting the formation of the transmitting tract in the female gynoecium. Thus, our findings in A. thaliana uncover a potentially general link between canonical poly(A) polymerases as components of mRNA processing and RdDM, reflecting an analogous interaction in fission yeast.

## INTRODUCTION

The addition of a poly(A) tail to the 3' end of pre-mRNAs represents an essential step in eukaryotic gene expression (Hunt 2008, Millevoi and Vagner 2010, Eckmann *et al.* 2011). The poly(A) tail stabilizes the mRNA, promotes its export from the nucleus and increases the efficiency of translation. The poly(A) tail is added after transcription by RNA polymerase II, via signal sequences within the 3' UTR of the pre-mRNA that are bound by two multi-protein complexes, cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor (CPSF) and cleavage stimulation factor (CstF), resulting in cleavage of the pre-mRNA (Hunt 2008, Millevoi and Vagner 2010).

CPSF and CstF also recruit poly(A) polymerase (PAPS), which catalyzes the synthesis of the poly(A) tail in concert with poly(A) binding protein (PABN in plants).

The bulk of pre-mRNA polyadenylation is performed by canonical nuclear PAPS belonging to the DNA polymerase β-like nucleotidyltransferase superfamily (Hunt 2008, Millevoi and Vagner 2010). While yeast and Drosophila melanogaster only contain a single, essential PAPS gene, mammalian and plant genomes encode more than one canonical nuclear PAPS isoforms. Both nuclear isoforms in humans, PAPOLA (PAP $\alpha$ ) and PAPOLG (PAP $\gamma$ ), are coexpressed throughout the body (Martin and Keller 2007). The Arabidopsis thaliana genome encodes three canonical nuclear PAPS isoforms: PAPS1, PAPS2 and PAPS4 are expressed throughout plant development with rather similar expression levels between different organs and growth stages (Hunt et al. 2008, Meeks et al. 2009). The three proteins share a highly similar N-terminal catalytic domain, whereas the C-terminal domain is diverged between PAPS1 compared to PAPS2 and PAPS4 (Addepalli et al. 2004). All three proteins possess non-specific polyadenylation activity in vitro (Hunt et al. 2000, Addepalli et al. 2004). The presence of more than one canonical nuclear PAPS isoform in plants and mammals raises the question of whether these isoforms fulfill specialized functions and if so. whether the organisms use the regulatory potential afforded by specialized isoforms to modulate gene expression.

Evidence for such functional specialization amongst the canonical nuclear PAPS isoforms has emerged in A. thaliana (Vi et al. 2013, Kappel et al. 2015, Czesnick and Lenhard 2016). Mutations in PAPS1 result in opposite effects on leaf and flower growth, with moderately reduced PAPS1 function leading to smaller leaves, yet larger flowers; the smaller leaves result from both a reduced SMALL AUXIN UP RNA (SAUR) activity and a constitutive immune response dependent on signaling by the two lipase-like proteins ENHANCED DISEASE SUSCEPTIBILITY1 (EDS1) and PHYTOALEXIN DEFICIENT4 (PAD4) (Trost et al. 2013, Vi, et al. 2013). A further reduction in PAPS1 function causes more severe growth inhibition in seedlings, and a complete loss of PAPS1 function results in a fully penetrant male gametophytic defect (Vi, et al. 2013). Thus, PAPS1 is an essential gene whose full activity is required for normal growth of shoot lateral organs. By contrast, even the combined full knock-out of PAPS2 and PAPS4 does not interfere with plant viability, and the only phenotype detected in paps2 paps4 double mutants is a moderate delay in flowering due to upregulation of the major flowering repressor FLOWERING LOCUS C (Czesnick and Lenhard 2016). These strikingly different phenotypes are not simply due to PAPS1 acting as the major poly(A) polymerase in A. thaliana; even when PAPS1 activity is severely compromised, the distribution of bulk poly(A)-tail lengths is indistinguishable from that in the wild-type, i.e. PAPS2 and PAPS4 can normally polyadenylate most pre-mRNAs in this

situation (Vi, *et al.* 2013). Rather, promoter- and domain-swap experiments indicate that the different activities of the PAPS protein isoforms reside in their divergent C-terminal domains (Vi, *et al.* 2013). Thus, these observations suggest that while most pre-mRNAs can be equally well polyadenylated by either PAPS1 or PAPS2/PAPS4, there are some critical transcripts whose polyadenylation depends primarily on PAPS1 due to its divergent C-terminal domain.

A set of such PAPS1-dependent mRNAs has been identified based on their altered poly(A)tail lengths in *paps1* mutants, including *SAUR* transcripts (see above), mRNAs encoding for ribosomal proteins and for redox regulators (Vi, *et al.* 2013, Kappel, *et al.* 2015). The biological relevance of these poly(A)-tail changes was underlined by the reduced ribosome content in *paps1-3* null mutant pollen grains and by an altered redox state and oxidativestress response in *paps1-1* mutant seedlings. Also, the mRNAs with an altered poly(A)-tail length in *paps1* overlap strongly with a cluster of genes that show tight co-expression with *PAPS1* across thousands of microarrays (Kappel, *et al.* 2015), suggesting that modulation of *PAPS1* activity may indeed be employed by the plant to alter gene expression patterns.

A key epigenetic process in plants is RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM), in which small RNA molecules trigger DNA and histone methylation at target loci and thus result in their heterochromatinization. In the sporophyte, many transposable elements (TEs) are targeted by the so-called canonical RdDM pathway (Zhang and Zhu 2011, Bond and Baulcombe 2013). In the upstream phase of canonical RdDM, the plant-specific RNA polymerase IV (PolIV) transcribes regions of the genome marked by lysine 9 methylation on histone H3, producing precursor transcripts that are converted into double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) by RNA-DEPENDENT RNA POLYMERASE2 (RDR2) (Haag et al. 2012). These dsRNAs are cleaved by DICER-LIKE3 (DCL3) into 24-nucleotide (24-nt) small-interfering RNAs (siRNAs), which bind to ARGONAUTE4 (AGO4) or AGO6 proteins (Xie et al. 2004, Havecker et al. 2010). SiRNA-charged AGO4/6 is recruited to nascent transcripts produced by RNA Polymerase V (PolV), presumably via base-pairing of the siRNA with the PolV-transcript (Wierzbicki et al. 2008, Wierzbicki et al. 2009), and AGO4/6 in turn recruits the DOMAINS REARRANGED METHYLTRANSFERASE1 (DRM1) and DRM2 enzymes that methylate cytosines in the genomic DNA of the targeted locus in all sequence contexts (CG, CHG, CHH) (Law and Jacobsen 2010, Matzke and Mosher 2014). At many deeply-silenced transposon loci transcriptional repression can be maintained even in the absence of canonical RdDM pathway function (Law and Jacobsen 2010, Panda et al. 2016). This depends on the action of the maintenance methylases MET1 and CMT3 that target cytosines in the symmetrical CG- and CHG-contexts, when these are hemimethylated after DNA replication (Stroud et al. 2013). Access of these methyltransferases to TEs in

heterochromatic regions depends on the SWI/SNF-chromatin remodeling factor DEFECTIVE IN DNA METHYLATION1 (DDM1), and many TEs are transcriptionally reactivated in *ddm1* mutants (Hirochika *et al.* 2000, Zemach *et al.* 2013).

In addition, several non-canonical RdDM pathways have been described over recent years (Cuerda-Gil and Slotkin 2016). In one of these, mRNAs generated by RNA-polymerase II (PollI) can be converted into dsRNAs by RNA-DEPENDENT RNA POLYMERASE6 (RDR6) and cleaved into 21-nt and 22-nt siRNAs by DCL4 and DCL2, respectively, following the classical post-transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS) mechanism (Nuthikattu et al. 2013). The 21-nt siRNAs can then initiate the *de novo* methylation of the loci giving rise to these transcripts, relying on PolV-transcripts for targeting the DNA-methylation machinery to the corresponding loci (Nuthikattu et al. 2013, Panda and Slotkin 2013). The activity of this pathway is important for example when TEs become de-repressed in *ddm1* mutants or when novel transposons are inserted into a genome (Nuthikattu, et al. 2013, Panda and Slotkin 2013). RDR6-mediated RdDM thus provides an independent entry into transposon silencing. RDR6 is also required for the formation of secondary siRNAs during the amplification stage of PTGS, for the production of transacting siRNAs (tasiRNAs) and for efficient defense against RNA viruses (Voinnet 2008). In the former two cases, RDR6 is recruited to target transcripts that are subject to an initial cleavage by primary siRNAs or microRNAs (miRNAs), with 22-nt miRNAs playing a particularly important role (Chen et al. 2010, Cuperus et al. 2010). However, whether all RDR6-recruitment occurs by this mechanism and in particular how PollI-transcripts of reactivated TEs and viral RNAs are selected for conversion to dsRNA by RDR6 is not well understood (Voinnet 2008, Panda and Slotkin 2013).

During pollen development, TEs that are transcriptionally silenced throughout the sporophyte become transcriptionally active in the pollen vegetative cell (Slotkin *et al.* 2009). This leads to the production of epigenetically activated siRNAs (easiRNAs) in a process that resembles the RDR6-dependent formation of secondary siRNAs after microRNA (miRNA)-mediated cleavage of these transposon transcripts (Creasey *et al.* 2014). In addition to miRNAs, specifically processed 19-nt tRNA-fragments accumulate to high levels in pollen grains and target additional TE transcripts for cleavage (Martinez *et al.* 2017). The transcriptional reactivation of many TEs in the vegetative cell results from the loss of RdDM-dependent CHH-methylation in microspores and the active demethylation of many TEs (Slotkin *et al.* 2009, Calarco *et al.* 2012). This is accompanied by downregulation of *DDM1* expression in the vegetative cell. As in *ddm1* mutants 21-nt easiRNAs derived from certain families of long-terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons in the Gypsy and Copia classes that are targeted by different miRNAs accumulate to high levels after their transcripts have been cleaved by miRNAs (Creasey *et al.* 2014). These easiRNAs are found both in the vegetative

nucleus and particularly in sperm cells, to where they are thought to be transported from the vegetative cell to reinforce transposon silencing in the sperm and ultimately the embryo (Martinez *et al.* 2016). These LTR retrotransposons also regain CHH-methylation in the vegetative nucleus, and this is likely to involve the RDR6-RdDM pathway, given the similarities between the situation in pollen and in *ddm1* mutants (Slotkin *et al.* 2009, Calarco *et al.* 2012, Nuthikattu *et al.* 2013). Recently, these easiRNAs have also been shown to contribute to hybridization barriers between parental plants at different ploidy levels (Borges *et al.* 2018, Martinez *et al.* 2018).

Here, we have characterized the *paps1-3* null mutant phenotype in pollen in detail. This uncovered an unexpected link between *PAPS1* function and the RdDM pathway, which we supported using double mutant analysis in the sporophyte. No such interaction was seen for *PAPS2* and/or *PAPS4*, identifying a further specific role of PAPS1 in plant gene regulation.

#### RESULTS

#### paps1-3 mutant pollen grains fail to germinate and show differentiation defects

The paps1-3 null mutant allele carries a T-DNA insertion in the middle of the coding sequence for the N-terminal catalytic domain of PAPS1 (Vi, et al. 2013). It was previously found to result in a strong male gametophytic defect, as the mutant allele could not be transmitted through the pollen in reciprocal crosses (Vi, et al. 2013). To characterize this male gametophytic defect in more detail, we employed a fluorescent labelling system that allows discriminating PAPS1 wild-type and paps1-3 mutant pollen grains produced by heterozygous mothers. This system makes use of a *pLat52::RFP* transgene that is inserted close to the PAPS1 allele, with a distance between the two loci of approximately 290 kb. As a result, there is tight linkage between the transgene and the wild-type PAPS1 allele, such that PAPS1 pLat52::RFP / paps1-3 heterozygous plants produce 50% red fluorescent pollen, of which virtually all carry the PAPS1 allele, while essentially all non-fluorescent pollen grains are paps1-3 mutant. Given that the genome-wide average relationship between physical and genetic distance in A. thaliana is 260 kb/cM (Meinke et al. 2009), we expect around 1% of recombinant pollen grains, i.e. red-fluorescent, but paps1-3 mutant pollen grains and non-fluorescent, but PAPS1 wild-type pollen grains. Indeed, the fraction is likely to be even lower, as we have not observed any recombination event between the *pLat52::RFP* reporter and the *paps1-1* mutant allele in more than 800 plants.

To test whether *paps1-3* mutant pollen grains were functional, we germinated pollen from *PAPS1 pLat52::RFP / paps1-3* plants *in vitro* and on wild-type styles using a semi-*in vivo* approach. However, in neither case did we observe any non-fluorescent pollen tubes (Figure

1A). 211 red fluorescent, but no non-fluorescent pollen tubes were seen for *in vitro* germination of 724 total pollen grains, and 161 red fluorescent, but no non-fluorescent pollen tubes emerged from the cut style in the semi-*in vivo* assay; for the latter, the total number of pollen grains placed on the stigmas could not be determined with certainty, yet the absence of any non-fluorescent tubes is consistent with the *in vitro* assay. Thus, *paps1-3* mutant pollen grains are unable to germinate. We also assessed the cellular phenotype of *paps1-3* mutant pollen grains using light microscopy as well as DAPI staining. Mutant pollen grains were reduced in length (27.6 µm versus 28.5 µm for wild-type grains; p<0.001) and in width (13.9 µm versus 14.6 µm for wild-type grains; p<0.001). DAPI staining of mature pollen grains indicated a higher percentage of pollen grains with fewer cells detected than the three cells typical of wild-type pollen (Figure 1B; experiment 1: 22% of 607 *paps1-3* pollen grains versus 19% of 198 wild-type pollen grains, p<0.001). In all cases the undetectable cells were one or both sperm cells.

#### Gene expression is strongly misregulated in paps1-3 mutant pollen

To determine the molecular basis of these pollen defects, we flow-sorted pollen grains from PAPS1 pLat52::RFP / paps1-3 plants based on fluorescence, extracted total RNA and used this for RNA-seq, with three biological replicates per pollen genotype. Reverse transcription was performed with a mixture of oligo(dT) and random-hexamer primers included in the library preparation kit that was used because of the low amounts of extracted RNA (see Methods); as a result, the RNA-seq reads do not only represent polyadenylated transcripts. Our previous results from *paps1-1* seedlings grown at high temperatues, in which PAPS1 activity appears to be very severely compromised, did not indicate a global loss of poly(A) tails (Vi, et al. 2013), suggesting that such a global loss should not occur in paps1-3 mutant pollen grains either. At the same time, a number of mRNAs have a shorter poly(A)-tail length in paps1-1 versus wild-type seedlings (Kappel, et al. 2015), and such transcripts indeed show a statistically significant, albeit moderate reduction in read numbers in the paps1-3 versus wild-type pollen samples (Figure S1). While we cannot discriminate whether these reduced read numbers reflect their shortened poly(A) tails, resulting in fewer oligo(dT)primed reads, or reduced transcript abundance, these findings support that this set of transcripts is consistently affected by reduced PAPS1 activity.

Overall *paps1-3* pollen showed a global change in gene expression, with very many genes with higher expression levels (Figure 2A,B), and this effect was strongest for mitochondrial transcripts. By contrast, especially genes with the highest expression levels in wild-type

pollen, including key genes for pollen differentiation like *SKU5 SIMILAR 13* (*SKS13*) and *SKS14* or the *LEUCINE-RICH REPEAT/EXTENSIN 10* receptor kinase, showed substantially reduced relative expression levels in *paps1-3* mutant pollen (Table S1, Table S2). The mutant pollen grains contain significantly less ribosomal RNA, but similar amounts of other RNA (Kappel, *et al.* 2015); thus, given our use of a mix of random-hexamer and oligo(dT) priming for reverse transcription, the increased relative expression of many genes could simply result from relatively more priming on non-rRNA transcripts. However, this seems unlikely, as the fraction of reads mapping to ribosomal RNA was not reduced in the *paps1-3* mutant samples; if anything, it tended to be higher. Also, the described effects were seen when normalization of read counts was performed after filtering out rRNA-derived reads. Thus, the global change in relative gene-expression levels likely reflects defects in pollen differentiation, resulting in large-scale inflation of relative expression levels likely non-protein coding transcripts were more abundant in the mutant pollen than in the wild-type pollen (Figure 2C).

The large-scale changes in relative expression make it difficult to estimate meaningful geneexpression differentials using conventional normalization methods. We therefore investigated only the strongest changes, which we identified using shrunken log2-fold changes computed by DESeq2 (Love *et al.* 2014; Table S2). We also used those estimates and the MapMan tool to study family-wise changes. The families with the most pronounced changes (all increases in expression) included genes encoding ribosomal proteins, microRNAs and natural antisense transcripts, and transposable elements (Table S3). TE genes as a class showed approximately a doubling of reads in the mutant versus the wildtype pollen (Figure 2C). The significantly changed families of TEs included both DNA transposons, e.g. AtENSPM and VANDAL elements, and retrotransposons, e.g. AtCOPIA, Athila and AtLINE families (Figure 3).

Different groups of TEs undergo different changes in DNA methylation during microspore and pollen development (Calarco *et al.* 2012). To determine whether TEs of these groups differed in their behaviour in *paps1-3* mutant pollen, we investigated expression profiles for transposable-element genes overlapping with differentially methylated regions between the vegetative nucleus and sperm cell or microspore as identified by Calarco *et al.* (Calarco *et al.* 2012). Those overlapping with differentially CG-methylated regions, which are mostly hypomethylated in the vegetative nucleus and correspond to DNA transposons, show higher expression in *paps1-3* mutants. By contrast, those overlapping with differentially CHHmethylated regions, which are mostly hypomethylated in the microspore and the sperm cells and correspond to LTR retrotransposons, were more weakly expressed in the mutant versus

wild-type pollen (Figure 2D). The differentially CG-methylated TEs are associated with the formation of 24-nt siRNAs in wild-type pollen, while the differentially CHH-methylated TEs give rise to 21-nt siRNAs. Thus, the loss of PAPS1 activity leads to major changes in gene expression in pollen, including that of many TEs. The different behaviour of the TE groups tested suggests that the main effect is on gene expression in the vegetative nucleus.

# paps1-3 mutant pollen grains fail to accumulate wild-type levels of miRNAs and siRNAs

The altered transcript levels of the TE classes suggest that PAPS1 may be required for the generation of the corresponding siRNAs and/or may modulate their relative levels, such that altered siRNA levels contribute to the changes in accumulation of the corresponding TE transcripts. To test this directly, we repeated the flow sorting of pollen grains from PAPS1 pLat52::RFP / paps1-3 plants, extracted small RNAs and used these for sRNA-seq, with three biological replicates each. We normalized abundances to all mapped reads of RNAs ranging from 18 to 28 nucleotides, as done previously (Nuthikattu et al. 2013). Focusing on sRNAs with a length of 21 to 24 nt, siRNAs directed against transposable elements were significantly less abundant in paps1-3 mutant than in wild-type pollen (Figure 4A). Overall the effect on 21-nt and 24-nt siRNAs was comparable (Figure 4A), yet at the level of individual TE families, significant differences in the accumulation of 21-nt siRNAs were only seen for some TE families, but for most in the case of 24-nt siRNAs (Figure 3). The reduction in transposable-element derived sRNAs contrasts with the increased transcript abundances of these elements (Figure 3). Regarding differentially CG-methylated TEs, the level of 24-nt siRNAs was strongly reduced in the mutant pollen, while that of 21-nt siRNAs was not significantly different (Figure 4B). For differentially CHH-methylated TEs, siRNAs of both length classes were less abundant in the mutant pollen (Figure 4C), even though also their transcripts accumulated less in paps1-3 mutant pollen (Figure 2D). In addition, levels of many miRNAs were reduced in paps1-3 mutant versus wild-type pollen (Figure 4D; Table S4); these included many that target epigenetically activated transposable elements for cleavage, such as miR156, miR399 and miR845. Correlating the abundances of miRNAs predicted to target TE transcripts with the abundances of siRNAs derived from these TE transcripts (Creasey et al. 2014) indicated a strong positive correlation, with all statistically significant correlations (34 of 109) being strongly positive (r>0.8: Table S5). In addition to miRNAs, TE transcripts in pollen are also targeted by specifically processed, mainly 19-nt long fragments of tRNAs (Martinez et al. 2017). However, except for a very small difference in the accumulation of 21-nt tRNA-derived small RNAs, there were no significant differences in the levels of the other, more abundant size classes of tRNA-derived sRNAs between the

two pollen genotypes (Figure 4E). This is consistent with previous reports that these tRNAfragments are not derived from polyadenylated transcripts (Martinez *et al.* 2017). As there was a difference in the number of small RNAs from rRNA between the genotypes (Figure S2A), we repeated the analysis excluding reads mapping to rRNA and tRNA. The above differences in accumulation of siRNAs against transposable elements and miRNAs were still observed (Figure S2B). Thus, together these transcriptomic data indicate that *PAPS1* is required for the generation of 24-nt and also some 21-nt siRNAs from transposable elements that are upregulated in pollen development.

To circumvent the limitations of available material for paps1-3 mutant pollen grains, we sought to recapitulate the effect on TE expression and siRNA production in double mutants of the viable, yet temperature-sensitive paps1-1 allele and the ddm1-2 allele. The paps1-1 allele carries a missense mutation in the catalytic domain that renders the protein temperature-sensitive (Vi et al. 2013). In ddm1 mutants the TEs that are derepressed in wildtype pollen development are upregulated, causing the accumulation of easiRNAs that depend on RDR6 activity (McCue et al. 2012, Nuthikattu, et al. 2013). As in the pollen, loss of PAPS1 function in this background would be expected to increase TE-transcript levels and decrease corresponding siRNAs. We constructed the paps1-1 ddm1-2 double mutant using a *ddm1-2* allele that had never been homozygous, but always maintained as a *ddm1-*2/+ line to avoid the accumulation of TE-induced background mutations (McCue et al. 2012). Morphologically, first-time homozygous paps1-1 ddm1-2 double mutants segregating from a paps1-1 ddm1-2/+ plant showed a variable phenotype, with many plants remaining severely dwarfed and only few reaching a similar size as the paps1-1 single mutants, indicating a synergistic effect of the two mutations (Figure 5A). In line with this, genotyping the progeny of paps1-1 ddm1-2/+ plants indicated a deficiency of paps1-1 ddm1-2 double mutants (Table S6), suggesting that part of the double-mutant zygotes do not develop into seedlings; by contrast, the perfect 1:2 ratio of 84 paps1-1 versus 168 paps1-1 ddm1-2/+ progeny plants suggests that paps1 ddm1 double-mutant pollen is fully functional. We used offspring of several such first-time homozygous paps1 ddm1 double mutants to test the expression of two TEs, AtENSPM6 and Athila6, via gRT-PCR. Both of these transcripts are strongly upregulated in *ddm1* mutants and are associated with high levels of RDR6-dependent siRNAs in this background (Nuthikattu, et al. 2013). However, in the paps1-1 ddm1-2 double mutant the transcript levels of both TEs were in fact decreased compared to the ddm1-2 single mutant (Figure 5B). In paps2-1 paps4-1 ddm1-2 triple mutants, which did not show an enhanced morphological phenotype (Figure 5A), Athila6 levels were unchanged, and AtENSPM6 levels were slightly increased (Figure 5B). The reduction in Athila6 levels in the

paps1-1 ddm1-2 double mutant appeared more pronounced when plants were grown at 30°C for two days before RNA extraction to more strongly inactivate the temperaturesensitive protein encoded by the paps1-1 allele (Figure S3A). Performing the qRT-PCR on random hexamer-primed cDNA confirmed the downregulation of Athila6 levels, whereas AtENSPM6 levels were unchanged in the double versus the ddm1-2 single mutant. A similar discrepancy between the results of oligo(dT)- and random hexamer-primed gRT-PCR assays was previously seen for SAUR transcripts in paps1 mutants. In this case, the reduced signal in the oligo(dT)-primed, but not in random hexamer-primed assays reflected a severe shortening of the poly(A) tails on SAUR transcripts. By analogy, the same discrepancy observed here for AtENSPM6 (Figure 5B, S3A) raises the possibility that PAPS1 may be involved in polyadenylating the *AtENSPM6* transcript. We also assessed the accumulation of Athila6-derived siRNAs by a small-RNA Northern blot. There was no difference in their levels between *paps1-1 ddm1-2* double mutant and *ddm1-2* single mutant seedlings, irrespective of whether plants had been grown at the standard temperature (Figure 5C) or at 30°C for two days before RNA extraction (Figure S3B). Therefore, even though the morphological phenotype is consistent with a functional interaction between PAPS1 and DDM1, the paps1-1 ddm1 background appeared unsuitable to study the influence of PAPS1 on TE silencing and siRNA generation in more detail.

### Genetic interactions of PAPS1 with components of the RdDM pathway

Our transcriptomic analysis described above had suggested a functional link between PAPS1-mediated polyadenylation and sRNA-based control of TE expression. To further investigate this putative connection between PAPS1 and small-RNA pathways, in particular the RdDM pathway, we tested for genetic interactions, both in the male gametophyte and in the sporophyte. To this end, we used the temperature-sensitive, male gametophytic and sporophytic viable paps1-1 allele. Mutants of paps1-1 were crossed with rdr6-13, rdr2-1 and nrpd2a-1 nrpd2b-1 mutants. NRPD2A and NRPD2B redundantly encode the shared secondlargest subunit of PolIV and PolV, and their loss of function abrogates the RdDM pathway (Matzke and Mosher 2014, Cuerda-Gil and Slotkin 2016). Plants homozygous for the RdDM pathway mutations and heterozygous for paps1-1 were crossed as pollen donors to wildtype Col-0 plants, and the segregation of the paps1-1 mutant allele was determined in the F1 progeny. The paps1-1 allele segregated in the expected 1:1 ratio in an otherwise wild-type background, as well as in rdr6-13 and rdr2-1 mutant backgrounds (Figure 6A). By contrast, transmission of the paps1-1 allele through the pollen was significantly reduced in an nrpd2a-1 nrpd2b-1 mutant background, indicating that combined reduction of PAPS1 and loss of PolIV and PolV activities compromises male gametophyte function.

Regarding a genetic interaction in the sporophyte, double or appropriate higher-order mutants were analyzed in the vegetative and the generative stage (Figure 6B,C; Figure 7). While in a wild-type background, the *paps1-1* mutation reduces leaf size by roughly 54%, this effect was much stronger in the *rdr6-13* and particularly in the *nrpd2a-1 nrpd2b-1* backgrounds, but very similar in the *rdr2-1* background (Figure 6B,C). In particular, *paps1-1 rdr6-13* double mutants formed leaves that were almost 70% smaller than those of *rdr6-13* alone, while *paps1-1 nrpd2a-1 nrpd2b-1* triple mutant leaves were even more than 80% smaller than those of the *nrpd2a-1 nrpd2b-1* double mutant. By contrast, the leaf-size reduction in the *paps1-1 rdr2-1* double mutant relative to *rdr2-1* was only 49%.

Also at the reproductive stage, *paps1-1 rdr6-13* and *paps1-1 nrpd2a-1 nrpd2b-1* mutants showed synergistic phenotypes compared to the *paps1-1* single and the corresponding RdDM-pathway mutants, with a similar, but weaker synergistic effect also in the *paps1-1 rdr2-1* double mutant (Figure 7A, Figure S4A,B). In particular, siliques of the respective combinations were much shorter (more than 60% in the *paps1-1 rdr6-13* double mutants), with many fewer seeds (80% reduction in the *paps1-1 rdr6-13* double mutants) than in the respective single or double mutants (Figure S4A,B). Notably, the corresponding triple or quadruple mutants with *paps2-1 paps4-1* instead of *paps1-1* did not show any phenotypic enhancement, indicating that the observed genetic interactions were specific for mutants with reduced *PAPS1* function, rather than reflecting a general effect of lower poly(A) polymerase activity.

Thus, together these results in the gametophyte and the sporophyte indicate that the *paps1-1* mutation shows a synergistic phenotype with mutations in the *RDR6*-dependent RdDM pathway, supporting their specific functional interaction.

### PAPS1 and RDR6 function are required for transmitting-tract development

Given the strength and specificity of its synergistic phenotype, we characterized the *paps1-1 rdr6-13* double mutant in more detail. In contrast to either single mutant, the double mutants only formed very short siliques with few seeds; in addition, these were mainly found at the tip of the silique (Figure 7A-C). Reciprocal crosses between the double mutant and Col-0 were used to determine whether a defect occurred on the male or on the female side (Figure 7B,C). Pollinating Col-0 gynoecia with pollen from the double mutant resulted in the same high number of seeds per silique as when Col-0 pollen was used, indicating that the double mutant pollen was fully functional. By contrast, when the double mutants were used as mother plants, only few seeds developed per silique, irrespective of the pollen genotype (Figure 7B,C). Thus, the reduced seed set of the double mutant was due to a maternal

sporophytic or a female gametophytic defect. To distinguish between these possibilities, we pollinated *paps1-1 rdr6-13*/+ plants with Col-0 pollen and determined the segregation of the *rdr6-13* mutation in the progeny; the *rdr6-13*/+ heterozygote was used as a control. In both cases, the *rdr6-13* mutation showed the expected 50:50 segregation (Figure 7D). Therefore, the *paps1 rdr6-13* double mutant female gametophyte appears to be functional, indicating that the reduced fertility of the double mutant results from a maternal sporophytic defect.

Closer inspection indicated that virtually all of the double mutant gynoecia had a split septum, a phenotype that was only seen in about 20% of the rdr6-13 single mutants and never in the paps1-1 mutants (Figure 7E). Like with other phenotypes, the paps2-1 paps4-1 mutations did not enhance this rdr6-13 defect. In addition, extensive stigmatic tissue developed inside the paps1-1 rdr6-13 double mutant gynoecia, replacing the transmitting tract (Figure S4C). To determine whether the split septum and the absence of a welldeveloped transmitting tract could underlie the double mutant's fertility defect, we visualized pollen tubes by aniline-blue staining in Col-0 or double mutant gynoecia at 24 hours after pollinating them with wild-type pollen (Figure 7F). In a wild-type gynoecium, the pollen tubes grew in a well-ordered manner through the transmitting tract, with many reaching the base of the gynoecium. By contrast, in the absence of a transmitting tract in the double mutant gynoecia the pollen tubes appeared to follow the two placental strands at the top of the gynoecium, and virtually none reached its base. Thus, we conclude that the combined loss of RDR6 and PAPS1 activity interferes with the development of the gynoecium, in particular the transmitting tract in the septum, resulting in strongly reduced fertility. As such, this synergistic phenotype underlines the functional relationship between PAPS1 and RDR6.

The seeds formed by the double mutants were significantly larger than those of the wild-type or either single mutant (Figure S4D,E). This was not merely due to the reduced seed number, as the effect was still seen when limiting the number of seeds developing per plant to comparable levels of 20 to 200 seeds per plant by reducing the number of siliques that were left to develop. However, this phenotype appeared to be merely additive compared to the two single mutants.

#### Molecular basis of the paps1-1 rdr6-13 double mutant phenotype

We next asked about the possible molecular basis of the synergistic gynoecium defect in the *paps1-1 rdr6-13* double mutant. In particular, we considered two non-exclusive hypotheses. First, as the double mutant resembles aspects of the phenotypes seen in *hecate* loss-of-function mutants (the defective transmitting tract and septum development) and in *HECATE* 

overexpressors (production of ectopic stigmatic tissue), changes in expression of the *HECATE* genes coding for basic helix-loop-helix transcription factors might be involved (Gremski *et al.* 2007, Schuster *et al.* 2015). Second, *rdr6* mutant phenotypes, including floral patterning defects, can be enhanced by increasing the copy number and expression of *AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR3* (*ARF3*), whose expression is limited by the *trans*-acting siRNA *TAS3* in wild-type (Fahlgren *et al.* 2006). Therefore, reduction of *PAPS1* activity in the *rdr6* mutant background could result in a further upregulation of *ARF3*.

To test these hypotheses, we determined transcript levels of the mentioned genes in developing gynoecia of paps1-1 rdr6-13 double mutants relative to the corresponding single mutants and wild-type; as a specificity control of PAPS1 activity, we also included the paps2-1 paps4-1 rdr6-13 triple mutant and the paps2-1 paps4-1 double mutant (Figure 8). While paps1-1 single mutants had significantly higher HEC1 expression levels than wild-type or rdr6-13 mutant gynoecia, there was no further increase in the paps1-1 rdr6-13 double mutant. HEC2 showed the highest expression level in the paps1-1 rdr6-13 double mutant, yet at a very low absolute level (Figure S5). Similarly, ARF3 and ARF4 had the highest levels of transcript accumulation in the double mutant. Levels of the TAS3a precursor transcript were increased in rdr6-13 mutants relative to wild-type, yet the double mutant with paps1-1 showed the same TAS3a precursor levels as wild-type, when tested on oligo(dT)primed cDNA, and higher than wild-type when assayed on random-hexamer primed cDNA. As for AtENSPM6 above, this discrepancy between a higher gRT-PCR signal after randomhexamer versus oligo(dT)-priming suggests a role for PAPS1 in polyadenylation of the TAS3a precursor transcript. Several other style development-related genes (e.g. STYLISH2 [STY2] and SPATULA [SPT]) were expressed more strongly in paps1-1 single and in paps1-1 rdr6-13 double mutant gynoecia than in wild-type or the rdr6-13 single mutant (Figure S5), yet the degree of upregulation was only moderate in all cases. Thus, together these results suggest that the synergistic gynoecium defects reflect the combined effect of changes to the expression of several style-related genes, including ARF3 and ARF4.

# DISCUSSION

The poly(A) polymerase PAPS1 plays diverse roles in plant development, growth and response to the environment. Here, we have characterized the *paps1* null-mutant phenotype in detail. Our transcriptomic and sRNA analyses led us to uncover a previously unknown functional interaction of PAPS1 with the RdDM pathway for gene silencing, which we substantiated by double mutant analyses.

### PAPS1 is essential for pollen differentiation and function

The paps1-3 null allele cannot be transmitted through the male gametophyte (Vi et al. 2013). We show here that this is based on a failure of mutant pollen grains to germinate, and is also associated with a significant fraction of mutant pollen showing defects in normal differentiation, as judged by their reduced sperm-cell numbers and shrunken size. The strict requirement for PAPS1 function in male gametophytes contrasts with the observation that even double mutants for the other two canonical nuclear PAPS isoforms are fully male fertile (Czesnick and Lenhard 2016), even though at least at the level of RNA expression PAPS4 is expressed much more strongly in pollen than PAPS1 (Table S1). Severe defects in paps1-3 pollen differentiation are also indicated by the strong changes in gene expression, in particular the strongly reduced relative expression of key pollen-differentiation and pollenfunction associated genes. Against this background, it is currently unclear which of the other prominent changes in gene expression, for example the increased abundance of mitochondrial transcripts and of transcripts encoding ribosomal proteins, merely represent an indirect stress response or rather a more direct function of PAPS1. The paps1-3 mutant pollen grains contain strongly reduced levels of rRNA, and thus presumably of functional ribosomes, and transcripts encoding ribosomal proteins were found to have shorter poly(A) tails in the viable paps1-1 mutant than in wild-type (Kappel et al. 2015). Therefore, the strong upregulation of ribosomal-protein transcripts is likely to represent an indirect effect, possibly via increased transcription to counteract the deficiency in functional ribosomes. Unfortunately, our use of a mixture of oligo(dT) and random-hexamer primers for reverse transcription precludes us from drawing conclusions about the polyadenylation status of these ribosomal-protein transcripts.

### PAPS1 is required for RdDM

Our transcriptomic and sRNA analyses indicate that *PAPS1* activity is required for RdDM. In the *paps1-3* mutant pollen, the abundance of TE transcripts was strongly increased, while the accumulation of corresponding 24-nt siRNAs and to a lesser extent also 21-nt siRNAs was reduced. Among the TEs, ones overlapping with differentially CG-methylated regions were particularly strongly overexpressed, while ones next to differentially CHH-methylated regions were less affected than TEs globally. Differentially CG-methylated regions generally lose CG-methylation in the vegetative nucleus due to the activity of DNA glycosylases such as ROS1, DML2, and DML3, and the corresponding TEs accumulate 21- and 24-nt siRNAs in sperm cells (Calarco *et al.* 2012). In the absence of *PAPS1* activity, formation of these siRNAs appears to be impaired, which may in turn contribute to the increased accumulation

of the corresponding TE transcripts, either via their increased transcription or reduced processing into siRNAs. In addition to siRNAs, also a large number of miRNAs are reduced in abundance in the mutant pollen grains; many of the affected miRNAs can target TE transcripts for cleavage, which contributes to the formation of RDR6-dependent easiRNAs against these TEs (Creasey *et al.* 2014). Thus, it is likely that this reduction in miRNAs also contributes to the observed changes in TE transcript and siRNA accumulation.

The functional interaction between *PAPS1* and the RdDM pathway is further supported by our double-mutant analyses. Introducing the viable *paps1-1* allele into the *nrpd2a nrpd2b* mutant background that lacks RdDM function due to loss of PoIIV and PoIV activity shows a strongly synergistic phenotype, both at the sporophytic and the male gametophytic level. As for the latter, transmission of the *paps1-1* mutant allele was reduced in the RdDM-defective background, while triple mutant sporophytes showed a dramatic dwarfing phenotype and reduced fertility. Similar phenotypes were seen in the double mutants with *ddm1* and *rdr6*, and to a lesser extent in the double mutant with *rdr2*, despite the stronger reduction of 24-nt than 21-nt siRNAs in the *paps1-3* pollen, whose synthesis depends on *RDR2*. Of note, the synergistic, rather than epistatic phenotype of the *paps1-1 nrpd2a nrpd2b* triple mutants argues against any role for PAPS1 in the processing of the PolIV- or PolV-dependent transcripts, as these are not formed in this background in the first place. Also, biochemical characterization of RDR2 and RDR6 indicate that they do not require polyadenylated transcripts as templates for conversion to dsRNA (Curaba and Chen 2008, Rajeswaran *et al.* 2012, Devert *et al.* 2015).

It is unclear, which branch of the RdDM pathway - the canonical or any of the non-canonical branches (Cuerda-Gil and Slotkin 2016) - *PAPS1* interacts with. In particular the consistently strongest genetic interaction with *RDR6*, and less so with *RDR2* argues for an RDR6-dependent non-canonical RdDM mechanism; however, this conclusion appears at odds with the more pronounced effect of the *paps1-3* mutation on the *RDR2*-dependent 24-nt rather than the *RDR6*-dependent 21-nt siRNAs in pollen. One *RDR6*-dependent mechanism involving both classes of siRNAs is RDR6-DCL3 RdDM (Mari-Ordonez *et al.* 2013, Cuerda-Gil and Slotkin 2016). In this case, high levels of RDR6-generated dsRNA resulting from strong TE expression are thought to exceed the processing capacity by DCL2/DCL4 into 21-nt siRNAs, allowing access of DCL3 to these dsRNAs for processing into 24-nt siRNAs and incorporation into the canonical RdDM pathway. Whether and if so, how PAPS1 is involved in this RDR6-DCL3 pathway will require further investigation.

As with overall pollen viability, the described genetic interactions are specific for *paps1* as opposed to mutations in the two other PAPS isoforms, underlining the specialized function of PAPS1.

#### Development of the transmitting tract depends on RDR6 and PAPS1 activities

The paps1-1 rdr6 double mutants show a dramatic disruption of transmitting tract development. This results in strong female sporophytic fertility defects in the double mutants, with pollen tubes - irrespective of their genotypes - failing to grow to the ovules except at the very tip of the gynoecia. The network of factors controlling transmitting-tract development has been elucidated in some detail; in particular, the basic helix-loop-helix proteins encoded by the HEC genes play an important role (Reyes-Olalde et al. 2013). Increased HEC1 expression, which causes the formation of ectopic stigmatic papillae in wild-type, is indeed seen in paps1-1 single mutant gynoecia, yet no further increase was detected in the double mutant. In addition, the rdr6 mutant phenotype is known to be enhanced by increased expression of ARF3 and ARF4, both of which are targeted by RDR6-dependent tasiRNAs in wild-type (Fahlgren, et al. 2006); the paps1-1 rdr6 double mutant showed increased ARF3 and ARF4 expression relative to rdr6 single mutants, suggesting that this effect likely contributes to the synergistic double mutant phenotype. Higher-order mutants would be required to assess the relative contribution of increased HEC1 and ARF3/4 expression to the enhanced phenotype. Also, the molecular mechanisms by which reduction of PAPS1 function causes increased HEC1 and ARF3/4 expression remain unclear.

Thus, overall our results support a functional and specific interaction of PAPS1 with the RdDM pathway. Notably, this interaction provides a further connection between the Polliassociated machinery responsible for mRNA formation from protein-coding genes and the pathway for RdDM; as such, it may contribute to the initiation of RdDM on PollI-transcribed, but hitherto non-silenced regions. There is precedence for the involvement of PAPS in siRNA biogenesis and heterochromatin formation from other organisms. Heterochromatin formation, including RdDM, in fission yeast requires the non-canonical poly(A) polymerase Cid12, which forms part of the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase complex RDRC and may target RDR activity to the 3' ends of transcripts that are to be converted to dsRNA (Martienssen et al. 2005). A related protein from C. elegans is also needed for siRNA production and RNA interference (Chen et al. 2005). Notably, recent studies also indicate a requirement for the canonical PAPS Pla1 from fission yeast in siRNA production and chromatin-mediated silencing of genes and retrotransposons, potentially by causing certain transcripts to be converted to dsRNA (Yamanaka et al. 2013). However, as for PAPS1 it remains unknown how Pla1 mechanistically contributes to siRNA biogenesis. A purely indirect effect via regulating the expression of key siRNA biogenesis or activity factors appears unlikely, though, as we did not detect significant reductions in expression of siRNA pathway genes in paps1-3 pollen, with the exception of AGO9 (Table S2); however, AGO9

only seems to be essential for siRNA activity in the ovule (Olmedo-Monfil *et al.* 2010), making it unlikely that its reduction is causal for the siRNA effects seen in the mutant pollen. Understanding this additional specific role of *PAPS1* in more detail would greatly benefit from the ability to recapitulate the effect of *PAPS1* on siRNA biogenesis in viable sporophytes to circumvent the limitations of using flow-sorted pollen grains to enable further mechanistic studies.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Plant materials and growth conditions

The following mutant alleles have been described previously: *paps1-1* (Vi *et al.* 2013), *paps1-2* (T-DNA line SAIL\_172\_F11; (Vi *et al.* 2013)), *paps1-3* (WiscDsLox413-416L14; (Vi *et al.* 2013)), *paps2-1* (SALK\_126395; (Czesnick and Lenhard 2016)), *paps4-1*(SALK\_007979; (Czesnick and Lenhard 2016)), *rdr2-1(Xie, et al.* 2004), *rdr6-13* (Peragine *et al.* 2004), *nrpd2a-1 nrpd2b-1* (Herr *et al.* 2005), *ddm1-2* (Jeddeloh *et al.* 1999). Col-0 was used as wild-type.

The mapped *pLat52*::*XFP* insertion lines have been described before (Francis *et al.* 2007). The line used here, 1376, is located at position 6,488,210 on chromosome 1, approximately 290 kb from the *PAPS1* locus. Plant growth was as previously described (Vi,*et al.* 2013).

## Flow sorting of pollen, RNA extraction and RNA sequencing

To characterize gene expression in *paps1-3* mutant pollen, pollen was collected from *PAPS1 pLat52::RFP / paps1-3* plants. Plant growth and pollen isolation were performed as previously described (Borges *et al.* 2012). Flow sorting of pollen according to RFP fluorescence was performed as previously described (Kappel *et al.* 2015).

Total RNA was isolated using a RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen) and RNA integrity and amount of ribosomal RNA was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyser with RNA 6000 Nano Assay (Agilent Technologies). Illumina-compatible sequencing libraries were generated using the Ovation RNA-seq system v2 (NuGen) and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq2000 instrument at the IPK Gatersleben in paired-end mode with 101 bases for read 1 and 99 bases for read 2.

#### **RNA-seq data analysis**

Reads were mapped against the Arabidopsis thaliana reference genome (TAIR10) using TopHat2 (Kim *et al.* 2013). Read counts per transcript were extracted using HTSeq-count (Anders *et al.* 2014). Follow-up analyses were done using R. In a first step reads were normalized to counts per million. Independently, R/DESeq2 (Love *et al.* 2014) was used for normalization and gene expression analysis. MapMan (Thimm *et al.* 2004) was used for gene-set enrichment analysis. Family-wise transposable element gene expression changes were assessed using a Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test. Transposable element genes were annotated based on overlaps with transposable element annotations from TAIR (TAIR10\_Transposable\_Elements.txt). Transcripts overlapping with differentially methylated regions identified by Calarco *et al.* (Calarco *et al.* 2012), were identified using bedtools intersect and the genome coordinates from their Supplementary Table 2.

#### small RNA sequencing and analysis

Following the sorting of pollen grains, whole RNA was isolated using the Direct-Zol<sup>™</sup> RNA MiniPrep Kit by Zymo Research (Irvine, California, US) since the specific columns provided in this kit prevent loss of small RNAs. The freshly sorted samples were centrifuged for 3 min at 3,000 rpm and the supernatant removed. Subsequently, the pollen were resuspended in 750 µl Tri-reagent LS (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, US) and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. The samples were ground in a cooled mortar to break up pollen grains until the samples liquefied and transferred to fresh reaction tubes. Afterwards, the protocol was followed according to the manufacturer's specification. RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop and analysed on a BioAnalyzer to verify the existence of small RNAs as well as to check for possible RNA degradation. Generation of small RNA libraries from three paired pollen RNA isolations (wild-type and *paps1-3* mutant pollen sorted from the same sample) and sequencing was carried out by Eurofins Medigenomix GmbH, Ebersburg, Germany. Complementary DNA (cDNA) libraries were constructed containing fragments with a length of approximately 19-25 bp with TruSeq<sup>™</sup> Small RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, US) and sequencing was performed using Illumina HiSeq 2500.

RA3 adapters were removed using Trimmomatic (Bolger *et al.* 2014). The resulting reads were mapped against the Arabidopsis thaliana reference genome (TAIR10) using bwa (Li *et al.* 2009) and further processed using samtools (Li *et al.* 2009). Bam files were split by read length for 18 to 28 nucleotides using reformat.sh (https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/). Reads mapping to TAIR10 annotated gene exons and transposable elements (excluding overlaps with the prior ones) were counted using bedtools multicov (Quinlan and Hall 2010).

Analyses and visualization were done using R. Read counts were normalized by dividing the counts by the total number of mapped reads between 18 and 28 nucleotides and them multiplying the value by one million. For comparison an analogous normalization was done after excluding rRNA and tRNA. Differences between mutant and wild-type pollen at transposon family level were assessed using paired t-test. Correlations between miRNA and siRNA abundances were determined as follows miRNAs and targeted transposons were extracted from Creasey *et al.*, 2014, Table S3. TAIR10 miRNA annotations were updated using miRBase release 22 (Kozomara and Griffiths-Jones 2014). CPM for 20 to 24 nucleotide long sRNA were combined, all target TEs per miRNA were summed up for each sample.

All next-generation sequencing data will be submitted to NCBI before publication.

### RNA extraction, qRT-PCR and Northern blotting

RNA was extracted from 14-day old seedlings (with or without 30°C treated for 2 days) and gynoecium using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen). For qRT-PCR, RNA was treated with TURBO DNase (Invitrogen) to remove DNA. First strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg total RNA with Oligo(dT) 17 or Random Hexamers (Invitrogen) by SuperScript III kit (Invitrogen). Transcript levels were determined by qRT-PCR using SensiMix<sup>™</sup> SYBR Low-ROX Kit (Bioline) and Roche LightCycler 480. At1g08200 was used as an internal control for quantitative normalization.

For Northern blotting, small RNA was concentrated from 300 µg total RNA with polyethylene glycerol. 10 µg enriched small RNA was loaded in each well. Gel electrophoresis, blotting, and cross linking were performed according to (Pall *et al.*, 2007) with minor modifications. In this study, enriched small RNA was transferred at a constant 300 mA for 50 min using Mini Trans-Blot Cell (BIO-RAD). Probe generation was performed as described (Slotkin,2013). Oligonucleotides used in this study are listed in Table S7.

## Plant phenotyping

# **DAPI-Staining**

Open flowers were collected into a 15 ml Falcon tube, containing approximately 5 ml of 4',6-Diamidin-2-phenylindol (DAPI) solution with a concentration of 1  $\mu$ g/ml in phosphate buffered saline (PBS, containing 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub> and 2 mM KH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>). After vortexing for 3 minutes, flower parts were removed by filtering the solution through a Miracloth mesh (22-25  $\mu$ m). Pollen grains could then be precipitated by centrifugation (1

minute, 2,600x g). Resuspended pollen was then spotted onto a microscopic slide and analyzed using a fluorescence microscope.

### in vitro pollen germination

Open flowers were taken with forceps and brushed lightly on pollen tube growth medium (10 % (w/v) sucrose; 1.5 % (w/v) low melt agarose; 1 mM CaCl<sub>2</sub>; 1 mM Ca(NO<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>; 1 mM KCl; 0.03 % (w/v) amicase; 0.01 % (w/v) myo-Inositol; 0.01 % (w/v) ammonium ferric citrate; 10 mM  $\gamma$ -aminobutyric acid (GABA); 0.1 mM spermidin; 0.01 % (w/v) boric acid; pH 8.0), which was covered with a cellulosic Cellophane membrane to prevent pollen grains from direct contact with the medium (Rodriguez-Enriquez *et al.* 2013). After incubation for 4 hours in a moist chamber at 22°C, growth was analyzed via a microscope.

#### Semi-in vivo pollen germination

Wild-type (Col-0) flowers were emasculated 1.5 days prior to the experiment. Then the stigma was pollinated extensively with *PAPS1 pLat52::RFP / paps1-3* pollen, respectively. After 30 to 45 minutes the style was cut a little below the stigma and placed vertically on pollen tube growth medium (10 % (w/v) sucrose; 1.5 % (w/v) low melt agarose; 1 mM CaCl<sub>2</sub>; 1 mM Ca(NO<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>; 1 mM KCl; 0.03 % (w/v) amicase; 0.01 % (w/v) myo-Inositol; 0.01 % (w/v) ammonium ferric citrate; 10 mM  $\gamma$ -aminobutyric acid (GABA); 0.1 mM spermidin; 0.01 % (w/v) boric acid; pH 8.0), which was covered with a cellulosic cellophane membrane to prevent direct contact with the medium and to keep cut stigmas from sinking into the medium (Rodriguez-Enriquez *et al.* 2013). After incubation for 4 hours in a moist chamber at 22°C, growth was analyzed via a microscope.

#### Leaf-size measurements

Four weeks after germination, the fifth leaves of plants were harvested and laid flat on A4 papers with transparent tape. The papers with leaves were scanned (EPSON Perfection V600) and measured by ImageJ (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/index.html).

#### Aniline-blue staining

Flowers were emasculated 1.5 days prior to the experiment. Then pollen grains from the wild-type or mutant plants were dispersed on the stigmas respectively. 24 hours after pollination, the pistils were cut, dissected to remove a pair of ovary walls and fixed in a fixative (90% ethanol and 10% acetic acid) for more than 2 hours. The pistils were washed with 70% ethanol for 30 minutes and immersed in 1 M NaOH overnight. After washing three times with 0.1 M K<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>4</sub> (pH8.0), the pistils were stained with aniline blue (0.1% aniline blue,

pH8.0 0.1 M  $K_3PO_4$ ) for 6 hours. The stained pistils were observed under a fluorescence microscope (BX51, Olympus, Hamburg, Germany) equipped with an ultraviolet filter set.

### Seed-number limitation experiment and seed size measurements

For seed-number limitation, the first five floral buds of each plant were removed. From the 6th flower, 1-, 2-, 3-, 4-flower per plant of Col-0, *paps1-1*, *rdr6-13* were hand pollinated by the pollen grains from the same plant; 4-, 8-, 12-, 16-flower per plant of *paps1-1 rdr6-13* were hand pollinated, at least three biological replicates. Additional floral buds were removed. Until the siliques turned completely yellow or brown, seeds were harvested in microcentrifuge tubes and dried for three days on bench with the caps open. Seeds were then spread onto a petri dish and scanned (EPSON Perfection V600) at 1200 dpi black & white using transmitting light mode. Images were set to 162 of the threshold value and seed area was measured by "analyze-measure" of ImageJ with a lower limit 50,000  $\mu$ m<sup>2</sup>. Data was analyzed by Microsoft Excel.
