# **Fasciclin-like arabinogalactan protein gene expression is associated with yield of four in the milling of wheat**

**Ravi C. Nirmal<sup>1</sup>, Agnelo Furtado1, Parimalan Rangan<sup>2</sup> & Robert J. Henry <sup>1</sup>**

**A large portion of the global wheat crop is milled to produce four for use in the production of foods such as bread. Pressure to increase food supplies sustainably can be address directly by reducing postharvest losses during processes such as four milling. The recovery of four in the milling of wheat is genetically determined but difcult to assess in wheat breeding due to the requirement for a large sample. Here we report the discovery that human selection for altered expression of putative cell adhesion proteins is associated with wheats that give high yields of four on milling. Genes encoding fasciclin-like arabinogalactan proteins are expressed at low levels in high milling wheat genotypes at mid grain development. Thirty worldwide wheat genotypes were grouped into good and poor millers based four yield obtained from laboratory scale milling of mature seeds. Diferentially expressed genes were identifed by comparing transcript profles at 14 and 30 days post anthesis obtained from RNA-seq data of all the genotypes. Direct selection for genotypes with appropriate expression of these genes will greatly accelerate wheat breeding and ensure high recoveries of four from wheat by resulting in grains that break up more easily on milling.**

Wheat is the leading crop in the temperate world[1](#page-9-0) . It provides 20% of the total calories and proteins consumed worldwide[2](#page-9-1) . Wheat is ground to four by the process of dry milling to make various food products such as bread which are suitable for human consumption. From a milling perspective wheat grain has three main components; endosperm, bran and germ, each with diferent mechanical propertie[s3](#page-9-2) . In milling, the endosperm of the grain is scraped from the bran by passing the grain between metal rollers and sieving out the four derived from the small particles of endosperm released. Clean separation of endosperm from bran and germ is desired in this process[4](#page-9-3) . Te efciency of the process as measured by the yield of four is the subject of selection by wheat breeders developing new wheat varieties for these products. Te recovery of four is largely determined by genotype and milling process with recoveries usually in the range 70 to 80[%5](#page-9-4) . However, the maximum theoretical yield expected is around 85% which is the percentage of endosperm in the wheat grain[4](#page-9-3) . In wheat breeding, small scale laboratory test mills are widely used for the selection of wheat genotypes that deliver a higher four yield. However, this process is difcult because the test has poor repeatability and still requires more than 1 kg of grain. Tese quantities of grain are not available from single plants to allow early generation selection for this key trait.

Flour yield is a complex trait controlled by interaction between genotype, environment and processing[5–](#page-9-4)[8](#page-9-5) . Tree main factors which infuence milling performance of the wheat are grain hardness[9](#page-9-6) , endosperm to bran ratio and ease of separation of endosperm from bran[3](#page-9-2) . Sof wheats usually produce ~2% less four than the hard wheats due to diferences in mechanical properties of the grains[4](#page-9-3) . Several QTLs associated with milling yield have been reported, on chromosome 6[A10,](#page-9-7) 3B[11](#page-9-8), 1DL, 2AS, 2BS, 2DS, 3DL, 4AC, 4DS, 5AL, 6AC, 6DS[12,](#page-9-9) 1B, 2A, 2B, 3B, 6A, 7A, 7B[13.](#page-9-10) Co-relation between starch granule size distribution (SGSD) and milling yield has been demonstrated by Edwards *et al*. [14](#page-9-11) in hard wheats. It was suggested that an even size distribution (increased volume of B- and C- type small granules and reduced % volume of A-type large granules) of starch granules is genetically controlled and associated with higher four yield. ### **Results**

**Milling yield.** Milling yield of the thirty wheat genotypes measured as % of total four yield is shown in Table 1. Among the thirty genotypes, 17 were identifed as high milling wheat genotypes (HM-group) (>/=77%) and 13 were identifed as poor milling wheat genotypes (PM-group) (<77%). Te milling yield of genotype NW51A was the lowest (71.4%) while that of Ellison was the highest (79.7%), respectively (Table 1). Te milling yield of Gregory and Bobwhite could not be determined due to lack of grain samples from these trails. However, these genotypes are commercially known as high milling wheats and thus were placed into the HM-group.

Among the thirty genotypes, 14 genotypes had *Pina-D1a/Pinb-D1a* alleles of *puroindoline* genes within which 7 genotypes showed sof grain texture (SKCS HI<50) and 7 showed hard grain texture (SKCS HI>50), 8 genotypes were hard with *Pina* mutation (*Pina-D1b/Pinb-D1a*), and 7 were hard wheats with *Pinb* mutation (*Pina-D1a/Pinb-D1b)* (Table 1[\)18.](#page-9-15) Genotypes in HM-group and PM-group were mixture of sof and hard wheats containing diferent set of *Pin* alleles. All the hard wheats with *Pinb* mutation were good millers, except Giza 139.

**Identifcation of Diferentially expressed genes in good millers using Baggerley's test.** RNA-Seq analysis of genotypes corresponding to the PM-group and HM-group, followed by application of the Baggerley's test for identifcation of signifcant diferences led to the identifcation of twelve genes at 14 DPA and eight genes at 30 DPA that were statistically diferentially expressed at a false discovery rate (FDR) p-value of <0.01 (Tables [2](#page-2-0) and [3](#page-2-1)). Sorting the gene list based on proportion diference led to the identifcation of both up- and down- regulated genes. At 14 DPA, highly signifcantly diferentially expressed transcripts GH726097 and TC379245 showed high homology with a *fasciclin-like arabinogalactan-8* (*FLA-8*). GH726097 and TC379245 were expressed at much lower levels in wheat genotypes with high four yield; 28.2 and 6.1 fold, respectively (Fig. [1](#page-3-0)). Tese two transcripts were also down regulated at 30 DPA in the HM-group. A third transcript (TAGI; TC429180) with homology with *FLA-8* was expressed at higher levels in high milling wheats at 30 DPA. Tis transcript was 4.5 fold up-regulated in the HM-group. Te top two up-regulated gene indices at 14 DPA, TC400069 and TC414662, at a fold change of 20.78 and 10.70 were annotated as Trypsin alpha amylase inhibitor and as wheat Serpin 3 gene, respectively. Te top two gene indices at 30 DPA, TC373342 and CJ624986, at a fold change of 9.3 and 45.3 respectively, were annotated as uncharacterized protein from *Triticum urartu* and as 40 s ribosomal protein from *Aegilops tauschii*.

Te expression of GH726097 and TC379245 was signifcantly up-regulated in all of the genotypes of the PM-group but signifcantly down-regulated in all genotypes from HM-group except for the genotype Jing Hong No.1. Jing Hong No.1 showed very high up-regulation of β-amylase genes (TAGI; CA713114 and CA717472) compared with all other good millers. CA713114 and CA717472 were 707 and 80 fold up-regulated in Jing Hong No.1.

### **Discussion**

Variation in the gene pool provides the resource for breeding improved genotype[s17.](#page-9-14) Te relationship between gene expression and four yield in milling was analysed for 30 diverse wheat genotypes. Gene expression varies throughout seed developmen[t21](#page-10-2) with genes expressed at diferent stages contributing to the fnal composition and properties of the grain. Much of the variation in hardness of the wheat genotypes was explained by diferences in *Pin* genes[18](#page-9-15) and these loci have been associated with four yield variation in some germplasm[8](#page-9-5) but hardness did not explain most of the variation in milling performance (Fig. [1](#page-3-0)).

Down regulation of expression of transcripts (TAGI; TC379245 and GH726097) encoding cell adhesion proteins, *FLA-8*, was found at the critical mid grain development stage (14 DPA) and also at a later stage, close to maturity (30 DPA) in genotypes that gave high four yield (Table [2\)](#page-2-0). Reduced expression of these genes may be associated with a reduction in the structural strength of the grain. Tese changes are likely to result in a grain that breaks up more easily in the mill and yields greater quantities of four.

Te arabinogalactan proteins (AGPs) are highly glycosylated proteins that are rich in hydroxyproline and found in plant cell wall and plasma membrane[22.](#page-10-3) AGPs that contain putative cell adhesion domain known as fasciclin domain are known as fasciclin like arabinogalactan proteins. FLA has been implicated in cell adhesion and may link the cell membrane and cell wall. AGPs carry glycosylphosphotidylinositol (GPI) anchor at the C-terminus which signals the peptide to the plasma membrane. However, wheat *FLA-8* has a shorter GPI-anchor which raises a question about its binding to plasma membrane[19.](#page-10-0) But it might have a role in cell to cell and cell to extracellular environment interaction as suggested by Faik *et al*. [19](#page-10-0). Modifcation of a *FLA* in poplar[23](#page-10-4) has been shown to reduce the mechanical strength of plant tissue[s24](#page-10-5)[,25](#page-10-6). Human selection for higher four yield has apparently resulted in the selection of wheat genotypes with greatly reduced levels of expression of this gene. Nanospherical arabinoxylan proteins have been recently identifed as the adhesive component secreted by the climbing plant, English ivy[26.](#page-10-7)

A third transcript (TAGI; TC429180) with homology with *FLA-8* was expressed at higher levels in high milling wheats at 30 DPA. Te tissue specifcity of expression is not known in this system but varies signifcantly in other plants[25](#page-10-6). Most gene expression at 30 DPA may be in the embryo as the endosperm is terminally diferentiated by this stage.

Recently Wilkinson *et al*. [27](#page-10-8) (2017) reported that the product of the granule sofness protein gene was an AGP. Tis would complicate any attempts to relate FLA levels to milling yield. Methods for distinguishing the FLAs from other AGPs would be required. Hard wheats are generally considered to be better milling wheat[s14](#page-9-11) and a reduced expression of AGP encoded by the *Gsp-1* gene at the hardness locus might contribute to improved four yield. However this study showed that four yield was not associated with the *pin* genotype at the hardness locus (Fig. [1](#page-3-0)).

*FLA-8* related genes were found to be located on chromosome 2BS and 4BS in the Chinese Spring wheat genome assembly with limited annotation to date ([http://www.wheatgenome.org/\)](http://www.wheatgenome.org/). A major QTL for four yield has been reported from chromosome 2[B7](#page-9-16),[12](#page-9-9). Te QTL on chromosome 2BS had the highest overall LOD score (13.1) in analysis of the progeny of a Sunco X Tasman cross, explaining 14.4–31.3% of the variation in four yield at diferent site[s7](#page-9-16) . Sunco was shown to have low expression of the *FLA* gene in the current study and was the source of the high four yield in the Sunco X Tasman cross[7](#page-9-16) . A QTL on chromosome 4B was the second most signifcant four yield QTL in this cross. However in another cross, Katepwa X CD87, the 4B QTL was the most important and explained 13.6–23.8% of variation in four yield. Te coincidence of the structural genes encoding FLA and the chromosomal locations of the major know QTLs for milling yield in these crosses on chromosomes 4B and 2A provides strong confrmation of the signifcance of these associations.

Te strength of the endosperm may be infuenced by properties of the protein bodies and starch granule[s28](#page-10-9) that fll the endosperm cells and account for most of the grain content. Te expression of storage protein genes was signifcantly altered in wheat with high four yield. Several genes may contribute to changes in protein synthesis in the better milling wheat genotypes. Te expression of 40 S ribosomal protein was signifcantly higher in the better milling wheat genotypes at both 14 and 30 DPA (Tables [2](#page-2-0) and [3\)](#page-2-1). Tis protein may regulate protein synthesis and down regulation of this gene has been reported in a late ripening citrus genotyp[e29.](#page-10-10) Selection for protein composition that better suits end uses such as bread making may have been more intense in the development of genotypes also selected for milling quality.

Earlier studies have suggested that starch granule size distribution may be associated with four yield diference[s9](#page-9-6)[,14](#page-9-11). Tis suggests that modifcation of starch may contribute to four yield. Jing Hoang No.1 had a very high level of expression of β-amylase and was the only genotype giving high four extraction without signifcantly reduced expression of the *FLA-8* genes. High four yield in wheat may be the result of unintended human selection for diferent types of genetic variation in diferent genotypes. New deliberate combinations of these individual genes may have application in development of wheat genotypes with very high four yields. Other wheat is processed by directly grinding the grain. Tis type of four is used to make products such as chapatti. Wheat genotypes in regions where these products predominate may not have been subjected to selection for four yield in roller milling.

Te tissue specifcity[30](#page-10-11) of the genes identifed in this study may be important as changes in the expression of many of these genes may have been achieved by human selection for four yield without perturbing critical gene expression in other parts of the plant. Tese genes are specifcally expressed in the pericarp where they may infuence the adherence of the pericarp to the Aleurone/starchy endosperm. In hexaploid wheat modifcation of sub-genome specifc expression (in the A, B or D sub-genomes) may allow tissue specifc alteration of function. Recent analysis of expression of defence genes and response to pathogen infection has been shown to result in highly sub-genome specifc expression[31.](#page-10-12)

Analysis of the wheat grain transcriptome has identifed a gene controlling bread quality[32](#page-10-13) and new pathways for carbon assimilation[33](#page-10-14). Although the gene controlling bread quality has at least fve copies in the wheat genome with high sequence homology, the cause of the diferential expression of this gene was identifed to be the promoter region of a specifc allele of this gene. A simple PCR-test was develop which allowed the identifcation of wheat genotypes for the presence or absence of the specifc allele of gene controlling bread qualit[y32](#page-10-13) which has been used by CIMMY[T34.](#page-10-15) A similar strategy can be used to identify the presence or absence of the specifc FLA allele which corresponds to high FLA-8 expression and thereby select for wheat genotypes for good milling.

Selection for these genes and the four yield genes identifed in the present study provides a new set of tools for wheat breeders that should reduce the constraints of selection for wheat quality[35](#page-10-16) on the rate of genetic gain in wheat breeding and assist the selection of new genotypes to support the adaptation of wheat production to climate change[36](#page-10-17),[37](#page-10-18).

#### **Experimental Procedures**

**Plant Material.** Wheat grains of a worldwide set of wheat genotypes were sourced from Australian Winter Cereal Collection (AWCC), Tamworth, NSW, Australia; now known as Australian Grains Genebank (AGG), Horsham, Victoria, Australia [\(http://www.seedpartnership.org.au/associates/agg](http://www.seedpartnership.org.au/associates/agg)). Plants of all thirty wheat genotypes were grown under feld conditions at Narrabri (NSW, Australia) and harvested at maturity. Another trial was grown at Biloela (Qld, Australia). Te conditions at this site were harsher resulting in some varieties not performing well. Milling of these samples gave four yield data that showed a highly signifcant (P=0.01) correlation with that from the Narrabri site but the data was not included in the association analysis because it was considered a far less reliable measure of the four milling qualities of these genotypes when grown in unfavourable environments.

**Test milling and grain hardness.** Milling yield of harvested grain, identifed by as % of total four yield, was determined using a Buhler mill as explained by Edwards *et al*. [9](#page-9-6) . Wheat genotypes were grouped as poor milling (PM) wheats and high milling (HM) wheats if their milling yield was equal to/above or below 77%, respectively.

Grain hardness for all the genotypes was measured using Single Kernel Characterization System (SKCS) as described by Nirmal *et al*. [18.](#page-9-15) Puroindoline alleles in all the genotypes were also characterized as described in Nirmal *et al*. [18.](#page-9-15)

**RNA isolation, cDNA preparation and NGS sequencing.** Total RNA was isolated from the whole caryopsis at 14 days post anthesis (DPA) and at 30 dp[a38.](#page-10-19) cDNA was prepared and used to produce indexed Illumina NGS libraries which were then multiplexed to allow the sequencing of eight indexed libraries in one lane on a GA IIx Illumina sequencing platform to obtain 100 bp paired-end reads. RNA sequencing of the thirty wheat genotypes generated a total of ~2.5million to ~7.2million reads varying with the genotype.

**RNA-Seq analysis.** All NGS data was analysed using CLC Genomic Workbench Version 9.0 (CLC Bio, Qiagen). All the reads were trimmed for quality to exclude calls with Fred quality scores less than 20. Following trimming all sequence data was subjected to quality analysis. After quality check reads were subjected to RNA-seq analysis. Triticum aestivum gene index (TAGI) database ([fp://occams.dfci.harvard.edu/pub/bio/tgi/](ftp://occams.dfci.harvard.edu/pub/bio/tgi/data/Triticum_aestivum/) [data/Triticum\\_aestivum/\)](ftp://occams.dfci.harvard.edu/pub/bio/tgi/data/Triticum_aestivum/) which contains 221,925 tentative consensus sequences obtained from EST, cDNA and mRNA libraries was used as a reference database for RNA-seq analysis. Read alignment parameters length fraction and similarity fraction were set to 0.8 and 0.9, respectively. Paired reads were counted as two. Gene expression was normalised by calculating reads per kilo base per million mapped reads (RPKM).

Tissue specifcity of expression of *FLA* genes in wheat grain was investigated in transcriptome data available from published data[20](#page-10-1) for outer pericarp, inner pericarp, and endosperm tissues at 12 days-post-anthesis in developing wheat grains (cv. Holdfast) by RNA-Seq analyses using the TAGI reference in CLC genomics workbench. Te data included three biological replicates and since there is was no signifcant diference between the replicates the mean values are shown in Fig. [5.](#page-7-0)

**Identifcation of diferentially expressed genes.** RNA-seq data of the PM-group and the HM-group was subjected to proportion-based Baggerley's tes[t39](#page-10-20) within CLC workbench to identify diferentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the HM-group when compared to the PM-group. Diferentially expressed genes with FDR (false discovery rate) p-value of <0.01 were considered as statistically signifcant candidate genes associated. A further sorting of the DEGs was undertaken based on Baggerley's weighted proportion diference, to identify those genes with consistent expression diference in genotypes within the PM and HM wheat genotypes.

**Functional annotations.** Differentially expressed genes were functionally annotated using Blast2GO PRO[40.](#page-10-21)

**Data Availability Statement.** All NGS sequence data as raw data was submitted to NCBI at the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) and is available as SRA Submission # SUB2954840 (under BioProject# PRJNA392390 and BioSample #SUB2843912).
