### abstract

UV radiation is a ubiquitous component of solar radiation that affects plant growth and development. Here we studied growth related traits of 345 Arabidopsis thaliana accessions in response to UV radiation stress. We analyzed the genetic basis of this natural variation by genome-wide association studies, which suggested a specific candidate genomic region. RNA-sequencing of three sensitive and three resistant accessions combined with mutant analysis revealed five large effect genes. Mutations in PHE AMMONIA LYASE 1 (PAL1) and putative kinase At1g76360 rendered Arabidopsis hypersensitive to UV stress, while loss of function from putative methyltransferase At4g22530, NOVEL PLANT SNARE 12 (NPSN12) and defense gene ACTIVATED DISEASE RESISTANCE 2 (ADR2) conferred higher UV stress resistance. Three sensitive accessions showed strong ADR2 transcriptional activation, accumulation of salicylic acid (SA) and dwarf growth upon UV stress, while these phenotypes were much less affected in resistant plants. The phenotype of sensitive accessions resembles autoimmune reactions due to overexpression of defense related genes, and suggests that natural variation in response to UV radiation stress is driven by pathogen-like responses in Arabidopsis.

### 1. Introduction

Solar radiation is the primary source of energy for plants and an important environmental factor regulating plant life cycle and development ([Jiao et al., 2007; Amthor, 2010; Kami et al., 2010\)](#page-9-0). Photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) corresponds to wavelengths between 400 and 700 nm, while the ultraviolet (UV) radiation as the non-PAR component consists of wavelengths from 100 to 400 nm. The UV-C fraction (100e280 nm) is fully filtered by the atmosphere, but approximately 2% of UV-B (280e315 nm) and 95% of UV-A (315e400 nm) reach earth's surface [\(Jenkins and Brown,](#page-9-0) [2007\)](#page-9-0). UV-B radiation may have contrasting effects on plants ([Jenkins, 2009](#page-9-0)). Low UV-B doses are sufficient to activate the UV-B photoreceptor UV-B RESISTANT 8 (UVR8) and thus trigger photomorphogenic UV-B responses ([Ulm et al., 2004; Favory et al., 2009;](#page-9-0) [Rizzini et al., 2011\)](#page-9-0). In contrast, higher UV-B doses cause UV radiation stress due to accumulation of DNA damage, oxidative stress, genome instability and activation of plant defense mechanisms ([Britt et al., 1993; Molinier et al., 2004; Kalbina and Strid, 2006;](#page-9-0)

Under natural conditions, the amount of UV-B radiation fluctuates depending on a number of factors such as latitude, altitude, surrounding vegetation, cloud-coverage or air humidity ([McKenzie](#page-9-0) [et al., 2007](#page-9-0)). Therefore, plants growing along specific gradients, e.g. altitude along mountain slopes, can be exposed to different doses of UV radiation [\(Murai et al., 2009](#page-9-0)). Analysis of natural variation in response to UV radiation revealed significant differences among natural accessions of Arabidopsis thaliana ([Torabinejad and](#page-9-0) [Caldwell, 2000; Cooley et al., 2001; Kalbina and Strid, 2006;](#page-9-0) [Conte et al., 2010; Jansen et al., 2010\)](#page-9-0). The negative growth effects of UV radiation were more apparent for fast growing accessions [\(Cooley et al., 2001\)](#page-9-0). In a set of different accessions, UV negative growth effects could partially be suppressed in F1 hybrids, indicating potential for increasing plant UV resistance by specific genotype combinations [\(Torabinejad and Caldwell, 2000\)](#page-9-0), but there was no correlation between UV resistance and geography among 224 analyzed A. thaliana accessions ([Jansen et al., 2010\)](#page-9-0).

While there have been multiple genes associated with UV-B responses based on genetic experiments ([Britt et al., 1993; Li](#page-9-0) [et al., 1993; Favory et al., 2009; Radziejwoski et al., 2011](#page-9-0)), only few studies exist addressing the genetic basis of natural variation to UV radiation. Hence, the presence of natural variation in response to UV radiation has been found in a number of studies but its genetic and molecular nature remains largely unknown.

We have analyzed natural variation of 345 A. thaliana accessions in response to UV radiation stress. Besides the moderately affected accessions, there were also several highly sensitive and resistant accessions. In order to understand genetic basis of this sensitivity, we first employed genome-wide association mapping. This revealed a single genomic region, supported by multiple significantly associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), which contained a structurally complex resistance gene cluster with one putative candidate gene. RNA-sequencing of three UV hypersensitive and three resistant accessions revealed massive genome-wide transcriptional reprogramming in response to UV radiation stress and suggested common sets of significantly up- and downregulated genes specific for hypersensitive and resistant accessions. Two candidates genes were confirmed as positive and three as negative regulators of plant growth in response to UV irradiation stress.

### 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Plant material, growth conditions, UV treatments and phenotypic measurements

A. thaliana accessions and mutant lines were obtained from the Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Center. Accession numbers are listed in the Supplementary Table 1. Seeds were sown in soil, stratified for 4 days at 4 C and transferred to a greenhouse (accession screens) or a growth chamber (mutant screens) with the long day conditions (day: 16 h, 6 am to 10 pm, light intensity ~150 mmol m<sup>2</sup> s 1 , 22 C; night: 8 h, no light, 18 C) and 50% relative humidity. UV treatments were carried out using four Philips TL40 W/12RS SLV UV fluorescent tubes (fluence rate at 312 nm: 4.35 W/m2 at the plant level) without filtering and dimming. Therefore, our irradiation regime corresponds to high doses of UV-A, UV-B and small amount of UV-C radiation. The distance between plants and the lamps was 150 cm. For phenotyping, plants were UV irradiated from day 14 after germination for 30 min for 10 consecutive days. The UV treatment was started 6 h 15 min after the beginning of the chamber day. Control plants were grown in a UV free chamber section.

For leaf area and leaf length phenotypes, plants were photographed from above (Alpha DLSR camera; Sony) and the color photos were transformed into binary mode with ImageJ. Binary images were calibrated using an internal reference and the number of pixels corresponding to leaves was quantified and recalculated as mm<sup>2</sup> and relative change after UV treatment was calculated with respect to mock-treated control. Fresh weight was quantified using entire dissected aerial parts. The measurements have been performed on 3e4 individuals per one biological replicate.

All T-DNA insertional mutants were in Col-0 background and were obtained from the Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Centre (NASC) and are listed in Supplementary Table 2. The homozygous mutants were used for experiments (Supplementary Table 2).

### 2.2. SA measurements

SA content was measured as described [\(Bednarek et al., 2005\)](#page-9-0). 100e200 mg of fresh leaf tissue was extracted with aqueous methanol, solvents were evaporated by vacuum-drying and concentrated samples were dissolved in 0.1 M sodium acetate. SA was then extracted three times with ethylacetate, the extracts were combined and vacuum-dried. Finally, the SA sample was dissolved in aqueous methanol and analyzed on an Agilent 1100 high pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC) system. For the release of conjugated SA, the samples were incubated with b-glucosidase (100 U/ml) prior to extraction with ethylacetate.

### 2.3. Nucleic acids isolation

Extraction of genomic DNA for genotyping, was carried out with the Biosprint96 robot using Biosprint96 DNA Plant kit (Qiagen). Plasmid-DNA was extracted with the NucleoSpin Plasmid kit (Macherey-Nagel). RNA extraction was performed using the RNeasy Plant Mini kit (Qiagen) with an on-column DNase I digestion (Roche).

### 2.4. PCR, product purification and single read sequencing

PCR reactions were carried out under standard conditions. Primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 3. For cloning, PCR products were cut out of gel and purified using the Nucleospin Gel and PCR kit (Macherey-Nagel). Single read sequencing was carried out on an 3730XL Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems) using the BigDye-terminator chemistry (Life Technologies). Reads were analyzed using LaserGene software.

### 2.5. Transcriptional analysis by reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and RNA-sequencing

For single gene transcriptional analysis, 1 mg total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (ThemoScientific). RT-qPCR was carried out by using the SensiMix SYBR & Fluorescein kit (peqLab) on an EP Realplex instrument (Eppendorf). Transcription fold changes were calculated with the standard curve method and normalized to ACTIN 7 (At5g09810).

For whole transcriptome sequencing, 1 mg total RNA with RNA integrity number (RIN) higher than 8.6 on Bioanalyzer (Agilent) was used. The sequencing libraries were prepared with the TruSeq RNA Kit (Illumina). Library insert size and integrity was analyzed on the Bioanalyzer with the DNA 1000 kit (Agilent) and the library concentration was determined by Qubit fluorometer (Life Technologies). The libraries were sequenced as 100 bp single end reads with a HiSeq2500 instrument (Illumina), and an average sequencing depth of 15 million reads. The raw reads were quality controlled with fastQC and trimmed in the FASTX-tool kit using the default parameters. The samples were mapped to accessionspecific (pseudo)reference genomes using TopHat2 and Bowtie2, allowing for 2 mismatches by default. Accession-specific (pseudo) reference were created by incorporating available SNP information into the Col-0 reference genome [\(http://1001genomes.org/](http://1001genomes.org/)). The number of mapped reads was estimated with QualiMap. Differential gene expression analysis was performed in R, using the DEseq package ([Anders and Huber, 2010](#page-8-0)).

Short read files and read counts per gene for all RNA-sequencing samples were deposited at the NCBI Sequence Read Archive [\(http://](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) [www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra)) with the code GSE64870.

### 2.6. Genome wide association (GWA) studies

GWA studies were performed by using the EMMA-X tool kit with default parameters ([Kang et al., 2010](#page-9-0)). Analysis and data visualization were done with R.

### 3. Results

3.1. Arabidopsis accessions show an extensive natural variation in response UV irradiation stress

In order to reveal Arabidopsis natural variation in response to UV radiation stress, 14-days-old naïve plants of 345 accessions from a worldwide sample, were grown for 10 consecutive days under UV free (mock) or high UV radiation conditions. This was expected to induce UV stress and cause damage to plant tissues [\(Kalbina and](#page-9-0) [Strid, 2006; Jenkins, 2009; Hideg et al., 2013\)](#page-9-0). Subsequently, three growth related traits: leaf length (defined as median of the five longest leaves), leaf area and fresh aerial biomass were scored. The values of UV-irradiated plants were normalized by mock-treated controls and yielded relative changes for each phenotype caused by UV irradiation stress. The results indicated a wide range of responses from slightly enhanced growth under UV up to a strong reduction for each of the traits (relative leaf length: 40%e97%, relative leaf area: 19%e123% and relative biomass: 22%e152%; calculated as percentage of UV-treated versus mock-treated; Fig. 1AeC and Supplementary Table 1). The three highly sensitive accessions: T1080, C24, Ba-1 and three resistant accessions: Lip-0, Got-7, Mh-0 were selected as representatives of each group and compared with the moderately sensitive Col-0 reference strain (Fig. 1D). Responses of individual accessions across the traits were highly and significantly correlated (Pearson correlation r <sup>¼</sup> 0.76 to 0.86, P < 0.05; Fig. 1E). However, we did not observe correlation between UV growth responses and the geographic origin of the accessions - neither in a latitudinal nor in a longitudinal trend (Supplementary Table 4), which is in agreement with previous data ([Jansen et al., 2010\)](#page-9-0).

Hence, Arabidopsis accessions show natural variation in UV-

induced growth suppression.

### 3.2. GWA studies indicated RECEPTOR-LIKE PROTEIN 7 locus as a major association region for natural variation to UV stress

All 345 accessions used in our study were previously genotyped with the 250k SNP Affymetrix chip array [\(Kim et al., 2007; Atwell et al., 2010; Li](#page-9-0) [et al., 2010\)](#page-9-0), and we identified a common set of 214,051 useful markers for our GWA. This corresponded to approximately one marker per 500 bp and should allow high-resolution association mapping [\(Atwell et al., 2010\)](#page-8-0). We performed mapping using a Wilcoxon linear model and the efficient mixed model approach X (EMMAX) designed to correct for population structure. Both methods revealed almost identical results, indicating that population structure did not play an important role in the given phenotype dataset.

There were no significantly associated SNPs for the relative biomass and relative leaf length traits ([Fig. 2](#page-3-0)B and C). In contrast, several significantly associated SNPs (Bonferroni corrected test, P < 0.05) grouping into two genomic regions occurred for the relative leaf area phenotype [\(Fig. 2](#page-3-0)A). # 3.3. RNA-sequencing of natural strains revealed novel candidate genes involved in UV radiation stress responses

Many genes involved in UV radiation responses show high amplitude transcriptional changes (Ulm et al., 2004; Favory et al., 2009). This prompted us to analyze transcriptional changes in response to UV irradiation stress by RNA-sequencing in three sensitive (T1080, C24, Ba-1) and three resistant (Lip-0, Got-7, Mh-0) accessions. We irradiated 14-days-old naïve seedlings by mock and standard 30 min UV treatment and harvested samples after 3 h of recovery at UV free conditions, which was shown to yield a high number of up- and down-regulated genes (Kilian et al., 2007). The data were processed using a custom-made analysis protocol and statistical significance was analyzed with DESeq package in R (for details see Materials and methods). This confirmed an activation of wide range of UV-responsive genes including photomorphogenic factors (HY5 and TT4) and UV stress factors (PR5, WRKY30; Supplementary Table 7). In order to identify common patterns of transcription among sensitive versus insensitive accessions, we performed an iterative analysis. Firstly, the UV-treated samples were compared to mock-treated ones for each accession to identify significantly (adjusted P-value < 0.05) up- and down-regulated genes. Secondly, the groups of significantly differentially transcribed genes were intersected with the other sensitive or resistant accessions, respectively. Thirdly, the lists of differentially regulated genes in all three sensitive accessions were compared to corresponding lists of resistant accessions. Of the total 24,185 genes accessible for our analysis, 18.8% and 18.9% (n = 4549 and n = 4583) of genes showed differential transcript amounts in all three sensitive and resistant accessions, respectively. However, only 3.4% (n = 821) and 3.5% (n = 855) of those were unique to sensitive and resistant accessions, respectively (Fig. 5A and B; Supplementary Table 8). The genes significantly up- and down-regulated in all sensitive accessions were further tested by analysis of loss-of-function mutants. In order to provide more specificity to this screen, we selected candidates with e.g. high amplitude fold-changes, transcriptional dependency on UVR8 and/or HY5 (Brown et al., 2005; Oravecz et al., 2006; Kilian et al., 2007; Lee et al., 2007; González Besteiro et al., 2011; Obayashi et al., 2011) and/or overlapping with lower association peaks in our GWA studies. Combination of these criteria together with mutant availability in major public collections reduced our screening population to 50 T-DNA insertion mutant lines, out of which 45 could be propagated and obtained as homozygous lines (Supplementary Table 2). These lines were grown under the standard UV stress conditions and their leaf area and aerial biomass were scored. This revealed five mutants significantly (t-test, P < 0.05) different from Col-0 wild type control. UV treatment reduced leaf area of lines 32 (N65580) and 40 (N661248) to 68% and 60%, respectively (Fig. 5C). Line 32 was mutated in an uncharacterized protein kinase (At1g76360), which showed transcriptional up-regulation in response to several abiotic stresses and pathogen treatments (Kilian et al., 2007; Chandran et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2011). Line 40 carried a mutation in PHE AMMONIA LYASE (PAL1; At2g37040). This enzyme is catalyzing the initial step in the phenylpropanoid pathway and is important for production of many secondary metabolites (Huang et al., 2010). In contrast, UV stress treated mutant plants of lines 5 (N655074), 22 (N664298), and 25 (N660251) showed significantly increased leaf area (170%, 171% and 195%, respectively) and biomass (148%, 171% and 152%, respectively) relatively to mock-treated plants of the same genotype (Fig. 5C). Line 5 is a mutant in an uncharacterized S-adenosyl-L-methioninedependent methyltransferase (At4g22530). To separate cis and trans effects, we inspected genomic regions using information of the 1001 genomes project. None of the genes showed different alleles between sensitive- or resistant accessions, with the exception of ADR2. For ADR2, the locus sequence was provided for all sensitive accessions Ba-1, C24 and T1080, but fully missing in resistant accessions Got-7 and Mh-0 and in promoter of Lip-0 (Fig. 5D). Hence, resistant accessions may carry an inactive allele of ADR2, due to presence of specific sequence polymorphism(s). This corresponded well with the transcriptomics data because UV irradiated sensitive accessions showed 3.5e5.3-fold

ARD2 induction over mock, while it was only 0.2e0.9-fold for resistant accessions [\(Fig. 5](#page-6-0)E). Hence, four of the five genes seemed to be regulated in trans and only ADR2 in cis.

Previously, ADR2 was found to confer plant responses pathogen attack [\(Borhan et al., 2008; Aboul-Soud et al., 2009\)](#page-9-0). Pathogen( like) stress frequently leads to an accumulation of SA [\(Bednarek](#page-9-0) [et al., 2005\)](#page-9-0). Therefore, we measured whether UV stress without pathogen infection leads to differential accumulation of free and/or conjugated SA using HPLC in sensitive and resistant accessions. Under mock treatment conditions, there was less than 1 mg of total SA per 1 g of fresh weight in all accessions ([Fig. 5F](#page-6-0)). This remained the same for the resistant accessions Lip-0 and Mh-0 and moderately sensitive Col-0 after UV stress irradiation. In contrast, Ba-1, C24 and T1080 accumulated 5e23 mg of total SA per 1 g of fresh weight under the same treatment ([Fig. 5](#page-6-0)F). This suggests high SA accumulation in three UV stress sensitive and low in three UV stress resistant accessions.

### 4. Discussion

Here we analyzed natural variation of A. thaliana in response to repeated high doses of UV radiation. Based on earlier observations, using three Arabidopsis natural strains Col-0, C24 and Ws-2, this was expected to induce UV stress and cause damage to plant tissues ([Kalbina and Strid, 2006; Jenkins, 2009; Hideg et al., 2013\)](#page-9-0). Indeed, we observed a strong transcriptional activation of UV stress markers WRKY30 and PR5 in six accessions with contrasting phenotypes (Supplementary Table 7), indicating extensive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and activation of defense responses [\(Kalbina and Strid, 2006; Jenkins, 2009](#page-9-0)).

### 4.1. Natural variation in response to UV radiation stress occurs without correlation with geography

Under UV radiation stress, we analyzed growth related traits: leaf area, leaf length and fresh biomass of 345 worldwide collected Arabidopsis accessions. The accessions showed a variety of growth reduction with several lines being either greatly suppressed or enhanced by UV stress, which suggested extensive natural variation in response to this treatment. However, in agreement with previous analysis of low UV radiation effects ([Jansen et al., 2010\)](#page-9-0), we did not observe a significant correlation of this variation with latitude, representing a major geographical UV cline. Functional correlation between phenotypes, genotypes and locations has been observed for several major environmentally determined traits such as flowering time, freezing tolerance or sodium accumulation ([Stinchcombe et al., 2004; Baxter et al., 2010; Zuther et al., 2012\)](#page-9-0). However, finding clinal variation is not a general rule ([Trontin et al.,](#page-9-0) [2011; Weigel, 2012\)](#page-9-0). Hence, there is an extensive natural variation in response to UV radiation without an obvious correlation with geography.

### 4.2. RLP7 is the major candidate gene within the AR1 identified by GWA studies

So far, only a small number of studies focused on understanding the genetic basis of natural variation in response to UV radiation ([Conte et al., 2010\)](#page-9-0). Therefore, we used our dataset and searched for genetic factors responsible for UV stress natural variation by GWA studies. This method has been successfully used for rapid and high resolution mapping in Arabidopsis accessions [\(Atwell et al., 2010\)](#page-8-0). GWA revealed a single significant association region on chromosome 1 (AR1) for the leaf area trait and no significant associations were observed for other traits. Lack of a higher number of significantly associated regions could be due to complex genetic architecture of our phenotypes or population structure effects. In order to solve these issues, we employed statistical models that correct for population structure bias and also a multi-locus mix model approach that allows testing for effects of more than one locus [\(Atwell et al., 2010; Segura et al., 2012](#page-8-0)). Nevertheless, even this did not increase the number of significantly associated regions (Supplementary Fig. 2), suggesting presence of either several similar-effect-loci and/or multiple independent causal alleles for one gene. These factors may greatly reduce GWA sensitivity as shown on the example of the key flowering gene FRIGIDA [\(Atwell](#page-8-0) [et al., 2010; Segura et al., 2012](#page-8-0)).

The AR1 spanned over 30 kb and contained several candidate genes that were analyzed with respect to transcriptional responses, sequence polymorphisms in seven accessions commonly used throughout our study and mutant phenotypes. Most candidate genes showed no transcriptional changes in response to UV irradiation, with the exception of RPL7 that was strongly activated in a number of natural strains. In addition, RLP7 contained a specific set of DNA sequence polymorphisms including a 12 bp promoter deletion and a 4 bp CDS insertion that led to a frame shift and caused a premature stop codon. These polymorphisms occurred significantly more frequently in the sensitive accessions than resistant ones, suggesting RLP7 as the top candidate within the AR1 region. The molecular function of RLP7 is not known. In a comprehensive survey of Arabidopsis RLPs, it has been detected to be transcribed mainly in senescent tissues but did not show an obvious phenotype with respect to bacterial resistance ([Wang et al.,](#page-9-0) [2008](#page-9-0)). More recently, RLP7 was found among genes up-regulated in response to enhanced production of chloroplastic ROS [\(Maruta](#page-9-0) [et al., 2012\)](#page-9-0). In none of these studies, a specific RLP7 mutant phenotype could be found. This holds true also for our study and could potentially be due to functional redundancy of RLP7 with its paralog RLP6 [\(Wang et al., 2008\)](#page-9-0). Hence, validation of RLP7 function in the context of UV stress natural variation requires further testing.

### 4.3. RNA-sequencing revealed novel genes involved in UV stress responses

UV radiation has been shown to induce genome-wide transcriptional changes, which allowed the discovery or functional characterization of several genes involved in UV-specific responses ([Ulm et al., 2004; Brown and Jenkins, 2008; Favory et al., 2009;](#page-9-0) [Morales et al., 2013](#page-9-0)). In order to assess natural variation in transcriptional responses to UV radiation stress, we scored for changes in mRNA levels among three sensitive and three resistant accessions by RNA-sequencing. Since high quality genomic sequences of these strains were not available, we used publicly available information to modify sequence of Col-0 reference strain and thus created appropriate pseudo-reference genomes (Ganga Jeena and Jose Jim enez-G omez, unpublished data). This increased the map ping efficiency and provided more accurate results. UV stress had major impact on the plant transcriptome as 8000e10,000 genes per accession were significantly altered in their transcript levels. By iterative analysis we identified approximately 821 common genes significantly up- or down-regulated in all three sensitive accessions and tested UV stress phenotypes of mutants corresponding to 50 selected candidate genes. This revealed five cases with significantly different phenotypes.

The first group contained three mutants with enhanced resistance to UV radiation stress. The molecular functions of two, the methyltransferase (At4g22530) and NPSN12 (At1g48240), remain currently unknown. The third was ADR2 (At1g56510), a known TIR-NB-LRR class resistance gene ([Borhan et al., 2008; Aboul-Soud et al.,](#page-9-0) [2009](#page-9-0)), that was not previously associated with response to UV radiation. ADR2 mutants are hypersensitive to pathogen infection,

<span id="page-8-0"></span>while over-expressor lines exhibited stunted growth and increased disease resistance (Aboul-Soud et al., 2009). Previous experiments also indicated a role of natural variation in ADR2 in resistance to Albugo candida ([Borhan et al., 2008](#page-9-0)). Indeed, we have observed approximately 10-fold higher transcriptional activation of ADR2 in sensitive accessions and possibly partial or full loss of this gene in resistant accessions.

The other group contained two mutants hypersensitive to UV radiation stress. The first one is a putative kinase At1g76360 with currently unknown target proteins and the second is PAL1 (At2g37040). PAL1 is a well characterized member of functionally redundant PAL gene family involved in early steps of biosynthesis of many secondary metabolites. PAL multi-mutants lack protective pigments, including flavonols, and have been show as hypersensitive to UV radiation ([Huang et al., 2010](#page-9-0)). However, we observed a stronger up-regulation of PAL1 in sensitive accessions. This suggests that highly stressed accessions try to gain greater protection or compensate for lack of other protective mechanisms via increased secondary metabolite production. Hence, up-regulation of PAL1 seems to be an in trans effect reflecting overall plant physiological status.

Four out of five validated candidate genes derived from RNAsequencing were not associated with specific sensitive or resistant alleles, suggesting that they were controlled in trans by an unknown upstream component(s). This most likely hindered their detection in GWA mapping. The only gene controlled in cis was ADR2. However, even this candidate was not considered as candidate based on GWA mapping. We speculate that this could be due to high genetic diversity of the ADR2 locus with several major haplotypes (1001 Genomes; <http://1001genomes.org/>). It could be compared to the situation of the FRI locus, a major effect gene that is very difficult to be detected via association mapping due to multiple causal haplotypes (Atwell et al., 2010; Segura et al., 2012).

### 4.4. Pathogen-like defense mechanism contribute to natural variation in response to UV radiation stress

Upon irradiation with repeated high doses of UV, we have observed differences in growth, SA accumulation and transcriptional activation of pathogenesis related genes among Arabidopsis accessions. Furthermore, there was strong transcriptional upregulation of the ADR2 resistance gene in the three sensitive accessions and its absence in the resistant accessions. This is in agreement with previous reports suggesting that responses to high UV radiation resemble pathogen attack ([Nawrath et al., 2002; Kunz](#page-9-0) [et al., 2008](#page-9-0)). Here we show that pathogen-like defense responses are the major cause for Arabidopsis natural variation in response to UV stress. None of the candidates we found overlapped with UV perception, photomorphogenic signaling or repair of UV damaged DNA. Furthermore, neither GWA studies nor transcriptomics experiments revealed candidates from jasmonic acid (JA) signaling pathway that has been shown to play a role in UV-B photomorphogenic (low dose) responses ([Morales et al., 2013; Ballare, 2014](#page-9-0) ). This is most likely either due to relatively minor contribution of these pathways in response to UV stress and/or complex architecture of their natural variation. The UV-induced negative growth effects are more pronounced in sensitive accessions and resembled a typical immune over-response (reviewed in Alc azar et al., 2012).

Previously it has been shown that stress pulses or specific treatments can activate the defense machinery and thus prime plants for later higher stress dose [\(Conrath, 2011](#page-9-0)). Plants are exposed to UV radiation from germination onwards. It was shown that UV can prime naïve plants against plant pathogens by activating specific components of pathogen defense machinery and thus provide protection for at least some time [\(Kunz et al., 2008\)](#page-9-0). The priming functions for photomorphogenic (UVR8-dependent) and also UV stress (UVR8-independent) responses as both treatments reduced amount of subsequent pathogen infection [\(Kunz](#page-9-0) [et al., 2008; Demkura and Ballare, 2012](#page-9-0) ). Hence, there is a partially common processing of UV and pathogen stress signals and UV pulse may function as a pathogen-priming factor.

In contrast, prolonged activation of defense related genes leads to SA accumulation, stunted leaf growth and leaf necrosis (Aboul-Soud et al., 2009; Alcazar et al., 2012 ). This strongly resembles phenotypes of UV stress-sensitive accessions. Such autoimmune diseases have been provoked by e.g. hybridization of different accessions (reviewed in Alc azar et al., 2012). Our data show that strong autoimmune responses can be induced to differential degree by environmental factor in Arabidopsis accessions.

### 5. Conclusions

Here, we analyzed growth performance of Arabidopsis accessions in response to UV radiation stress. Our GWA studies revealed one candidate genomic region on chromosome 1. We considered RLP7 as the major candidate gene within this association region. The genome-wide transcriptional analysis revealed over 800 genes significantly up- or down-regulated in three sensitive but not three resistant accessions. Mutant analysis of 45 selected candidates identified five cases with altered sensitivity to UV radiation, including UV sensitive pal1 and UV resistant adr2, which were not associated with UV responses and/or natural variation. From the broader perspective, we showed that Arabidopsis natural variation in response to UV stress is mainly due to pathogen-like defense mechanisms and not the core UV signaling or DNA damage components.
