#### ABSTRACT

28 While substantial progress has been made in understanding defense responses of cereals to insect 29 herbivores, comparatively little is known about responses to feeding by spider mites. Nevertheless, 30 several spider mite species, including the generalist *Tetranychus urticae* and the grass specialist 31 *Oligonychus pratensis*, cause damage on cereals such as maize and wheat, especially during drought 32 stress. To understand defense responses of cereals to spider mites, we characterized the 33 transcriptomic responses of maize and barley to herbivory by both mite species, and included a 34 wounding control against which modulation of defenses could be tested. T. urticae and O. pratensis 35 induced highly correlated changes in gene expression on both maize and barley. Within 2 hours, 36 hundreds of genes were upregulated, and thousands of genes were up- or downregulated after 24 37 hours. In general, expression changes were similar to those induced by wounding, including for 38 genes associated with jasmonic acid biosynthesis and signaling. Many genes encoding proteins 39 involved in direct defenses, or those required for herbivore-induced plant volatiles, were strongly 40 upregulated in response to mite herbivory. Further, biosynthesis genes for benzoxazinoids, which are specialized compounds of Poaceae with known roles in deterring insect herbivores, were induced in 41 42 maize. Compared to chewing insects, spider mites are cell content feeders and cause grossly different 43 patterns of tissue damage. Nonetheless, the gene expression responses of maize to both mite 44 herbivores, including for phytohormone signaling pathways and for the synthesis of the benzoxazinoid 2-hydroxy-4,7-dimethoxy-1,4-benzoxazin-3-one glucoside (HDMBOA-Glc), a known 45 defensive metabolite against caterpillars, resembled those reported for a generalist chewing insect, 46 47 Spodoptera exigua. On maize plants harboring mutations in several benzoxazinoid biosynthesis 48 genes, T. urticae performance dramatically increased as compared to wildtype plants. In contrast, no 49 difference in performance was observed between mutant and wildtype plants for the specialist O. 50 pratensis. Collectively, our data provide little evidence that maize and barley defense responses 51 differentiate herbivory between T. urticae and O. pratensis. Further, our work suggests that the likely 52 route to specialization for O. pratensis involved the evolution of a robust mechanism to cope with the benzoxazinoid defenses of its cereal hosts. 53

# 56 **INTRODUCTION**

57 Cereal crops of the grass family (Poaceae) account for the majority of human calories, and reductions in their yield dramatically impact human welfare. Abiotic factors, such as drought, are a major source 58 59 of unrealized yield (Boyer, 1982), while another well-characterized source of loss is from herbivory by insects (Oerke, 2006). Spider mites (Acari: Tetranychidae) belong to the Chelicerata, an arthropod 60 lineage that diverged more than 450 million years ago (Dunlop, 2010), and hence evolved herbivory 61 independently from insects. Crops including maize (Zea mays) and wheat (Triticum sp.) are 62 63 susceptible not only to insects but also to spider mites, especially during drought conditions (Al-Kaisi et al., 2013), where yield losses as high as 47.2% for maize have been reported (Bacon et al., 1962). 64 Nevertheless, relatively little is known about the molecular nature of the defenses plants use to deter 65 66 spider mites, especially for grasses.

As shown by molecular studies of plant-herbivore interactions, largely with insects and dicots such as 67 Arabidopsis thaliana and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), many plants complement constitutive 68 defenses (like trichomes) with rapid, inducible ones that negatively impact herbivores (Howe and 69 70 Jander, 2008). For instance, herbivore-associated triggers like physical damage, oral secretions, or 71 frass, alone or in combination, lead to changes in the production of specialized metabolites or 72 defensive proteins that deter herbivores (Howe and Jander, 2008; Ray et al., 2015). In dicots, 73 molecular responses to insect herbivores are mediated largely by phytohormones, especially 74 jasmonates (jasmonic acid, or JA, and its derivatives or conjugates), which induce transcriptomic 75 reprogramming within hours (Howe and Jander, 2008). Some defenses act directly, such as toxic compounds or protease inhibitors that retard digestion in an herbivore's gut. Others act indirectly, 76 77 like plant volatiles, which can serve as olfactory cues for predators to locate herbivores at feeding 78 sites (Turlings and Erb, 2018).

79 The type and magnitude of inducible defenses is influenced by several factors. One of these is 80 feeding guild. Chewing insects like caterpillars, for instance, cause extensive tissue damage and elicit 81 different defense responses compared to phloem-feeding insects like aphids, which cause minimal 82 loss of plant tissue (Howe and Jander, 2008). Additionally, plant responses to generalist herbivores, 83 to which ~10% of plant-feeding insects belong (Ali and Agrawal, 2012), can differ from those 84 induced by specialists. Generalist herbivores feed on hosts in many plant families. They are typically 85 thought to rely on broad detoxification capabilities to overcome the challenges they encounter on 86 phylogenetically (and chemically) divergent plant hosts (Dermauw et al., 2013), or to potentially 87 suppress plant defense responses that are broadly conserved (Ali and Agrawal, 2012). Alternatively, some specialists have evolved the ability to suppress or otherwise circumvent plant defenses, 88 89 potentially ameliorating the role of detoxification, or instead have evolved highly specialized detoxification abilities to cope with the toxins they encounter in their preferred plant hosts (Dobler et 90 91 al., 2012; Glas et al., 2014; Maag et al., 2014; Wouters et al., 2014).

Like dicots, monocots, including grasses, are attacked by generalist and specialist herbivores of 92 93 diverse feeding guilds, including leaf-chewing (e.g., caterpillars) and piercing-sucking (e.g., aphids and whiteflies). As for dicots, JA signaling and the production of specialized compounds feature 94 95 prominently in monocot responses to insect herbivory (Meihls et al., 2012; Tzin et al., 2015a, 2017). 96 Of the downstream specialized compounds in grasses, the best studied are benzoxazinoids, which are 97 1,4-benzoxazin-3-one derivatives produced by cereals including maize, wheat, and rye (Niemeyer, 2009; Zúñiga et al., 1983). In maize, levels of benzoxazinoids are highest in seedlings (Cambier et 98 99 al., 2000), but can be locally induced at feeding sites in the leaves of older plants (Köhler et al., 2015; 100 Maag et al., 2016). The most studied benzoxazinoid, 4-dihydroxy-7-methoxy-1,4-benzoxazin-3-one

101 (DIMBOA), is stored in vacuoles as an inactive glucoside (Glc) conjugate. Upon tissue damage by

102 herbivores, DIMBOA-Glc, as well as derivatives such as 2-hydroxy-4,7-dimethoxy-1,4-benzoxazin-

103 3-one glucoside (HDMBOA-Glc), are exposed to glucosidases in plastids (Meihls et al., 2012). This 104 leads to the release of the aglucones, which are toxic to herbivores, potentially by several modes of

104 leads to the release of the aglucones, which are toxic to herbiv105 action (Wouters et al., 2016).

106 Several spider mite species are significant field pests on cereals. These include *Tetranychus urticae* 107 (the two-spotted spider mite) on maize, and Oligonychus pratensis (the Banks grass mite) on both 108 maize and distant relatives including wheat (Figure 1A) (Archer and Bynum, 1993; Blasi et al., 109 2015; Brandenburg and Kennedy, 1982; Mansour and Bar-Zur, 1992; Tadmor et al., 1999). T. urticae is an extreme generalist that has been documented on more than 100 plant families (Grbić et al., 110 111 2011). In contrast, O. pratensis is a specialist on plants in the Poaceae, though it has been reported on 112 a few non-grass hosts including date palm (which is also a monocot) (Archer and Bynum, 1993; 113 Bynum et al., 2015; Chandler et al., 1979; Foster et al., 1977; Holtzer et al., 1984; Negm et al., 2015; 114 Ward et al., 1972). As cell-content feeders, spider mites belong to a different feeding guild than the 115 best studied insect herbivores (Bensoussan et al., 2016). Currently, knowledge of plant responses to 116 spider mites comes mainly from A. thaliana, tomato, and grapevine (Vitis vinifera), where T. urticae 117 feeding was shown to induce robust JA responses (Díaz-Riquelme et al., 2016; Martel et al., 2015; 118 Zhurov et al., 2014). This suggests that T. urticae relies in large part on a broad detoxification 119 capacity to enable its exceptionally wide host range. Nonetheless, the sister species T. evansi, a 120 specialist of plants in the Solanaceae, has been documented to suppress plant defenses in tomato 121 (Alba et al., 2015). Further, another specialist mite herbivore of tomato, Aculops lycopersici, is able 122 to potently suppress plant defenses (Glas et al., 2014). While O. pratensis is a pest on diverse cereal 123 crops like maize and wheat, little is known about its ancestral host range within Poaceae. Whether the 124 grass specialist O. pratensis relies on detoxification, suppression of plant defenses, or both, to 125 colonize its Poaceae hosts is unknown.

In this study, we have characterized defense responses to herbivory by *O. pratensis* and *T. urticae* in grasses. As a plant host, we chose maize, for which defense responses to insects have been comparatively well studied (Meihls et al., 2012; Tzin et al., 2015a, 2017). We also included barley (*Hordeum vulgare*) as it is a close relative of wheat with a more tractable genome (Mayer et al., 2012). By including both maize and barley, we were able to compare variation in plant defense responses to both mite species in each of two major cereal crops in phylogenetically distant lineages within Poaceae (subfamilies Panicoideae and Pooideae, respectively).

#### 163 **Quantification of feeding damage**

To assess the extent of foliar damage caused by mite feeding, mites of each species were placed on 164 165 either the fifth leaves of 30-day-old barley plants or on the fourth leaves of 22-day-old maize plants. The studies for barley and maize were performed in a walk-in growth chamber or a greenhouse. 166 167 respectively, under the conditions described above. Briefly, barley and maize seeds were sown 1-2 168 cm deep in  $5 \times 5$  cm plastic pots in Metro-Mix® 900 growing mix. Ten days after sowing, seedlings 169 were transplanted into 20 cm diameter pots. As the maize plants were grown in a greenhouse bay, 170 plants were maintained inside insect-free enclosures until the day before the experiment to exclude 171 potential greenhouse pests (insect enclosures were constructed with PVC piping and No-See-Um 172 nylon netting, BioQuip Products, California, USA, catalog number 7250NSB). To minimize the 173 impact of environmental variation, a randomized block design was employed.

174 While mites cannot fly, they can disperse quickly by crawling on leaves. Therefore, for both control 175 plants and those used for mite infestations, we established leaf enclosures using non-phytotoxic wax 176 barriers that mites do not cross, an approach previously employed in studies with both mite species 177 (Feese and Wilde, 1977; Mansour, F. and Bar-Zur, A., 1992). For our study, we used Tree Tanglefoot wax (The Scotts Miracle-Gro Company, Ohio, USA), with two barriers established perpendicular to 178 179 the length of the leaf blades (Tanglefoot was placed in stripes across the adaxial and abaxial surfaces 180 of leaves and joined at the edges; Supplementary Figure 1). Mites added between the two barriers 181 were thus free to move along and between the leaf surfaces internal to the Tanglefoot boundaries. Maize leaves are well separated, and hence enclosures were easily established on free-standing 182 183 plants. However, the plant architecture of barley presented an additional challenge, necessitating 184 immobilization of leaf-harboring enclosures as shown in Supplementary Figure 1.

Twenty-four hours after establishing 12.5 cm Tanglefoot enclosures, 250 and 350 female mites were used to infest barley and maize leaves, respectively; as maize leaves are wider, more mites were added to keep mite density roughly constant (no mites were added to enclosures on the control plants). Adult mites of both species, which are only ~0.6 mm in length (**Figure 1A**, insets), were

189 collected under dissection microscopes from the bulk populations maintained on the respective hosts.

197 At 24 hours post-infestation, leaf enclosures were harvested by cutting across the leaf blade 198 immediately internal to the Tanglefoot barriers. Both sides of each leaf segment were then scanned at 199 1200 dpi using an Epson Perfection V550 Photo scanner (Epson, Suwa, Japan). To ensure the mite 200 damage was quantified from leaf segments of exactly the same length, the leaf segments were 201 immobilized to a frame to reveal the middle 7.5 cm and 10 cm for barley and maize enclosures, 202 respectively, before scanning. Barley leaf segments were sufficiently flat to be scanned without 203 additional manipulation. #### 224 Collection of plant tissue for transcriptomic studies

225 Plant leaf tissue was collected from barley (Morex) and maize (B73) plants with the following 226 treatments: (1) control (uninfested leaves), (2) wounding, (3) T. urticae infestation, and (4) O. pratensis infestation. As a time course, we selected 2- and 24-hour time points because dynamic 227 228 expression changes in defense genes in response to T. urticae were observed in the dicots A. thaliana, tomato and grapevine within 24 hours (Díaz-Riquelme et al., 2016; Martel et al., 2015; Zhurov et al., 229 230 2014). Further, in maize, time points for analogous studies with insects from several feeding guilds 231 were similar, and included 24-hour time points (Tzin et al., 2015a, 2017). The experimental design 232 employed for assessing transcriptomic responses was similar to that for assessing quantification of

233 feeding damage with several modifications. For barley, 30-day-old plants were used with enclosures 234 of length 10 cm on the fifth leaves, and for maize, 22-day-old plants were used with enclosures of 235 length 9 cm on the fourth leaves (mite density was therefore held roughly constant). A randomized 236 block design was used to minimize environmental variation. For the wounding treatments, plant 237 tissue in enclosures was gently pressed between two pieces of 60 grit sandpaper (particle size ~260 238 um: the blades on each side of the midrib were wounded along the lengths of the enclosed leaf 239 segments). For mite infestation, 400 and 200 adult female mites were used per leaf enclosure for the 240 2- and 24-hour time points, respectively. Briefly, because it takes tens of minutes to several hours for 241 released mites to fully untangle themselves, disperse and start feeding, the density of applied mites 242 was doubled for the 2-hour time point to ensure feeding by 2 hours. For RNA preparation, we 243 collected tissue from within the enclosures as a major component of induced plant defense responses 244 is local (Maag et al., 2016; Tzin et al., 2017). Following best practices in the field (Tzin et al., 2015a, 245 2017), mite infestation and wounding treatments were staggered prior to a single collection time 246 (Supplementary Figure 3). In all cases, a biological replicate consisted of the entire leaf enclosure 247 from a single leaf. To minimize the effects of the circadian cycle, for both the barley and maize 248 experiments all samples were collected within a 15-minute time window 3 hours into the 16-hour 249 light period.

### 250 RNA isolation, RNA-seq library construction and sequencing

251 Total RNA was prepared from leaf material from Tanglefoot enclosures with the DirectZol RNA 252 extraction kit (Zymo Research, California, USA). Barcoded RNA-seq libraries were constructed at 253 the High Throughput Genomics Core Facility (University of Utah) using the Illumina (Illumina, 254 California, USA) TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Preparation Kit with poly(A) selection, and 125 255 bp paired-end reads were generated on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencer with HiSeq SBS Kit v4 256 sequencing reagents. Briefly, four lanes were run in total for each of the barley and maize 257 experiments, each consisting of 28 samples (four replicates each for the control, wounding at 2 and 258 24 hours, T. urticae infestation at 2 and 24 hours, and O. pratensis infestation at 2 and 24 hours). 259 Biological replicates were evenly distributed across the four sequencing lanes to reduce the 260 possibility of confounding due to lane-level effects.

# 261 Detection of differentially expressed genes

The barley genome version ASM3268v1 (Release 33) (Mayer et al., 2012) and the maize genome 262 263 version AGPv3 (Release 31) (Schnable et al., 2009) were downloaded from Ensembl (Yates et al., 264 2016). To examine expression of benzoxazinoid biosynthesis genes in maize, the gene model for BX7 265 (GRMZM2G441753) was taken from an earlier annotation (Ensembl Release 20), as it was 266 considered a low-confidence model and not included in the newer AGPv3 release. RNA-seq reads 267 were aligned to their respective genomes using the two-pass alignment mode of STAR 2.5.2b (Dobin 268 et al., 2013) with a maximum intron size of 20 kb. The number of reads uniquely aligned to each 269 locus was counted using HTSeq 0.6.0 (Anders et al., 2015) with --stranded reverse and --feature 270 transcript. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected using the DESeq2 package (version 271 1.14.0) (Love et al., 2014) with a false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted *P*-value of 0.01 and an absolute 272 value log<sub>2</sub> fold change cut off of 1. Fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads 273 (FPKM) (Mortazavi et al., 2008; Trapnell et al., 2010) values were calculated with Python scripts 274 using the BCBio (https://github.com/chapmanb/bcbio-nextgen) GFF parser.

## 275 Cluster analyses

276 Independently for each of the maize and barley time course RNA-seq data sets, gene-level k-means 277 clustering was performed on the set of genes that was differentially expressed in at least one 278 treatment. Briefly, the log<sub>2</sub> fold change estimates of each gene across treatments compared to the 279 control were used as the input to the *kmeans* function of the base "stat" package in R with the default 280 Hartigan and Wong algorithm. Different k numbers were tested with k = 6 chosen as it resulted in 281 clusters with distinct expression profiles across the treatments. The *kmeans* function was run with 282 100 iterations (nstart = 100) of the algorithm to optimize the clustering output. To compare the 283 expression profiles across host species, the centers of the barley and maize gene k-means clusters 284 were subjected to hierarchical clustering using the *hclust* function in R (distance: squared Euclidean; 285 linkage: complete). While the k-means clusters were numbered arbitrarily by the kmeans function, 286 barley and maize gene clusters with similar expression patterns identified by hierarchical clustering 287 were manually renumbered for simplicity of comparison.

#### 288 Gene ontology annotations and gene set enrichment analysis

Gene ontology (GO) annotations of the barley genome version ASM3268v1 (Release 33) and maize genome version AGPv3 (Release 31) were obtained by parsing the respective EMBL flat files provided by Ensembl (Biopython package SeqIO, version 1.69; Cock et al., 2009). The GO annotations were used to classify gene sets using the biological process (BP) and molecular function (MF) ontologies. The MF ontology was used to identify genes encoding defensive proteins and enzymes (i.e., protease inhibitors, chitinases, and terpene synthases).

295 Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the BP ontology. To enable a comparison of gene 296 set enrichments between barley and maize gene sets, we modified the BP ontology annotation of each 297 species so that one-to-one orthologs between barley and maize were associated with the same GO terms. The one-to-one orthologs were determined by reciprocal BLASTP (BLAST+ version 2.5.0+; 298 Camacho et al., 2009) searches with an E-value cut-off  $< 10^{-5}$ . The search was limited to the longest 299 300 isoform of each protein. For each gene with a best reciprocal BLASTP hit, the list of BP ontology 301 terms was updated to contain the union of annotated BP ontology terms from the barley and maize 302 orthologs. Gene set enrichment analyses were performed using the "weight01" algorithm with 303 Fisher's test statistic as implemented in the topGO 2.32.0 package (Alexa et al., 2006).

#### **304** Hormonometer analyses

305 We adapted the Hormonometer tool (Volodarsky et al., 2009) to assess plant hormone signaling in 306 barley and maize following the approach used by Tzin et al. (2015a, 2017) in studies of maize 307 responses to herbivory by insects. This tool searches for the similarity of gene expression changes to 308 signatures of transcriptomic responses diagnostic for specific plant hormone signaling pathways. 309 Briefly, experimentally assessed reference expression data sets are available from A. thaliana, and 310 were generated by exogenous application of plant hormones (or hormone precursors) that induce JA, 311 salicylic acid (SA), ethylene, abscisic acid (ABA), brassinosteroid, cytokinin, auxin, and gibberellic 312 acid (GA) signaling (Volodarsky et al., 2009). As the Hormonometer tool was developed with A. 313 thaliana expression data, to use the tool with barley and maize we performed reciprocal BLASTP 314 searches with A. thaliana proteins to identify orthologs in both grass species. As a result, we identified 8236 barley genes and 8904 maize genes expressed in our study that had corresponding 315 316 Arabidopsis Probeset IDs (Supplementary Data Sheet 1 and Supplementary Data Sheet 2, 317 respectively), and we used these as input for the Hormonometer tool to assess the similarity of gene 318 sets induced by spider mite feeding and wounding to those induced by specific phytohormones.

#### 319 **Peroxidase activity assays**

320 Plants were grown and infested with mites as described for damage quantification. The leaf samples 321 were collected 24 hours after infestation and ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Peroxidase 322 activity in leaf samples was quantified with a microplate reader (Biotek EPOCH, Vermont, USA) as 323 described previously (Ramirez and Spears, 2014). Briefly, frozen leaf powder was thawed and 324 suspended in 1 mL of sodium phosphate buffer (0.05 M, pH 7.0). Leaf homogenates were 325 centrifuged for 12 minutes at 12,000 rpm to extract soluble leaf proteins in the supernatant. 326 Peroxidase activity was detected in soluble protein at 470 nm following the oxidation of guaiacol for 327 1 minute (Moran and Cipollini, 1999), and expressed as the change in absorbance per mg of total 328 protein. The impacts of the treatments on peroxidase activity were assessed by ANOVA factoring in 329 the block design; where significant effects were observed, Tukey's HSD tests were performed.

#### 330 Mite productivity on *bx* mutant plants

The number of eggs and viable mites (larvae, nymphs, and adults) found on each plant 346 was then subsequently counted under a dissecting microscope. The impact of maize genotype on mite 347 productivity by species per plant was assessed with ANOVA factoring in the block design. 348 Subsequent pairwise tests were performed with Tukey's HSD method.

#### 458 Genes for JA biosynthesis and signaling respond rapidly to mite herbivores and wounding

459 The GO enrichment analysis suggested a prominent role for phytohormone signaling in responses to 460 both mite herbivores and wounding. Therefore, we adapted the Hormonometer tool (Volodarsky et al., 2009) to relate mite- and wounding-induced changes in gene expression to those induced by 461 462 diverse phytohormones. As displayed in dendrograms for both plant hosts (Figure 6A,B), changes in 463 most phytohormone response pathways were readily apparent by 2 hours in response to herbivory by O. pratensis and T. urticae, as well as wounding. In general, patterns of up- or downregulation by 464 465 plant hormone treatments were similar between barley and maize, and at 24 hours responses were generally attenuated. Response genes for JA, SA, ABA, and auxin were most dramatically 466 467 upregulated, while response genes associated with ethylene, GA, cytokinin, and brassinosteroid 468 responses were either downregulated, variable, or largely unaffected.

469 Because of the strong JA responses, and functional-genetic studies demonstrating a role for JA 470 signaling in deterring T. urticae feeding on A. thaliana and tomato (Martel et al., 2015; Zhurov et al., 471 2014), we characterized transcriptomic responses of this pathway further. While the JA pathway is well characterized in dicots, less about it is known in monocots. However, candidate genes for JA 472 473 biosynthesis, signal transduction, and mediation of transcriptional changes have been identified in 474 maize (Borrego and Kolomiets, 2016), and genetic studies provide functional evidence for the 475 involvement of several of these genes in JA biosynthesis and signaling (Tzin et al., 2017; Yan et al., 476 2012). Initially, we focused our analysis on the JA pathway in maize (Figure 6C), describing general 477 patterns of responses to both mite species as induced gene expression is highly correlated between 478 them (Figure 3).

Lipoxygenases (LOXs) catalyze the production of oxylipins, which are involved in multiple aspects 479 480 of plant defense (Borrego and Kolomiets, 2016). The first step in the JA biosynthesis pathway is the production of the oxylipin 13(S)-hydroperoxylinolenate (13-HPOT) from  $\alpha$ -linolenic acid. Putative 481 482 lipoxygenases for catalyzing this reaction belong to the 13-LOX group, of which maize has five 483 (LOX7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 13) (Borrego and Kolomiets, 2016). Of these, all but LOX11 were induced at one 484 or more time points, albeit with modest fold changes. As assessed by normalized expression values 485 (FPKMs), basal levels of *LOX10* were ~90-fold higher than for other 13-LOX genes, consistent with 486 its role in the production of green leaf volatiles (Christensen et al., 2013), which are abundant in 487 grasses. The next two steps in JA synthesis involve the conversion of 13-HPOT to 12,13(S)-488 epoxylinolenic acid (12,13-EOT), followed by cyclization to yield 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid (12-489 OPDA) (Borrego and Kolomiets, 2016). Differential expression was also observed for putative genes 490 mediating these steps, including the upregulation of four ALLENE OXIDE SYNTHASE (AOS) genes 491 and two ALLENE OXIDE CYCLASE (AOC) genes, respectively. 493 included genes in the AOSI clade, but also genes in clade 2 for which a role in JA biosynthesis is 494 uncertain (Borrego and Kolomiets, 2016). # 610 **DISCUSSION**

Whether specialist and generalist herbivores induce different plant responses, and if so, to what 611 612 degree the herbivore or the plant benefits, has attracted long-standing interest (Ali and Agrawal, 613 2012). Experimental approaches to tackle these questions have often been confounded by factors 614 including differences in feeding guild, comparisons between phylogenetically divergent herbivores, 615 inclusion of single plant hosts, variation in the extent of tissue damage, and the lack of an expectation 616 of plant defense responses in the absence of (potential) manipulation by herbivores (Ali and Agrawal, 617 2012). Our study addresses several of these confounding factors. While O. pratensis and T. urticae 618 are in different genera, they are closely related within the family Tetranychidae (Matsuda et al., 619 2014), differ little in size and morphology, and cause similar levels of tissue damage on barley and 620 maize leaves. At the cellular level, we found that both species feed on mesophyll cells. This pattern 621 of damage agrees with reports for *Tetranychus* mites in dicots, where empty mesophyll cells were 622 reported at feeding sites (Bensoussan et al., 2016, and references therein). Further, Bensoussan et al. 623 (2016) showed that adult T. urticae females feed on mesophyll cells in A. thaliana and bean by 624 inserting their stylets either through stomata or between epidermal pavement cells. Our observation 625 of a seemingly intact epidermis overlying empty mesophyll cells in barley suggests that spider mites 626 use a similar feeding mechanism in grasses.

627 Although spider mites cause less dramatic tissue damage than insects like caterpillars, both O. 628 pratensis and T. urticae induced pronounced changes in gene expression and peroxidase activity in 629 barley and maize. Strikingly, the DEG sets induced by the two mite species in both plant hosts were 630 similar in composition as well as in the direction (up- or downregulation) and magnitude of fold changes. For the latter, several modest differences in magnitude were apparent. For instance, O. 631 632 pratensis induced a slightly stronger transcriptomic response than T. urticae at 24 hours in barley. 633 and at 2 hours in maize. Whether these differences reflect modulation of plant responses by either herbivore, or alternatively arise from behavioral differences or other factors, is not clear. 634 635 Nevertheless, neither mite species appears to differentially manipulate barley or maize defenses to a 636 great extent (or alternatively, the two plants do not distinguish between the two mite species to mount different responses). A caveat is that our study does not rule out the possibility that manipulation 637 638 could occur post-transcriptionally. It should further be noted that our study used only one strain of T. 639 *urticae*, which we maintained on bean until several generations before collecting barley and maize 640 transcriptomic data. Several experimental studies have documented that the generalist T. urticae can 641 adapt to its hosts when continuously maintained over many generations (Agrawal, 2000; Fry, 1989; 642 Gould, 1979; Magalhães et al., 2007). If T. urticae (and possibly O. pratensis) populations adapt to 643 specific grass hosts, potentially by gaining the ability to modulate plant defenses, is not known. Therefore, whether our findings generalize to all T. urticae and O. pratensis populations is an 644 outstanding question. However, our experimental design mimicked the agriculturally relevant setting 645 646 for cereal crops in which spider mites invade fields from weeds or other crops, persist during the growing season for a modest number of generations, and then move to other hosts for overwintering 647 648 (Margolies and Kennedy, 1985).

649 Our findings of similar barley and maize responses to O. pratensis and T. urticae herbivory do not 650 rule out the possibility that both suppress plant responses similarly. To test this, as well as to 651 understand how grasses perceive mite herbivores, we also included a wounding treatment against 652 which potential suppression of defenses could be assessed (Ali and Agrawal, 2012; Howe and Jander, 653 2008). The utility of a wounding treatment depends largely on how well the treatment mimics patterns of herbivore tissue damage (Mithofer et al., 2005). In the case of mites, mimicking 654 655 mechanical damage to individual mesophyll cells is not possible. Further, our wounding treatments 656 did not replicate the continuous nature of mite feeding, and consisted instead of marked and 657 instantaneous tissue damage at the beginning of treatments. Despite these caveats, in both barley and 658 maize, genes that responded most strongly to herbivory at both 2 and 24 hours also responded 659 strongly to wounding alone. This suggests that grasses readily perceive physical tissue damage by 660 spider mites and mount strong defense responses. Additionally, for many known defensive genes, it 661 was striking that expression changes induced by wounding (up- or downregulation, as well as 662 magnitude of fold changes) were similar to those induced by mite feeding. Therefore, within the 663 limits of our experimental design, we found no obvious signs that O. pratensis or T. urticae suppress plant defenses associated with tissue disruption. 664

In both barley and maize, reprogramming of the transcriptome in response to mite feeding was 665 666 dynamic over a 24-hour time period. At 2 hours, most DEGs were upregulated, including genes associated with JA and other phytohormone signaling, the production of some specialized 667 metabolites, and the synthesis of HIPVs. More dramatic changes in gene expression were observed at 668 24 hours, including a mix of up- and downregulated genes. These dynamics resemble those reported 669 670 previously in studies with T. urticae in dicots including A. thaliana, tomato, and grapevine (Díaz-671 Riquelme et al., 2016; Martel et al., 2015; Zhurov et al., 2014). Recently, Santamaria et al. (2018) 672 examined transcriptomic responses to T. urticae in barley in a design that examined both biotic and 673 abiotic stresses. Although their experimental design differed markedly from ours, with transcriptomic

674 responses assessed at long time points (RNA-seq data was collected after seven days of herbivore 675 exposure), they also observed upregulation of genes associated with JA biosynthesis and signaling. In 676 maize, our findings are also consistent with those of Szczepaniec et al. (2013), who found by reverse 677 transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction that several marker genes for JA and SA 678 signaling were upregulated after three days of herbivory by T. urticae. More generally, at the whole 679 transcriptome level for maize, the transcriptomic changes we observed following herbivory by spider 680 mites are also similar to those reported for herbivory by the caterpillar S. exigua (Tzin et al., 2017). These similarities encompassed rapid induction of diverse LOX genes, including but not limited to 681 682 those involved in JA biosynthesis and signaling. This suggests that chewing insects and mesophyll-683 feeding mites induce globally similar transcriptomic responses even though patterns of tissue damage 684 differ radically. The downregulation of genes involved in photosynthesis that we observed in 685 response to mite herbivory has been reported to be a general response to biotic stress (Bilgin et al., 686 2010).

687 We found that genes in several families encoding defensive proteins, including protease inhibitors 688 and chitinases, were upregulated in our study. Transgenic expression of plant protease inhibitors has 689 been shown to reduce T. urticae's performance on several plant hosts (Carrillo et al., 2011; 690 Santamaria et al., 2012). The same was also observed for transgenic expression of a chitinase, albeit from an insect source (McCafferty et al., 2006), although it should be noted that plant-produced 691 692 chitinases in the frass of S. frugiperda were found to suppress plant defenses and favor the herbivore 693 (Ray et al., 2016). These transgenic studies relied on overexpression, however, and the extent to 694 which endogenous production of protease inhibitors or chitinases in barley and maize leaves impacts 695 spider mites is not known. In insects, protease overexpression or expression of alternative proteases 696 is one route to overcome ingested, plant-produced inhibitors (e.g., Kuwar et al., 2015). This 697 mechanism is likely relevant for T. urticae, as sequencing of the T. urticae genome revealed 698 expansions of protease families, some of which were found to be highly induced upon plant host 699 shifts (Grbić et al., 2011). Whether protease families are expanded in O. pratensis, or whether this 700 specialist has evolved digestive proteases to overcome the inhibitors produced by its hosts in 701 Poaceae, is an outstanding question.

702 Beyond defensive proteins, HIPVs released from feeding sites on grass leaves may play important 703 roles in indirect defenses against mites. In both barley and maize, genes for the synthesis of SA (the 704 precursor to the volatile MeSA), as well as for the synthesis of terpenes, were upregulated in 705 response to mite herbivory. These can serve as cues to predators of spider mites, which include 706 predatory mites as well as winged ladybird beetles (family Coccinellidae). Within Coccinellidae, 707 minute species of the tribe Stethorini feed primarily on mites in the Tetranychidae family (Biddinger 708 et al., 2009). For instance, *Parastethorus nigripes* is an introduced species that has established on O. 709 pratensis on maize in the southern United States (Pollock and Michels, 2002). In Y-tube olfactometer 710 experiments, predatory mites were attracted by MeSA or terpenes (De Boer et al., 2004; Kappers et 711 al., 2005), and synthetic MeSA was shown to attract a Stethorini species (James and Price, 2004). 712 *IGL* was also modestly upregulated in maize in response to herbivory, suggesting that volatile indole 713 is released at mite feeding sites. While indole is a priming agent in maize (Erb et al., 2015), whether 714 it also serves as an attractant for predators of spider mites is unknown.

Apart from defensive proteins and HIPVs, several specialized compounds are likely to play roles in defense against mite herbivores. Mirroring findings reported for herbivory by *S. exigua* (Tzin et al., 2017), genes encoding 9-LOX proteins were rapidly induced by mite feeding. Unlike 13-LOX proteins involved in JA synthesis or the production of green leaf volatiles, LOX3, 4, and 5 belong to the 9-LOX clade, which likely has diverse functions including the production of "death acid"

compounds; 10-OPEA, one such compound, was shown to reduce the performance of fungal pathogens and the lepidopteran herbivore *Helicoverpa zea* (Christensen et al., 2015). More recently, it was shown that *S. exigua* growth increased on maize plants with transposon insertions in *LOX4* as compared to wildtype plants (Woldemariam et al., 2018). These findings, coupled with the upregulation of *LOX3*, *4*, and *5* that we observed in our study, suggest that members of the 9-LOX clade should also be assessed for roles in deterring spider mites.

726 Additionally, we observed modest upregulation of genes involved in the synthesis of DIMBOA-Glc. 727 However, we found dramatic and rapid upregulation of BX10 and BX11 that modify DIMBOA-Glc to produce HDMBOA-Glc, which has been associated with resistance to multiple lepidopteran species 728 729 that feed on maize (Glauser et al., 2011; Tzin et al., 2015b). In contrast, we did not observe as rapid 730 or as strong an induction for genes needed for the synthesis of HDM2BOA-Glc. This may suggest 731 different transcriptional regulation of the biosynthesis genes for major classes of benzoxazinoids 732 derived from DIMBOA. Further, despite globally similar transcriptomic responses to mite herbivory 733 in our study as compared to those reported for S. exigua (Tzin et al., 2017), some BX genes, 734 especially those required for DIMBOA-Glc and HDM2BOA-Glc synthesis (e.g., BX1 and BX14, 735 respectively), were more strongly induced by S. exigua. In Tzin et al.'s (2017) study, younger plants 736 were used for the transcriptomic analysis, and the inducibility of benzoxazinoids has been shown to 737 decrease as maize plants age (Köhler et al., 2015). Whether differences in plant stage, the scope of 738 tissue damage, or other factors explain differences in relative induction for some benzoxazinoid 739 synthesis genes between our study and that of Tzin et al. (2017) warrants additional investigation.

740 Mutations in the benzoxazinoid pathway in maize allowed us to test if benzoxazinoids deter O. 741 pratensis or T. urticae, both, or neither. For T. urticae, performance was markedly reduced on wildtype plants as compared to homozygous *bx1::Ds* and *bx2::Ds* plants. Our finding that *T. urticae* 742 743 performed better on bx2::Ds plants than on bx1::Ds plants was unexpected, as both are reported to 744 reduce DIMBOA to the same low level in W22 (Tzin et al., 2017). One possibility is that indole produced by BX1, which would be anticipated to accumulate in *bx2::Ds* mutant plants, negatively 745 746 affects *T. urticae*. Previously, Tzin et al. (2015a) found that DIMBOA-Glc levels were only modestly 747 reduced ( $\sim$ 70%) in *bx6::Ds* plants, suggesting substantial functional redundancy at the respective step 748 in the benzoxazinoid pathway. This likely explains our finding that mite performance was not 749 significantly different on *bx6::Ds* as compared to wildtype plants.

750 In line with our finding for *T. urticae* and benzoxazinoids in maize, *A. thaliana* plants unable to make 751 indole glucosinolates – a class of specialized compounds in the Brassicaceae – are less resistant to T. 752 urticae (Zhurov et al., 2014). Combined with results from our study, this observation is at odds with 753 the supposition, for which there is mixed experimental support in insects, that generalists should be 754 good at suppressing phylogenetically conserved plant defense pathways (like canonical 755 phytohormone signaling upstream of plant family-specific defensive compounds) (Ali and Agrawal, 756 2012). It is consistent, however, with an important role for detoxification in underlying T. urticae's 757 extreme host range, as supported by the finding of expansions of diverse detoxification genes in this 758 species' genome (Grbić et al., 2011), and its known ability to detoxify compounds from diverse 759 chemical classes (Van Leeuwen and Dermauw, 2016). However, specialized compounds that typify 760 plant families, like benzoxazinoids in Poaceae, nonetheless appear to be costly for T. urticae. It 761 should also be noted that our performance assessments for T. urticae on bx mutants were performed 762 on young plants, in which levels of benzoxazinoids are expected to be higher than in older plants (Cambier et al., 2000; Köhler et al., 2015). Although benzoxazinoid synthesis can be induced in 763 764 maize (Köhler et al., 2015; Maag et al., 2016), screens of maize lines for resistance to T. urticae have 765 shown that while many lines are resistant when young, resistance is ameliorated or essentially lost in

older plants for some, although not all, inbred lines (Tadmor et al., 1999). Therefore, determining the

- respectively 767 extent to which benzoxazinoids deter herbivory by *T. urticae* in field settings where infestation and
- significant plant damage typically occur later in the growing season requires further study.

769 In contrast, our characterization of *O. pratensis* performance on wildtype and *bx* mutant plants 770 suggests that O. pratensis is not affected by benzoxazinoids. The simplest interpretation of this 771 finding is that the route to specialization for *O. pratensis*, at least with respect to this class of toxic 772 compounds, lies in a specialized mechanism of detoxification or inactivation. Precedence for this 773 comes from insects, where larvae of several caterpillars have been shown to enzymatically render 774 ingested benzoxazinoids non-toxic (or less toxic) by glucosylation (Glauser et al., 2011; Maag et al., 775 2014). Our findings with O. pratensis contrast with those of other specialist mite herbivores like T. 776 evansi and A. lycopersici, for which suppression of host plant defenses has been documented (Alba et 777 al., 2015; Glas et al., 2014). Thus, multiple paths to specialization appear to have been taken by 778 different herbivorous mites.

## 779 CONCLUDING REMARKS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

780 Our findings revealed dynamic vet highly correlated transcriptomic responses of two major cereal 781 crops to two spider mite herbivores. Further, the plant responses resembled those observed for 782 wounding, a physical component of herbivory. Taken together, these results suggest that neither mite 783 species manipulates defense responses of these grasses, or that the grass hosts distinguish between 784 the generalist and specialist mites to initiate different (and potentially adaptive) defensive programs. 785 Nevertheless, we found that the generalist T. urticae is negatively impacted by the benzoxazinoid 786 defenses of maize. Our study included the widely used maize inbreds B73 and W22, both of which 787 are readily fed upon by T. urticae. These lines are representative of most maize inbred lines in that 788 they are comparatively sensitive to T. urticae: nevertheless, a small number of lines have been 789 reported to be resistant throughout their ontogeny (Tadmor et al., 1999). Our findings suggest that 790 variation in benzoxazinoid levels or types should be investigated as a potential factor in explaining 791 resistance in maize to T. urticae, and intraspecific variation in benzoxazinoid biosynthesis or 792 accumulation in maize has been documented (Meihls et al., 2012, 2013). Further, in agriculture, 793 economic damage to maize from spider mites is typically observed under drought conditions. As we 794 did not include drought stress as a factor in our experimental design, a future challenge will be to test 795 whether this key abiotic stress influences the relative defense responses of cereals to generalist and 796 specialist spider mites. Given the importance of resistant germplasm for breeding programs, these 797 studies should be extended as well to examine maize varieties previously reported to be mite 798 resistant.
