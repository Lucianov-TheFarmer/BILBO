Running title: Stress responsive polyadenylation of rice IncRNAs

Key words: IncRNA, polyadenylation, Oryza sativa, Stress, post-transcriptional regulation

#### Summary

Recently, long noncoding RNAs (IncRNAs) have been demonstrated to be involved in many biological processes of plants. However, a systematic study on transcriptional and, especially, post-transcriptional regulation of stress-responsive IncRNAs in *Oryza sativa* is lacking. We sequenced three types of RNA libraries, poly(A)+, poly(A)- and nuclear RNAs, under four abiotic stresses, cold, heat, drought, and salt. Based on an integrative bioinformatics approach and ~200 high-throughput datasets, of which ~170 have been published, we revealed over 7,000 IncRNAs, of which nearly half were identified for the first time. Notably, we found that majority of the ~500 poly(A) IncRNAs that were differentially expressed under stresses were significantly down-regulated, but approximately 25% of which were found to have up-regulated nonpoly(A) forms. Moreover, hundreds of IncRNAs with down-regulated polyadenylation (DPA) tend to be highly conserved, show significant nuclear retention, and are co-expressed with protein-coding genes functioning in stresses. Remarkably, these DPA IncRNAs are significantly enriched in quantitative trait loci (QTLs) for stress tolerance or development, suggesting their potential important roles in rice growth under various stresses. Particularly, we observed substantially

accumulated DPA IncRNAs in plants exposed to drought and salt, which is consistent with severe reduction of RNA 3'-end processing factors under these conditions. Taken together, the results of this study reveal that polyadenylation and sub-cellular localization of many rice IncRNAs are likely regulated at the post-transcriptional level. Our findings strongly suggest that many up-regulated/down-regulated IncRNAs previously identified by traditional RNA-seq analyses need to be carefully reviewed to assess the influence of post-transcriptional modification.

### Introduction

Plants possess an elaborate physiological system that responds to external abiotic stress conditions (Hirayama and Shinozaki, 2010) such as extreme temperature and water deprivation. Recently, long noncoding RNAs (IncRNAs) have been demonstrated to have essential roles in abiotic stress responses. For example, cold assisted intronic noncoding RNA (COLDAIR) and cold induced long antisense intragenic RNA (COOLAIR) can repress the FLOWERINGLOCUS C (FLC) gene by mediating histone modifications (Swiezewski et al., 2009, Heo and Sung, 2011, Csorba et al., 2014). In Arabidopsis thaliana, overexpression of IncRNA npc536 enhances tolerance to salt (Ben Amor et al., 2009). Moreover, genome-wide studies in A. thaliana have revealed hundreds of IncRNAs that respond to various external stimuli (Di et al., 2014, Yuan et al., 2016). Rice is an essential crop that grows in a wide range of environments characterized by various temperatures, salinity levels, and soil-water conditions. Abiotic stresses can severely affect the growth and productivity of rice (Zhang et al., 2014c). Thousands of stress-responsive IncRNAs have been identified in Arabidopsis and maize (Di et al., 2014, Li et al., 2014, Zhang et al., 2014b, Zhang et al., 2014c). However, systematic studies of stress in rice have primarily focused on protein-coding genes (Mizuno et al., 2010). Although a recent study identified cisnatural antisense transcripts (cis-NATs) in rice that respond to osmotic stress (Lu et al., 2012), This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

IncRNAs transcribed from other genomic elements (e.g. intergenic and intron) and those that respond to non-osmotic stresses have not been systematically studied.

In the past decade, many IncRNAs have been identified by high throughput sequencing of *Arabidopsis* (Liu *et al.*, 2012, Di *et al.*, 2014, Wang *et al.*, 2014), rice (Lu *et al.*, 2012, Zhang *et al.*, 2014c), maize (Li *et al.*, 2014, Zhang *et al.*, 2014b), brassica (Yu *et al.*, 2013), populous (Shuai *et al.*, 2014), wheat (Xin *et al.*, 2011), cotton (Wang *et al.*, 2015b, Zou *et al.*, 2016), tomato (Wang *et al.*, 2015a, Zhu *et al.*, 2015, Wang *et al.*, 2016), cucumber (Hao *et al.*, 2015) and other plants. Most of these genome-wide studies have focused on identifying novel IncRNAs and profiling expression patterns, but some of them have assessed transcription factor regulation at the transcriptional level (Di *et al.*, 2014). These studies reveal that IncRNAs are always transcribed in a tissue/organ specific manner or induced by biotic and abiotic stimuli (Di *et al.*, 2014, Li *et al.*, 2014, Yuan *et al.*, 2016) due to precise regulation at the transcriptional or post-transcriptional level. Recent studies have revealed that certain transcription factors, histone modifiers and RNA polymerases are crucial for initiation of IncRNA transcription (Sun *et al.*, 2013b, Ariel *et al.*, 2014).

In addition to transcriptional regulation, IncRNAs are also controlled by some posttranscriptional events, such as alternative splicing, 3' end processing, polyadenylation, nuclear export and degradation (Liu *et al.*, 2007, Marquardt *et al.*, 2014, Li *et al.*, 2015). In *Arabidopsis*, a core spliceosome component (PRP8), RNA binding proteins (FPA and FCA), and RNA 3' processing factors (FY, CstF64 and CstF77) are involved in post-transcriptional regulation of COOLAIR (Liu *et al.*, 2007, Marquardt *et al.*, 2014, Li *et al.*, 2015). A lack of polyadenylation may inhibit IncRNA export and affect the abundance of nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA fractions (Chen, 2016, Tian and Manley, 2017). The functions of RNAs are tightly linked to their polyadenylation status and subcellular localization (Chen, 2016).

In this work, we used large-scale sequencing data, including sets of poly(A)-enriched RNAs, poly(A)-depleted RNAs and nuclear RNAs, as well as a coding potential calculation tool based on multiple features (COME-Rice) (Hu *et al.*, 2015, Hu *et al.*, 2017), to identify and characterize stress-responsive IncRNAs in rice. In addition to differentially expressed IncRNAs (DE IncRNAs), we revealed hundreds of IncRNAs showing significant down-regulation of polyadenylation (DPA) under stress conditions. These DPA IncRNAs are conserved, enriched in nuclei, derived from QTL regions significantly associated with stress tolerance and co-expressed with coding genes functioning in stress-responsive pathways. Finally, we suggest that the DPA events are potentially due to substantial down-regulation of RNA 3' end processing factors in plants exposed to drought and salt stresses. Collectively, our genome-wide study provides a rich resource of stress-responsive IncRNAs regulated at the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels in rice.

#### Results

#### Identification of novel IncRNAs in Oryza sativa

To identify novel lncRNAs involved in the stress-responsive processes of rice, we first sequenced 29 RNA libraries representing three types of RNAs in *Oryza sativa* (japonica), poly(A)-enriched (poly(A)+) RNAs, poly(A)-depleted (poly(A)-) RNAs and nuclear RNAs, which were isolated in stress-treated (cold, drought, heat, salt) samples (See Experimental Procedures, Figures 1a and S1). To achieve complete identification of rice lncRNAs, we combined our datasets with 170 published RNA-seq datasets (Figure S2 and Appendix S1). We mapped the reads to the rice reference genome RGAP7 (Kawahara *et al.*, 2013) and assembled them into 232,352 transcripts (97,399 genes). The assembled set contained 31,872 (89%) protein-coding genes and 3,821 annotated lncRNAs (IRGSP-1.0.29, http://plants.ensembl.org/ and (Zhang *et al.*, 2014c)), which suggested good recovery of the rice transcriptome. To identify This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

novel IncRNAs, we first removed 71,031 genes (including 203,590 transcripts) that overlapped with protein-coding genes, annotated IncRNAs, and other canonical ncRNAs. Next, we removed 7,230 genes (including 7,245 transcripts) that were shorter than 200 nt. Subsequently, we calculated the coding potential for the remaining transcripts based on our in-house method, COME-Rice (See Experimental Procedures, Figures 1b and S3) (Hu et al., 2015, Hu et al., 2017), and removed 9,812 genes (including 11,861 transcripts). By utilizing multiple types of datasets (Figure S2), 105 features were identified and subsequently used to train the COME-Rice model (a random forest model) (Figure 1b). To overcome the effect of the imbalanced sizes of the protein-coding and noncoding sets, we generated balanced sets by using a downsampling strategy to reduce the size of the protein-coding set until it was comparable to that of the noncoding set (Figure 1c) (Hu et al., 2015, Hu et al., 2017). Subsequently, we found that COME-Rice classified coding and noncoding transcripts quite well, with its accuracy reaching 0.958. Finally, we removed 3,551 low-abundance (RPKM <= 1) genes (including 3,700 transcripts) and filtered NAT genes without strand-specific RNA-seq reads. In total, we assembled and identified 7,231 IncRNAs (7,336 transcripts), including 3,410 novel IncRNAs (3,493 transcripts) (Figure 1a, Table S1 and Appendix S2).

#### The majority of rice IncRNAs are enriched in the poly(A)+ RNA library

To classify the poly(A) transcripts among our newly identified lncRNAs, we compared the relative abundance of lncRNAs and protein-coding genes in the poly(A)+ and poly(A)- RNA-seq datasets. Notably, more than 80% of lncRNAs and 95% protein-coding genes were found to have enriched poly(A) signals (Figure 2a). This is consistent with previous observations that many mammalian lncRNAs are capped and polyadenylated (Kung *et al.*, 2013). Usually, poly(A) lncRNAs are defined as those with at least 2-fold greater enrichment in the poly(A)+ dataset

compared to the poly(A)- dataset (Yang *et al.*, 2011, Di *et al.*, 2014). Thus, we defined 4,515 poly(A) IncRNAs (Figure 2b). In addition, 1,187 IncRNAs that did not meet the cutoff were defined as bimorphic IncRNAs. To confirm expression of the poly(A) IncRNAs identified by the RNA-seq analyses, we randomly selected and validated the existence of poly(A) IncRNAs by RNA fractionation and RT-PCR; IncRNAs enriched in the poly(A)- dataset were selected as a control set (Figure 2c).

#### Rice IncRNAs tend to be down-regulated in response to stresses

To identify the dynamic expression patterns of stress-responsive IncRNAs, we systematically compared the relative abundance of poly(A) IncRNAs (Figures 2d, S4a-c) and other IncRNAs (Figure S4d) in rice plants under four stress conditions to their abundance under normal conditions. Remarkably, these IncRNAs were further clustered into five clusters based on their expression patterns, revealing that they were expressed in a highly stress-specific manner (Figure 2e). Subsequently, we identified 453 poly(A) IncRNAs that were significantly differentially expressed under stress conditions (*p*-value<0.05) (Figure 2f and Appendix S3) (Love *et al.*, 2014), of which 375 IncRNAs were specifically induced/reduced under one stress while 78 IncRNAs were differentially expressed under at least two stresses (Figure 2g). Drought and salt stress shared more IncRNAs (33 IncRNAs) than did the other pairs of stresses. These shared IncRNAs account for 33% of all differentially expressed IncRNAs (DE IncRNAs) induced by salt stress (96 IncRNAs), which implies a conserved regulatory mechanism underlying the response to salt and drought in rice (Hirayama and Shinozaki, 2010). Moreover, further similarities in the effects of drought and salt on gene expression were found in other published datasets (Figure S5).

Interestingly, most of the poly(A) IncRNAs (Figures 2e-f) and other IncRNAs (Figure S4d) that were differentially expressed under the tested stresses were down-regulated. To confirm the expression patterns of these DE IncRNAs based on RNA-seq analyses, we randomly selected 50 IncRNAs and successfully validated their expression levels under the stress treatments (Figures 2h, S6 and Appendix S5).

## Many poly(A) IncRNAs down-regulated in response to stress have up-regulated nonpoly(A) forms

Although most of the IncRNAs regulated in response to the tested stresses were found to be down-regulated based on traditional RNA-seq methods, i.e. poly(A)+ RNA-seq, we found that many of these down-regulated IncRNAs had up-regulated nonpoly(A) forms. In total, we found 356 poly(A) IncRNAs that were significantly down-regulated in response to the four tested stresses via poly(A) RNA-seq. However, using poly(A)- RNA-seq, we found that 111 of these IncRNAs showed increased abundance (Appendix S3). Consistent and opposite trends in expression changes were validated for the poly(A) and nonpoly(A) forms of three IncRNAs (Figures 3a-c). We confirmed the observed changes in gene expression for these genes by analyzing both RNA-seq reads normalized by total mapped reads and performing qRT-PCR normalized by the relative abundance of housekeeping genes (Figures 3a-c).

To perform a genome-wide statistical analysis of poly(A) and nonpoly(A) lncRNAs, we compared the relative abundance of both forms in stressed plants and control plants (Figures 3d and S7). LncRNAs were separated into four quadrants; notably, lncRNAs with fewer poly(A) transcripts and more nonpoly(A) transcripts are specifically located in the second quadrant. The analysis showed that 35%-80% of the tested lncRNAs were located in the second quadrant (Figure 3e). With a more stringent significance cutoff (Fisher's exact test, *p*-value<0.001), we This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

defined a group of IncRNAs as IncRNAs with down-regulation of polyadenylation (DPA IncRNAs) (See Experimental Procedures and Figure 3f). In total, 751 DPA IncRNAs were identified, most of which were detected in plants exposed to drought and salt stresses (Figure 3f and Appendix S3). Similar to the results of the analysis of DE IncRNAs (Figure 2g), more (126) DPA IncRNAs were shared by the drought and salt conditions than were shared by any other pair of stresses, which suggests a shared pattern of IncRNA regulation in response to these two stresses (Figure 3g).

#### Stress-responsive DPA IncRNAs are enriched in the nucleus

Previous studies have shown that the poly(A) tail is crucial for the process of transportation of mRNAs from the nucleus to the cytoplasm (Huang and Carmichael, 1996, Shen *et al.*, 2008, Neve *et al.*, 2016). Thus, we examined changes in the sub-cellular localization of DPA lncRNAs under various stresses. First, we calculated the nuclear ratios (nuclear abundance versus whole cell abundance) of lncRNAs under normal condition (Figure 4a). Consistent with previous studies, transcripts of snoRNAs showed more nuclear bias; however, transcripts of protein-coding genes showed only limited nuclear preference, which indicated cytoplasmic localization. The localization of lncRNA transcripts was found to be divergent; poly(A) lncRNAs are predominantly localized in the cytoplasm, while nonpoly(A) lncRNAs are preferentially located in the nucleus (Figure 4a).

Next, we determined whether the sub-cellular localization of IncRNAs was also changed when DPA occurred and nonpoly(A) forms were retained. We calculated the nuclear ratios of DPA IncRNAs/non-DPA IncRNAs under drought and salt conditions, which revealed that DPA IncRNAs had a nuclear ratio significantly greater than that of non-DPA IncRNAs (t-test, *p*-value < 0.05), which suggests that DPA contributes to nuclear retention of IncRNAs (Figure 4b). To This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

further confirm DPA-mediated nuclear retention of IncRNAs, we randomly selected several DPA IncRNAs under the drought condition or salt condition. For example, the abundance of the poly(A) transcripts of *RICEG\_050237* and *RICEG\_065111* was decreased under salt and drought, respectively (Figures 4c and 4e). In contrast, the abundance of the nonpoly(A) transcripts of *RICEG\_050237* and *RICEG\_065111* was increased under these conditions. Consistent with the proposed effect of increasing the abundance of nonpoly(A) transcripts, the nuclear abundance of *RICEG\_050237* and *RICEG\_065111* was increased. We confirmed the changes in expression described above by analyzing RNA-seq reads normalized by total mapped reads and performing qRT-PCR normalized by the relative abundance of housekeeping genes (Figures 4c-f).

#### Stress-responsive DPA IncRNAs are relatively more conserved in plant lineages

The conservation of stress-responsive lncRNAs among plant species has not been well determined. Here, we compared the rice lncRNAs identified in this study with lncRNAs from 9 other plant species: *Zea mays, Glycine max, Arabidopsis thaliana, Solanum tuberosum, Vitis vinifera, Amborella trichopoda, Selaginella moellendorffii, Physcomitrella patens,* and *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii.* A phylogenetic tree of 10 plant species was constructed using orthologous lncRNAs, and the number of orthologous lncRNAs on each node was calculated (See Experimental Procedures, Method S1, Figure 5a and Appendix S4). In total, 4,095 lncRNA orthologs were defined as conserved lncRNAs with similar sequences in at least two species. Among them, 1,575 lncRNAs (38%) are conserved in at least three plant species, while the remaining 2,520 lncRNAs (62%) are conserved in only two plant species (Appendix S4). Interestingly, 53% of lncRNA orthologs are shared by recently separated dicot and monocot plants.

Consistent with this finding, 513 IncRNAs identified in rice are conserved in at least one of the other 9 species in the analysis; however, most of these shared IncRNAs are also conserved in dicot and monocot species (168 in Z. mays, 49 in G. max, 159 in A. thaliana, 134 in S. tuberosum and 164 in V. vinifera) rather than in A. trichopoda, S. moellendorffii, P. patens, and C. reinhardtii. We found that conserved lncRNAs in rice tended to be polyadenylated and enriched in the cytoplasm, indicating that a majority of IncRNAs likely function in the cytoplasm (Figures 5b-c). Forty DE IncRNAs and 121 DPA IncRNAs of rice have orthologs in other 9 species, in which, thirty-nine DE IncRNAs and 115 DPA IncRNAs are conserved in eudicot and monocot species (Figure 5d). Thus, only a few DE IncRNAs and DPA IncRNAs orthologs were found in A. trichopoda, S. moellendorffii, P. patens, and C. reinhardtii. Further analysis showed that DPA IncRNAs are more conserved than DE IncRNAs and other IncRNAs with low abundances (Figure 5e). In order to confirm that this trend was not caused by differences in the abundance of various IncRNA groups, we sampled IncRNAs at the same expression level (Figure S8). Taken together, these results suggest that IncRNAs evolved rapidly, and stressresponsive IncRNAs are more conserved than other IncRNAs because of their important functions.

#### Stress-responsive IncRNAs are enriched in QTLs related to stress tolerance

The conservation status of the DE IncRNAs and DPA IncRNAs identified in this study suggests that they are probably important and functional. Therefore, we assessed whether they play roles in stress tolerance and growth by investigating their relationships with QTLs related to stress tolerance (See Experimental Procedures). We found that the DE IncRNAs are slightly enriched in QTLs related to stress tolerance (Figure 6a). Interestingly, the DPA IncRNAs regulated by the cold and salt treatments are specifically enriched in QTLs related to cold and salt tolerance

(Fisher's exact test, *p*-value<0.05), respectively (Figure 6b). This suggests that the DE IncRNAs and DPA IncRNAs identified in this study may play important roles in stress tolerance and growth in rice.

## Stress-responsive DPA IncRNAs are associated with protein-coding genes functioning in stress

The biological functions of IncRNAs are tightly associated with their sub-cellular locations (Kino et al., 2010). As mentioned above, subcellular localizations of many DPA IncRNAs are altered under stress, which suggests their regulatory functions. To identify the regulatory functions of DPA IncRNAs, we utilized a co-expression network to investigate the potential biological roles of DPA IncRNAs in response to stresses. Interestingly, most of co-expressed DPA IncRNA and protein-coding gene pairs show positive correlation (Figure S9a). Based on a GO enrichment analysis of protein-coding genes co-expressed with DPA IncRNAs, we found that DPA IncRNAs accumulated in nucleus have functions related to stress response processes (Figures 7a, b and S9b). For example, many DPA IncRNAs participate in biosynthesis, transport, and metabolism of signaling molecule abscisic acid (ABA), which can activate sets of stress-responsive genes (Hirayama and Shinozaki, 2010). Some cold and heat related DPA IncRNAs are involved in negative regulation of catalytic activity during exposure to cold and heat in case that extreme temperatures affect the activity of enzymes. Some drought related DPA IncRNAs are involved in abscisis acid (ABA) transport and cell wall thickening, which are important biological defenses against osmotic stress (Zhu, 2016). Different from DPA IncRNAs, non-DPA IncRNAs are mainly involved in post-transcriptional regulatory processes such as translation, protein localization and stabilization (Figures 7c and S9c). In addition, DPA mRNAs is more likely to be related to basal and general events, like biosynthetic and catabolic processes (Figure S10).

In addition to sub-cellular localization, the genomic locations of IncRNAs and and distances between IncRNAs and protein-coding genes can provide another layer of information regarding the cis or trans regulatory roles of DPA IncRNAs (Chen, 2016). Remarkably, DPA IncRNAs accumulated in nuceus are involved in more cis-regulatory relationships with the nearest co-expressed genes, while non-DPA IncRNAs are always involved in trans regulation (Figure 7d). Having compared results obtained using different cutoffs to define cis and trans, we found that the above conclusion is independent of these cutoffs (Figure S11). Consistently, the distance between DPA IncRNAs and the nearest co-expressed genes is significantly shorter than that of the control pairs (t-test, *p*-value<0.05) (Figure 7e), indicating that IncRNAs accumulated in the nucleus may have more potentials to regulate genes in cis. Collectively, our results suggest that DPA IncRNAs may cis-regulate many stress-responsive genes in the nucleus during responses to stresses.

#### 3' processing factors may participate in DPA events

Based on the findings described above, we sought to identify the mechanisms underlying the dynamics of DPA IncRNAs during stress responses. We determined the expression patterns of key factors involved in RNA 3' end processing, including cleavage stimulation factors (CstFs), cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factors (CPSFs), cleavage factor I and II (CFIm and CFIIm), and poly(A) polymerase (PAP) (Hunt *et al.*, 2012). Remarkably, we found that the expression levels of 90% of the tested polyadenylation factors are indeed dramatically decreased by drought and salt exposure (Figure 8a). We used qRT-PCR to confirm the abundance of polyadenylation factors, including *CstF77, CstF64, CPSF160* and *FY*, during exposure to four stress conditions at additional time points. The results of these experiments confirm that these polyadenylation factors are specifically down-regulated under drought and, especially, salty conditions (Figures 8b, S12 and Appendix S5). Based on the results described above, we propose a model in which down-regulation of polyadenylation factors inhibits This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

polyadenylation of IncRNAs and induces DPA, after which newly expressed DPA IncRNAs cisregulate stress-responsive genes in the nucleus (Figure 8c).

### Discussion

Previous genome-wide profiling studies are mainly based on utilizing poly(A)+ RNA-seq or total RNA-seq datasets to identify differentially expressed mRNAs or IncRNAs, which are regarded as regulated at the transcriptional level (Lu *et al.*, 2012, Zhang *et al.*, 2014b, Zhang *et al.*, 2014c). By combining poly(A)- RNA-seq datasets, we found that many poly(A) IncRNAs that were relatively less abundant in poly(A)+ samples were more abundant in poly(A)- samples, which reminded us of the post-transcriptional regulation that IncRNAs could undergo when exposed to external stimuli. Interestingly, we found that IncRNAs with regulated polyadenylation are enriched in QTLs related to stress tolerance, suggesting that these IncRNAs might play important roles in rice growth under various stress conditions.

When comparing gene expression/abundance between two conditions, no normalization method is perfect, because one must assume that either the amount of total RNA or marker gene RNA is consistent. These assumptions do not have to be true in different cells and tissues. In most differential expression studies, transcript reads are normalized by the total number of mapped reads, because this method was found to be more robust than normalization based on expression levels of marker genes (Loven *et al.*, 2012). In this paper, we carefully analyzed different types of expression changes for IncRNAs. On one hand, we identified changes in expression only by comparing RNA-seq values from the same type of RNA library, because different RNA purification methods may lead to various biases, like extraction of poly(A) RNA may cause internal priming signals in genome (Appendix S6). On the other hand, we confirmed

these changes in expression with both RNA-seq reads normalized by total reads and qRT-PCR normalized using multiple housekeeping genes.

It was interesting to observe that DPA events varied markedly under the four tested stresses. For example, salt exposure induced DPA IncRNAs accounted for the majority of all DPA IncRNAs, which suggests that DPA is a complicated event in which many factors may participate. The expression patterns of 3' processing factors were different under the tested stress conditions. For example, more than 90% of the tested factors were significantly repressed in plants exposed to salt, while only approximately 20% of the tested factors were down-regulated by exposure to cold and heat (Figure 8a). Furthermore, multiple factors polyadenylation process. For example, CstFs and CPSFs are mainly participate in the 3' responsible for cleavage (Zhao et al., 2009), while PAPs are involved in adding poly(A) tails (Li and Hunt, 1997), and PABNs stabilize poly(A) tails (Hunt et al., 2012). Thus, repression of different 3 ' processing factors may affect different steps of 3' processing, which can influence mRNA polyadenylation. In addition, 3' processing factors may recognize various sequence motifs around poly(A) sites. We defined the far upstream element (FUE) and near upstream element (NUE) of IncRNAs to find enriched polyadenylation sequence motifs (Figure S13). This analysis showed that IncRNAs have unique enriched sequences that are different from the sequence motifs of mRNAs (Table S2). Interestingly, DPA IncRNAs also showed stress-specific motifs in plants exposed to stress conditions (Table S3).

Meanwhile, we did observe a general DPA for mRNAs as well (Figure S10). However, unlike IncRNAs' potential cis-regulating function on chromosomes (Figure 7), such as *COLDAIR* and *COOLAIR* (Swiezewski *et al.*, 2009, Heo and Sung, 2011, Csorba *et al.*, 2014), the DPA of mRNAs is more likely to be a passive event because the major function of mRNA is unlikely to be happening in nucleus.

Previous studies showed that RNA polyadenylation is inhibited by heat shock (Di Giammartino et al., 2013). Many IncRNAs have nuclear localization patterns related to their distinct nuclear functions, including XIST, BORG, and Gomafu (Sone et al., 2007, Zhang et al., 2007, Zhang et al., 2014a, Cerase et al., 2015). However, other IncRNAs, such as terminal tissue differentiation-induced ncRNA (TINCR), competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) and BACE-1 AS, are exported to the cytoplasm to perform their regulatory roles (Franco-Zorrilla et al., 2007, Faghihi et al., 2010, Salmena et al., 2011, Kretz et al., 2013). Sub-cellular localization of IncRNAs is tightly associated with their biological functions. However, the sub-cellular locations of IncRNAs can change dynamically when they interact with target proteins. In the presence of dexamethasone, a fraction of cytoplasmic IncRNA Gas5 interacts with GR protein and is translocated to the nucleus, where it inhibits association between GR protein and its target genes (Kino et al., 2010). We found the DPA IncRNAs were more enriched in the nucleus by exposure to multiple stresses, especially salt and drought. Further studies on the regulatory targets of these dynamically regulated IncRNAs may illuminate their biological functions. Furthermore, some of the DPA IncRNAs identified in this study are located in QTLs that related to stress-tolerance and development, which indicates that IncRNAs may affect the traits of rice at the post-transcriptional level. Therefore, these results could help us understand the development of agronomic traits in rice and may assist researchers in improving crop yield under natural abiotic stresses.

In this study, we have identified and characterized hundreds of stress-responsive IncRNAs in rice. The abundance and polyadenylation status of IncRNAs are regulated during stress responses, which alters their subcellular locations and functions. Thus, some down-regulated poly(A) IncRNAs have up-regulated nonpoly(A) forms and function actively in response to stress. Consistently, we observed severe down-regulation of RNA 3' processing and polyadenylation factors in plants subjected to drought and salt exposure, which may have impaired This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

polyadenylation and nuclear retention of drought/salt responsive IncRNAs. Overall, our data strongly suggest that, in addition to differential expression analyses based on traditional RNA-seq libraries such as poly(A) enriched RNAs, special RNA libraries, such as poly(A)-depleted and nuclear-enriched RNAs, as well as analyses at the post-transcriptional level, are useful for studies of stress-responsive IncRNAs.

### **Experimental Procedures**

#### Sample preparation

We plant the *Oryza sativa* japonica on the ½ MS medium in a growth chamber under conditions of 12 hours light at 28°Cand 12 hours dark at 22°Cfor 2 weeks. Then we treated the seedlings under the stress conditions. We choose four stress conditions, including cold, heat, drought and salt. For cold treatment, 2-week seedlings were transferred to 4°Cfor 3, 6, and 12 hours. For heat treatment, seedlings were transferred to 50°Cfor 3, 6, and 12 hours. For drought treatment, 2-week seedlings were air-dried for 3, 6, and 12 hours. For salt treatment, 2-week seedlings were transferred to 1/2 MS with 300 mM NaCl for 3, 6, and 12 hours. Then, we checked the expression pattern of marker genes (induced during stress treatment) by qRT-PCR (Figure S1) to make sure the efficiency of stress treatment. Finally, we choose 6 hours treated plants for RNA-seq.

#### Construction of NGS libraries and high throughput sequencing

We purified poly(A) enriched (poly(A)+), poly(A) depleted (poly(A)-) RNAs and nuclear RNA. For poly(A)+ RNA purification, two rounds of oligo(dT) beads (Invitrogen) treatment were performed. For poly(A)- RNA purification, two rounds of ribosomal RNA removal were performed using Ribo

minus plant kit (Invitrogen) and then, two rounds of poly(A)+ RNA removal were performed by using oligo(dT) beads (Invitrogen). For nuclear RNA purification, rice nuclei were extracted from whole cell by density gradient centrifugation (for more details see Method S1) and then total RNAs was isolated from nucleus. Then two rounds of ribosomal RNA removal were performed by using Ribo minus plant kit (Invitrogen). Strand specific RNA-seq libraries were constructed using NEXTflex Rapid Directional RNA-seq kit (Bioo Scientific). The qualities of all RNA-seq libraries were checked by Agilent bioanalyzer 2100 and sequencing was performed on Hiseq2000 platform with paired-end 100 strategy.

#### Pipeline of identifying novel IncRNAs in rice

We firstly performed quality control and trimmed adapter using FastQC and FASTX-Toolkit for each RNA-seq data set (Andrews, 2010). After removing reads mapped to ribosomal RNAs, we then mapped them to rice genome (RGAP7) (Kawahara *et al.*, 2013) and assembled transcripts using Tophat and Cufflinks (Trapnell *et al.*, 2012) based on our sequenced RNA reads and 170 published RNA-seq data sets (Figure S2 and Appendix S1). We used rice annotation (IRGSP-1.0.29) (http://plants.ensembl.org/) as the assemble template. Next, we filtered transcripts overlapped with annotated genes and canonical noncoding RNAs (i.e. rRNAs, tRNAs, snoRNAs, snRNAs, miRNAs, etc). Transcripts shorter than 200 nt were also removed. Subsequently, we adapted our published method, COME-Rice (Hu *et al.*, 2015, Hu *et al.*, 2017), to calculate coding potentials of the retained transcripts (for more details see Method S1 and Figure S3). To improve the accuracy, we also used four other coding potential calculators, CPC, CNCI, HMMER and RNAcode (Kong *et al.*, 2007, Finn *et al.*, 2011, Washietl *et al.*, 2011, Sun *et al.*, 2013a). Transcripts were kept when they were recognized as noncoding RNA by COME-Rice and at least one another tool. Finally, lowly expressed (RPKM<1 in all datasets) lncRNAs were removed.

#### Identify IncRNAs with poly(A) signals

We calculated the expression level (RPKM) of each protein-coding gene and IncRNA by using poly(A)+ and poly(A)– RNA-seq datasets, respectively. For each IncRNA or protein-coding gene, maximum value was kept. Poly(A) coding gene or IncRNA was defined as  $RPKM_{poly(A)+}/RPKM_{poly(A)-} \ge 2$  by adapting a previous method (Yang *et al.*, 2011). LncRNAs or protein-coding genes which are not meet criteria above were defined as bimorphic group. Most of the poly(A) IncRNAs are antisense to protein-coding genes. Therefore, we carefully filtered the antisense IncRNAs with strand-specific sequencing data (Figures 1a and S2), requiring antisense IncRNAs can still be assembled only based on strand-specific sequencing reads.

#### **Differential expression analysis**

We used DEGSeq (Wang and Wang, 2016) to calculate expression level of each protein-coding gene and lncRNA. Public stress-related or organ-related RNA-seq datasets were added into this analysis and treated as replicates to make our results more robust (Figure S2a). DESeq2 was used to identify differentially expressed lncRNAs and protein-coding genes (Love *et al.*, 2014). RPKMs for poly(A) lncRNAs were calculated from poly(A)+ RNA-seq datasets. Differentially expressed lncRNAs were defined when RPKM<sub>stress</sub>/RPKM<sub>control</sub>  $\geq$  2 or  $\leq$  0.5 and *p*-value < 0.05.

#### Cluster samples based on expression profiles

We adapted a published statistical method, Transcriptome Overlap Measure (TROM), to integrate a number of public RNA-seq data from various samples to find closely related samples (Yang *et al.*, 2016). This method identified associated genes or IncRNAs of a condition as these coding gene or IncRNAs have relatively high abundance in this sample while relatively low abundance in other samples. If more genes or IncRNAs are shared between two samples, they are more inclined to be clustered into a color block.

#### Define DPA IncRNAs responding to stress

To identify candidate DPA IncRNAs with significant poly(A)+ and poly(A)– level changes, we set cutoffs as below:

i. Expression level (RPKM) >0 in both poly(A)+ samples and poly(A)– samples under a certain stress condition and control.

ii.  $FC_{poly(A)+} \le 0.5$ , and  $FC_{poly(A)-} \ge 2$ . For each IncRNA, two-fold change (FC) values are calculated based on expression levels in poly(A)+ samples and poly(A)– samples under stress condition and control, respectively. The fold change (FC) values are calculated as below two formulas:

FC<sub>poly(A)+</sub> = RPKM<sub>stress\_poly(A)+</sub>/RPKM<sub>control\_poly(A)+</sub>

$$FC_{poly(A)-} = RPKM_{stress_poly(A)-}/RPKM_{control_poly(A)-}$$

iii. *p*-value<0.001. Read count numbers are used to calculate *p*-value with Fisher's exact test, including Count<sub>poly(A)+</sub> in stress, Count<sub>poly(A)-</sub> in stress, Count<sub>poly(A)+</sub> in control, Count<sub>poly(A)-</sub> in control. Considered the null distribution of lncRNAs' nucleus enrichment is an abnormal distribution and the sample size varies, we used Fisher's exact test to measure the dependence of stress treatment and poly(A)+ enrichment. Before the test, we did quantile normalization to eliminate biases caused by different sequencing depth of samples. Then, we used the normalized read count via fisher.test function in R to obtain a *p*-value for each lncRNA.

#### Identification of orthologous IncRNAs in plants

Except for rice IncRNAs, IncRNAs of other 9 species are all downloaded from the CANTATAdb (http://cantata.amu.edu.pl/) (Szczesniak *et al.*, 2016). Then two methods were used for comparison of IncRNA sequences (Hezroni *et al.*, 2015) (for more details see Method S1).

Besides, we also downloaded protein-coding genes orthologous relationship from RAP-DB (http://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/).

#### Gene co-expression network construction for IncRNAs and protein-coding genes

We adapted a published method (Ma *et al.*, 2007) to construct a co-expression network of IncRNAs and protein-coding genes in rice. Gene expression profiling of IncRNAs and protein-coding genes for network reconstruction were derived from 180 RNA-seq datasets (including poly(A)+ RNA-seq and total RNA-seq) (Appendix S1). Markov clustering (MCL) was used to detect highly inter-connected gene clusters (van Dongen and Abreu-Goodger, 2012). The partial correlations were used as edge weights for MCL (for more details see Method S1).

#### Gene Ontology enrichment analysis for the co-expressed gene/IncRNA clusters

In Gene ontology (GO) analysis, we downloaded the GO annotation for *Arabidopsis thaliana* from Gene Ontology Consortium (GOC) (Submission date: 4/1/2016). Because the GO annotation for *Oryza sativa* from GOC (Submission date: 9/22/2009) was too old, we obtained the RAP-DB (http://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp) that provided GO annotation for many protein-coding genes. In addition, we have added more GO annotation for rice via the orthologous genes between rice and *Arabidopsis*. We used an R package, *topGO* (Alexa and Rahnenfuhrer, 2016), to estimate the enrichment of biological process (BP) terms for a certain gene set by Fisher's exact test.

#### QTL enrichment analysis

In the QTL analysis, we first overlapped the genomic coordinates of DE and DPA IncRNAs with that of QTLs. The QTL table was downloaded from http://qtaro.abr.affrc.go.jp/. Here, we only kept QTLs with more than 30 traits for further enrichment analysis. We normalized the raw

overlapped number by the number of QTL traits and overlapped DE or DPA IncRNAs. Then, we did the Fisher's exact test to measure the significance of QTL enrichment.
