#### SUMMARY

GUN1 integrates retrograde signals in chloroplasts but the underlying mechanism is elusive. FUG1, a chloroplast translation initiation factor, and GUN1 are co-expressed at the transcriptional level, and FUG1 co-immunoprecipitates with GUN1. We used mutants of GUN1 (gun1-103) and FUG1 (fug1-3) to analyse their functional relationship at the physiological and systems-wide level, the latter including transcriptome and proteome analyses. Absence of GUN1 aggravates the effects of decreased FUG1 levels on chloroplast protein translation, resulting in transiently more pronounced phenotypes regarding photosynthesis, leaf coloration, growth and cold acclimation. The gun1-103 mutation also enhances variegation in the var2 mutant, increasing the fraction of white sectors, while fug1-3 suppresses the var2 phenotype. The transcriptomes of fug1-3 and gun1-103 plants are very similar, but absence of GUN1 alone has almost no effect on protein levels, whereas steadystate levels of chloroplast proteins are markedly decreased in *fug1-3*. In *fug1 gun1* double mutants, effects on transcriptomes and particularly on proteomes are enhanced. Our results show that GUN1 function becomes critical when chloroplast proteostasis is perturbed by decreased rates of synthesis (fug1) or degradation (var2) of chloroplast proteins. The functions of FUG1 and GUN1 appear to be related, corroborating the view that GUN1 helps to maintain chloroplast protein homeostasis (proteostasis).

# INTRODUCTION

Plastids produce specific signalling molecules that relay information to the nucleus to modify nuclear gene expression (NGE) ("plastid-nucleus signalling" or in short "plastid signalling") (reviewed in: Nott *et al.*, 2006; Kleine *et al.*, 2009; Chi *et al.*, 2013; Chan *et al.*, 2016; Colombo *et al.*, 2016; Kleine and Leister, 2016). The "operational" (Pogson *et al.*, 2008) type of plastid signalling occurs in adult plants with fully functional photosynthesis, and appears to involve reactive oxygen species that can trigger the production of metabolites with putative signalling functions (reviewed in: de Souza *et al.*, 2017; Leister, 2017). In seedlings without fully functional photosynthesis, the "biogenic" (Pogson *et al.*, 2008) type of plastid signalling is triggered. For instance, the reduced expression of nuclear genes for chloroplast proteins (like *LHCB1.2*) seen in seedlings treated with the herbicide norflurazon (NF), which causes oxidative damage and arrest of chloroplast biogenesis, is thought to involve biogenic plastid signals (reviewed in: Kleine and Leister, 2016). Accordingly, mutant *A. thaliana* seedlings

(the so-called "genomes uncoupled" (gun) mutants) have been identified that continued to express *LHCB1.2* although plastid development had been blocked by treatment with NF (Susek *et al.*, 1993). The mutated proteins were identified and four of them (GUN2 to GUN5), as well as another (GUN6) identified later by screening activation-tagged lines, were found to be involved in tetrapyrrole biosynthesis (Mochizuki *et al.*, 2001; Larkin *et al.*, 2003; Woodson *et al.*, 2011). These findings clearly pointed to the involvement of tetrapyrroles in biogenic plastid signalling, but exactly how these compounds contribute to signalling remains under debate (Mochizuki *et al.*, 2001; Strand *et al.*, 2003; Mochizuki *et al.*, 2008; Moulin *et al.*, 2008). Currently, heme – specifically the fraction produced by ferrochelatase 1 (FC1/GUN6) – is thought to be the tetrapyrrole that is relevant for plastid signalling (Woodson *et al.*, 2011).

In contrast to all other gun mutants, the gun1 mutation suppresses not only the effects of NF, but also those of the plastid translation inhibitor lincomycin (LIN) on LHCB1.2 expression, providing a link between tetrapyrrole- and plastid gene expression (PGE)-related signalling. GUN1 is a PPR-SMR (pentatricopeptide repeat-small MutS-related) protein (Koussevitzky et al., 2007). Other proteins containing such domains regulate PGE (Liu et al., 2013) and GUN1 is therefore thought to integrate signals derived from perturbations in the redox state, PGE, and tetrapyrrole biosynthesis in seedlings (Koussevitzky et al., 2007). It was recently shown that GUN1 also acts in adult plants, where it contributes to operational control of chloroplasts (Tadini et al., 2016) and developmental transitions (Wu et al., 2018). Notably, GUN1 interacts with proteins involved in plastid protein homeostasis (proteostasis) and with enzymes of tetrapyrrole biosynthesis rather than nucleic acids (Tadini et al., 2016) and double mutants defective in both GUN1 and PGE (or Clp protease activity) are seedling lethal, implying that GUN1 plays a role in maintaining proteostasis in chloroplasts (Tadini et al., 2016; Llamas et al., 2017). GUN1 itself accumulates to detectable levels only at very early stages of leaf development, and its rapid turnover is controlled mainly by the chaperone ClpC1 (Wu et al., 2018).

In this work, we explore the link between GUN1 and plastid proteostasis by studying the relationship between GUN1 and the chloroplast translation initiation factor IF-2 (cpIF2/FUG1), which is co-expressed with *GUN1* at the mRNA level and is also detected in co-immunoprecipitates of tagged GUN1 (Tadini *et al.*, 2016). We combine this with a comprehensive analysis of the effects of the *gun1* mutation on the total transcriptome and proteome. We find that mutations in GUN1 and FUG1 elicit very similar transcriptome profiles. As observed previously in other mutants that affect plastid proteostasis (Tadini *et al.*, 2016).

2016; Llamas *et al.*, 2017; Paieri *et al.*, 2018), the impact of the *fug1-3* mutation, which depresses FUG1 levels and perturbs plastid translation, are exacerbated by lack of GUN1. In line with this observation, lack of GUN1 exerts an epistatic effect on the *variegated2* (*var2*) mutation (Chen *et al.*, 2000), acting as an enhancer, while *fug1* suppresses the *var2* phenotype (Miura *et al.*, 2007). Together, these findings support the notion that GUN1 is a modulator of plastid proteostasis, whose function only becomes clearly manifest when plastid proteostasis is perturbed.

# RESULTS

**GUN1 becomes critical for photosynthesis and growth when FUG1 function is impaired** We have previously identified the chloroplast translation initiation factor IF-2, or FUG1, as a GUN1 interactor based on co-immunoprecipitation and co-expression analysis (Tadini *et al.*, 2016). To further investigate the functional relationship between these two proteins, we crossed the *gun1-103* knock-out mutant (Sun *et al.*, 2011; Page *et al.*, 2017) and the *fug1-3* knock-down mutant (Miura *et al.*, 2007) and identified homozygous *fug1-3 gun1-103* double mutants in the F2 generation (**Figure S1**). Note that knock-out of *FUG1* results in embryo lethality (Miura *et al.*, 2007).

At early stages of development, *fug1-3 gun1-103* seedlings displayed a chlorotic phenotype associated with a significant decrease in maximum photosystem (PSII) quantum yield (Fv/Fm) when compared to wild type (Col-0) and the respective single mutants (**Figure** 1a, b). A similar phenotype was observed when crossing *fug1-3* with a second knock-out allele of gun1, gun1-102 (Tadini et al., 2016) (Figure S2). At later developmental stages, the additive phenotype of *fug1-3 gun1-103* seedlings becomes less pronounced, and by 6 days after germination, the *fug1-3 gun1-103* mutant is more similar to the parental line *fug1-3*, which showed a slight retardation of growth and a lower Fv/Fm than both WT and gun1-103 (Figure 1). However, after 4 weeks of growth, at the transition to the flower stage, the additive phenotype of *fug1-3 gun1-103* re-appeared, with the new emerging leaves being clearly chlorotic compared to the only slightly chlorotic leaves of *fug1-3* (Figure 1c). Moreover, gun1-103 and fug1-3 gun1-103 plants exhibited a delay in flowering time, which is compatible with the higher leaf numbers at the bolting stage in these two genotypes (Figure S3), corroborating previous results obtained with gun1 (Wu et al., 2018). Overexpression of GUN1 (in the range of 300 to 400% of WT mRNA levels) in *fug1-3* fails to alter the effects of the fug1-3 mutation on Fv/Fm in seedlings (Figure S4).

Thus, absence of GUN1 enhances the phenotypic effects of the *fug1* knock-down especially at the seedling stage during the transition from heterotrophic to autotrophic growth, and again at the transition to the generative stage. Conversely, overexpression of GUN1 could not attenuate the phenotypic effects of the *fug1* knock-down, possibly because the levels of the GUN1 protein were not sufficiently increased in these lines. Given FUG1's role in the translation of plastid proteins, this points to a function of GUN1 in protein proteostasis which becomes apparent in situations where a high rate of protein synthesis is required.

### **GUN1 and FUG1 are required for effective cold acclimation**

Defects in plastid translational capacity affect the acclimation of plants to cold, as indicated by the increased sensitivity to cold stress displayed by mutants with defects in plastid ribosomal activity, such as  $\Delta rpl33$ , rps5, rdb1, rh50 or crass (Rogalski *et al.*, 2008; Wang *et al.*, 2016; Zhang *et al.*, 2016; Paieri *et al.*, 2018; Pulido *et al.*, 2018). The gun5 mutant also shows a cold-sensitive phenotype, which is probably due to a decrease in overall protein synthesis (Kindgren *et al.*, 2015).

When plants were grown after germination on  $\frac{1}{2}$  MS medium supplemented with 1% sucrose for 6 weeks at 4°C, the double mutant *fug1-3 gun1-103* accumulated less chlorophyll and displayed lower *Fv/Fm* values than WT plants (**Figure 2a**). After transferring plants to 22°C for 4 days, chlorophyll content and PSII efficiency were restored to nearly the same levels as were observed in plants grown without cold stress (see **Figure 1**). Interestingly, the single mutants *gun1-103* and *fug1-3* also displayed a cold acclimation phenotype, albeit less pronounced than that in *fug1-3 gun1-103* (**Figure 2**, **Figure S5**). Control plants defective for specific plastid ribosomal proteins (*prpl11-1* and *prps17-1*) were even more severely affected than *fug1-3 gun1-103* by the cold treatment, which killed *prps17-1* plants.

These results indicate that FUG1 is required for effective cold acclimation, as expected for a component of the plastid translation machinery. GUN1 is also required for successful cold acclimation, particularly when FUG1 levels are decreased, again implying a role for GUN1 in plastid proteostasis.

### Lack of GUN1 does not aggravate the protein synthesis defect of *fug1-3*

The additive phenotype observed when GUN1 is absent and the abundance of the chloroplast translation initiation factor FUG1 decreased, which is particularly evident in the cold, points to an enhanced defect in chloroplast protein synthesis in the double mutant. Therefore, the synthesis of the plastid-encoded D1 protein was quantified as an indicator of the efficiency of mRNA translation in chloroplasts, employing *in-vivo* labelling of chloroplast proteins with [<sup>35</sup>S]Met. In the single mutant *fug1-3*, the D1 synthesis rate, normalized to that of the nucleus-encoded LHCII, has been shown to be markedly lower than in WT at early labelling points (5 min), whereas at the steady-state level, no differences between *fug1-3* and WT were detectable for D1 based on immunoblot analysis (Miura *et al.*, 2007). Using 4-day-old seedlings, we also observed a delay in chloroplast protein synthesis in *fug1-3*, while the *gun1-103* mutant behaved like WT as described before (Tadini *et al.*, 2016) (**Figure 3a, b**). In the double mutant *fug1-3 gun1-103*, the rate of D1/D2 synthesis was similar to that found in *fug1-3*, with only a slightly enhanced reduction at 15 min after labelling, indicating that lack of GUN1 does not aggravate the effects of impaired translation initiation (caused by *fug1-3*) on the overall chloroplast protein synthesis rate.

Polysome loading of the *psaA* mRNA encoding the A subunit of photosystem I (PSI) was studied by isolating polysomes from leaves, centrifuging extracts on sucrose gradients, extracting RNAs from gradient fractions and subjecting them to Northern analysis to detect *psaA* mRNA. In WT and *gun1-103, psaA* mRNA is efficiently loaded onto polysomes and hence migrates deep into the Suc gradient (**Figure 3c**), as described before (Tadini *et al.*, 2016), whereas the low-molecular weight fractions are more enriched in *fug1-3* and *fug1-3 gun1-103* compared to WT and *gun1-103* plants. This reduced sedimentation rate of the *psaA* mRNA indicates a reduction in the number of associated ribosomes and implies that translation initiation and/or a step very early in elongation is affected (McCormac and Barkan, 1999). This result is in line with the function of FUG1 in chloroplast translation initiation and indicates that the defect caused by reduced FUG1 levels is not enhanced by concomitant removal of GUN1 in the *fug1-3* background. Similar polysome loading results were obtained after cold treatment of the different genotypes (**Figure S6**).

Taken together, concomitant lack of GUN1 and reduced amounts of FUG1 fail to provide an additive effect on chloroplast protein synthesis or on translation initiation. Therefore, GUN1 appears not to act at the level of translation initiation like FUG1, but probably influences other steps of plastid proteostasis.

### Lack of GUN1 increases the fraction of white sectors in var2 mutant leaves

The *yellow variegated2* (*var2*) mutant, which is defective in the chloroplast protease FtsH2, displays a variegated phenotype characterized by green- and white-sectored leaves (Chen *et al.*, 2000; Takechi *et al.*, 2000) – and FUG1 was initially identified in a screen for suppressors of *var2*-dependent variegation (Miura *et al.*, 2007). Moreover, mutation of SVR7 or SOT1 – which like GUN1 are plastid-localized PPR-SMR proteins (Liu *et al.*, 2013) – also suppresses variegation in the *var2* background (Liu *et al.*, 2010; Wu *et al.*, 2016).

To clarify whether GUN1 behaves like FUG1 or the other two PPR-SMR proteins SVR7 and SOT1 in the context of leaf variegation, we generated the double mutant gun1-103 *var2*. While *fug1-3 var2* showed improved growth relative to *var2*, in *gun1-103 var2* growth was dramatically impaired (Figure 4). Strikingly, while the variegated leaf phenotype of *var2* was suppressed by *fug1* mutations, which caused the white sectors to disappear and true leaves were only slightly paler in comparison to WT at early developmental stages (Miura et al., 2007), the true leaves of gun1-103 var2 were completely white. This supports the conclusion that GUN1 has a different function in PGE than FUG1, as suggested by the results of the polysome loading analysis (see Figure 3). Indeed, in light of the notion that the var2dependent leaf variegation is due to decreased protein degradation, which can be compensated for by a decrease in protein synthesis rate, as in case in *fug1-3* (Miura *et al.*, 2007), the increase in the fraction of white sectors in gun1-103 var2 could be understood in terms of the gun1 mutation either enhancing protein synthesis or decreasing protein degradation. Because the gun1 mutation has been found to stabilise PRPS1 levels (without enhancing its expression) in gun1 prps1 double mutants (Tadini et al., 2016), a gun1dependent decrease in protein degradation is the most plausible explanation for the observed phenotype of gun1-103 var2.

## Retrograde signalling is not affected by decreased levels of FUG1

The clearly opposing epistatic effects of the *gun1* and *fug1* mutations on *var2* suggest that GUN1 and FUG1 might have antagonistic effects on proteostasis in chloroplasts, prompting the question whether this might be associated with corresponding changes in their retrograde signalling phenotypes. If this were the case, then the double mutant *fug1-3 gun1-103* should display an attenuated *gun* phenotype compared to *gun1-103*, balancing the effects on retrograding signalling caused by the two single mutations. To test this, we analysed the *gun* phenotypes of *fug1-3* and *fug1-3 gun1-103* in terms of *LHCB1.2* mRNA expression in the presence of LIN or NF. This analysis was done by Northern blot and RT-qPCR in seedlings

grown on MS supplemented with 1% sucrose for 6 days in long-day conditions or 2% of sucrose for 5 days under continuous light (**Figure S7**). The level of *LHB1.2* mRNA expression in *fug1-3* seedlings treated with LIN or NF was similar to that in Col-0, while in the case of *fug1-3 gun1-103*, it was similar to *gun1-103*; occasionally, we observed a slightly enhanced *gun* phenotype in the double mutant under LIN treatment.

Thus, although FUG1 and GUN1 might have opposite functions in chloroplast proteostasis, this antagonism is not reflected in their retrograde signalling phenotype, as monitored by *LHCB1.2* mRNA expression in the presence of inhibitors of chloroplast carotenoid or protein synthesis. Therefore, the clearly opposing epistatic effects of the *gun1* and *fug1* mutations on *var2* might not, or not only, related to antagonistic effects of GUN1 and FUG1 on proteostasis in chloroplasts, but also to the fact that GUN1 has an additional role in plastid signalling.

Impact of lack of GUN1 and/or decreased FUG1 levels at the whole transcriptome level To unravel the molecular effects of impaired GUN1 and/or FUG1 functions and to study the similarities and differences between these effects at the systems level, comparative transcriptome and proteome analyses were performed on *gun1-103* and *fug1-3*, as well as on the double mutant *fug1-3 gun1-103*. For the comparative analysis of transcriptomes, deep sequencing of RNA (RNA-Seq) of each genotype was done with three biological replicates from 4-day-old plants, acquiring approximately 3 GB output per sample on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Of these reads, on average about 96% for wild-type plants, 96% for *gun1-103*, 80.17% for *fug1-3* and 96% for *fug1-3 gun1-103* mutant plants could be mapped to the TAIR10 release of the Arabidopsis reference genome. Genes with transcripts showing a llog<sub>2</sub>(ratio)| relative to the wild type (Col-0) greater than 1 (i.e. more than 2-fold up- or down-regulated) and with an adjusted p-value (p-adj) < 0.05 were considered as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). A total of 2049 DEGs (1226 up- and 823 down-regulated) were found in *fug1-3 gun1-103*. This number is larger than the figures for the single mutants, with 699 (464/235 up/down) in *gun1-103*, and 1561 (881/680 up/down) in *fug1-3* (**Table S1**).

Analysis of the (predicted) subcellular locations of the DEG products in the two single mutants and the double mutant revealed that genes whose products localize to chloroplasts and particularly in thylakoids are preferentially down-regulated in all three genotypes (**Figure 5**, **Table S2**). This is not unexpected, since both mutations primarily affect the chloroplast, and would be expected to induce a compensatory response predominantly in the same compartment.

Comparison of the transcript profiles of the three different genotypes showed that a core set of 489 DEGs was shared amongst all the three genotypes. Pairwise comparisons showed that 536 (77%) of the 699 DEGs in *gun1-103* and 1050 (67%) of the 1561 DEGs in *fug1-3* were shared with the double mutant *fug1-3 gun1-103* (**Figure 6a**). The two single mutants *gun1-103* and *fug1-3* shared 563 DEGs, representing 80% and 36% of their total numbers of DEGs, respectively. Strikingly, the change in expression of all 1171 genes common to these sets showed the same trend (up/up or down/down) in the three genotypes, as expected if a common biological process were perturbed in all of them (**Figure 6b**). This high level of co-regulation of transcript abundances between the three genotypes prompted us to compare the transcript profiles of the three genotypes with publicly available transcript profiles in the Genevestigator database (**Figure 6c**). This analysis showed that our three genotypes display a distinct transcriptomic response that differs from those of other mutants affected in chloroplast functions

To investigate which biological processes are affected at the transcript level in response to the (double) mutations, gene ontology (GO) analysis of DEGs was performed (**Figure S8, Table S2**). By comparing the first five non-redundant enriched GO terms, lack of GUN1 resulted in enriched up-regulation of genes for nematode response, some catabolic processes and response to oxidative stress, whereas photosynthetic genes are preferentially down-regulated. Decreased FUG1 levels led to a response similar to that observed in *gun1-103*, except that response to auxin or endogenous stimulus were also down-regulated at the transcript level. In the double mutant, the same gene classes as in the single mutants are regulated, with a few notable exceptions: genes involved in antibiotic-induced catabolic processes are not preferentially up-regulated in the *gun1-103* and *fug1-3* single mutants, whereas two classes of genes are preferentially down-regulated in both *fug1-3* and the double mutant, but not in *gun1-103*. Moreover, translation and generation of precursor metabolites and energy are specifically down-regulated in the double mutant.

Taken together, these data show that the *gun1-103* and *fug1-3* mutations cause very similar transcriptional responses, which are distinct from those provoked by other chloroplast mutations.

### Impact of lack of GUN1 and/or decreased FUG1 levels at the proteome level

For proteomic data analysis, total proteins were prepared from leaves of 4-day-old plants and 2316 different proteins were identified and quantified, including 2269 proteins encoded by the nuclear genome, 42 by the chloroplast genome and 5 by the mitochondrial genome. As for the DEGs, proteins with  $|\log_2(\text{ratio})|$  relative to the wild type (Col-0) larger than 1 and with an adjusted p-value (p-adj) < 0.05 were designated as differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). A total of 275 DEPs (101/174 up/down) were found in the double mutant *fug1-3 gun1-103*, considerably more than in the single mutants *gun1-103* and *fug1-3*, with 5 (3/2 up/down) and 60 (32/28 up/down) DEPs, respectively (**Table S3**).

As expected for the *fug1-3* mutant with a defect in chloroplast protein translation, a disproportionately large number of chloroplast proteins was found to be less abundant than in WT (**Figure 5**). Removal of GUN1 in the *fug1-3* background clearly increased the number of DEPs. Of the five DEPs found for *gun1-103* vs. WT, only one is localized to plastids (Ferritin-4; AT2G40300), whereas the other DEPs are (according to SUBA4) localized to the cytosol (AT5G15960, stress-induced protein KIN1 and AT3G52990, pyruvate kinase), cytosol/nucleus (AT2G30050/AT3G01340, protein transport protein SEC13 homolog A/B), vacuole (AT5G47020, uncharacterized protein) or extracellular (AT5G59320, non-specific lipid-transfer protein 3).

Comparison of the distribution of DEPs among the genotypes revealed that only two DEPs, the stress-induced protein KIN1 and the non-specific lipid-transfer protein 3, are shared among all the three genotypes (**Figure 6a**). Moreover, pairwise comparisons showed that 4 out of 5 DEPs of *gun1* and 44 (73%) of the 60 DEPs in *fug1-3* were also found in *fug1-3 gun1-103*. As in the case of changes in transcript levels, the change in expression of almost all members in these common DEP sets showed the same trend (up/up or down/down) (**Figure 6b**).

To investigate which biological processes are regulated at the protein level in response to the (double) mutations, gene ontology (GO) analysis of DEPs was performed (**Figure S8, Table S2**). Enrichment of proteins associated with certain functional classes was easily detectable for DEPs in the double mutant. Here, proteins involved in generation of precursor metabolites and energy, or in photosynthesis (light reactions) were clearly down-regulated, whereas proteins involved in responses to oxygen-containing compounds and heat and in glutamate catabolic process were up-regulated. Due to the small number of DEPs in *gun1-103* no enrichment could be detected, whereas DEPs from *fug1-3* associated with water stress response and lipid transportation were up-regulated (**Figure S6**)

### Thylakoid and ribosomal protein levels are down-regulated in *gun1 fug1* seedlings

A closer inspection of the DEG and DEP profiles of the three genotypes for components of the light reactions of photosynthesis and the plastid ribosome allowed us to recognize a major trend (**Figure 7**): in *gun1-103* and *fug1-3* largely the same sets of transcripts are significantly down-regulated, but without any (*gun1-103*) or only mild consequences (*fug1-3*) at the protein level. This down-regulation at the transcript level is enhanced in the double mutant, and transcripts derived from additional genes are significantly decreased in their abundance. In contrast to the two single mutants, in *fug1-3 gun1-103* the downregulation of transcripts is associated with decreased levels of the corresponding proteins and of proteins for which the corresponding transcripts are not down-regulated (e.g. PsaF and PsaO subunits of PSI; PsbC and PsbT of PSII; PetB, C and D from the cytochrome  $b_6f$  complex, RPS1, 5, 6, 10, 13 and 20; RPL13, 14, 16, 17, 18, 28 and 35).

In addition, some notable exceptions to this general trend are observed. In *fug1-3* but not in *gun1-103*, some chloroplast genes were significantly up-regulated at the transcript level (e.g. *psbA*, *psbB* and *psbC*; *petB* and *petD*; *rps11*, *19*; *rpl22* and *33*). This significant up-regulation was mostly maintained in the double mutant (*psbA*, *rps19*, *rpl22* and *rpl33*) and additional transcripts were found to be significantly increased compared to WT (*rps3* and *16*). However, the up-regulation of these chloroplast transcripts was not reflected at the protein level; instead, some of them were actually down-regulated at the protein level (e.g. PsbA in *fug1-3* and the double mutant; PetD in *fug1-3*; RPS3, RPS19, RPL22, RPL33 in the double mutant).

Together, this indicates that lack of GUN1 strongly enhances the effects of the *fug1-3* mutation at the proteome level. Decreased levels of FUG1 cause a specific response at the transcript level, with down-regulation predominating, while some plastid transcripts are up-regulated. This specific up-regulation of plastid genes persists even in the absence of GUN1, but is associated with decreased levels of the encoded proteins.

### DISCUSSION

Lack of GUN1 aggravates the effects of disturbances in chloroplast proteostasis Under normal physiological conditions, lack of GUN1 alone has no obvious effect on photosynthesis or growth. Accordingly, proteome profiling of *gun1-103* seedlings revealed as few as five differentially expressed proteins (see **Figures 5** and **6**). However, lack of GUN1 can markedly exacerbate the effects of other mutations that alter plastid proteostasis. These include mutations in components of the chloroplast ribosome (PRPS17, PRPL11, PRPL24/SVR8; Tadini *et al.*, 2016; Llamas *et al.*, 2017; Paieri *et al.*, 2018), plastid RNA processing (exoribonuclease RIF10; Llamas *et al.*, 2017), plastid ribosomal RNA maturation (RNA helicase RH50; Paieri *et al.*, 2018), and chloroplast protease activity (ClpR1; Llamas *et al.*, 2017). Here, we showed that loss of GUN1 also worsens defects in chloroplast translation initiation (*fug1-3*) and variegation/protein degradation (*var2/ftsh2*). The fact that the mutations whose phenotypes are intensified in *gun1* mutants all impinge on chloroplast proteostasis allows us to conclude that GUN1 is itself involved in chloroplast proteostasis, but makes it very difficult to further narrow down its function.

This situation is complicated even more by two additional findings. 1. Absence of *GUN1* can also *suppress* the phenotypes of certain mutations that disrupt chloroplast proteostasis, including *prps1* (which affects in the S1 subunit of the plastid ribosome) and *prors1*, with reduced levels of a chloroplast tRNA synthetase (Tadini *et al.*, 2016; Paieri *et al.*, 2018). At least in the case of *gun1 prps1*, this suppressor effect could be assigned to an increased accumulation of PRPS1, probably resulting from decreased degradation of the protein (Tadini *et al.*, 2016). 2. Other mutants display *gun1*-like epistatic effects when combined with mutations affecting chloroplast proteostasis. These include *rh50*, which like *gun1* enhances the phenotypes of certain plastid ribosome mutants and suppresses the phenotypes of both *prps1* and *prors1* (Paieri *et al.*, 2018).

Therefore, analysis of genetic interactions between gun1 and other mutations in chloroplast proteostasis has clear limitations as a strategy for identifying the molecular function of GUN1, because the phenotypes observed are neither specific to gun1 (since unrelated mutants like rh50 can phenocopy gun1 in such experiments) nor are they restricted to a distinct type of chloroplast proteostasis component.

### Functions of GUN1 and FUG1 appear to be closely related

In light of the limitations of double mutant analyses highlighted above, what do the results obtained here for *fug1 gun1* double mutants tell us about the molecular function of GUN1? First of all, FUG1 is not just another factor that is relevant for chloroplast proteostasis. Its close relationship with GUN1 is evidenced by the co-expression of GUN1 and FUG1 mRNAs and by the physical association of the two proteins revealed by coimmunoprecipitation experiments (Tadini et al., 2016). The additive phenotype of the fug1-3 gun1-103 double mutant is relatively subtle and becomes evident only at developmental stages in which GUN1 is normally expressed (Wu et al., 2018), which may imply a specific functional interaction. Conversely, the transcriptome changes caused by the gun1-103 and *fug1-3* mutants are strikingly similar: there is 80% overlap between the sets of transcripts that are significantly altered in the two mutants, and these changes are always in the same direction (see Figure 6). This clearly indicates that the defects caused by the two mutations must be similar, although translation initiation itself appears to be unaffected by lack of GUN1 alone, as demonstrated by the *in-vivo* labelling of thylakoid proteins and polysome loading assays (see Figure 3). One observation, however, is in conflict with the notion that GUN1 and FUG1 have similar functions, namely the contrasting effects of gun1-103 and *fug1-3* on the variegation phenotype of *var2* (see Figure 4). To accommodate the enhancer effect of gun1 on the var2 phenotype within the concept developed by Miura et al. (2007), one must either assume that protein synthesis is enhanced or protein degradation is decreased in the absence of GUN1 – and the latter possibility agrees with previous finding that PRPS1 levels are increased in the gunl prps1 double mutant relative to the prps1 line (Tadini et al., 2016). However, epistatic interactions leading to enhanced variegation of *var2* mutants are complex and can also involve defects in cytosolic translation (Wang et al., 2018), such that the *var2* enhancer function of *gun1* might be related to the retrograde signalling function of GUN1 rather than to its function in chloroplast proteostasis. With respect to retrograding signalling, it is clear that FUG1 is not involved in this process, because decreased levels of FUG1 fail to alter the gun phenotype (see Figure S7), and the expression of GLKs -- that are transcription factors involved in gun signalling downstream of GUN1 (Waters et al., 2009; Leister and Kleine, 2016; Martín et al., 2016) -- is not altered in fug1-3 gun1-103 mutant (see Table S3).

Although the steady-state levels of only five proteins are altered in the absence of GUN1, future experiments should address whether GUN1 has an effect on cytosolic translation rate. Furthermore, it needs to be established whether overexpression of *GLK* in the *var2* background has the same effects as the one seen in the *gun1 var2* mutant to clarify whether gun signalling per se or other chloroplast-specific functions of GUN1 interact with the *var2* phenotype. Moreover, while overexpression of *GUN1* appears to slightly attenuate the effects of the *fug1-3* mutation (see **Figure S4**), it remains to be shown whether overexpression of *GUN1* in the *var2* background exerts a similar effect.

## GUN1 is a conditional modulator of plastid proteostasis

Our results indicate that, apart from its subtle role in regulating flowering and de-etiolation under strong light or after prolonged periods of dark growth, the function of GUN1 becomes critical for the plant under normal physiological conditions when plastid proteostasis is perturbed by other mutations. In such cases, the *gun1* mutation enhances the effects of other mutations, as seen in the *fug1-3 gun1-103* double mutant in our study (see **Figures 5, 6** and **7**). But at what level of proteostasis does GUN1 act, and does this function only become manifest in other mutant backgrounds? While previous results (Tadini *et al.*, 2016) and the results shown here suggest that GUN1 might promote protein degradation, our data (see **Figure 2**) also show that GUN1 is required for effective cold acclimation. Lack of GUN1 reduces the capacity of plants to cope with prolonged periods of cold, providing a hitherto unknown physiological readout for GUN1 functions that does not require genetic interactions with additional mutations. Future experiments focusing on the cold sensitivity of *gun1* plants might help to identify which aspect of chloroplast proteostasis is primarily impaired by the absence of GUN1, employing the *fug* mutants -- that are very similar in many aspects to *gun1* mutants but without the signalling phenotype -- as control.

# **EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES**

# **Plant Material and Growth Conditions**

The *Arabidopsis thaliana* (ecotype Columbia-0) T-DNA insertion mutant lines *gun1-103* (SAIL\_742\_A11), *gun1-102* (SAIL\_290\_D09), *fug1-3* (SAIL\_209\_E08) and *var2* (SALK\_055162) were described before (Sun *et al.*, 2011; Tadini *et al.*, 2016; Miura *et al.*, 2007; Chen *et al.*, 2006, respectively). The double mutants *fug1-3 gun1-103*, *fug1-3 gun1-102*, *fug1-3 var2* and *gun1-103 var2* were obtained by manually crossing the respective

single mutants and screening F2 plants by PCR of genomic DNA with specific oligonucleotides (see **Table S4**). The *oeGUN1-HA* lines were generated by introducing the *GUN1* coding sequence under the control of the 35S promoter from *Cauliflower mosaic virus* into *fug1-3* using the vector pGWB14 (Nakagawa *et al.*, 2007). Additional mutant lines used in this work were: *prpl11-1* (Pesaresi *et al.*, 2001), *prps17-1* (Romani *et al.* 2012), *gun5* (Mochizuki *et al.*, 2001), *gun1-9* (Koussevitzky *et al.*, 2007), and the GUN1 overexpressor line oeGUN1-GFP (Tadini *et al.*, 2016).

Surface-sterilized wild-type (Col-0) and mutant seeds were plated on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 1% (w/v) sucrose and 1% (w/v) agar. After stratification for 2 days in darkness at 4 °C, plants were grown at 22 °C under long-day conditions (LD, 16 h light/8 h dark) using white light (100  $\mu$ mol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>). Four-day-old seedlings were either harvested or transferred onto soil and maintained under the same LD conditions. For *gun* phenotype analysis, MS plates containing 1% or 2% sucrose were supplemented with 5  $\mu$ M norflurazon (NF) or 0.5 mM lincomycin (LIN) and seedlings were grown for 6 days under LD conditions or 5 days under continuous light. For cold-stress treatments, seeds were germinated directly at 4 °C in a LED climate chamber (16 h light/8 h dark, 100  $\mu$ mol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) and, after 6 weeks at that temperature, were kept for 4 days at 22 °C under the same long-day conditions. For growth measurements, leaf area was determined with ImageJ (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/index.html).

## Chlorophyll a Fluorescence Measurements

*In vivo* chlorophyll *a* fluorescence was measured using an Imaging PAM chlorophyll fluorimeter (Imaging PAM, M-Series; Walz, Effeltrich, Germany). The maximum PSII quantum yield [determined as the ratio of variable fluorescence (*Fv*) to maximal fluorescence (*Fm*), *Fv/Fm*] was measured after dark adaptation of the plants for 30 min, applying a single pulse (0.8 s) of saturating light (5,000 µmol photons  $m^{-2} s^{-1}$ ) as described previously (Pulido *et al.*, 2018).

## **Pigment Analysis**

Seedlings were weighed, and incubated in 1 ml 80% (v/v) acetone overnight at 4 °C. After extraction, chlorophyll levels were measured spectrophotometrically as described (Wellburn, 1994), and normalized to fresh weight.

### **Nucleic Acid Analyses**

Genomic DNA was isolated from rosette leaves using 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1 M NaCl, 0.5 M EDTA and 10% (w/v) SDS extraction buffer. Total RNA was extracted from seedlings using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA samples were treated with DNase I (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and, in the case of transcriptome analysis, purified using Direct-zol<sup>™</sup> RNA MiniPrep Plus columns (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Transcript levels were measured by realtime qPCR or Northern blot. For qPCR, cDNA was synthesized from 0.5 µg RNA with the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Real-time qPCR was performed with specific oligonucleotides (see Table S4) and iQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) in an IQ5 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) following a standard thermal profile (95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 20 s). All reactions were performed in triplicate on three biological replicates, and the relative level of each transcript was referred to the level of the UBIQUITIN or ACTIN transcript. For Northern blot analysis, 5 µg of total RNA from each sample was electrophoresed on a 2% agarose gel containing formaldehyde, and then blotted onto a hybond-N<sup>+</sup> nylon membrane (Amersham Biosciences, Little Chalfont, United Kingdom). Membranes were hybridized with <sup>32</sup>P-labeled cDNA probes (primers used to amplify the probes are listed in Table S4). Signals were quantified with ImageJ (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/index.html).

### In Vivo Translation Assay

In vivo labelling of chloroplast proteins was performed essentially as described (Meurer *et al.*, 1998). Four-day-old seedlings (100 mg) were pre-incubated in 2 ml of labelling solution containing 20  $\mu$ g ml<sup>-1</sup> cycloheximide, 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 6.8), 10 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 20 mM KCl, and 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20, for 30 min under a light intensity of 20  $\mu$ mol m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> to block cytosolic translation. Then [<sup>35</sup>S]Met was added to the buffer (0.1 mCi ml<sup>-1</sup>) and samples were collected after 5, 15, and 30 min of incubation under the same light intensity. Proteins from thylakoid membranes were extracted (Zhang *et al.*, 1999) and subjected to SDS-PAGE on a 15% polyacrylamide gel. Signals were detected and quantified using a PhosphoImager (GE Healthcare Life Sciences; www3.gehealthcare.com) and the program Image Quant (GE Healthcare Life Sciences).

### **Polysome Analyses**

Polysomes were isolated from 4-day-old seedlings growth under long-day conditions or after cold treatment (6 weeks at  $4^{\circ}C + 4$  days at  $22^{\circ}C$ ) as described (Barkan, 1993). RNA gel blot analysis of gradient fractions was performed as described above. The *psaA* probe was amplified with specific primers indicated in **Table S4**.

# Deep RNA-Sequencing (RNA-Seq) and Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 4-day-old wild-type and *gun1-103*, *fug1-3*, and *fug1-3 gun1-103* seedlings (three biological replicates for each genotype), as described above. RNA integrity and quality were assessed by spectrophotometry, agarose gel electrophoresis and by the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only RNAs with an Integrity Number (RIN)  $\geq$  7 were further processed. Generation of RNA-Seq libraries and long-non-coding RNA (lncRNA) sequencing using the paired-end mode were performed at Novogene Biotech (Beijing, China) with standard Illumina protocols. The RNA-Seq libraries were sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq 2500 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

RNA-Seq datasets were analysed as follows: adaptor removal and sequencing quality was carried out with Trimmomatic (Bolger *et al.*, 2014) and FastQC (Andrews, 2010), respectively. Reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis genome (TAIR10) with the gapped-read mapper Tophat 2.1.1 (Kim *et al.*, 2013) for first read fr-unstranded libraries and adjusting the maximum intron length to 5000 bp. Reads were counted with featureCounts (Liao *et al.*, 2014) according to the gene annotation Araport11 (www.araport.org/data/araport11; Krishnakumar *et al.*, 2017). Differentially expressed genes were identified with DESeq2 (Love *et al.*, 2014) by comparing mutants against the wild type (Col-0) and applying a cut-off log<sub>2</sub> fold-change greater than |1| and a *P* value threshold of less than 0.05, calculated with the multiple testing Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for false-discovery-rate (FDR) (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). All of these analyses were performed on a Galaxy platform (Giardine *et al.*, 2005).

### **Proteomics and LC-MS/MS Analysis**

Total proteins were extracted from 4-day-old wild type, *gun1-103*, *fug1-3* and *fug1-3 gun1-103* seedlings (four biological replicates for each genotype) by grinding 200-mg aliquots of frozen plant material into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. After suspending the powder in 1 ml of extraction buffer (100 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM DTT, 1% (w/v) SDS, 1X Roche cOmplete<sup>TM</sup> Protease Inhibitor Cocktail), each sample was sonicated (3

cycles, 10 s ON/OFF) using a Branson Sonifier B-12 (Branson Ultrasonics, Danbury, CT, USA). Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 10, 000 g for 30 min. Proteins were then precipitated in chloroform-methanol (Wessel and Flugge, 1984), pellets solubilized in 6 M guanidine hydrochloride and protein concentration was determined with the bicinchoninic assay (BCA, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Proteome aliquots of 100  $\mu$ g were reduced with 10 mM DTT (30 min, 37 °C), alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetamide (30 min, room temperature, in the dark), and purified by chloroform-methanol precipitation before trypsin digestion (proteome/enzyme ratio 100:1 w/w) at 37 °C overnight. Resulting peptides were desalted with home-made C18 stage tips (Rappsilber *et al.*, 2003), vacuum dried to near dryness and stored at –80 °C.

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was performed on a nano-LC system (Ultimate 3000 RSLC, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled to an Impact II high-resolution Q-TOF (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) using a CaptiveSpray nano ESI source (Bruker Daltonics). The nano-LC system was equipped with an Acclaim Pepmap nano-trap column (C18, 100 Å, 100  $\mu$ m × 2 cm) and an Acclaim Pepmap RSLC analytical column (C18, 100 Å, 75  $\mu$ m × 50 cm), both from ThermoFisher Scientific. The peptide mixture was separated over a 150-min linear gradient of 5-45% (v/v) acetonitrile at a constant flow rate of 250 nl / min. The column was kept at 50°C in a column oven throughout the run. MS1 spectra with a mass range from m/z 200–2000 were acquired at 3 Hz, and the 18 most intense peaks were selected for MS/MS analysis using an intensity-dependent spectrum acquisition rate of between 4 and 16 Hz. Dynamic exclusion duration was 0.5 min.

Raw MS files were processed using MaxQuant software (version 1.6.1.0) (Cox and Mann, 2008). Peak lists were searched against the *Arabidopsis thaliana* Uniprot database (www.uniprot.org, version February 2017) using the built-in Andromeda search engine (Cox *et al.*, 2011). Trypsin was defined as the digestion enzyme specificity, allowing a maximum of two missed cleavages. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as the static modification, and methionine oxidation and N-terminal acetylation as variable modifications. Sequences of 248 common contaminant proteins and decoy sequences were automatically added during the search. FDR was set to 1% for both peptides and proteins. The match-between-runs option was enabled, and proteins were quantified across samples using the label-free quantification (LFQ) algorithm (Cox *et al.*, 2014) with default settings. Downstream statistical and bioinformatics analysis were performed in Perseus (version 1.6.1.3; Tyanova *et al.*, 2016) and R (version 3.5.0, Team, 2018). Potential contaminants, proteins identified only by site

modification, and reverse hits were excluded from further analysis. Protein groups were retained if they were quantified in at least three of the four replicates in at least one genotype. Protein LFQ intensities were log<sub>2</sub>-transformed and missing values imputation of protein intensities was performed from a normal distribution (width: 0.3, down shift: 1.8) within Perseus. The resulting matrix was exported, and quantile normalized using the R package Process (Bolstad, 2018) available via Bioconductor (Gentleman et al., 2004). Significantly differentially abundant protein groups were calculated employing the R/Bioconductor package limma (Ritchie et al., 2015), with P values adjusted for multiple comparisons according to the Benjamini-Hochberg approach (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). Proteins with a  $|\log_2(\text{ratio})|$  relative to the wild type (Col-0) larger than 1 and with an FDR less than 0.05 were considered to show a significant changing or to be differentially expressed. Hierarchical clustering analysis A hierarchical clustering analysis of differentially expressed genes with adjusted p-values < 0.05 was performed using the R package Hclust (Müllner, 2013). Transcriptomic data of gun1-103, fug1-3 and fug1-3 gun1-103 mutants were compared with those of gun5 (Gene Expression Omnibus: GSE12887; Koussevitzky et al., 2007), sig6 (SRA accession number SRA052959; Woodson et al., 2013), prors1-2 (Gene Expression Omnibus: GSE54573; Leister et al., 2014), tpt-1 (Gene Expression Omnibus: GSE5737), ppi2 (Gene Expression Omnibus: GSE17675). Accession Numbers ATG accession numbers: GUN1 (At2g31400), FUG1 (At1g17220), FTSH2 (At2g30950),

PRPL11 (At1g32990), PRPS17 (At1g79850) and GUN5 (At5g13630). RNA-Seq data: Gene Expression Omnibus GSE123583. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE (Vizcaíno *et al.*, 2016) partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD012067.
