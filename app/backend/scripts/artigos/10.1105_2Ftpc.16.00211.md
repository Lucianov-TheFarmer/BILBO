#### **Abstract** 30

Leaf morphogenesis and differentiation are highly flexible processes, resulting in a large 31 diversity of leaf forms. The development of compound leaves involves an extended 32 morphogenesis stage compared to that of simple leaves, and the tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*) 33 mutant *clausa* (*clau*) exposes a potential for extended morphogenesis in tomato leaves. Here we 34 report that the *CLAU* gene encodes a MYB transcription factor that has evolved a unique role in 35 compound-leaved species to promote an exit from the morphogenetic phase of tomato leaf 36 development. We show that CLAU attenuates cytokinin signaling, and that *clau* plants have 37 increased cytokinin sensitivity. The results suggest that flexible leaf patterning involves a 38 coordinated interplay between transcription factors and hormones. 39

#### **Introduction** 41

Leaves are determinate organs, which differentiate to become major providers for the 42 plant following a limited growth period. However, a transient period of indeterminate growth, 43 termed primary morphogenesis, occurs during early stages of leaf development. During this 44 stage, the leaf attains its basic shape, and generates lateral appendages such as leaflets (Dengler 45 and Tsukaya, 2001; Hagemann and Gleissberg, 1996; Kaplan, 2001a). The spatial and temporal 46 extent of this morphogenetic phase responds flexibly to the genetic, developmental and 47 environmental context. This flexibility gives rise to a wide diversity of leaf sizes and shapes that 48 vary within and between species. Compound leaves, such as those of tomato (*Solanum* 49 *lycopersicum*), are composed of multiple leaflets and are characterized by a prolonged 50 morphogenetic phase. This extended morphogenesis is enabled by the activity of a meristematic 51 region at the leaf margin, termed marginal blastozone (Hagemann and Gleissberg, 1996). The 52 morphogenetic stage is particularly long in tomato, resulting in a wide diversity of leaf forms and 53 unique developmental flexibility (Bar and Ori, 2014; Bar et al., 2015; Burko and Ori, 2013). 54 Recent studies have uncovered an even higher potential for prolonged organogenesis in tomato 55 leaf development, which is normally restricted by differentiation-promoting factors (Bar et al., 56 2015; Shani et al., 2010; Yanai et al., 2011). Characterization of mutants with extended or 57 restricted organogenesis has pointed to this potential and led to the identification of factors that 58 balance organogenesis and differentiation during leaf development (Sluis and Hake, 2015; 59 Canales et al., 2010; Efroni et al., 2010; Bar and Ori, 2014; Blein et al., 2010). 60

Transcription factors from the class I knotted1-like homeobox (KNOXI) family were 61 implicated in promoting the extended organogenesis in leaves of many plant species, including 62 tomato (Hareven et al., 1996; Janssen et al., 1998; Kimura et al., 2008; Bharathan et al., 2002; 63 Hay and Tsiantis, 2006; Shani et al., 2009; Barth et al., 2009; Hake et al., 2004; Rast-Somssich et 64 al., 2015). The plant hormone cytokinin acts downstream of KNOXI proteins to promote 65 morphogenesis, mainly during relatively late stages of leaf development (Shani et al., 2010). By 66 contrast, transcription factors from the CIN-TCP (CINNCINATA- TEOSINTE BRANCHED1- 67 CYCLOIDEA-PCF) family, belonging to class II TCPs, restrict the organogenetic window and 68 promote differentiation (Burko et al., 2013; Efroni et al., 2008; Shleizer-Burko et al., 2011; Ori 69 et al., 2007; Palatnik et al., 2003). The hormone gibberellin also promotes differentiation and 70 partially mediates CIN-TCP function in this process (Yanai et al., 2011; Fleishon et al., 2011; 71 Van Tuinen et al., 1999; Jasinski et al., 2008). The ratio of SINGLE FLOWER TRUSS (SFT) 72 and SELF PRUNING (SP) has also been shown to affect the balance between growth and 73 differentiation in many developmental processes including leaf development, in addition to their 74 role in regulating flowering time (Lifschitz et al., 2014; Shalit et al., 2009). 75

c*lausa* (*clau*), first described in the 1960s (Khush and Rick, 1967), is a classical tomato 76 mutant characterized by increased morphogenetic potential that leads to abnormal, excessively 77 divided leaves, often carrying shoot-like structures on the rachis. CLAU is a potential negative 78 regulator of KNOXI genes and of the boundary gene *GOBLET* (*GOB*) (Bar et al., 2015; Avivi et 79 al., 2000; Jasinski et al., 2007, 2008; Naz et al., 2013). 80

CLAU therefore emerges as a factor that puts a hold on the organogenetic potential of 81 tomato leaves, enabling controlled maturation and morphogenesis. Here, we identify CLAU as a 82 unique MYB transcription factor that promotes differentiation and attenuates CK responses. 83

#### **Results** 85

#### *CLAU* **is a unique MYB transcription factor** 86

The classical tomato mutant *clau* possesses highly compound leaves (Figure 1A). We 87 previously quantified the *clau* phenotype, demonstrating that *clau* leaves are on average 11 times 88 more compound than wild type leaves (Bar et al., 2015). The *clau* mutant phenotypes suggest 89 that CLAU plays a central role in modulating the flexibility of leaf morphogenesis. We therefore 90 wished to identify the *CLAU* gene to further understand this flexibility. We obtained eight *clau* 91 alleles (Figure 1B), and for the new alleles, confirmed allelism by complementation tests. Of 92 these, *clau:e3545* and *clau:e2522* were identified in an EMS population (Menda et al., 2004), 93 generated in the *Solanum lycopersicum* M82 background. Leaves of all eight alleles were 94 similar, allowing for slight differences resulting from the different background cultivars 95 (Supplemental Figure 1). *CLAU* was previously mapped to the short arm of chromosome 4 using 96 radiation ablation mutagenesis (Khush and Rick, 1967). We created an F2 mapping population 97 from a cross between *clau:e3545* in the *Solanum lycopersicum* M82 background and *S.* 98 *pimpinellifolium*, and used this mapping population to pinpoint the physical location of *CLAU* 99 between bases 2094866 and 2122247 on the short arm of chromosome 4, which contains two 100 genes. RNA sequencing of shoot apices of the EMS allele *clau*-*e3545*, in comparison to wild- 101 type M82 apices, revealed a mutation in the first methionine codon of a gene encoding a MYB 102 transcription factor (Solyc04g008480, named *CLAU* hereafter), which is physically located 103 within this interval. The mutation is predicted to prevent the formation of any protein from the 104 *CLAU* ORF, which encods a 297 amino acid SHAQKYF-class MYB transcription factor, 105 possessing 6 exons (Figure 1B). *clau* and five additional alleles contain the exact same mutation, 106 an ATG to AAG substitution in the codon for the initiation methionine. *clau-shl* is a deletion 107 allele, where no other annotated genes are deleted, and *clau*-*ff* results from a splicing mutation 108 leading to a truncated protein of 150 amino acids, lacking exons 5 and 6 (Figure 1B). We found 109 *CLAU* homologs in several plant species (Figure 1C and Supplemental Data set 1), including 110 Arabidopsis (*AT1G14600* and *AT2G02060*). Figure 1C depicts a phylogenetic analysis of *CLAU* 111 homologs in a variety of different plant species that have published annotated genomes. CLAU is 112 not highly conserved in Arabidopsis, as the Arabidopsis homologs, AT1G14600 and 113 AT2G02060, possess only 35% and 32% global identity, respectively, to CLAU, with over 30% 114 gaps in the alignment, and cluster in a different clade than CLAU, closer to its distant tomato 115

homolog SolyC12g006280 (Figure 1C). To further examine possible *CLAU* homologs between 116 different plant species, we examined the synteny of the genes within the tomato chromosomal 117 region containing *CLAU* to *Arabidopsis* and several Solanaceae species. Although AT1G14600 118 is slightly more homologous to CLAU than AT2G02060, the genes within the respective region 119 in *Arabidopsis* chromosome 2 that contains *AT2G02060* show greater synteny with the *CLAU* 120 region in tomato chromosome 4 (Supplemental Figure 2). Comparison of these syntenic regions 121 suggests several rearrangements. The additional *Solanaceae* species presented have significant 122 gene synteny in this region to tomato, in particular potato (chromosome 4) and pepper 123 (chromosome 5). The other species have only partially annotated published genomes, so the 124 analysis may not be complete. Comparison between CLAU and other tomato MYB transcription 125 factors shows that CLAU is relatively unique in tomato (Supplemental Figure 3 and 126 Supplemental Data set 2; see below), as the two closest tomato homologs, SolyC02g080860 and 127 SolyC12g006280 (Supplemental Figure 3A,C; Figure 1C), share only 28% and 22% global 128 identity, respectively, with CLAU. Local identities of 78% and 49%, respectively, are limited to 129 the DBD region, where the SHAQKYF motif is highly conserved (amino acids 10-100, 130 Supplemental Figure 3A,B). Overall, the 20 tomato proteins closest to CLAU all contain 131 SHAQKYF domains (interpro IPR006447) and cluster on separate but close branches to CLAU, 132 when compared with several tomato MYBs that are involved in developmental processes and 133 contain R2R3 MYB domains (interpro IPR17930) (Supplemental Figure 3A). 134

#### *CLAU* **is expressed at the leaf margin to restrict the organogenetic window** 135

Pursuant to the identification of the *CLAU* gene, we proceeded to examine the expression 136 patterns of the *CLAU* mRNA during leaf development using qRT-PCR (Figure 2). *CLAU* is 137 expressed primarily during the P3-P7 stages of leaf development (Figure 2A), with expression 138 peaking between P4 and P6. *In situ* mRNA hybridization localized *CLAU* mRNA expression to 139 developing leaflets at the leaf margin (Figure 2B), correlating with the developmental stages 140 during which the *CLAU* mRNA expression peaks. Expression of YFP under the control of the 141 *CLAU* promoter, consisting of 2700 bp upstream of the *CLAU* translational start site, showed 142 promoter activity at the leaf margin and in developing leaflets (Figure 2C), in a pattern similar to 143 that observed for the *CLAU* mRNA in the *in situ* mRNA hybridization (compare 2B with 2C). 144 The 2700-bp *CLAU* promoter was able to rescue the *clau* mutant when driving *CLAU* cDNA 145 expression (Figure 2D). 146

Loss of *CLAU* function results in extended morphogenesis and extremely elaborate 147 leaves. Identification of the *CLAU* gene allowed us to examine the effect of *CLAU* gain of 148 function. Overexpression of *CLAU* under the control of its own promoter or the *FIL* promoter 149 (Lifschitz et al., 2006; Shani et al., 2009), using a *trans*-activation system (Moore et al., 1998), 150 led to a simplified leaf possessing primary leaflets and very few secondary leaflets (Figure 3). 151 This confirms that CLAU is involved in curbing leaf morphogenesis, in line with the more 152 elaborate leaf observed in the *clau* mutant. The simplified-leaf phenotype caused by expressing 153 *CLAU* under the control of its own promoter suggests that CLAU affects organogenesis in a 154 dose-dependent manner. This is also supported by the observation that *clau/+* heterozygotes 155 occasionally showed a very mild *clau* phenotype. To further investigate the role of CLAU in leaf 156 development, we overexpressed a fusion of *CLAU* with the *SRDX* repression motif (Hiratsu et 157 al., 2003) in wild-type leaves, under the control of the *FIL* promoter, using the *trans*-activation 158 system. The *CLAU-SRDX* fusion was expected to downregulate CLAU targets, affecting the 159 downstream developmental pathways and providing insight into the usual mode of action of 160 CLAU. Overexpression of *CLAU-SRDX* resulted in leaves very similar to those resulting from 161 overexpression of *CLAU* alone (Figure 3E and 3D), suggesting that CLAU may act primarily as 162 a repressor of its downstream targets. 163

#### **CLAU has a unique role in compound leaf development** 164

As detailed above, *CLAU* is relatively unique in tomato and its closest *Arabidopsis* 165 homolog has low identity (32%) to CLAU. CLAU has close orthologs in additional Solanaceae 166 species. To compare the nature of the *CLAU* genes in simple- and compound-leaved species, we 167 analyzed the closest *CLAU* homologs in several additional *Solanaceae* species (Figure 4): potato 168 (*Solanum tuberosum*), which has compound leaves, and eggplant (*Solanum melongena*), tobacco 169 (*Nicotiana tabaccum*) and pepper (*Capsicum annum*), with simple leaves. A phylogenetic tree of 170 the two closest CLAU homologs in several *Solanaceae* species is presented in Figure 4A 171 (sequence alignment in Supplemental Data set 3). Potato CLAU (St-CLAU, 172 PGSCDMP400051255) is the closest ortholog to CLAU; pepper (CA0506710) and eggplant 173 (Sme2.501601) sequences are slightly more removed, and those of *N. benthamiana* 174 (Nbs00013875) and tobacco (NT 33284) are less similar (Figure 4A). St-CLAU bears 87% 175

8

global identity to Sl-CLAU, spanning the entire protein. The St*-CLAU* mRNA had similar 176 expression dynamics to Sl-*CLAU* during early leaf development (Figure 4C; compare with 177 Figure 2A). The eggplant ortholog, Sm-CLAU, is 72% identical globally to CLAU. Its 178 expression decreased at the P5 stage, when Sl-*CLAU* and St*-CLAU* were still highly expressed 179 (Figure 4C). The pepper ortholog Ca*-CLAU* contains only 5 exons (Figure 4B) and encodes a 180 shorter protein than Sl-CLAU. Ca-CLAU shares 54% global identity and 71% local identity with 181 the N-terminal two-thirds of the Sl-CLAU protein. Ca*-CLAU* mRNA was expressed at earlier 182 developmental stages and its expression decreased at the P3 stage, when Sl-*CLAU* and St*-CLAU* 183 started to increase in expression, toward the formation of leaflets at the leaf margin (Figure 4C). 184 The *N. benthamiana* Nb-CLAU and *N. tabaccum* Nt-CLAU proteins share about 55% global 185 identity with Sl-CLAU. Similar to the observed expression pattern in pepper, the expression of 186 Nt*-CLAU* decreased at the P3 stage (Figure 4C; developmental stages collected in tobacco were 187 slightly different than in the other species due to technical constraints). Thus, the degree of 188 similarity to Sl-CLAU, the expression dynamics and leaf shape were correlated in the tested 189 species. This suggests that CLAU exerts part of its effect on leaf form during the formation of 190 leaflets, in a developmental window that exists only in compound leaves. Simple leaves have a 191 shorter morphogenetic window and thus lack the developmental window in which CLAU is 192 active in compound leaves. In both leaf types, CLAU likely functions during early leaf 193 development to attenuate meristematic activity and promote leaf fate. 194

#### **CLAU attenuates cytokinin signaling** 195

Overexpression of the Arabidopsis cytokinin (CK) biosynthesis gene 196 *ISOPENTENYLTRANSFERASE 7* (*IPT7*) in tomato leaves led to the formation of highly- 197 compound leaves, whereas decreasing the CK levels via the expression of the Arabidopsis CK 198 degradation gene *CYTOKININ OXIDASE* (*CKX3*) resulted in reduced leaf complexity (Shani et 199 al., 2010) (Figure 5). Some aspects of the leaf phenotype caused by *IPT7-*overexpression are 200 reminiscent of the *clau* phenotype, including a highly compound leaf, many ectopic meristems 201 on the leaf, and an extension of the developmental window of leaflet morphogenesis (Bar et al., 202 2015). The simplified leaf phenotypes resulting from overexpression of CLAU and CKX were 203 also similar. This prompted us to examine whether an altered CK response could be involved in 204 the *clau* phenotype. Overexpressing *pFIL>>IPT7* in a *clau* background (Figure 5G,H) enhanced 205 the phenotype caused by elevated CK levels alone (Figure 5C,D). Conversely, reducing CK 206 biosynthesis in the *clau* background was substantially epistatic to CKX overexpression (Figure 207 5F), as the leaf was only slightly more complex than the leaf overexpressing CKX alone (Figure 208 5B), suggesting that the *clau* phenotype depends at least in part on CK. Interestingly, 209 overexpressing *IPT7* and *CLAU* simultaneously under the control of the *FIL* promoter resulted in 210 a significant rescue of the *FIL>>IPT7* phenotype (Figure 5K,L), indicating that CLAU can 211 affect the CK response. Taken together, these results suggest that CK dynamics may mediate 212 CLAU function. 213

#### **Expression of the CK-response reporter TCS is upregulated in** *clau* 214

To further examine whether CK dynamics could be altered by CLAU, we compared the 215 expression of a CK activity reporter TCS fused to VENUS (Zürcher et al., 2013; Steiner et al., 216 2016) between wild-type tomato plants and the *clau* mutant (Figure 6). We first confirmed that 217 TCS is CK-responsive in tomato as was reported in *Arabidopsis* (Supplemental Figure 4). To 218 avoid positional effects, we compared a wild-type TCS-expressing line with the same line that 219 was introgressed into *clau* by a cross. In wild-type shoot apices, TCS drove expression in the 220 SAM and at the leaf margin of developing leaves (Figure 6A-E). TCS-driven expression was 221 strongly upregulated in the *clau* background (Figure 6F-J). Quantification of TCS-driven 222 fluorescence showed that TCS was significantly up-regulated in *clau* at all developmental stages 223 (Figure 6K), except in the SAM where its expression was already high in the wild-type 224 background, and where *CLAU* expression was low (Figure 2A-B). In the wild-type, TCS-driven 225 expression was high in the SAM, decreased in P1, peaked again between P2-P5, and 226 subsequently decreased (Figure 6K). In *clau* however, all developmental stages showed similar, 227 relatively high TCS-driven expression. 228

#### *clau* **has increased CK sensitivity** 229

The genetic results suggesting an involvement of CK in the *clau* phenotype and the 230 increase in TCS reporter-driven expression prompted us to examine whether *clau* could have 231 increased CK content (Figure 7). Surprisingly, young *clau* primordia have a lower amount of 232 CKs (Figure 7A), including trans-zeatin, which is the most abundant CK in these tissues. An 233 increase in CK signaling coupled with a decrease in CK content led us to investigate whether 234

*clau* affects CK sensitivity. In three independent biological assays (Coenen and Lomax, 1998; 235 Fleishon et al., 2011), *clau* plants responded to CK concentrations a tenth (anthocyanin 236 production – Supplemental Figure 5B) to a hundredth (root growth- Figure 7B and hypocotyl 237 height – Supplemental Figure 5A) lower than the concentrations required to elicit a response in 238 wild-type plants. Another hallmark of CK response is an increase in the transcripts of type A 239 response regulator (RR) genes (Keshishian and Rashotte, 2015; Shi et al., 2013; D'Agostino and 240 Kieber, 1999; Brandstatter and Kieber, 1998). The Arabidopsis type A RRs *ARR3* and *ARR5* 241 respond strongly to CK and inhibit CK response (Shi et al., 2013; To et al., 2004). Interestingly, 242 the baseline transcript level of the tomato type A RRs *TRR3* and *TRR5* was significantly lower in 243 *clau* than in wild type (Figure 7C), suggesting that TRRs may mediate the effect of CLAU on 244 cytokinin sensitivity. Examining the type A TRR transcript response to CK treatment 245 demonstrated that the response of *TRR3* and *TRR5* to CK was stronger in *clau* than in the wild 246 type (Figure 7D,E). We also examined the response of *TRR 8/9A*, *8/9B*, *8/9C*, *16/17* and *16B* to 247 CK, and with the exception of *TRR8/9C*, all demonstrated a statistically significant higher 248 transcriptional response following CK treatment in *clau* than in the wild type (Supplemental 249 Figure 5C-H). We conclude that an absence of an active CLAU protein leads to increased CK 250 sensitivity, suggesting that CLAU functions in attenuating the response to CK during 251 development, possibly via positive regulation of TRR expression. A possible mechanism to 252 counterbalance the increased CK sensitivity found in *clau* could be through CKX regulation. 253 *CKX2*, 5 and 6 were all induced in tomato leaves following CK treatment (Shi et al., 2013). 254 Interestingly, *clau* had similar levels of *CKX* 2,5 and 6 prior to CK treatment (Figure 7F). 255 However, while *CKX2* (Figure 7G), *CKX5* (Figure 7H) and *CKX6* (Figure 7I) were induced by 1 256 μm CK to a similar degree as in the wild type, they were induced to a lower degree in *clau* than 257 in wild type by 10 μm CK. This suggests that CKXs may feed back to the already low levels of 258 CKs in *clau* rather than respond to the increased CK sensitivity. 259

#### **Overexpressing CLAU decreases CK sensitivity** 260

To complement the analysis presented in Figure 7, we examined whether overexpression 261 of *CLAU* could decrease CK sensitivity. We first compared the expression of the 262 *TCSv2:3XVENUS* reporter detailed above between wild-type tomato plants and several 263 transgenic lines overexpressing *pFIL>>CLAU* (Figure 8). Once again, we compared a wild-type 264 TCS-expressing line with the same line that was introgressed into *pFIL>>CLAU*. TCS-driven 265 expression was downregulated in a *pFIL>>CLAU* background (Figure 8C-F). Transgenic lines 266 overexpressing the CLAU protein under the control of the leaf specific *FIL* promoter showed a 267 decrease in CK sensitivity, requiring higher CK concentrations to elicit a decrease in hypocotyl 268 height (Figure 8G) or an increase in anthocyanin content (Figure 8H). 269

#### **Overexpressing** *CLAU* **leads to reduced** *GOB* **expression** 270

In previous work (Bar et al., 2015), we showed that part of the CLAU mechanism of 271 action in tomato involves negative regulation of the *NAM/CUC* gene *GOBLET* (*GOB*). We 272 previously demonstrated that *GOB* is upregulated in a *clau* background, and that downregulation 273 of *GOB* via overexpression of its regulatory miRNA, miRNA164, results in a dramatic rescue of 274 the *clau* phenotype (Bar et al., 2015). Upon identification of the *CLAU* gene, we wanted to 275 examine whether upregulation of *CLAU* has an effect on *GOB* expression. Figure 8I shows that 276 overexpression of *CLAU* caused downregulation of *GOB*, strengthening the assertion that CLAU 277 negatively regulates *GOB* in leaf development. 278

#### **CK application affects** *CLAU* **expression** 279

To further understand the interaction between CLAU and cytokinin, we examined 280 whether *CLAU* expression is affected by CK (Figure 9). CK application led to a decrease in 281 *CLAU* expression (Figure 9A), suggesting that CLAU promotes differentiation by inhibiting CK 282 responses, and CK promotes morphogenesis by inhibiting *CLAU* expression (Figure 9B). 283

#### **Discussion** 285

Tomato leaves undergo extended morphogenesis, resulting in elaborate and variable leaf 286 forms (Burko and Ori, 2013). Among tomato leaf phenotypes, the *clau* mutant is extreme in its 287 leaf elaboration, exemplifying the potential of further extending the tomato leaf morphogenetic 288 window and its possible results. Overexpressing *CLAU* curbs leaf morphogenesis, demonstrating 289 that CLAU regulates the length of the leaf morphogenetic window. 290

How does CLAU regulate morphogenetic competence in the leaf? We recently reported 291 that CLAU is involved in delimiting the expression of *GOB*, a central regulator of leaf 292 morphogenesis (Bar et al., 2015; Ben-Gera et al., 2012; Berger et al., 2009; Brand et al., 2007; 293 Busch et al., 2011; Rossmann et al., 2015). Here *GOB* is shown to be downregulated by *CLAU* 294 overexpression. In addition, *clau* has abnormally high expression of the KNOXI gene 295 *LeT6/Tkn2*, which plays important role in promoting leaf morphogenesis (Avivi et al., 2000; 296 Jasinski et al., 2007). Here, we show that a central mechanism via which CLAU regulates the 297 leaf morphogenetic window is attenuation of CK response. Interestingly, KNOXI proteins 298 positively regulate CK levels in the contexts of SAM maintenance and compound leaf 299 morphogenesis (Sakamoto et al., 2006; Scofield et al., 2013; Shani et al., 2010). It therefore 300 appears that to promote differentiation and attenuate morphogenesis, CLAU interferes with the 301 activity of several interconnected pathways that promote morphogenesis. It will be interesting to 302 see which of these pathways are directly affected by CLAU, and which are affected secondarily 303 by the molecular and developmental context of differentiation. 304

How does CLAU affect CK response? *clau* mutants have decreased CK levels and 305 increased CK sensitivity, and *IPT* overexpression phenotypes are enhanced by *clau*. These 306 observations suggest that CLAU may negatively affect CK response, which in turn feeds back to 307 CK levels. Notably, while TCS-driven reporter accumulation is increased and type A response 308 regulators (RR) respond to CK with higher sensitivity in *clau*, the steady-state levels of these 309 type A RRs are reduced in *clau*. These results suggest that CLAU is a positive regulator of type 310 A RRs, which negatively regulate CK response (Kiba et al., 2004; To et al., 2004; Lee et al., 311 2008). In *clau*, their expression is reduced, leading to increased CK sensitivity. The effect of 312 CLAU on type A RRs may be direct, or possibly, additional regulators are feedbacking to 313 downregulate type A RRs. In contrast to the increased CK sensitivity of TRR response in *clau*, 314 *CKX* has reduced response to CK treatment in *clau* when compared with the wild type. These 315 results suggest that the response of *TRR* and *CKX* expression to CK may be regulated by distinct 316 pathways, and that CK affects *CKX* expression in a pathway not affected by CLAU. In other 317 words, some downstream components of the CK pathway respond to the elevated CK sensitivity 318 mediated by CLAU, while other downstream components of the CK pathway respond to the 319 decrease in CK content present in *clau*. 320

Our results show that *CLAU* is primarily expressed at the leaf margin and during the 321 developmental stages where leaflets are formed, suggesting that CLAU functions to restrain 322 morphogenesis. This is also evident from the fact that in wild-type leaves, *CLAU* expression 323 decreases after leaflets have been determined (P8 onwards). Essentially CLAU can be viewed as 324 the gatekeeper for exiting morphogenesis, which can explain why its loss of function increases 325 leaf complexity so drastically – CLAU functions as a repressor during the morphogenetic stage, 326 and removing CLAU function causes an already elaborate leaf to continue elaboration 327 indefinitely, subsequently being regulated only by the plant senescing (Bar et al., 2015). 328

TCP transcription factors, which also promote leaf differentiation and attenuate 329 organogenesis, have been shown in Arabidopsis to decrease CK sensitivity by an interaction with 330 the chromatin remodeling ATPase BRHAMA and direct binding to the *ARR16* gene, which is 331 rapidly induced by CK and inhibits CK response (Efroni et al., 2013). Therefore, transcription 332 factors that antagonistically affect the balance between organogenesis and differentiation appear 333 to converge on modulating the CK response. As the tomato TCP factor LA was shown to also 334 affect GA metabolism, the window of organogenesis may be regulated through the balance 335 between CK and GA (Shani et al., 2010; Bolduc and Hake, 2009; Steiner et al., 2012; Efroni et 336 al., 2013; Yanai et al., 2011; Hay et al., 2002). 337

CLAU encodes a MYB transcription factor that possesses a DNA-binding SHAQYF- 338 domain. Several MYB transcription factors have been previously reported to be involved in leaf 339 development (Barkoulas et al., 2007; Luo et al., 2012; Busch et al., 2011; Naz et al., 2013), but 340 most of them encode proteins containing R2R3- or R3-type DNA binding domains, which 341 cluster distantly from CLAU (Supplemental Figure 3). CLAU homologs exist in simple-leaved 342 species, where they are possibly involved in *GOB* regulation and restriction of the meristematic 343 morphogenetic potential, but CLAU appears to have an expanded function in compound leaf 344 development. In tomato, and possibly additional compound-leaved species, CLAU is expressed 345 and functions primarily at the stage when leaflets are already being formed, suggesting that it 346 may have a more restricted role in the development of leaves that lack leaflets. In the Solanaceae, 347 CLAU increases in expression at developmental stages where leaflets are being formed in the 348 compound-leaved tomato and potato, decreasing after leaflets have been formed, while its 349 expression decreases much earlier in the simple-leaved pepper and tobacco, possibly supporting 350 a role in restricting lateral organ morphogenesis from the meristem. Interestingly, the pepper 351 CLAU homolog is truncated, possessing 5 exons rather than the 6 in tomato and potato. The 352 portion of the protein missing in Ca-CLAU is somewhat comparable to the *clau-FF* mutation 353 (Ca-CLAU is ~70% identical to amino acids 1-207 of CLAU, while the predicted protein in the 354 *clau-FF* allele is composed of amino acids 1-150 of CLAU), which results in a loss-of-function 355 *clau* phenotype. This suggests that the CLAU protein may not be functional or may have 356 decreased or different function in pepper, demonstrating that the activity of CLAU in curbing 357 leaf morphogenesis may not be required in the simple-leaved pepper. The function of CLAU in 358 simple-leaved species remains to be investigated. 359

To summarize, we have shown here that CLAU is a MYB transcription factor that 360 modulates leaf morphogenesis by curbing the morphogenetic potential, in part through 361 attenuation of CK signaling. It appears that *CLAU* is of particular spatio-temporal relevance in 362 compound leaves. 363

#### **Materials and Methods** 366

#### **Plant Material** 367

Tomato seeds (*Solanum lycopersicum* cv M82 or as indicated) were sown in a 368 commercial nursery and grown in the field, or in a glasshouse under natural daylight with 369 25 : 18°C (day : night) temperatures and a maximum light intensity of *c*. 450 μmol m−2 s−1. For 370 *in situ* hybridization and expression analyses, plants were grown in a controlled growth chamber 371 under fluorescent bulbs providing *c*. 300 μmol m−2 s−1 at an 18h/6h light/dark regimen. The *clau* 372 allele used throughout all experiments, *clau:e3545*, is from a tomato EMS population (Menda et 373 al., 2004), and was previously characterized (Bar et al., 2015). Tomato (M82, sp) plants 374 expressing *pFIL>>IPT and pFIL>>CKX* were generated by the LhG4 transactivation system 375 (Moore et al., 1998), and described previously (Shani et al., 2010). Tomato plants overexpressing 376 TCSv2:3XVENUS were obtained from the Muller, Eshed and Weiss labs (Steiner et al., 2016; 377 Zürcher et al., 2013), confirmed to be CK responsive in tomato, and backcrossed into a *clau* 378 background. 379

#### **Cloning and plant transformation** 380

The *CLAU* gene was amplified from tomato M82 cDNA and cloned into the pENTR/dTM 381 vector using a TOPO isomerase cloning system (Invitrogen). To generate *op:CLAU* and 382 *op:CLAU-SRDX*, *CLAU* from pENTR was sub-cloned downstream to an Operator array (Moore 383 et al., 1998). The SRDX fusion was generated by assembly PCR. At least 5 independent 384 kanamycin-resistant transgenic lines from each transgene were crossed to the *pFIL* and *pCLAU* 385 (see below) driver lines, examined, and a representative line was selected for further analysis. 386 The *CLAU* promoter was generated by amplifying 2700 bp upstream of the *CLAU* ATG from 387 genomic DNA (M82 background) and sub-cloned upstream to NLS-YFP generating 388 *pCLAU:nYFP* or sub-cloned upstream to LhG4, generating a vector for driving expression in the 389 *trans*-activation system (Moore et al., 1998). 390

#### *CLAU* **mapping and RNAseq** 391

We created an F2 mapping population from a cross between *clau:e3545* in the M82 392 background and *S. pimpinellifolium*. Markers used in the mapping of this population are 393 presented in Supplemental Table 1. Using this population and these markers, we mapped *CLAU* 394

between bases 2094866 and 2122247 on the short arm of chromosome 4. RNA was prepared 395 from the shoot apices of 10-day-old *clau:e3545* and wild-type M82 plants (m-P4) and subjected 396 to RNAseq. Two libraries per genotype were prepared according to the Illumina TruSeq RNA 397 protocol and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq2000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA) at 398 the Genome Center of the Max Planck Institute for Plant Breeding Research. We obtained 399 between 17.9 and 29.9 million 96 base pair single-end reads per library (average of 25,8 million). 400

Reads were aligned to the *S. lycopersicum* reference sequence v2.40 401 (ftp://ftp.solgenomics.net/tomato\_genome/wgs/assembly/build\_2.40/) using Tophat v2.0.6 (Kim 402 et al., 2013) with the following parameters: --max-insertion-length 12 --max-deletion-length 12 - 403 g 1 --read-gap-length 12 --read-edit-dist 20 --read-mismatches 12 --no-coverage-search --read- 404 realign-edit-dist 0 --segment-mismatches 3 --splice-mismatches 1. An average of 93.2 % of all 405 reads were uniquely aligned to the reference genome. 406

To detect polymorphisms between *clau:e3545* and the wild type M82, we first removed 407 duplicated reads using default settings in Picard (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/), 408 realigned indels and called variants using default parameters in GATK v2.2-8 (DePristo et al., 409 2011). For all analyses we retained only homozygous, bi-allelic variants covered in both 410 genotypes with a phred-scaled variant quality higher than 30. This analysis returned 26,995 411 variants between the mutant and its M82 background. We estimated the effect of each variant in 412 annotated transcripts (ITAG 2.3) using ANNOVAR (Wang et al., 2010). Deleterious variants in 413 the candidate region in chromosome 4 were evaluated manually. 414

#### **Phylogenetic analysis** 415

Protein sequence alignment was carried out using MUSCLE (Edgar, 2004). The 416 evolutionary history was inferred using the Maximum Likelihood method based on the JTT 417 matrix-based model (Jones et al., 1992). In each case, the tree with the highest log likelihood is 418 shown. Initial trees for the heuristic searches were obtained automatically by applying Neighbor- 419 Join and BioNJ algorithms to a matrix of pairwise distances estimated using a JTT model, and 420 then selecting the topology with superior log likelihood value. The trees are drawn to scale, with 421 branch lengths measured in the number of substitutions per site. All positions containing gaps 422 and missing data were eliminated. Evolutionary analyses were conducted in MEGA7 (Kumar et 423 al., 2016). 424

#### **Synteny** 425

CLAU-like genes were chosen based on BLASTP to *SlCLAU*. Chromosomal regions 426 were checked for synteny by seeking neighbor gene annotations, and verified by global Pairwise 427 Sequence Alignment (Needle, EMBOSS). Genomic synteny was analyzed for chromosome 2 in 428 Arabidopsis (TAIR10) and chromosome 4 in potato (ITAG1). Gene models for the synteny were 429 drawn using published gff3 files. The following genomes were used for synteny analyses: 430 Tomato ITAG2.4; Arabidopsis TAIR10; Potato ITAG1, PGSC3.4 and 4.03; Pepper CAv1.55; 431 Eggplant SME2.5.1; Nicotiana benthamiana Niben101. 432

#### **Tissue collection and RNA analysis** 433

Tissue collection, RNA preparation and qRT-PCR analysis were performed as previously 434 described (Shleizer-Burko et al., 2011). Values are means of at least three biological repeats, 435 each containing the shoot apices, including the SAM and P1-P5 leaf primordia, of 8-12 436 seedlings. Expression of all assayed genes was normalized relative to tomato *EXPRESSED* 437 (*EXP*), except in the results presented in Figure 4, where each *CLAU* ortholog was normalized 438 relative to the expression of *Tubulin* within the same species. Primer sequences used for the 439 qRT-PCR analyses are detailed in Supplemental Table 2. Student's t-test (two-tailed) was used 440 for comparison of means, which were deemed significantly different at p≤0.05. RNA *in situ* 441 hybridization was performed as previously described (Shani et al., 2010). The *CLAU* mRNA 442 antisense probe was generated as described previously (Berger et al., 2009) from a pENTR- 443 *CLAU* construct (see above) containing the full-length *CLAU* cDNA, digested with HindIII. 444

#### **Imaging** 445

Intact leaves were photographed using a Nikon D5200 camera. For analysis of 446 TCSv2:3XVENUS expression, dissected whole-leaf primordia were placed into drops of water 447 on glass microscope slides and covered with cover slips. The pattern of VENUS expression was 448 detected by a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSMmodel SP8; Leica), with the solid-state 449 laser set at 514 nm for excitation and 530 nm for emission. Chlorophyll expression was detected 450 at 488 nm for excitation and 700 nm for emission. The pattern of VENUS expression was also 451 observed with a Nikon SMZ1270 stereomicroscope equipped with a Nikon DS-RI2 camera and 452 NIS elements software. 453

*In situ* hybridization slides were photographed with an Olympus 1X81 microscope using 454 CellR software as described (Berger et al., 2009). LAS AF and ImageJ software were used for 455 analysis, quantification, and measurements of captured images. Student's t-test (two-tailed) was 456 used for comparison of means, which were deemed significantly different at p ≤ 0.05. Images 457 were adjusted uniformly using Adobe Photoshop CS6. 458

#### **CK content analysis** 459

Cytokinins were isolated and purified as outlined by Novák *et al*. (2003) with some 460 modifications. Frozen tomato tissue (30 mg) was homogenized using vibration mill MM 301 at a 461 frequency of 30 Hz for 3 min (3-mm zirconium oxide beads; Retsch GmbH & Co. KG, Haan, 462 Germany) with 1 mL ice-cold Bieleski solution (methanol-chloroform-formic acid-water; 463 12:5:1:2) as extraction solution. The cytokinins were then extracted overnight at 4°C using a 464 benchtop laboratory rotator Stuart SB3 (Bibby Scientific Ltd., Staffordshire, UK) after addition 465 of heavy labeled internal standards (OlChemIm Ltd, Olomouc, Czech Republic). The samples 466 were further purified by SPE (SCX) and the eluate was concentrated *in vacuo* at 37 °C. For 467 analysis, samples were re-dissolved in mobile phase and analyzed by ultra-high performance 468 liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS; Acquity UPLCTM System, 469 Xevo TQ, Waters MS Technologies, Manchester, U.K) according to Novák *et al. (2008)* (Novák 470 et al., 2008). Cytokinins were ionized by electrospray in positive mode and detected using 471 multiple-reaction monitoring. Masslynx 4.1 software (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) was used to 472 analyze the data and processed by the standard isotope dilution method (Rittenberg and Foster, 473 1940) 474

#### **CK treatment and root growth, hypocotyl height and anthocyanin measurements** 475

For anthocyanin measurement and hypocotyl elongation, plants were sprayed with the 476 indicated CK concentrations 3 times a week for 3 weeks prior to analysis, starting upon 477 emergence of the first leaf. Anthocyanins were extracted from the terminal leaflet of the third 478 leaf by incubation overnight in methanol supplemented with a final concentration of 1% HCl. 479 OD was measured in a plate spectrophotometer and anthocyanin content was calculated 480 according to the following: (OD530-(0.25\*OD660)), normalized to the starting tissue weight. 481 Three technical replicates of 10-15 biological repeats were performed for each sample. 482

For root growth, seeds were germinated on sterile wetted paper filters and transferred to 483 plates containing the indicated CK concentrations after 5 d, then grown upright on the CK 484 concentrations for an additional 3-5 d in a growth chamber. 485

For qRT-PCR, 10-day-old seedlings were dipped in the indicated CK concentrations for 5 486 min and then incubated at room temperature for 3 h. Shoot apices containing the SAM and P1-P5 487 leaf primordia were then collected for RNA extraction. 6-Benzyl-aminopurine (Sigma) was used 488 for all CK treatments. 489
