# **Maize** *rea1* **mutant stimulates ribosome use efficiency and triggers distinct transcriptional and translational responses**

# **Abstract**

Ribosome biogenesis is a fundamental cellular process in all cells. Impaired ribosome biogenesis causes developmental defects; however, its molecular and cellular basis is not fully understood. We cloned a gene responsible for a maize small seed mutant *dek\**, and found it encodes Ribosome export associated 1 (ZmRea1). Rea1 is an AAA-ATPase that controls 60S ribosome export from the nucleus to the cytoplasm after ribosome maturation. *dek\** is a weak mutant allele with decreased Rea1 function. In *dek\** cells, mature 60S ribosome subunits are reduced in the nucleus and cytoplasm, but the proportion of actively translating polyribosomes in cytosol is significantly increased. Reduced phosphorylation of eIF2α and the increased eEF1α level indicate an enhancement of general translational efficiency in *dek\** cells. The mutation also triggers dramatic changes in differentially transcribed genes (DTGs) and differentially translated RNAs (DTRs). Discrepancy was observed between DTGs and DTRs, indicating distinct cellular responses at transcription and translation levels to the stress of defective ribosome processing. DNA replication and nucleosome assembly related gene expression are selectively suppressed at translational level, resulting in inhibited cell growth and proliferation in *dek\** cells. This study provides insight into cellular responses due to impaired ribosome biogenesis.

# **Introduction**

Ribosomes are organelles that translate genetic information into proteins. A great percentage of total RNA transcription is devoted to ribosomal RNA synthesis, and a great part of RNA polymerase Ⅱ transcription and mRNA splicing are devoted to synthesis of ribosomal proteins (Warner, 1999). Ribosome biosynthesis consumes ~80% of a cell's energy (James et al., 2014). In eukaryotes, ribosome biogenesis begins in the nucleolus with the transcription of a large ribosomal precursor RNA that gives rise to the 90S pre-ribosomal particle. Cleavages of the 90S particle generate two subunits: the pre-40S and pre-60S complexes. The pre-40S and pre-60S subunits mature in the nucleolus and nucleoplasm before being exported to the cytoplasm (Venema and Tollervey, 1999; Fromont-Racine et al., 2003; Granneman and Baserga, 2004). Inhibition of ribosome biogenesis causes developmental defects in yeast, humans and plants (Tschochner and Hurt, 2003; Galani et al., 2004; Ruan et al., 2012; Weis et al., 2014; Brooks et al., 2014).

A great deal of research has revealed that hundreds of ribosomal-biogenesis factors contribute to maturation of the ribosome in eukaryotes (Tschochner and Hurt, 2003; Henras et al., 2008), including three essential AAA-ATPases: Ribosome export (Rix) 7, Ribosome export associated (Rea) 1, and Diazaborine resistance gene (Drg) 1 (Pertschy et al., 2007; Kressler et al., 2008; Ulbrich et al., 2009; Bassler et al., 2010; Kressler et al., 2012). Rea1 AAA-ATPase is the best-characterized ATPase in ribosome biogenesis and is conserved from yeast to humans (Bassler et al., 2010; Kressler et al., 2012). Rea1 promotes stripping of other biogenesis factors from the pre-60S particle in the nucleolus and nucleoplasm (Ytm1-Erb1-Nop7 and Rsa4) prior to the export of the large ribosomal subunit to the cytoplasm (Bassler et al., 2010). However, there is not a comprehensive understanding of cellular responses to the impaired large ribosomal subunit export.

The regulation of mRNA translation is a critical feature of gene expression in eukaryotes (Bailey-Serres, 1999). Previous studies highlight the importance of translational control in determining protein abundance, underscoring the value of measuring gene expression at the level of translation. Mechanisms

that underlie differential mRNA translation are likely to involve nucleotide sequence features and the phosphorylation status of initiation factors (Bailey-Serres and Dawe, 1996; Pop et al., 2014). Transcriptome and translatome analyses of the cellular response to heat shock, cell-cycle arrest and mating pheromone in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (Preiss et al., 2003; Serikawa et al., 2003; MacKay et al., 2004), the hypoxia response of HeLa cells (Blais et al., 2004), and the drought and oxygen deprivation responses in *Arabidopsis* (Kawaguchi et al., 2004; Branco-Price et al., 2005) have shown the importance of translational regulation. These researchers investigated the correlation between total and poly-ribosome (polysome) -bound mRNA accumulation and provided extensive evidence of variation in the translational regulation of individual mRNAs. These studies showed mRNAs differ in their association with polysomes under different circumstances, and gene expression can be regulated at the translational level without a change in mRNA abundance.

Maize (*Zea mays*) is especially well suited for genetic studies, partly because of the feasibility to generate a wide range of easily observable phenotypes (Neuffer and Sheridan, 1980). Many kernel mutants are known (Neuffer et al., 1968), among which one class is defective kernel (dek) mutants (Neuffer and Sheridan, 1980). dek mutants are good resource to investigate seed development. For example, *Dek1* encodes a large membrane protein of the calpain gene superfamily (Lid et al., 2002). In *dek1* mutants, embryogenesis is blocked, while the endosperm lacks the aleurone layer and is chalky (Becraft et al., 2002). Other *dek* mutants offer opportunities to investigate many basic biological processes, because embryo formation is the first developmental process after the fertilization. Such defects in basic biological processes create visible phenotypes during kernel development.

In this study, we characterized *dek\**, a novel mutant with small kernels, and delayed developmental of the embryo, endosperm and seedling. We report the map-based cloning of *Dek\** and demonstrate it encodes Rea1 in maize. *dek\** is a weak mutant allele that only partly represses the maturation and export of the 60S ribosomal subunit. Taking advantage of this mutant allele, we were able to obtain comprehensive information about the cellular responses to impaired 60S ribosomal subunit biogenesis.

# **Results**

### *dek\** **produces small kernels with delayed development**

The *dek\** mutant was isolated from an opaque mutant stock obtained from the Maize Genetic Stock Center. It was crossed to the W64A inbred line to produce a F2 population that displayed a 1:3 segregation of dek (*dek\**/ *dek\**) and wild type (+/+ and *dek\**/+) phenotypes (Figure 1A&B). At 15 days after pollination (DAP), homozygous *dek\** kernels exhibited a small, vague phenotype (Figure 1A), and mature kernels were small and shrunken (Figure 1B). The 100-kernel weight of *dek\** was nearly 39.5% less than wild type (Figure 1C), but there was no significant difference in the total protein and zein content (Figure 1D, Supplemental Figure 1), although, there was a slight increase in the amount of non-zeins (13.5%, Figure 1D). Among zein proteins, the 22kD α-zeins were relatively more abundant in *dek\** endosperms (Supplemental Figure 1). We found no obvious difference in total starch content and the percentage of amylose in *dek\** and wild type endosperms (Supplemental Figure 2). We analyzed soluble amino acids to determine if the slight increase of non-zeins in *dek\** altered their composition. The results showed that the amount of lysine was most significantly increased (23.1%) might due to the slight increase of non-zein content (Figure 1E), for zeins lack lysine residues (Mertz *et al.,* 1964).

Wild type and *dek\** kernels of 15 DAP and 18 DAP were analyzed by light microscopy to compare their development. Longitudinal sections of the embryos indicated development of the plumule and seminal was delayed more than three days in *dek\** compared to wild type (Figure 1F). To investigate the endosperm development, we observed 15 DAP and 18 DAP immature endosperm cells using optical microscopy. The endosperm cells of *dek\** kernels were less cytoplasmic dense with fewer starch granules compared to the wild type of the same stage, also indicating more than a three day delay in development (Figure 1G).

At 4 and 7 days after germination (DAG), seedlings of *dek\** showed a three day developmental delay compared to wild type (Figure 1H). By 4 DAG, wild type seedlings had two leaves, one completely expanded and the other emerging; the *dek\** seedlings had only one leaf at this stage. The 7 DAG wild type seedlings had three leaves, while the *dek\** seedlings had only two leaves. The heading stage of the *dek\** plant was delayed approximately fifteen days compared to wild type, and its height was only 50% of the wild type (Supplemental Figure 3). These results demonstrated that the growth and development of *dek\** kernels and seedlings is delayed compared to wild type.

# **Positional cloning of** *Dek\**

Genetic fine mapping of *Dek\** was carried out with the F2 mapping population and the *Dek\** gene was placed between the simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers, mmc0241 and umc2162, on the long arm of chromosome 6 (Figure 2A). After characterizing a mapping population of 864 individuals, *Dek\** was mapped between the self-created SSR markers 153.7M-2 (19 recombinants) and 155.1M-1 (29 recombinants). Additional markers InDel438, InDel428, SNP064 and SNP165 were developed, and *Dek\** gene was eventually placed between SNP064 (1 recombinant) and SNP165 (2 recombinants), a region encompassing a physical distance of 101.6kb (Figure 2A).

Nucleotide sequence analysis within this region identified ten predicted open reading frames (ORFs) with gene model information (GRMZM2G405052, GRMZM2G387038, GRMZM5G873561, GRMZM5G807823, GRMZM2G361064, GRMZM5G892685, GRMZM2G059268, GRMZM2G059278, GRMZM2G323939 and GRMZM2G128315). Expression analysis revealed no expression of GRMZM5G892685, GRMZM2G059268 and GRMZM2G059278 based on reverse transcription (RT)-PCR and expressed sequence tag (EST) information (http://www.maizegdb.org/); consequently, these three might be pseudogenes. DNA sequence analysis revealed GRMZM2G405052, GRMZM2G387038, GRMZM5G873561, GRMZM5G807823, together with GRMZM2G092001 and GRMZM2G149586 which are up-stream of the candidate region, produced one huge transcript that was identified as candidate Gene1. There is a single nucleotide polymorphism in Gene1 resulting in an amino acid replacement between the alleles of *dek\** and wild type. GRMZM2G361064, GRMZM2G323939 and GRMZM2G128315 were identified as candidate Gene2, Gene3 and Gene4, respectively; however, their consideration for *Dek\** was eliminated due to no

sequence differences between alleles in *dek\** and wild type (Figure 2A). Therefore, Gene1 appeared to be the best candidate for the *Dek\** locus.

#### *Dek\** **encodes the 60S-specific ribosome biogenesis factor Rea1**

The genomic DNA sequence of candidate Gene1 spans ~50 kb and produces a huge transcript containing a 16,278 bp coding sequence (Figure 2B). Sequence data for this gene has been deposited in GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ ) as accession number KP137367. Gene1 encodes a ~600 kD protein of 5,425 amino acids. BLASTP searches of Genbank indicated that Gene1 encodes a ribosome biogenesis factor, AAA-ATPase Rea1, with several conserved domains in maize (Figure 2B). ZmRea1 contains different kinds of molecular domains: a weakly conserved N-terminal region, a dynein-like tandem array of six AAA-type ATPase domains (Neuwald et al., 1999), a large linker, a D/E rich region and a metal ion dependent adhesion site (MIDAS) domain (Figure 2B). Rea1 promotes release of Ytm1 which associates with nucleolar pre-60S particles, and later also promotes release of Rsa4 associates with nucleoplasmic pre-60S particles via the MIDAS-MIDAS interacting domain (MIDO) using the mechanical force created by the ATPase ring domain for the export of the large ribosomal subunit to the cytoplasm (Ulbrich et al., 2009; Bassler et al., 2010). The mutation in the *dek\** allele of *ZmRea1* is a single nucleotide polymorphism in the 2,359th codon of *ZmRea1*, which results in Ala (GCC) replaced by Val (GTC; Figure 2B). This mutation alters the highly conserved region between the dynein-like array of six AAA-type ATPases and the large linker, which could affect transduction of the mechanical force created by the ATPase ring domain to the large tail for release of the ribosome biogenesis factors.

To confirm if *ZmRea1* is the *Dek\** gene, we carried out an allelism test with a *Mu* induced mutant of *ZmRea1* (Figure 2B&C). A UniformMu insertion mutant (r*ea1-Mu*) stock for GRMZM2G092001 was obtained from the Maize Genetics Stock Center. This mutant has a Mutator-8 insertion after the 4th nucleotide of the *ZmRea1* coding sequence and is not viable (Figure 2B). The allelism test was done by crossing *dek\** F1 (*dek\**/*+*) and *rea1-Mu* F1 (*rea1-Mu* /*+*). The kernel phenotypes in the F2 ears displayed a 1:3 segregation of 82 dek (*dek\**/*rea1-Mu*) and 249 wild type phenotype kernels (Figure 2C), indicating that *rea1-Mu* can't complement *dek\**. Therefore, Gene1 (*ZmRea1*) is indeed the *Dek\** gene. We hence named *dek\** as *rea1-ref*.

Maize endosperm is a triploid tissue with 2 maternal and 1 paternal genomes. The mutant kernels in *rea1-ref*/+ (maternal) X *rea1-Mu*/+ (paternal) F2 ear are small and shallow, similar to homozygous *rea1-ref* kernels. The mutant kernels in *rea1-Mu*/+ (maternal) X *rea1-ref* /+ (paternal) F2 ears display an even more severe phenotype with dramatically shrunken kernels. The mutant kernels from *rea1-Mu*/+ selfing are non-viable (Figure 2C). Thus, results of the allelism test show *rea1-ref* is a weak allele compared to r*ea1-Mu*, which has a lethal phenotype.

To examine *Rea1* mRNA expression in *rea1-ref*, we performed quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) with the total RNA extracted from 15 and 18 DAP mutant and wild type kernels. Surprisingly, mRNA expression of *Rea1* was significantly up-regulated in *rea1-ref* (Figure 2D). Because ZmRea1 is too large (~600kD) to perform a regular western blot analysis, we used dot-immunoblot analysis on quantified and gradient diluted total protein samples with Rea1-specific antibody to detect its existence in 15 and 18 DAP *rea1-ref* and wild type kernels (Figure 2E). The results demonstrated that Rea1 is present in *rea1-ref* and accumulates *in rea1-ref* at normal levels, but might be only partly functional.

# *Rea1* **is highly conserved in different organisms and is constitutively expressed in maize**

Rea1 was first identified as a component of pre-60S ribosome complex in yeast and is conserved from yeast to humans (Bassler et al., 2001; Bassler et al., 2010; Kressler et al., 2012). We constructed a phylogenetic tree on the basis of the ZmRea1 full length protein sequence and Rea1 protein sequences from *Brachypodium distachyon*, *Triticum*, *Oryza sativa*, *Setaria*, *Arabidopsis thaliana*, *Populus*, *Glycine-max*, *Dictyostelium*, *Monodelphis domestica*, *Saprelegnia*, *Mortierella*, and *Saccharomyces Cerevisiae*. The results suggeste that ZmRea1 is highly conserved with the Rea1 proteins in other plants as well as the Rea1 proteins of yeast, mammals and micro-organisms (Figure 3A).

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed *ZmRea1* is expressed in a broad

range of maize tissues, including silk, tassel, ear, root, husk, stem, leaf and kernel (Figure 3B). During kernel development, expression of *Rea1* occurs before 5 DAP and continues later than 25 DAP (Figure 3C). Dot-immunoblot analysis on quantified and gradient diluted total nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins detected Rea1 in these subcellular fractions, and it was predominantly found in the nuclear fraction, consistent with Rea1 localization in the nucleus (Figure 3D).

# *rea1-ref* **affects the biogenesis of 60S ribosomal subunits**

To investigate the effect of *rea1-ref* on ribosomal subunit biogenesis and the formation of monosome and polysome complexes, polysome profiles of 15 DAP *rea1-ref* and wild type kernel extracts were analyzed by 15-45% (w/v) sucrose gradient centrifugation. Two independent biological replicates were performed. This analysis revealed a significant reduction of 60S ribosomal subunits, as compared to 40S ribosomal subunits, in the mutant (Figure 4A). To compare the levels of monosomes and polysome complexes, calculation of the peak areas of A254 absorbance revealed that about 40.2% of the ribosomes in wild type kernel extracts were in polysomes, while the level of polysome complexes in *rea1-ref* kernel extracts was 57.2 % (Figure 4A). Thus, there are 1.4-Fold greater polysomes/total ribosomes in *rea1-ref*. The decrease in 60S subunits and increase in polysomes is consistent with inhibition of large ribosomal subunit export and promotion in initiation of protein synthesis as a consequence of down-regulated ribosome biogenesis in *rea1-ref*.

To confirm that maturation and export of 60S subunits is reduced in *rea1-ref*, immunoblot analysis with 60S and 40S subunit antibodies was performed on nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions from 15 and 18 DAP *rea1-ref* or wild-type kernels. Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were subjected to immunoblot analysis with antibodies against Bip (cytoplasm marker), and histone (nucleus marker). Tubulin and TATA-box binding protein (TBP) served as sample loading controls of cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins, respectively. The level of eRPL13 was examined using a L13-specific antibody. L13 protein was markedly decreased in both the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions of *rea1-ref* compared to wild-type kernels (Figure 4B). The level of eRPS14 was also examined using a specific antibody, and its content was the same in both the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions of *rea1-ref* and wild-type kernels (Figure 4B). Meanwhile, we also observed slight decrease of histone protein in *rea1-ref* nuclear fraction (Figure 4B).

A reduction of 60S ribosomal subunits in the cytoplasm of 15 and 18 DAP *rea1-ref* and wild-type endosperms was also observed by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) analysis. There were fewer ribosomes on rough ER (RER), and the RER around protein bodies (PBs, Supplemental Figure 4). Nucleolus stress due to ribosomal failure alters the morphology and increases the surface area of nucleolus in humans (Bailly et al., 2015). This stress, which misshapes and expands nucleolus, was also observed in *rea1-ref* endosperm by TEM (Figure 4C). All these data are consistent with a biogenesis defect of 60S subunits and demonstrates it is specific to the 60S maturation and export pathway.

# *rea1-ref* **affects transcription of ribosome biogenesis, translational elongation and nucleosome-related genes**

We compared the transcript profile of 15 DAP *rea1-ref* and wild type endosperm using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Among the 45,730 gene transcripts detected by RNA-seq, significantly differentially transcribed genes (DTGs) were identified as those with a threshold fold change>2 and p-value<0.05. Based on this criterion, 2,076 genes showed significant altered expression between *rea1-ref* and the wild type. There were 1,518 genes with increased transcription, while 558 genes showed decreased transcription.

Within the 2,076 DTGs, 39.9% could be functionally annotated (annotations were found using BLASTN and BLASTX analyses against the Genbank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database). Gene Ontology (GO; http://bioinfo.cau.edu.cn/agriGO/) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) pathway analysis indicated that 828 DTGs were mostly related to four GO terms: GO: 0005840 (ribosome, p-value=2.34E-130); GO: 0006414 (translational elongation, p-value=2.30E-17); and GO: 0000786 (nucleosome, p-value= 8.22E-29); GO: 0045735 (nutrient reservoir activity, p-value=6.47E-33). This analysis is illustrated in Figure 5A and Supplemental Table 1.

Ninety-eight DTGs classified to GO: 0005840 (ribosome) could be divided into three categories: small ribosomal subunit proteins, e.g., eRPS6 (GRMZM5G851698), eRPS13 (GRMZM2G130544); large ribosomal subunit proteins, e.g. eRPL14 (GRMZM2G168330), eRPL18 (GRMZM2G030731); and ribosome biogenesis factors. Transcription of all the genes related to ribosome biogenesis was increased in *rea1-ref* endosperm. *rea1-ref* also has a strong impact on translational elongation. The twenty DTGs involved in GO: 0006414 (translational elongation) could be divided into two categories: 60S acidic ribosomal proteins, e.g. eRPLP0 (GRMZM2G066460), eRPLP1 (GRMZM2G157443); and translation elongation factors, e.g. eEF1α (GRMZM2G151193), eEF1β (GRMZM2G122871). These genes were also up-regulated. Fifty-two DTGs related to GO: 0000786 (nucleosome) could be divided into two categories: histones, e.g., H2A (GRMZM2G056231), H2B (GRMZM2G401147); and nucleosome assembly protein (NAP, GRMZM2G176707). Transcription of these genes, which are related to nucloesome assembly and cell cycle, was markedly induced in *rea1-ref* endosperm. DTGs involved in GO: 0045735 (nutrient reservoir activity) were storage proteins, including 22kD α-zein (GRMZM2G346897), and 19kD α-zein (GRMZM2G059620), e.g. these genes were down-regulated. To validate the differences observed by RNA-Seq, we performed qRT-PCR on the most significant DTGs from each GO category, and the results confirmed similar differences of mRNA accumulation (Figure 5B).

# *rea1-ref* **exhibits uncoordinated expression of distinct groups of genes at**

# **the translational level**

The increase in polysomes in *rea1-ref* indicated promotion in initiation of protein synthesis in response to down-regulated ribosome biogenesis. The mechanisms that underlie differences of mRNA translation involve sequence features of individual mRNAs and the phosphorylation status of translation initiation factors (Bailey-Serres and Dawe, 1996). General Control Non-derepressing kinase-2 (GCN2) was reported to phosphorylate eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) to down-regulate translation (Zhang et al., 2008). We firstly measured the phosphorylation levels of eIF2α in 15 and 18 DAP *rea1-ref* and wild-type cytoplasms by protein gel blot analysis with P-eIF2α and eIF2α (total eIF2α as control) antibodies. Compared with wild type, eIF2α in *rea1-ref* was significantly less phosphorylated, while the eIF2α protein level was not altered (Figure 6A). The level of eEF1α protein in *rea1-ref* and wild-type cytoplasms was examined using specific antibody. eEF1α was markedly increased in *rea1-ref* (Figure 6A). These results indicated that initiation and elongation of translation are promoted in *rea1-ref.*

The level of an mRNA in polysomes reflects its translation (Branco-Price et al., 2005). To examine the effects of *rea1-ref* on translational regulation of individual mRNAs, we evaluated the amount of RNA in polysomes, relative to the total amount of transcript in 15DAP *rea1-ref* and wild type kernels. Kernel extracts were centrifuged (170,000 g) to obtain a polysome pellet for comparing total extract and polysome-bound RNA samples by RNA-Seq analysis. The polysome-bound/total RNA ratios in 15DAP *rea1-ref* and wild type kernels were 36.4% and 25.6%, respectively. Consequently, there was a 1.4-Fold increase of polysome-bound/total RNA in *rea1-ref*, which is consistent with the polysome complexes/total ribosomes A254 absorbance by ribosome profile analysis.

Within the 30,188 gene transcripts detected by RNA-seq, significantly differentially translated RNAs (DTRs) were identified as those with a 2nP/T (normalized polysome-bound/total) ×100% (see Methods) between *rea1-ref* and wild type, fold change>2.0 or <0.5. Based on this criterion, 1,802 genes showed significantly increased translation in *rea1-ref* compared to the wild type, while 2,959 genes showed decreased translation. To confirm the differences between wild type and *rea1-ref* endosperm observed by RNA-Seq, we performed qRT-PCR on the most significantly increased or decreased DTRs selected from each category and the results were consistent (Figure 6B&C). We also performed puromycin treatment for the releasing of polysome as negative control (Supplemental Figure 5). No significant difference of sequence features was observed between the up-regulated and down-regulated DTRs (Supplemental Table 2).

Within the increased DTRs, 687 could be functionally annotated. GO analysis indicated these RNAs are mostly related to three GO terms, GO: 0045449 (Regulation of transcription, p-value=1.33E-03), GO: 0045735 (Nutrient reservoir activity, p-value=3.51E-12) and GO: 0006414 (Translational

elongation, p-value= 1.17E-07). For the decreased DTRs, 601 could be functionally annotated, belonging to GO: 0006334 (Nucleosome assembly, p-value=6.59E-10), GO: 0006260 (DNA replication, p-value=1.18E-03) and GO: 0033279 (Ribosomal subunits, p-value= 1.16E-03). Ten most strongly up-regulated or down-regulated DTRs of each classification are illustrated in Table 1 and all DTRs are shown in Supplemental Table 2.

Transcriptional factors (e.g. NAC domain transcription factors, MADS-box transcription factors) and translation elongation-related genes had a markedly higher translation level in *rea1-ref*. Although the expression of zeins was down-regulated in *rea1-ref* (Figure 5), surprisingly their translation level is significantly increased (Table 1& Supplemental Table 2). There is significant overlap (p-value=2.23E-16, chi-sq test) for zeins between transcriptional down-regulated genes and translational up-regulated genes (Figure 6E). Meantime, although the expression of histone RNAs was up-regulated in *rea1-ref* (Figure 5), their translation level was dramatically reduced. There is also significant overlap (p-value=1.57E-14, chi-sq test) for histones between transcriptional up-regulated genes and translational down-regulated genes (Figure 6E). DNA replication related genes (e.g. DNA polymerase subunits, minichromosome maintenance proteins (MCMs)) had lower translation level in *rea1-ref*. Histones and DNA replication related genes are both related to nucloesome assembly and cell cycle. Although the transcription of ribosomal subunit proteins is up-regulated in *rea1-ref* (Figure 5), their translation level is dramatically down-regulated. There is overlap between transcriptional up-regulated genes and translational down-regulated genes for ribosomal proteins (Figure 6E). These results demonstrate that the transcriptional and translational regulation of individual genes responding to reduced 60S ribosome exportation is not always consistent.

We measured the level of 22kD α-zeins in 470 ng and 1,190 ng total proteins of 15 DAP *rea1-ref* and wild-type kernels, respectively, by protein gel blot analysis with 22kD α-zein antibodies. Compared with the wild type, 22kD α-zeins in *rea1-ref* are significantly increased. Meanwhile, there is no effect on 19kD α-zein content (Figure 6D). Increased eEF1α protein content (Figure 6A) and lower protein content of histone (Figure 4B) in *rea1-ref* also confirmed their increased or decreased translation level.

# *rea1-ref* **inhibits cell proliferation and cell growth**

The synthesis of nucloesome assembly proteins that related to cell cycle transition is markedly reduced in *rea1-ref* (Table1&Figure 6). This mutant exhibits a slow growth phenotype for both kernels and seedlings. There was a more than three days' delay in endosperm development (Figure 1). Endoreduplication is a general feature of endosperm development in maize, involving replication of the nuclear genome without cell division, and leading to elevated nucleic acid content (Sabelli and Larkins, 2009). Endoreduplication includes only G1 and S phase, which is different from the mitotic cell cycle (G1-S-G2-M).

Flow cytometry (FCM) analysis of 15 DAP *rea1-ref* and wild type endosperms showed endoreduplicated nuclei with C-values of 12C or greater, accounting for 18.1% of the DNA in 15 DAP endosperm of *rea1-ref*, and 22.2% of the DNA in 15 DAP wild-type endosperm (Figure 7A). At 18 DAP, there are 19.3 and 24.2% endoreduplicated nuclei with C-values of 12C or greater in *rea1-ref* and wild-type endosperm, respectively (Figure 7B). The mitotic cell cycle was also assessed in 7 DAG seedlings by FCM. The result showed that 63.3% of the nuclei are with 2C DNA content in *rea1-ref* 7 DAG seedlings, while 45.1% of the nuclei in the wild type seedlings are with 2C DNA content (Figure 7C). These results demonstrate that mutation of *rea1-ref* affects the cell proliferation. The first leaf of 7 DAG *rea1-ref* and wild-type seedlings was analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to observe the cell size of lower epidermis (Figure 7D). There was significantly smaller cell size in *rea1-ref* seedling than wild type; with cell width and cell length both decreased (Figure 7E). These results demonstrate that cell growth and cell proliferation are suppressed as a result of impaired 60S ribosome maturation, resulting in a developmental delay.

# **Discussion**

# *rea1-ref/dek\** **is a weak mutant allele that functionally suppresses ZmRea1 and affects 60S ribosome biogenesis**

Rea1 is a highly conserved ribosome biogenesis factor first identified in the

Nug1-purified pre-60S subunit in yeast, which is the pre-ribosomal particle at the export from the nucleolus to the nucleoplasm (Bassler et al., 2001). The six ATPase modules of Rea1 create a ring domain, while the large linker and the MIDAS domain compose the tail structure (Ulbrich et al., 2009). Rea1 attaches the pre-ribosome at the Rix1 complex (Rix1-Ipi3-Ipi1) via the ATPase ring domain (Nissan et al., 2002; Nissan et al., 2004; Galani et al., 2004). The tail of Rea1 containing the MIDAS domain contacts the pre-ribosome at other positions where the pre-60S factor, Rsa4 or Ytm1, is located. Ytm1 associates with nucleolar pre-60S particles, while Rsa4 associates with nucleoplasmic particles (Bassler et al., 2001; Miles et al., 2005; De la Cruz et al., 2005; Ulbrich et al., 2009). The MIDAS domain of Rea1 interacts with the MIDO domain of Ytm1or Rsa4; this interaction is essential for 60S unit maturation and export from the nucleolus to the nucleoplasm, or from the nucleoplasm to cytoplasm, respectively (Bassler et al., 2001; Ulbrich et al., 2009). Rea1 is bound to the pre-60S ribosome at two distinct sites: one is mediated via the motor ring domain and the other via MIDAS interaction with Ytm1 or Rsa4, creating a mechanochemical device to release Ytm1 or Rsa4 for 60S ribosome export (Kressler et al., 2012).

Compared to *dek1*, which creates severe effects on kernel development (Becraft et al., 2002), *dek\** causes only mild effects. The mutant kernels have an obvious small phenotype and decreased seed weight (Figure 1), with a delay of embryo, endosperm and seedling development (Figure 1). *rea1-ref/dek\** is a weak, non-lethal mutant allele, where the 2,359th Ala (GCC) of ZmRea1 is replaced by Val (GTC, Figure 2). The *rea1* mutant allele derived from a Mutator transposon insertion in the coding region has a defective phenotype and is lethal. *rea1-ref* is defective at the highly conserved linkage region and might suppress function of ZmRea1 by affecting the mechanical force of the ATPase ring domain to the MIDAS tail that releases Rsa4 or Ytm1 factors. The expression of *Rea1* is increased at the transcript level in *rea1-ref*, which might be a response to its functional suppression (Figure 2).

The characterization of *dek\** provides the first description of Rea1 in maize. A significant reduction of mature 60S subunits were observed in yeast *rea1* mutants at restrictive conditions (Garbarino and Gibbons, 2002; Galani et al., 2004). *Rea1* homologous gene in *arabidopsis* is essential for female gametophyte development (Chantha et al., 2010). Rea1 AAA-ATPase is conserved from yeast to humans (Bassler et al., 2010). Phylogenetic analysis of ZmRea1 suggests Rea1 protein is highly conserved in plants, and is homologous to proteins in yeast, mammals and micro-organisms (Figure 3). A significant reduction of mature 60S ribosomal subunits is observed in the *rea1-ref* mutant, consistent with the effects observed in yeast *rea1*, indicating an biogenesis defect specific to the 60S subunit maturation pathway; maturation and export of 40S subunits is not affected (Figure 4). This indicates conserved function on 60S subunit biogenesis of Rea1 in yeast and plants. There is reduction of mature 60S subunits in cytoplasm and pre-60S subunits in nucleus (Figure 4&8), indicating the nucleus detained pre-60S subunits might be degraded, for pre-mature ribosomal particles with biogenesis failure would be dispersed and degraded in the nucleoplasm (Andersen et al., 2005; Lam et al., 2007).

#### **Impaired ribosome biogenesis enhances ribosome use efficiency**

Regulation of protein synthesis is a key control point in cellular responses to distinct stresses (Faye et al., 2014). The proportion of actively translating ribosomes is reflected by the percentage of polysome complexes/total ribosomes (Branco-Price et al., 2005; Faye et al., 2014). We observed a 1.4-Fold increase of polysome complexes/total ribosomes in *rea1-ref* (Figure 4). This increase in polysomes in *rea1-ref* is indicative of a promotion in the initiation of protein synthesis. The proportion of actively translating ribosomes might be in response to the down-regulation of ribosome biogenesis.

The results of our study of *rea1-ref* suggest suppressed ribosome export enhances ribosome use efficiency and cellular translational efficiency. mRNA, the 40S ribosomal subunit and eIF2α comprise the 43S pre-initiation complex, termed "half-mer", before attachment of the 60S ribosomal subunit (Helser et al., 1981; Moy et al., 2002). Multiple eukaryotic protein kinases each of which responds to different signals are known to phosphorylate eIF2α and down-regulate general translation initiation, (Chen and London, 1995; Harding et al., 1999; Williams, 1999; Harding et al., 2000; Huizen et al., 2003). GCN2 is the only eIF2α kinase found in all eukaryotes, including plants like *Arabidopsis* (Berlanga et al., 1999; Zhang et al., 2008). Phosphorylation of eIF2α is

significantly reduced in *rea1-ref* (Figure 6), indicating increased formation of pre-initiation complexes for protein synthesis, consistent with an increased proportion of actively translating ribosomes. Further more, the level of eEF1α protein is markedly increased in *rea1-ref* (Figure 6), suggesting a promotion of both initiation and elongation of translation in *rea1-ref.* Consequently, there is evidence for increased efficiency of ribosome usage during translation in *rea1-ref* to ensure normal rates of protein synthesis (Figure 8).

# **Impaired ribosome biogenesis triggers distinct transcriptional and translational cellular responses**

We have found the suppressed ribosome maturation associated with *rea1-ref* brings about global changes in transcription and translation. Translation of individual mRNAs is regulated, producing discrepancies between mRNA and protein levels. mRNAs have a distinct pattern of ribosome loading under certain conditions, resulting in altered translational efficiencies (Branco-Price et al., 2005; Gawron et al., 2014). Thus, analysis of transcript level is insufficient to completely describe cellular responses under different conditions. There is also alternative translation that contributes to the complexity of proteomes (Preiss et al., 2003; Serikawa et al., 2003; MacKay et al., 2004; Blais et al., 2004; Kawaguchi et al., 2004; Branco-Price et al., 2005; Lin et al., 2014). According to our transcriptome and translatome analysis, there is evidence for consistent and inconsistent transcriptional and translational regulation of genes in *rea1-ref* endosperm (Figure 8). The large amount of transcriptionally up-regulated genes is not the consequence of developmental delay, according to the expression data for developing maize kernels (Chen et al., 2014). Our transcriptome analysis revealed immediate cellular responses, while the translatome revealed specific protein changes that are independent of transcriptional regulation for efficient use of limited ribosomes and energy.

For nucleus located proteins, transcription of small and large ribosomal subunit proteins is increased in *rea1-ref* endosperm (Figure 5), whereas their translation is down-regulated (Table 1). The transcriptional up-regulation of ribosomal proteins might be a response to a decreased level of mature ribosomes in the cytoplasm in *rea1-ref* (Figure 4). But increased transcription is incapable of rescuing 60S ribosome export in *rea1-ref*. Consequently, the percentage of polysome-bound RNA of these genes might be reduced for more efficient usage of limited ribosomes. Similar to ribosomal proteins, there is also a discrepancy in expression of nucleosome assembly-related genes. Transcription of histones is increased in *rea1-ref* (Figure 5), while their translation is down-regulated (Table 1). DNA replication-related genes also have reduced translation in *rea1-ref* (Table 1). The polysome-bound RNA of histone- and DNA replication- related genes are markedly decreased (Figure 6); and the histone protein content is down-regulated in *rea1-ref* (Figure 4). Up-regulation of histone transcription might be a response to decreased growth vigor of *rea1-ref* (Figure 1), in order to accelerate growth rate. But accelerated growth might be lethal due to ribosome shortage. Different kinds of transcriptional factors have markedly higher translation in *rea1-ref* (Table 1), including auxin-signaling related genes (GRMZM2G081930 and GRMZM5G809195, Zhang et al., 2014). Among cytosol-located proteins, transcription and translation of translation elongation-related genes are both up-regulated in *rea1-ref* (Figure 5&Table 1). The final polysome-bound RNA content of eEF1α and eEF-Tu, as well as the protein level of eEF1α are markedly increased in *rea1-ref* (Figure 6). There is also inconsistent transcriptional and translational levels of Rea1 itself (Figure 2), and it might be a developmental stage-dependent translational regulation

In maize kernel, the most abundant protein is zein storage protein, that accounts for 50%-70% of the total protein (Holding and Larkins, 2009), and α-zein is the largest class of zein protein (Heidecker and Messing, 1986). The transcriptionally down-regulated zeins might be the consequence of developmental delay. α-zeins have an increased translation level (Table 1), especially the 22kD α-zeins show markedly increased protein in both SDS-PAGE and immunoblot analysis (Figure 1&Figure 6). The percentage of α-zein polysome-bound RNA might be increased to ensure basic protein storage in *rea1-ref*.

The mechanisms that underlie variation in translation of individual genes are likely to involve features of the mRNA sequence (Bailey-Serres and Dawe, 1996). Evaluation of highly translated genes under hypoxia in *Arabidopsis*  showed mRNA sequences with a low GC nucleotide content in the 5'-untranslated region (UTR) (Branco-Price et al., 2005). RNA 5'- UTR GC content, 5'- UTR length, and ORF length were also observed to affect ribosome loading (Jiao and Meyerowitz, 2010; Yángüez et al., 2013). When we analyzed sequence features that might affect ribosome loading of individual gene transcripts affected by shortage of 60S ribosome subunits (Supplemental Table 3), no significantly different features were observed between the 1,802 up-regulated DTRs and 2,959 down-regulated DTRs, compared to 3,000 randomly selected control genes. However, there might be other feedback pathways for independent regulation at the transcriptional and translational levels.

# **Impaired ribosome biogenesis affects cell growth and proliferation**

Cell growth and proliferation are tightly linked, as enhanced protein synthesis is required for cell proliferation (Thomas, 2002). The increase in protein synthesis is accomplished by an enhanced rate of ribosome biogenesis in support of the metabolic effort for cell proliferation (Sollner-Webb and Tower, 1986). Normal mitosis includes four successive phases: G1 (postmitotic interphase), S (DNA synthesis phase), G2 (postsynthetic phase), and M (mitosis) (Fowler et al., 1998; Riou-Khamlichi et al., 2000), where as endoreduplication of endosperm includes only G1 and S phases (Sabelli and Larkins, 2009). *rea1-ref* exhibits slower growth and cell proliferation (Figure 7), indicating an intrinsic link between ribosome biogenesis and cell cycle transition. Based on our analysis, this linkage is through regulation of DNA replication and nucleosome assembly.

In mammalian cells, the tumor suppressor, p53, has been shown to arrest the cell cycle at the G1–S transition in response to impaired ribosome biogenesis, while p53-independent cell cycle arrest in response to alteration of ribosome biogenesis has also been described (Mayer and Grummt, 2005; Zhang and Lu, 2009; Deisenroth and Zhang, 2010; Grimm et al., 2006; Donati et al., 2011). p53 stabilization leads to cell cycle arrest through the regulation of cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) (Sherr and McCormick, 2002). But the expression level of cyclins does not appear to be affected in *rea1-ref* according to our trancriptome and translatome analysis. The target of rapamycin (TOR) kinase is evolutionarily conserved among plant, yeast, and animal cells, and is reported to integrate nutrient and energy signaling partly

through the phosphorylation of RPS6 to promote ribosome biogenesis, polysome accumulation, translation initiation, cell growth and cell proliferation (Xiong and Sheen, 2014). The transcription and translation level of TOR signaling-related genes does not appear to be affected in *rea1-ref*, but there might be an altered phosphorylation level of RPS6 or other post-translational level regulation that is responsible for immediate transcriptional up-regulation of genes in *rea1-ref*. The underlying mechanisms merit further explorations.

S phase is the period for DNA replication, histone synthesis and nucleosome assembly. Nucleosome assembly is essential for a variety of biological processes, such as cell cycle progression, development and senescence (Gal et al., 2015). The synthesis of nucleosome assembly-related proteins (histones and DNA replication-related enzymes) might be reduced to decelerate the growth rate for survival under the suppressed ribosome biogenesis condition in *rea1-ref* (Table 1&Figure 6). As a result, the nucleosome assembly process during S phase would be dramatically suppressed. The cell proliferation is slowed in *rea1-ref* (Figure 7), thus together with impaired cell growth kernel and seedling development are slowed for more than three days (Figure 1).

*rea1-ref/dek\** as a non-lethal maize small kernel mutant offered an opportunity to explore comprehensive cellular responses to impaired 60S ribosome biogenesis. Based on our results, we propose reduced 60S ribosome biogenesis lead to differentially regulating the transcription and translation of distinct groups of genes that affect translation efficiency and cell proliferation (Figure 8). Firstly, there is increased efficiency of translation initiation and ribosome usage. Secondly, there is selective translational regulation of different groups of genes for intensive usage of quantitatively limited mature ribosome. Finally, there is inhibited cell proliferation, leading to slower growth and survival.

# **Materials and Methods**

# **Plant Materials**

The o\*-N1117 mutant stock was obtained from the Maize Genetics Cooperation stock

center and identified as an EMS-induced allele of the *opaque15* mutant. The *dek\** mutation was separated from the o\*-N1117 stock as an independent mutant. The *dek\** mutant was crossed to the W64A inbred line and a F1 and F2 was produced to generate a mapping population. All plants were cultivated in the field at the campus of Shanghai University. Seeds were harvested at 5, 9, 13, 15, 17, 18, 21, 25 and 36 DAP.

#### **Paraffin and resin sections**

The 15 and 18 DAP embryos were fixed at 4°C overnight in FAA [ethanol 50% (v/v), acetic acid 5.0% (v/v) and formaldehyde 3.7% (v/v)]. After embedding in paraffin, 10 μm microtome sections on glass slides were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated, and stained with fuchsin. The 15 and 18 DAP endosperm tissues were fixed at 4°C overnight in FAA [ethanol 50% (v/v), acetic acid 5.0% (v/v) and formaldehyde 3.7% (v/v)]. After embedding in Spurr's epoxy resin, thin sections (1μm) were heat fixed to glass slides and stained with fuchsin. Stained sections were rinsed in water three times and air dried. Bright-field photographs of the sections were taken using a Leica microscope.

#### **Transmission Electron Microscopy**

The 15 and 18 DAP kernels of *rea1-ref* and wild type were prepared according to Lending and Larkins, (1992), with some modifications: kernels were fixed in paraformaldehyde and post-fixed in osmium tetraoxide. After dehydration in an ethanol gradient, samples were transferred to a propylene oxide solution and gradually embedded in acrylic resin (London Resin Company). Sections (70 nm) were made with a diamond knife microtome (Reichert Ultracut E). Sample sections were stained with uranyl acetate, post-stained with lead citrate, and observed with a Hitachi H7600 transmission electron microscope.

# **Scanning Electron Microscopy**

The first leaf mature region of 7 DAG *rea1-ref* and wild-type seedlings was fixed at 4°C overnight in FAA [ethanol 50% (v/v), acetic acid 5.0% (v/v) and formaldehyde 3.7% (v/v)]. Samples were critically dried and spray coated with gold. Gold-coated samples were then observed with a scanning electron microscope (S3400N; Hitachi).

# **Protein Quantification**

Mature kernels of *rea1-ref* and wild type were soaked in water and endosperm was separated from the embryo and pericarp. Endosperm samples were critically dried to constant weight, powdered in liquid N2, and measured according to (Wang et al., 2011). Proteins were extracted from 50 mg of 3 pooled endosperm flour samples. Extracted proteins were measured using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Pierce) according to instructions. Measurements of all samples were replicated three times.

#### **Measurement of starch**

Mature kernels of *rea1-ref* and wild type were soaked in water, and endosperm was then separated from the embryo and pericarp. Endosperm samples were dried to constant weight, pulverized in liquid N2, and starch was extracted and measured using an amyloglucosidase /α- amylase method starch assay kit (Megazyme) according to the instructions adapted as: add 0.2 mL of aqueous ethanol (80 % v/v) to 100 mg sample and aid dispersion; immediately add 3 mL of thermostable α-amylase. Incubate the tube in a boiling water bath for 6 min; place the tube in a bath at 50°C; add 0.1 mL amyloglucosidase; stir the tube on a vortex mixer and incubate at 50°C for 30 min; transfer duplicate aliquots (0.1 mL) of the diluted solution to the bottom, add 3.0 mL of GOPOD reagent and incubate the tubes at 50°C for 20 min; read the absorbance for each sample, and the D-glucose control at 510 nm against the reagent blank. The percentage of starch content in per mg dried sample was analyzed.

Soluble amino acids were analyzed according to (Holding et al.*,* 2010): 3 mg samples were refluxed for 24 hr in 6N HCl. Samples were hydrolyzed at 110℃ for 24 hr. Sample hydrolysates were critically dried and dissolved in 10 ml of citrate buffer. The amino acids were analyzed with a Hitachi-L8900 amino acid analyzer at Shanghai Jiaotong University. On the wt and dek kernels analyses were replicated three times.

# **Ribosome profile and isolation of polysomal RNA**

For the ribosome profile, approximately 2.5ml of pulverized tissue (approx. twenty 15 DAP kernels) was hydrated in two volumes of polysome extraction buffer (PEB) [200mM Tris–HCl (pH 9.0), 200mM KCl, 25mM EGTA, 35mM MgCl2, 1% (w/v) Brij-35, 1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 1 % (v/v) Tween-20, 1% (v/v) Igepal CA–630, 1% (w/v) deoxycholic acid, 1% (v/v) polyethylene-10-tridecylether, 1mM Phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 0.5mg/ml heparin, 5mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 50 mg/ml cycloheximide, 50 mg/ml chloramphenicol] (Kawaguchi et al., 2004), homogenized, filtered through two layers of sterile Miracloth (Calbiochem, La Jolla, CA), and cleared by centrifugation (16,000 g, 4℃, 15 min). Four hundred A260 units of the supernatant was layered over a 15–45% (w/v) sucrose density gradient, centrifuged (237,000 g, 2.5h, at 4℃, Beckman Optima™ L-100 XP) and the A254 nm absorbance profile was recorded with chart recorder by using a gradient fractionator connected to a UA-5 detector (BIOCOMP,Canada) as described previously (Kawaguchi et al., 2003, 2004). Two independent biological replicates were performed.

For isolation of polysomal RNA, approximately 5 ml of pulverized tissue powder (approx. forty 15 DAP kernels) was hydrated in two volumes of PEB, homogenized, filtered through two layers of sterile Miracloth (Calbiochem, La Jolla, CA), and cleared by centrifugation (16,000 g, 4℃, 15 min). The supernatant was layered over a 1.75 M sucrose cushion [400mM Tris–HCl (pH 9.0), 200mM KCl, 30mM MgCl2, 1.75 M sucrose, 5mM DTT, 50 mg/ml chloramphenicol, 50 mg/ml cycloheximide], and centrifuged at 170,000 g, at 4 ℃ for 3 h (modified from Fennoy and Bailey-Serres, 1995). The polysome pellet was washed with sterile water and resuspended in 700 μl PEB lacking heparin and detergents. Total or polysome-bound RNA was precipitated from total supernatant or the ribosome fraction of the same amount of sample powder, by addition of 2.5 vol of 8 M guanidine chloride and 3.5 vol of 99% (v/v) ethanol, and extracted by use of Qiagen Plant RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. For negative control, same amount of pulverized tissue powder was hydrated in two volumes of PEB buffer with 2mM puromycin.

The polysome-bound/total RNA value for individual genes was determined from the ratio of the signal in the polysome RNA sample divided by the signal in the total RNA sample. Normalization was performed according to Branco-Price et al., 2005. Polysomes accounted for 57.2% and 40.2% of the total absorbance in *rea1-ref* and wild type kernels, respectively. The percentage of an individual mRNA species in polysomes was calculated as, 2nP/T (normalized polysome-bound/total) ×100%.
