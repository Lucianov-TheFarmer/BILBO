#### **Abstract**

Cucumber is one of the most important vegetables grown worldwide due to its important economic and nutritional value. The cucumber fruit morphologically consists of the undesirable stalk and the tasty fruit; however, physiological differentiation of these two parts and the underlying molecular basis remain largely unknown. Here we characterized the physiological differences among the pedicel, stalk, and fruit, and compared the respective phloem transcriptomes using laser capture microdissection coupled with RNA-Seq. We found that the pedicel was marked with minor cell expansion and high concentration of stachyose, the stalk was featured with rapid cell expansion and high raffinose accumulation, and the fruit was highlighted with transition from cell division to cell expansion and high levels of monosaccharides. Analyses of transcriptome data indicated that cell wall and calcium ion binding related genes contributed to the cell expansion in the pedicel and stalk, whereas genes implicated in cell cycle and hormone actions regulated the transition from cell division to cell expansion in the fruit. Differential sugar distribution in these three phloem-connected tissues resulted from tissue-specific sugar metabolism and transport. Enrichment of transcription factors in the stalk and fruit may facilitate nutrient accumulation in these sink organs. As such, phloem accommodated gene expression partially orchestrated physiological differentiation of pedicel, stalk and fruit in cucumber. In addition, we identified 432 cucumber-unique genes and five phloem markers guiding future functional studies.

**Keywords:** phloem; cucumber; laser capture microdissection; RNA-Seq; physiology.

#### **Introduction**

Cucumber (*Cucumis sativus L.*) is a globally cultivated vegetable that is of important economic and nutritional value (Huang et al. 2009). Cucumber fruit develops from an enlarged ovary through three successive phases: a short fruit set, followed by rapid cell division, and then by an exponential cell expansion (Fu et al. 2010; Marcelis and Hofman-Eijer 1993; Yang et al. 2013). Cucumber fruits are usually harvested and consumed 7-14 days after anthesis (DAA), and can be served as fresh product or processed pickles. Morphologically, cucumber fruit can be divided into two parts: the stalk (Figure 1A, red square brackets) and the fruit (Figure 1A, blue square bracket). The stalk usually has no placenta inside, much less spines on the surface, and less desirable taste as compared to the rest of fruit. Therefore, the stalk length directly affects the product appearance and quality. The stalk length of cucumber varies from less than 1 cm to over 12 cm, accounting for as much as 35% of the total fruit length (4 cm to 35 cm, depending on varieties and geographic regions) (Figure 1A) (Qi et al. 2013). However, physiological differentiation of the stalk and fruit, as well as the underlying molecular basis remains largely elusive.

Both stalk and fruit are edible sink organs, with most organic nutrients transported in via the un-edible pedicel (Figure 1A, black square brackets) through vascular system. The vascular system plays essential roles in the development of land plants. Xylem and phloem are the primary components of vascular system, with xylem transporting water and solute minerals, and phloem distributing photosynthetic products as well as various systemic signals from source to sink organs, such as fruits, tubers, and roots (Lucas et al. 2013; Ruiz-Medrano et al. 2001). Recent studies showed that large amounts of macromolecules including proteins, peptides, mRNAs, small RNAs and phytohormones are present in the phloem translocation system, and serve as effective long-distance signals that orchestrate physiological, developmental and defense-related processes in plants (Corbesier et al. 2007; Kunkel and Brooks 2002; Lough and Lucas 2006; Xoconostle-Cazares et al. 1999). The phloem tissue develops from the phloem mother cell through asymmetric divisions and a series of differentiation, and is comprised of three structurally and functionally distinct cell types: the sieve elements for transporting organic materials, and the companion cells and phloem parenchyma for providing metabolic support to the sieve elements (Knoblauch and Oparka 2012).

 Cucumber has served as a model system for plant phloem research due to the ease of collecting phloem sap and readily visualizing the large sieve elements (Zhang et al. 2010). Different from most other higher plants, cucumber has two sets of phloem tissues: fascicular phloem and extrafascicular phloem (Clark et al. 1997; Crafts 1932; Eschrich et al. 1971). The fascicular phloem includes phloem tissues that are internal and external of xylems in the fascicular vascular bundles, a form known as bicollateral (Figure 1B). The extrafascicular phloem disperses throughout the cortex, peripheral to the vascular bundles, both outside and inside the sclerenchyma ring, forming a vein-like interwork that interconnects the vascular bundles (Crafts 1932; Zhang et al. 2010). In the fruit of cucumber, the extrafascicular phloem consists of phloem tissues from the extrafascicular vascular bundles, carpellary vascular bundles, and the placental vascular bundles (Figure 1B).

Despite the crucial role of the phloem tissue in developmental biology, only a few regulators have been identified to specifically regulate phloem formation, including the transcription factors *ALTERED PHLOEM DEVELOPMENT (APL)* and *OCTOPUS (OPS)*, and the peptide ligand *CLAVATA3/EMBRYO SURROUNDING REGION 45* (*CLE45*) and its receptor *BARELY ANY MERISTEM 3 (BAM3)* (Bonke et al. 2003; Rodriguez-Villalon et al. 2014; Truernit et al. 2012). To date, most studies of phloem functions have used phloem exudates collected after vasculature incision, or excised aphid stylets as plant materials, and the focus has been the determination of phloem sap composition using proteomic and transcriptomic analyses (Le Hir et al. 2008; Lough and Lucas 2006). This approach has two intrinsic drawbacks: First, the phloem exudate and the phloem tissue are different, and thus analyses of the phloem exudate cannot directly illustrate the function of phloem tissue. Second, there is unavoidable contamination of the contents from surrounding non-phloem cells (Zhang et al. 2010).

In order to explore the function of phloem tissue directly, and uncover the molecular basis for physiological differentiation of pedicel, stalk and fruit in cucumber, we used laser capture microdissection (LCM) coupled with the RNA-Seq technology to obtain phloem transcriptional profiling. LCM enables isolation of specific cell or tissue types from microscopic samples using a cutting or harvesting laser. Cells harvested by LCM have been used for gene expression analyses in a variety of plant cell types (Matas et al. 2011; Nelson et al. 2006; Scanlon et al. 2009; LCM to acquire phloem cells from three tissue types (pedicel, stalk and fruit as shown in Figure 1A) from the inbred line 9930. Comparative transcriptomic analyses showed that different types of genes were activated in the phloem cells of different tissues, matching their distinctive physiological characteristics. Additionally, we identified 432 cucumber-unique genes and five phloem markers for future research.

To quantitatively differentiate the development patterns of pedicel, stalk and fruit, we measured the daily length and diameter of the pedicel, stalk and fruit of a classic cucumber inbred line 9930 from anthesis to 10 days after anthesis (DAA). The pedicel showed no obvious change in length or diameter within 3DAA (Figure 2A-2B), suggesting an early developmental rhyme among the three tissues. Meanwhile, the stalk and fruit grew rapidly in both length and diameter, and displayed 7.8 and 16.8 fold increase in area (diameter x length), respectively (Figure 2A-2B). By 10 DAA, the fruit was two times larger in diameter and five times longer in length compared to the stalk, suggesting dramatically accelerated cell division or expansion in the fruit after anthesis (Figure 2A-B). Next, cell morphology was examined from 0 DAA to 10 DAA. The cell size of pedicel was much larger than that of the stalk or fruit at 0 DAA (Figure 2 C-E), and there was only mild expansion afterwards (Figure 2C, 2F, 2I). The cell size in the stalk displayed dramatic expansion at 3 DAA and kept expanding afterwards (Figure 2D, 2G, 2J), while the cell size in the fruit showed no expansion until after 3 DAA (Figure 2E, 2H, 2K), suggesting that the cell phase progressed from cell division to cell expansion first in the pedicel, then in the stalk, and finally in the fruit. By 3 DAA, cells in the pedicel displayed minor or no expansion, cells in the stalk were rapidly expanding, while cells in the fruit were still dividing.

 To further analyze physiological differences among the pedicel, stalk and fruit, we measured concentrations of soluble proteins, vitamin C, tannin, soluble sugars, and pigments in these three tissues at two developmental stages (3 DAA and 10 DAA) (Figure 3). Despite that the concentration of soluble proteins showed 15% decrease in the pedicel, and a substantially decrease in the stalk (60%) and fruit (77%) from 3 DAA to 10 DAA, the total amount of proteins reduced only in the pedicle, but increased in the stalk and fruit due to dramatic growth in these two tissues (Figure 3A, Figure 2A-2B). The concentration of vitamin C, an essential nutrient for humans and an important player in plant antioxidant system and stress response (Karpinski et al. 1997; Padayatty et al. 2003), increased in all three tissues from 3 DAA to 10 DAA, with the highest value being in the fruit at both stages (Figure 3B). We also measured the concentration of tannin, one of the major secondary metabolites (Takshak and Agrawal 2014). At 3 DAA, the fruit had the highest tannin concentration (116 µg g-1); however, the tannin concentration in the fruit decreased to 44 µg g-1 at 10 DAA, significantly lower than that in the pedicel (238 µg g -1) and stalk (55 µg g -1) (Figure 3C). This finding explains why young cucumber fruits (e.g. at 3 DAA) have a dry and puckery taste, but commercially mature fruits do not (e.g. at 10 DAA).

The concentration of soluble sugars was significantly higher in the stalk and fruit than that in the pedicel at 3 DAA (Figure 3D). By 10 DAA, the fruit contained the highest while the stalk had the lowest concentration of sugar (Figure 3D), suggesting preferential accumulation of sugars in the fruit. Quantification of each sugar type showed that monosaccharides such as glucose and fructose were the primary soluble sugar components in the stalk and fruit, whereas oligosaccharides such as stachyose and raffinose were the major components in the pedicel (Figure 3E). At 3 DAA, the pedicel had the highest concentration of stachyose but lowest raffinose, and the stalk had the highest concentration of raffinose and monosaccharides (Figure 3E). By 10 DAA, however, stachyose was undetectable in the stalk and fruit, whereas monosaccharides including glucose and fructose were highly accumulated, especially in the fruit, implying that oligosaccharides including stachyose and raffinose were converted into monosaccharides after being transported into the stalk and fruit (Hu et al. 2009). We also measured the pigment concentration (Figure 3F). The pedicel and stalk contained higher concentration of chlorophyll and carotene than the fruit at 3 DAA. But by 10 DAA, the concentration of chlorophyll and carotene in the stalk decreased to the same level as in the fruit (Figure 3F) (Vila-Aiub et al. 2005; Yang et al. 2005).

As such, from 3 DAA to 10 DAA, the pedicel was marked with minor cell expansion, decreased protein content, and high stachyose but low raffinose. The stalk was featured with rapid cell expansion and high raffinose. The fruit was highlighted with transition from cell division to cell expansion, and high amount of monosaccharides. In addition, both pedicel and stalk displayed high content of pigments, while both stalk and fruit had increased vitamin C and reduced tannin level. **Laser capture microdissection and RNA-Seq analysis of phloems from the pedicel, stalk, and fruit in cucumber** 

The pedicel, stalk, and fruit are innately connected by the phloem system that plays critical roles in transport and allocation of sugars, proteins, and many other metabolites examined in our study (Crafts 1932; Lucas et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2010). To better understand how phloem function affects physiological differentiation of these three tissues, we compared the phloem transcriptomes among pedicel, stalk and fruit using LCM combined with RNA-Seq. Our study, together with previous work, showed that the cucumber fruit started rapid growth at 3 DAA (Figure 2) (Yang et al. 2013), therefore we chose 3 DAA samples for tissue-specific gene expression analysis (Figure 4). LCM was used to collect the phloem tissues from the fascicular vascular bundle in the pedicel, stalk, and fruit (Figure 4A-4I). After two rounds of amplification, we were able to obtain enough RNA to construct RNA-Seq libraries and generate 55.7 to 81.8 million paired-end raw reads for each sample (Table 1). After removing low quality regions and adapter sequences, 49.3-74.1M clean reads were mapped to the cucumber genome and used for phloem gene expression analysis in the pedicel, stalk, and fruit (Anders et al. 2014; Robinson et al. 2010) (Table1).

In order to confirm our RNA-Seq data were phloem specific, the LCM derived phloem transcripts were compared with our RNA-Seq data from the whole fruit of cucumber inbred line 409 (wf409) (Jiang et al. 2015). Principal component analysis (PCA) using the normalized expression levels (Transcripts Per Million) showed that the whole fruit samples were clearly separated from the three sets of phloem samples derived from pedicel, stalk and fruit, indicating that the whole fruit samples had very different overall expression profiles (Figure S1). Pair-wise comparisons of the phloem and the whole fruit RNA-Seq data identified 5213, 5163, 5100 up-regulated, and 6096, 5959, 5720 down-regulated genes in the pedicel versus wf409, stalk versus wf409 and fruit versus wf409 comparison, respectively (Figure S2, Table S1-S3), suggesting that a large number of genes were differentially expressed between the whole fruit samples and the phloem samples, which had distinct expression profiles. Furthermore, we compared the expression levels of known phloem-specific genes and found that most of them indeed had much higher expressions in our phloem LCM samples than the whole fruit samples. For example, most of the phloem protein 2 related genes (18/28, 64%) showed higher expression in the phloem samples. In particular, all phloem protein 2-A1 related genes including the *CsPP2*(Csa1G542510), a phloem specific gene encoding a 26 kDa protein in Cucumber(Dinant et al. 2003) , had significantly higher expressions in the phloem samples than in the whole fruit (Figure S3). In addition, the phloem marker *CsAPL* (Csa7G240140), which was homolog of phloem marker gene *APL*(At1g79430) in Arabidopsis(Bonke et al. 2003), displayed very high enrichment in the phloem tissues than the whole fruit (Figure S3). Taken together, our RNA-Seq data obtained by LCM of the phloem tissues were indeed phloem specific and were suitable for the analysis of phloem-specific expression patterns.

A total of 15,720 genes were expressed in at least one of the three tissues at the level of at least 1 TPM (Transcripts Per Million) (Figure 5), among which 339, 145, and 469 genes were specifically expressed in the pedicel, stalk, and fruit, respectively (Figure 5); 241, 238, and 649 genes were only expressed in two tissues as the pedicel and stalk, pedicel and fruit, and stalk and fruit, respectively; and the remaining 13,639 genes were expressed in all three tissues (Figure 5). To further understand the function of the tissue-specific expressed genes, GO enrichment analysis was performed (Figure 5). GO terms "transporter activity" and "cellulose microfibril organization" were the most significantly enriched in the genes that were specifically expressed in the pedicel and stalk, respectively (Figure 5), consistent with the physiological role of pedicel in transporting organic nutrients and of stalk in rapid cell expansion during the development of cucumber fruit. GO terms "growth factor activity" and "DNA binding transcription factor" were highly enriched in the genes that were co-expressed in pedicel and fruit, and in stalk and fruit, respectively (Figure 5).

To further understand the function of the DEGs, GO enrichment analysis was performed for the DEGs from each comparison (Figure 6). GO terms "calcium ion binding" (*p*= 3.0E-05) and "pathogenesis" (*p*=3.3E-04) were highly enriched in the upregulated DEGs in pedicle, and "cell wall" (*p*=2.4E-11) and "DNA binding transcription factor" (*p*=8.7E-10) were significantly enriched in the upregulated DEGs in stalk, while "DNA binding transcription factor" (*p*=7.3E-07) and "defense response" (*p*=5.1E-04) were dramatically enriched in the upregulated DEGs in fruit (Figure 6). To understand the gene expression patterns of the DEGs, we clustered genes with similar expression patterns across the three samples using the K-means clustering algorithm. As shown in Figure 7 and Table S7, a total of 3,984 genes displayed differential expression in at least one comparison and were clustered into six groups. DEGs in clusters 1, 2 and 3 were specifically upregulated in the pedicel, stalk, and fruit, respectively; clusters 4, 5 and 6 were commonly up-regulated in two tissues as the stalk and fruit, pedicel and fruit, and pedicel and stalk, respectively (Figure 7). Cluster 4 (commonly upregulated in the stalk and fruit) was the largest group and it accounted for 31% of the DEGs. In contrast, cluster 2 (specifically upregulated in the stalk) was the smallest group and it only accounted for 10% of the DEGs, consistent with the "bridge" role of stalk between pedicel and fruit, and therefore very few genes that were specifically expressed in the stalk.

GO enrichment analysis was also performed for the DEGs in each cluster (Figure 7). As expected, for genes in cluster 1 (specifically upregulated in pedicel), the top two significantly enriched GO terms were "proteasome core complex" and "threonine−type endopeptidase activity", both of which are related to protein degradation (Sadanandom et al. 2012), suggesting that the reduced protein content from 3 DAA to 10 DAA as revealed by physiological measurement is due to protein degradation in the pedicel. GO term "raffinose alpha−galactosidase activity", which refers to the hydrolysis of raffinose into monosaccharides, was also enriched in cluster 1 (Gao and Schaffer 1999). This enrichment matches the physiological data in sugar distribution: hydrolysis of raffinose in the pedicel results in low level of raffinose, and the resultant monosaccharides are transported and accumulated in the stalk and fruit (Figure 7, Figure 3E, left). For DEGs in cluster 2 (specifically upregulated in stalk), the most significantly enriched GO term was "cell wall" (p=4.3E-09), which is in support to the rapid cell expansion in the stalk (Figure 7, Figure 2). GO term "cellular polysaccharide metabolic process" was also overrepresented in cluster 2, which explains the reduced amount of stachyose but increased raffinose in the stalk at 3 DAA (the time LCM was performed) (Figure 7, Figure 3E). Cluster 3 contained fruit-specifically expressed DEGs, and the top enriched GO terms in this cluster were mostly functioning in cell cycle arrest, which matches the physiological data showing that cells in the fruit were preparing for cell expansion (Figure 7, Figure 2). Moreover, GO term "alpha−mannosidase activity", which is involved in metabolism of sugar monomers, was also overrepresented in cluster 3, consistent with the accumulation of monosaccharides in the fruit (Figure 7, Figure 3E).

For DEGs in cluster 4 (commonly upregulated in stalk and fruit), the most

enriched GO term was "sequence-specific DNA binding transcription factor activity" (*P*-value=1.4E-04) (Figure 7), which is consistent with the findings that transcription factors were overrepresented in the co-expressed genes in stalk and fruit (Figure 5) and in the upregulated DEGs in the stalk and fruit during pairwise comparisons (Figure 6). For example, *Csa6G076710*, a cucumber *AGAMOUS*-like 6 (*AGL6*) gene showed a 512 fold (log2FC=9.0) expression in the stalk and a 588 fold (log2FC=9.2) expression in the fruit as compared to that in the pedicel. Similarly, *Csa6G426940*, a plant-specific transcription factor *YABBY* family protein, displayed a 119 fold (log2FC=6.9) and a 239 fold (log2FC=7.9) higher expression in the stalk and fruit respectively than that in the pedicel. For genes in cluster 6 (commonly up-regulated in pedicel and stalk), "calcium ion binding" (*p*-value=2.2E-05) was the most significantly enriched GO term. Calcium ion has been shown to mediate plant cell division and growth (Vissenberg et al. 2001; Zhang et al. 2002). Given that cells in the pedicel and stalk are expanding at 3DAA, calcium ion may be one of the major factors that regulate cell expansion in cucumber. In addition, genes involved in "photosynthesis, light harvesting" were also enriched in cluster 6, which explains why the pedicle and stalk contain higher levels of pigment than fruit (Figure 7, Figure 3 F, left).

The MapMan software was used to further characterize the functional categories of the DEGs. As compared to the pedicel, the DEGs in the fruit and stalk displayed similar patterns, so we only used fruit vs pedicel as an example (Figure S4). Regulation overview of the DEGs between fruit and pedicel showed that genes implicated in hormone actions (especially auxin and ethylene) were predominantly upregulated in the fruit (Figure S4A), suggesting that hormones such as auxin and ethylene may contribute to the development of cucumber fruit. Metabolism overview of the DEGs between fruit and pedicel indicated that genes involved in secondary metabolism (especially genes implicated in phenylpropanoids and phenolics) were mostly upregulated in the fruit (Figure S4B), which explains why the tannin content was significantly reduced as the fruit develops from 3 DAA to 10 DAA. Large enzyme family overview of the DEGs between fruit and pedicel showed that 18 out of 28 UDP glycosyltransferases were upregulated in the fruit (Figure S4C). For example, a putative sucrose transporter (*Csa4G617410*) (Ruan 2014) displayed a 471 fold (log2FC=8.9) in the stalk and a 1209 fold (log2FC=10.2) in the fruit higher expression than that in the pedicel. Similarly, the putative cucumber *SWEET 1* (*Csa6G343690*) and *SWEET12* (*Csa1G051720*) genes that transport monosacchride and disacchride, respectively, in *Arabidopsis* (Chen et al. 2010; Chen et al. 2012), showed a 8-16 fold and 324-344 fold higher expression in the stalk and fruit as compared to those in the pedicel, suggesting that the sugar distribution from the pedicel, stalk to fruit results not only from distinct sugar metabolism, but also from different sugar transport.

#### **Identification of cucumber-unique genes**

To identify cucumber-unique genes, we searched the sequenced plant genomes included in the Phytozome website and the watermelon genome database, and we found 432 cucumber genes that were not present in the other available plant genomes and were thus considered as cucumber-unique genes (Table S8). We used K-means clustering to classify the 432 genes into six groups (Figure S5), in which genes in cluster 2 (90 genes) displayed high expression in the pedicel, genes in cluster 3 (55 genes) were commonly upregulated in the pedicel and stalk, and genes in cluster 4 (104 genes) were co-induced in the stalk and fruit (Figure S5). Consistent to the findings in Figure 7, cluster 4 was the largest cluster and it accounted for 24% of the cucumber-unique genes, supporting the similar gene expression profiles in the stalk and fruit.

#### **Cucumber phloem markers identified by RNA-Seq**

To verify the expression pattern of DEGs, we chose six genes for *in situ* hybridization (Table S9). On the first run, we did *in situ* hybridization using materials at the same developmental stage as in the LCM and RNA-Seq (3 DAA). Unfortunately, almost no signals were observed for all gene probes we tested (data not shown), probably due to the technical obstacles of accessing mature tissues using *in situ* hybridization. Therefore, we used the fruits and pedicels at the very early stage (about 4-5 days before anthesis) as materials instead, and we were able to detect signals for five genes (except *Csa6G092560*) (Figure 8). Putative *CsXTR6* (*Csa1G422470*) (Figure 8A-8C), *CsMYB77* (Csa4G638510) (Figure 8D-8F) and *CsLOB41* (*Csa1G023060*) (Figure 8G-8I) showed highest expression in the phloem cells of the stalk (middle panel) as compared to those in the pedicel or the fruit, consistent with the expression patterns in the RNA-Seq data (Figure S7, Table S9). In addition to the predominant phloem expression, *CsXTR6* was also highly expressed in the epidermis of the stalk and the developing placenta in the fruit (which will produce placental vascular bundles) (Figure 8B-8C). Interestingly, signals of *CsLOB41*  appeared to be present only in the phloem cells of sink organs including stalk and fruit, but not in the pedicel, stem or petiole (Figure 8G-8I, Figure S8A-8B). Therefore, *CsLOB41* can serve as a developmental marker for phloem tissues specifically in the cucumber sink organs. The putative *CsEBF1* (*Csa3G878200*) and *CsNZF* (*Csa2G380050*), which encode an EIN3-binding F box protein and a Ran BP2/NZF zinc finger-like superfamily protein, respectively, showed the highest signal in the phloem of fruit, consistent with the expression patterns revealed by RNA-Seq (Figure 8J-8O, Figure S7, Table S9). *CsMYB77* and *CsNZF* can serve as housekeeping phloem markers because they were expressed in the phloem cells of all tissues we examined, including pedicel, stalk, fruit, stem and petiole, with *CsNZF* displaying much higher expression than *CsMYB77* (Figure 8, Figure S8). Negative controls using the sense probe of *CsNZF* showed no signals as expected (Figure 8P-8R). In addition, all five genes (*CsXTR6, CsMYB77, CsLOB41, CsEBF1* and *CsNZF*) had much lower expression in the whole fruit (wf409) than the phloem samples. In particular, *CsMYB77* and *CsXTR6* had the average expression level of 2.15 TPM and 0.6 TPM in the whole fruit samples, but their expression levels in the phloem samples were at least 60 fold and 20 fold higher, respectively (Figure S7, Table S12), supporting that these five genes can serve as phloem tissue markers.

#### **Discussion**

## **Growth regulation of the pedicel, stalk, and fruit by genes controlling cell wall, cell cycle, calcium signaling, and hormone actions in cucumber**

The cucumber fruit displayed the highest cell expansion, followed by the stalk, and the pedicel had the least cell expansion from 3 DAA-10 DAA (Figure 2A-2B). Specifically, cells in the pedicel are in minor or no expansion, cells in the stalk are rapidly expanding, while cells in the fruit are transiting from cell division to cell expansion at 3 DAA (Figure 2C-2K). Phloem transcriptome analyses showed that the rapid cell expansion in the stalk may be due to the upregulated genes involved in cell wall and cellulose microfibril organization (Baskin 2005; Cosgrove 2001; Nicol and Hofte 1998) (Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 7). Further, GO analyses showed that "calcium ion binding" was the most significantly enriched term for DEGs in cluster 6 (commonly up-regulated in the pedicel and stalk) (Figure 7B). Calcium ion has been shown to be a versatile intracellular messenger, and it regulates multiple signaling cascades in which many transcription factors and protein kinases are involved (Kanchiswamy et al. 2014). In *Nicotiana tabacum*, calcium ion signaling mediates cell polarity and cell elongation through regulation of auxin transport (Vissenberg et al. 2001). In rice, calcium ion can bind protein kinase to regulate cell division (Zhang et al. 2002). Calcium ion was also shown to regulate the arrangement of pectin structures to control fruit softening in tomato (Hyodo et al. 2013). Our morphological data showed that cells in both the pedicel and stalk were in expansion at 3 DAA (Figure 2), suggesting that calcium ion binding, together with the cell wall related genes, may be the primary regulators for cell expansion in the pedicel and stalk. On the other hand, cells in the fruit tissue are undergoing a transition from cell division to cell expansion at 3 DAA (Figure 2E, 2H, 2K). Consistently, genes implicated in cyclin-dependent protein kinase inhibitor and cell cycle arrest were significantly upregulated specifically in the fruit (Figure 7B). For example, 17 out of the 20 cell cycle related genes were induced in the fruit versus pedicel (Table S10). In addition, genes involved in hormone actions such as auxin and ethylene related genes were mostly upregulated in the fruit (Figure S4A), suggesting that genes related to cell cycle control and hormone actions may contribute to the progression from cell division to cell expansion in the fruit.

## **Sugar compartmentations among pedicel, stalk, and fruit are derived from differential sugar metabolism and transport**

From 3 DAA to 10 DAA, the concentration of sugar displayed a dynamic change in the stalk and fruit (Figure 3). The stalk had the highest sugar content at 3 DAA, but the fruit had the highest and the stalk had the lowest sugar content at 10 DAA (Figure 3D). Considering that the stalk and fruit experienced 7.8 and 16.8 fold increase in area (diameter x length), respectively, from 3 DAA to 10 DAA (Figure 2A-2B), the total sugar amount in the fruit was much more than that in the stalk or pedicel, consistent with the notion that sugars are transported from the pedicel to the stalk and fruit, and accumulated in the fruit. Cucumber is a typical stachyose-allocation species in which stachyose instead of sucrose is the major sugar type transferred via the phloem (Hu et al. 2009). Accordingly, sugar measurements indicate that the pedicel has high stachyose but low raffinose levels, the stalk contains a relative high amount of raffinose, while the fruit possesses high levels of monosaccharides such as glucose and fructose (Figure 3E). Our LCM-transcriptome data showed that genes functioning in hydrolysis of raffinose, polysaccharide and mannose were upregulated in the pedicel, stalk and fruit, respectively (Figure 7B). Further, most genes implicated in UDP glycosyltransferases, which function in sugar transport, were upregulated in the stalk and fruit (Figure S4C). These data suggest that photoassimilates such as stachyose, raffinose and sucrose are transported from the cucumber leaves into the pedicel (Gross and Pharr 1982), where the raffinose is quickly metabolized into monosaccharides such as glucose and fructose, and the resultant monosaccharides are transported to the stalk and fruit. Stachyose is transported through the phloem until it reaches the stalk, where it will be converted into raffinose and monosaccharides, whereas glucose and fructose are transported to and accumulated in the fruit, matching the sugar distribution in the three tissues in cucumber.

#### **Transcription factors are enriched in the stalk and fruit**

Transcription factors are a group of DNA-binding proteins that control the level of gene expression and as such play essential roles in multiple cellular processes, including transcription regulation, development, cell cycle control, response to internal or environmental signals and pathogenesis (Karin 1990; Latchman 1997). In this study, we found that transcription factors were highly enriched in the co-expressed genes in the stalk and fruit (Figure 5), in the upregulated DEGs in the fruit and stalk according to pairwise comparisons (Figure 6), as well as in the cluster 4 DEGs that were commonly upregulated in the stalk and fruit (Figure 7A). Specifically, there are 319 transcription factors that were differentially expressed in the fruit vs pedicel, in which bHLH, C2H2-Zinc Finger, Homeobox and WRKY families were highly enriched (Figure S6, Table S11). WRKY transcription factors have been shown to play important roles in stress response (Qin et al. 2013; Ren et al. 2010; Yan et al. 2014). For example, rice WRKY13 regulates the crosstalk between abiotic (drought) and biotic (disease resistance) signaling pathways through binding to different DNA sequences (Xiao et al. 2013). *Arabidopsis* WRKY33 responds to pathogen infection through functions downstream of MPK3/MPK6 (Mao et al. 2011). Here, transcripts of putative *CsWRKY13* (*Csa2G297220)* and *CsWRKY33* (*Csa3G730800*) increased 106 and 3 fold, respectively, in the fruit as compared to that in pedicel (Table S11). In a previous study on cucumber fruit development, it was shown that transcription factors were significantly enriched at the post exponential expansion stage (12-16 DAA) (Ando et al. 2012), which is consistent with the fundamental role of fruit in protecting seed development and seed dispersal. Given that the stalk and fruit displayed increased cell growth and contained more nutrients such as sugars, proteins and vitamin C than the pedicel (Figure 2, Figure 3), the enhanced expression of transcription factors in the stalk and fruit may be because the sink organs are still forming new vascular bundles or are still expanding, and thus related transcription factors are needed to tightly regulate these processes.

#### **New developmental markers for phloem cells and cucumber**

Our phloem RNA-Seq data identified several phloem markers with different

intensities. Putative *CsNZF* and *CsMYB77* were expressed in the phloem cells of all the tissues we examined (pedicel, stalk, fruit, stem and petiole), thus can be used as phloem markers throughout the cucumber (Figure 8M-8O, Figure S8). Putative *CsLOB41* and *CsEBF1* had relatively weaker signals*,* and were expressed specifically in the phloem tissues of sink organs (stalk and fruit), but not in the pedicel (Figure 8G-8L), therefore they can serve as developmental markers for phloem tissues in the sink organs of cucumber. In addition, we found that 104 out of the 432 cucumber-unique genes displayed sink organ-specific expression, and 90 genes were upregulated specifically in the pedicel (Figure S4, Table S8). Further functional studies of these tissue-specific or cucumber-specific genes will shed light on the molecular basis of the unique features of cucumber, such as phloem loading/unloading and sex determination.

#### **Materials and Methods**

#### **Plant growth conditions and sample preparation**

Cucumber (*Cucumis sativus L*.) inbred line 9930 was used in this study. Seeds were grown in pots in a growth chamber in 16h/8h, 28 ºC/18 ºC day/night cycle. Upon the two true-leaf stage, cucumber seedlings were transferred to a greenhouse in the experimental field of China Agricultural University in Beijing. Pest control and water management were carried out according to standard protocols. Cucumber young fruits at 3 DAA and 10 DAA were harvested in the morning together with the respective pedicels, and then pedicel, stalk and fruit were separated and cut into 3-mm cylinders for fixation or for physiological measurements.

#### **Determination of chlorophyll levels**

About 0.5 g fresh tissue was smashed and transferred into a 50 ml tube. Chlorophyll was extracted with 25 ml 95%(v/v)ethanol, and the resultant supernatant was measured using a spectrophotometer (UV-2102C, YOUNIKE, China) at 649 nm (A649), 665 nm (A665) and 470 nm (A470) wavelengths, respectively. Contents of chlorophyll were calculated using formula Ca = 13.95 × A665 - 6.88 × A649, Cb =24.96 × A649 - 7.32 × A665, and Cc =(1000 × A470 - 2.05 × Ca - 114.8 × Cb)/245, where Ca, Cb, and Cc denote content of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoid, respectively. Total content of chlorophyll (C) per fresh weight (mg/gFW) = C × 0.025 L/fresh weight (g)(Zaharieva and Goltsev 2003).

#### **Determination of soluble protein content**

About 1g fresh tissue was collected and extracted with 5 ml 0.05 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH = 7.8), followed by centrifugation at 4000× g for 10 min in 4°C. The resultant supernatant was admixed with 5 ml coomassie brilliant blue G-250-protein reagent, and the optical density (OD) value at the 595 nm wavelength was measured using a spectrophotometer (UV-2102C, YOUNIKE, China) (Jiang and Huang 2002).

#### **Determination of vitamin C content**

About 1g fresh tissue was fully grounded with 2 ml 2% oxalic acid solution, and the content of vitamin C was determined using calibrated 2, 6-dichloro indophenols (Karpinski et al. 1997).

#### **Measurement of the sugar content**

For determination of the soluble sugar content, about 1 g fresh sample was grounded and boiled in water bath. Soluble sugars were measured using the traditional anthrone colorimetry method by measuring the OD value at the 630nm wavelength (Hu et al. 2009). For determination of the content of each sugar type**,** about 1g fresh sample was grounded and extracted with 5 ml 80%(v/v)ethanol for 30 min at 80 ºC, followed by centrifugation at 4000× g for 10 min three times. The supernatants were combined, decolorized and dried at 40 °C. The residues were re-dissolved in 1 ml distilled water and filtered through a 0.45 um filter. High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Agilent 2100 system; Palo Alto, CA, USA) was used to measure the content of each sugar type based on standard sugar contents (Sigma) as described (Wang et al. 2014).

#### **Measurement of tannin content**

Tannin measurement was performed using the prussian blue method. About 2 g fresh samples were grounded with 5 ml distilled water and heated in 80°C water bath for 30 min. After cooling and filtering, 0.1 mol/L FeCl3 and 0.008 mol/L K3Fe (CN)<sup>6</sup> were added, and the tannin concentration was calculated based on the OD value at 650 nm wavelength and the standard curve.

#### **Tissue fixation, embedding, sectioning and laser capture microdissection**

Fresh plant samples were fixed in Farmer's Fixative, dehydrated through an ethanol/xylene series followed by a xylene/paraplast series, and embedded in paraplast and sectioned as described previously (Takacs et al. 2012). Laser capture microdissection was performed using a Leica LMD 6500 (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Two biological replicates were performed for each tissue. Each replicate comprised about 5, 000 phloem cells from 6-10 cucumber fruits or pedicels.

#### **RNA extraction and amplification**

Total RNA was extracted from the collected phloem tissues using the Arcturus PicoPure RNA isolation Kit (Applied Biosystems). DNase I (Qiagen) treatment was performed to remove potential DNA contamination. The TargetAmp 2-Round Aminoallyl-aRNA Amplification Kit 1.0 (Epicentre Biotechnologies) was used for RNA amplification according to the manufacture's protocol. RNA extracted from the phloem cells was subjected to two rounds of amplification to produce 35 to 55µg RNA per sample (Brooks et al. 2009).

#### **cDNA library construction and deep sequencing**

Amplified RNA was used as input material for RNA-seq. The RNA-Seq libraries were constructed using the NEBNext®Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, Ispawich, USA) following manufacturer's instructions, and four index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample (Wang et al. 2009). 100 bp paired-end reads were generated from the RNA-Seq libraries with an Illumina HiSeq 2000 sequencer. Sequencing data were deposited to the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) with access number GSE62338.

#### **Bioinformatics analysis of RNA-Seq data**

 Raw reads were pre-processed to remove low quality regions using the SolexQA tool (v2.2) and remove adapter sequences using the cutadapt tool (v1.3) (Martin 2011). Clean reads were mapped to the cucumber genome sequence (http://cucumber.genomics.org.cn, v2i) using TopHat (Huang et al. 2009; Trapnell et al. 2009). Read counts of annotated genes were summarized by HTSeq-count (Anders et al. 2015). Lowly expressed genes were removed and only genes with an expression level of at least 1 TPM (Transcripts Per Million) in at least two samples were retained for further analysis. The R package edgeR, which use counts per gene from different samples as input and performs data normalization using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method, was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Robinson et al. 2010). The genes with at least two-fold change in expression and a False Discovery Rate (FDR) of less than 0.05 were considered to be differentially expressed. Expressions of each gene were first normalized to transcripts per million (TPM) according to the total number of mapped clean reads in each of the six libraries. We then used the log2 values of normalized expressions to build an expression matrix and used the prcomp function in R (R-Core-Team 2014) for principle component analysis (PCA).

#### **MapMan analysis of expressed genes and DEGs**

 Genes with at least 1 TPM were considered as expressed genes. Venn Diagram (Chen and Boutros 2011) was used to indicate the overlaps of expressed genes among three tissues. In order to obtain detailed functional categorization of the commonly expressed genes between fruit and stalk or stalk and pedicel, we used the online web tool Mercator (Lohse et al. 2014) to obtain the cucumber protein annotation mapping file for MapMan with default parameters and then used the Java software MapMan to assign functional bins to each gene (Thimm et al. 2004). We used MapMan to obtain the regulation overview, metabolism overview, large enzyme family overview and transport overview for the DEGs of each comparison.

#### **Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis**

 The K-means method was used to cluster all the DEGs identified through pairwise comparisons and GO term enrichment analysis was performed for each cluster. The R package pheatmap (Kolde 2011 ) was used to draw the heat map of each cluster. Because the GO terms are not well annotated for cucumber genes, we combined blast2go (Conesa et al. 2005) and interProScan (Jones et al. 2014) search results to assign GO terms to cucumber genes. The GO term enrichment analysis was conducted for genes included in each cluster using the R package TopGO (Alexa et al. 2006). Adrian Alexi's improved weighted scoring algorithm and Fisher's test was used to determine the significance of GO term enrichment. Significantly enriched GO terms were identified as those with a p-value less than 0.05.

#### **Identification of cucumber-unique genes**

For each annotated cucumber gene, we used blastp (Camacho et al. 2009) to search for homologs (e-value < 1e-5) in other sequenced plant genomes that are included in Phytozome (http://www.phytozome.net/), and retained cucumber genes that had no hit. In addition, we removed cucumber genes that had a significant hit in the watermelon genome (http://icugi.org/cgi-bin/ICuGI/misc/download.cgi), a cucurbit genome that is not included in Phytozome. The remaining 432 cucumber genes were retained as putative cucumber-unique genes.

#### *In situ* **hybridization**

Young cucumber fruits (around 4-5 days before anthesis and at 3DAA) and the respective pedicels, together with stems close to the shoot apex and the third true leaf petioles were fixed in 3.7 % FAA, embedded, sectioned and hybridized with digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled sense and antisense gene-specific probes as described (Zhang et al. 2013). The primer pairs used for probe synthesis were listed in Supplemental Table S13.
