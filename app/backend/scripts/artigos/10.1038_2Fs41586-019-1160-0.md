# A vitamin-C-derived DNA modification catalysed by an algal TET homologue

Jian-Huang Xue<sup>1,18</sup>, Guo-Dong Chen<sup>1,18</sup>, Fuhua Hao<sup>2,18</sup>, Hui Chen<sup>1,3,18</sup>, Zhaoyuan Fang<sup>1</sup>, Fang-Fang Chen<sup>4</sup>, Bo Pang<sup>5</sup>, Qing-Lin Yang<sup>1</sup>, Xinben Wei<sup>6</sup>, Qiang-Qiang Fan<sup>1,7</sup>, Changpeng Xin<sup>8</sup>, Jiaohong Zhao<sup>9</sup>, Xuan Deng<sup>10</sup>, Bang-An Wang<sup>1</sup>, Xiao-Jie Zhang<sup>1</sup>, Yueying Chu<sup>2</sup>, Hui Tang<sup>1</sup>, Huiyong Yin<sup>6,7</sup>, Weimin Ma<sup>9</sup>, Luonan Chen<sup>1,7,11</sup>, Jianping Ding<sup>1,12</sup>, Elmar Weinhold<sup>13</sup>, Rahul M. Kohli<sup>14,15</sup>, Wen Liu<sup>5</sup>, Zheng-Jiang Zhu<sup>4</sup>, Kaiyao Huang<sup>10</sup>\*, Huiru Tang<sup>2,16\*</sup> & Guo-Liang Xu<sup>1,17\*</sup>

Methylation of cytosine to 5-methylcytosine (5mC) is a prevalent DNA modification found in many organisms. Sequential oxidation of 5mC by ten-eleven translocation (TET) dioxygenases results in a cascade of additional epigenetic marks and promotes demethylation of DNA in mammals<sup>1,2</sup>. However, the enzymatic activity and function of TET homologues in other eukaryotes remains largely unexplored. Here we show that the green alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii contains a 5mC-modifying enzyme (CMD1) that is a TET homologue and catalyses the conjugation of a glyceryl moiety to the methyl group of 5mC through a carbon-carbon bond, resulting in two stereoisomeric nucleobase products. The catalytic activity of CMD1 requires Fe(11) and the integrity of its binding motif His-X-Asp, which is conserved in Fe-dependent dioxygenases<sup>3</sup>. However, unlike previously described TET enzymes, which use 2-oxoglutarate as a co-substrate<sup>4</sup>, CMD1 uses L-ascorbic acid (vitamin C) as an essential co-substrate. Vitamin C donates the glyceryl moiety to 5mC with concurrent formation of glyoxylic acid and CO<sub>2</sub>. The vitamin-C-derived DNA modification is present in the genome of wild-type C. reinhardtii but at a substantially lower level in a CMD1 mutant strain. The fitness of CMD1 mutant cells during exposure to high light levels is reduced. LHCSR3, a gene that is critical for the protection of C. reinhardtii from photo-oxidative damage under high light conditions, is hypermethylated and downregulated in CMD1 mutant cells compared to wild-type cells, causing a reduced capacity for photoprotective non-photochemical quenching. Our study thus identifies a eukaryotic DNA base modification that is catalysed by a divergent TET homologue and unexpectedly derived from vitamin C, and describes its role as a potential epigenetic mark that may counteract DNA methylation in the regulation of photosynthesis.

Enzymes that target or modify DNA are involved in the epigenetic control of multiple biological processes. In *Arabidopsis*, 5mC can be targeted directly by specific glycosylases to generate abasic sites<sup>5</sup>. In mammals, 5mC can be oxidized by TET dioxygenases to 5-hydroxy-methylcytosine (5hmC), 5-formylcytosine (5fC) and 5-carboxylcytosine (5caC)<sup>6-9</sup>. Both of these 5mC-processing mechanisms promote DNA demethylation<sup>2,10,11</sup>. Although many other organisms—including the amoeba *Naegleria gruberi* and the fungus *Coprinopsis cinerea*—contain

 $5 \rm mC$  and its oxidative derivatives  $^{12-14}$  , other modes of  $5 \rm mC$  processing have not been reported  $^{15}$  .

TET homologues contain a conserved TET–JBP domain that is responsible for enzymatic activity and can be readily identified in a wide variety of organisms, including *C. reinhardtii*—a unicellular green alga, the lineage of which diverged from land plants more than one billion years ago<sup>16</sup>. The genome of *C. reinhardtii* contains eight TET homologues<sup>17</sup> (Extended Data Fig. 1). These *C. reinhardtii* (*Cr*) TET proteins share the conserved His-X-Asp motif for Fe(II) binding with the dioxygenases from *N. gruberi* and mammals<sup>12,18</sup>. However, the binding sites for 2-oxoglutarate (2-OG) appear to be absent in the *Cr*TET proteins, even though 2-OG is an essential co-substrate of all known dioxygenases in this family<sup>19</sup>.

To characterize the CrTET proteins, we performed a dioxygenase activity assay on recombinant proteins purified from Escherichia coli. After incubation of a 5mC-containing DNA substrate with wild-type CrTET1, we detected two unknown products (P1 and P2) in highperformance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis at retention times that differed from those of the anticipated nucleosides. These products were not detected with mutant protein controls that lacked His-X-Asp or other conserved motifs (Fig. 1a, Extended Data Fig. 2a-c). The accumulation of these two products correlated with the reduction of 5mC abundance (Extended Data Fig. 2d, e). Neither 5hmC nor unmodified cytosine could be converted under the same conditions (Extended Data Fig. 2f). Thin-layer chromatography analysis using <sup>14</sup>C to trace the methyl group in 5mC confirmed that two unidentified nucleotides had been generated and indicated that the methyl carbon had been retained in the products (Fig. 1b). These observations thus suggested that CrTET1 is a previously undescribed 5-methylcytosine-modifying enzyme, which we named CMD1. Of note, two minor peaks that appeared in the reaction products of wild-type, but not mutant, CMD1 (Fig. 1a, Extended Data Fig. 2f) represented 5hmC and 5caC (Extended Data Fig. 3a, b). Thus, CMD1 has an intrinsic capability similar to that of a conventional 5mC dioxygenase, at least in vitro.

We determined structures of P1 and P2 using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and density functional theory (DFT) calculation. Cross-peaks in the <sup>1</sup>H-<sup>1</sup>H correlation spectroscopy (COSY) and total correlation spectroscopy (TOCSY) spectra (Extended Data Fig. 4a-c) showed that P1 had deoxyribose, cytosine and trihydroxybutyl (THB) moieties, and that cytosine and THB were connected by an oxygen-free CH<sub>2</sub>. The <sup>1</sup>H-<sup>13</sup>C heteronuclear single-quantum correlation (HSQC) and heteronuclear multiple-bond correlation (HMBC) spectra (Extended Data Fig. 4d-f) further confirmed the structure of P1 as 5-(1-[2, 3, 4-trihydroxybutyl])-2'-deoxycytidine (Fig. 2c) with all proton and carbon signals unambiguously assigned (Extended Data Table 1). We used the J-coupling constants of protons attached to the chiral carbons (C8 and C9) to determine the absolute configurations of these two carbons according to the Karplus equation<sup>20</sup>. Comparison of these J-coupling constants from NMR experiments and DFT calculations (Extended Data Table 1) showed that P1 has an 8S and 9S configuration, and P2 is a stereoisomer of P1 (Extended Data Fig. 5) that differs only in the configuration at C8, with an 8R and 9S geometry (Fig. 2c). P1 and P2 were thus identified as 5-glyceryl-methylcytosines (5gmC).

To identify the origin of the glyceryl group that was transferred to 5mC, we expressed CMD1 in *E. coli* growing in M9 minimum medium containing <sup>13</sup>C-glucose as the only carbon source. We observed no increase in the m/z of P1 resulting from the use of a <sup>13</sup>C-CMD1 enzyme (Extended Data Fig. 6a), excluding the possibility that the glyceryl group arose from a component associated with CMD1. Although O<sub>2</sub> was indispensable for the reaction (Extended Data Fig. 6b), no oxygen atom from either O<sub>2</sub> or H<sub>2</sub>O was incorporated into the P1 nucleoside (Extended Data Fig. 6c). Because CMD1 requires both Fe<sup>2+</sup> and its binding motif His-X-Asp, but has distinct co-substrate requirements (Fig. 3a, b, Extended Data Fig. 6d-i), we propose that CMD1 uses a reaction mechanism similar to that of 5mC oxidation catalysed by Fe(II)- and 2-OG-dependent TET dioxygenases<sup>3,12</sup>. CMD1 uses vitamin C in the place of 2-OG, and O<sub>2</sub> for coordination with ferrous iron, yielding an Fe(IV)=O intermediate through oxidative decarboxylation of vitamin C (Extended Data Fig. 7c). This mechanism is consistent with the mixed stereochemistry observed at C8 in P1 and P2. To confirm this hypothesis, we used gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) to detect CO<sub>2</sub>. We detected 5gmC unambiguously in vivo at a level of about 10 per million cytosines, or 0.25% of 5mC, in the genomic DNA of wild-type C. reinhardtii (Fig. 4a). To verify that 5gmC is generated by CMD1, we generated a CMD1 mutant (hereafter cmd1) strain using the CRISPR-Cas9 system (Extended Data Fig. 8a-f). The cmd1 strain contained about 60% less 5gmC and twice as much 5mC as did wild-type cells (Fig. 4a). To investigate whether vitamin C is the glyceryl donor for 5gmC formation in vivo, we generated vitamin-C-deficient strains (*vtc2*) by knocking out the key vitamin-C synthesis gene  $VTC2^{21}$ (Extended Data Fig. 9a). In vtc2 mutant strains, vitamin C content was reduced to about 10% of the wild-type level (Extended Data Fig. 9b). Consequently, vtc2 mutant cells contained about 80% less 5gmC and twice as much 5mC as did wild-type cells (Fig. 4a). When wild-type C. reinhardtii was grown in the presence of the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5-azacytidine, the 5mC level was reduced by over 50%. As a consequence, the 5gmC level was decreased by 13% (Extended Data Fig. 10a). These data support the idea that 5gmC is derived from vitamin C and 5mC in vivo.

Despite the marked alteration in genomic 5gmC and 5mC levels, the *cmd1* strain did not exhibit noticeable morphological and growth phenotypes under low light. However, when cultured photo-autotrophically under high light, *cmd1* cells were more prone than wild-type cells to light-induced damage (Fig. 4b). This phenotype co-segregated with the *CMD1* mutation following mating and tetrad dissection (Extended Data Fig. 8g). Non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) is a photoprotective process that promotes fitness under high light and requires LHCSR3 (light-harvesting complex stress-related protein 3)<sup>22</sup>. Complete knockout of *LHCSR3 (npq4)* resulted in a severe impairment of NPQ (Fig. 4c). In *cmd1* cells, NPQ induction under high light was similarly compromised (Fig. 4c). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) revealed that more than 20 photosynthesis-related genes showed alterations in expression in *cmd1* cells, including a reduction in transcripts of *LHCSR3* (Extended Data Fig. 10c–e). In addition, both protein and mRNA expression of *LHCSR3* were lower in the *cmd1* mutant strain than in the wild-type after exposure to high light (Fig. 4d, e).

To investigate the link between altered gene expression and localized changes in 5mC, we performed whole-genome bisulfite sequencing on both wild-type and *cmd1* strains. In the wild-type strain, genes that were expressed at lower levels tended to be more methylated, and

genes that gained promoter methylation in the *cmd1* strain tended to be downregulated compared to the wild-type (Extended Data Fig. 10f–k). This indicates that DNA methylation and gene expression are inversely correlated in *C. reinhardtii*. Bisulfite sequencing confirmed that the analysed region 5' of *LHCSR3* in *cmd1* cells was hypermethylated (Fig. 4f, Extended Data Fig. 10l), which may have led to the impaired expression of *LHCSR3* in *cmd1* cells. Consistently, *vtc2* cells also showed increased methylation and reduced expression of *LHCSR3*, as well as compromised induction of NPQ (Extended Data Fig. 9c–e). On the other hand, *VTC2* expression and vitamin C content were higher in *cmd1* cells than in wild-type cells (Extended Data Fig. 9b, f). This implies both the existence of a compensatory mechanism and a functional connection between vitamin C content and NPQ capacity regulated by CMD1.

To confirm that CMD1 function is required for the regulation of photo-acclimation, we carried out complementation experiments. Constitutive expression of wild-type CMD1—but not the catalytically inactive mutant—in *cmd1* cells restored hypomethylation and expression of *LHCSR3*, together with NPQ-mediated photoprotection (Fig. 4c, f, Extended Data Fig. 11a–d). In addition, constitutive expression of LHCSR3 in *cmd1* cells rescued their phenotypic and molecular defects (Fig. 4c, Extended Data Fig. 11a, c, d). These data link the function of CMD1 with the regulation of photosynthesis through the control of *LHCSR3* expression.

The increase in 5mC in *cmd1* cells, both globally and at the 5' region of LHCSR3 (Fig. 4a, f), suggested that CMD1 may counteract cytosine methylation in suppressing transcription, reminiscent of the role of TET dioxygenases in antagonizing DNA methyltransferases in mammals<sup>23</sup>. However, the biological relevance of cytosine methylation in C. reinhardtii has been under-investigated and, unlike in other organisms, no correlation between 5mC deposition and gene expression has been established<sup>24</sup>. To test the effects of 5mC and 5gmC on gene expression, we introduced these two modifications into two luciferase reporter plasmids in vitro before transformation into C. reinhardtii. Whereas 5mC alone strongly and stably repressed transcription of the reporter, its conversion to 5gmC substantially alleviated this repression in a time-dependent manner (Extended Data Fig. 11e); this effect was correlated with the de-modification of 5gmC to cytosine (by 13.4% at 48 h), as revealed by 5gmC mapping experiments (Extended Data Fig. 11f-h). These observations indicate that 5gmC can promote demethylation and thus increase gene expression. Of note, the relevance of 5gmC in the control of target genes in vivo was also supported by chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) data showing that CMD1 was enriched at the 5' region of LHCSR3 (Extended Data Fig. 11i).

We have shown that 5mC in the C. reinhardtii genome can be further modified by the addition of a glyceryl group from vitamin C to form 5gmC, a nucleobase generated by the TET homologue CMD1. Vitamin C is widely believed to function as an antioxidant and promotes the recycling of Fe<sup>2+</sup> in numerous contexts, including the epigenetic reprogramming of cell fates by TET enzymes and histone demethylases<sup>25,26</sup>. Our observation that vitamin C acts as an essential co-substrate raises the possibility that vitamin C might have a more direct role in epigenetic regulation. Functionally, our data implicate CMD1-catalysed glycerylation of 5mC in the control of the transcriptional competence of LHCSR3, a gene that is critical for the acclimation of algal cells to excess light<sup>22,27</sup>. This observation adds an additional layer of complexity to the regulation of photoprotection by LHCSR3, which is induced by a blue-light-sensing photoreceptor<sup>28</sup>. Our data suggest that suppression of transcription by 5mC might be conserved in C. reinhardtii. Although 5gmC itself appears to negatively affect transcription, it can promote demethylation and thus de-repression over time. Among other possibilities, the demethylation process triggered by 5gmC could take place either through passive dilution owing to inhibition of maintenance methylation or through base excision repair by a specific glycosylase capable of excising 5gmC. The C. reinhardtii genome contains homologues of TDG and AlkD glycosylases, which can excise 5caC and bulky base modifications, respectively<sup>6,29</sup>. Further understanding of the roles

of and interactions between the two cytosine modifications in the *C. reinhardtii* genome requires the generation and analysis of mutants that completely lack 5gmC. In principle, 5gmC can function as an independent epigenetic mark, similar to 5mC and  $N^6$ -methyladenine marks in various organisms<sup>30</sup>. Finally, given its selectivity, modification of 5gmC by CMD1 might be useful for DNA technologies such as the genome-wide mapping of 5mC.

### **METHODS**

**Data reporting.** No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. The experiments were not randomized and the investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.

**CMD1 recombinant protein expression and purification.** The open reading frame (ORF) of *CMD1* (Cre12.g553400.t2.1, Phytozome) was cloned into modified pET28a (pPEI-His-SUMO, supplied by Y. Xu)<sup>18</sup> and the construct was transformed into *E. coli* strain BL21 (DE3). The *CMD1* mutants were constructed in the same vector. The bacterial cells were grown to an absorbance of 0.8 at OD<sub>600</sub> and induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 16°C for 16 h. Tagged CMD1 protein was bound to Ni-NTA beads (Qiagen) and cleaved from the His-SUMO tag by overnight incubation with His-tagged Ulp1 protease at 4°C. The collected CMD1 protein was further purified using a Resource Q anion exchange column (GE Healthcare) with a linear gradient of buffer A (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5, 1 M NaCl) from 100/0 to 50/50, and a Superdex 200 10/300 GL gel filtration column (GE Healthcare) in buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.0, 100 mM NaCl). The protein was concentrated to 10 µg/µl using Ultracel-10K centrifugal filters (Millipore).

**Preparation of DNA substrates for CMD1 reaction in vitro.** A 1.1-kb 5mCcontaining DNA fragment (5mC–DNA) was prepared by PCR amplification from a randomly selected portion of *C. reinhardtii* genomic DNA using 5-methyl-dCTP. We also used 5-hydroxymethyl-dCTP and unmodified dCTP in PCR to prepare 5hmC-DNA and C-DNA, to test the substrate specificity of CMD1. The forward primer used was 5'-biotin- AAGGGTTGGATTGTAGGTAGTTTAGAAAT-3' and the reverse primer was 5'- TGAGGGTGGTAAATTAG-3'.

**Dioxygenase assay.** Typically, 0.5  $\mu$ g of biotinylated 5mC–DNA was incubated with 4  $\mu$ g CMD1 or human (h) TET2 enzyme (at about 1:2 molar ratio of enzyme to 5mC) in a total volume of 100  $\mu$ l at 37 °C for 1 h in the presence of 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0 for CMD1, pH 8.2 for hTET2), 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM L-ascorbic acid, 1 mM 2-OG, 0.1 mM Fe(NH<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>(SO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>, 1 mM DTT and 1 mM ATP as previously described<sup>6</sup>. For the <sup>13</sup>C-tracing experiment, <sup>13</sup>C-labelled L-ascorbic acid (Omicron Biochemicals) was used in the reaction. After treatment with proteinase K (Lifefeng, RE111-03), the DNA was purified using streptavidin sepharose beads (GE Healthcare) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

HPLC analysis of nucleoside hydrolysates of CMD1-modified DNA. HPLC analysis was performed as previously described<sup>6</sup>. In brief, purified biotinylated DNA was digested by nuclease P1 (Sigma) in the presence of 0.2 mM ZnSO<sub>4</sub> and 20 mM NaAc (pH 5.3) at 55 °C for at least 1 h and then was dephosphorylated with calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase (CIAP, Takara) at 37 °C for an additional 1 h. The samples were centrifuged and the supernatants were analysed using an Agilent 1260 HPLC with a Welch AQ-C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 µm) at 15 °C. The mobile phase was 10 mM KH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub> (pH 3.95), running at 0.6 ml/min or 20 mM NH<sub>4</sub>Ac (pH 5.21), running at 1 ml/min, and the detector was set to 280 nm.

Labelling of DNA substrate at 5mC with <sup>14</sup>C isotope or D (deuterium or <sup>2</sup>H). Plasmid DNA (5 µg) was incubated with 20 units of M.SssI CpG methyltransferase (Zymo Research) and 8 µl S-[methyl-<sup>14</sup>C]-adenosyl-L-methionine (<sup>14</sup>C-SAM, 1.48-2.22 GBq/mmol, PerkinElmer) or S-[methyl-D<sub>3</sub>]-adenosyl-L-methionine ([methyl-D<sub>3</sub>]-SAM, Zzstandard) in a total volume of 100 µl at 30 °C overnight. The DNA was purified using Qiaquick Nucleotide Removal Kit (Qiagen) and used for CMD1 reaction.

Analysis of 5mC derivatives using thin-layer chromatography. In brief, after incubation of <sup>14</sup>C-5mC–DNA with CMD1, the samples were treated with proteinase K and purified by phenol-chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation before being dissolved in 8  $\mu$ l water. The DNA was digested using nuclease P1 and then 0.5  $\mu$ l of the digestion product was spotted onto a PEI-cellulose thin-layer chromatography plate (Merck). The plate was developed in isopropanol:H-Cl:H<sub>2</sub>O (70:15:15) and then analysed by phosphorimager scanning with FujiFilm Fluorescent Image Analyzer FLA-3000.

LC-MS analysis. To determine the molecular mass of the new products of 5mC generated in the CMD1-catalysed reaction, we collected nucleoside fractions of interest from HPLC and subjected them to mass spectrometry analysis. Ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) was performed using a Q Exactive (Thermo Scientific) mass spectrometer in positive-ion mode with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (1.8  $\mu$ m, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Waters) column. Buffers A (water containing 0.05% CH<sub>3</sub>COOH) and B (acetonitrile, ACN) were used as mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.3 ml/min. The gradient began with a condition of 100% A, followed by a linear gradient of 95% A at 2 min, 50% A at 4 min, which was held for 1 min, followed by 0% A at 5.1 min, then re-equilibrated to the starting condition at 8 min, holding for 1 min.

For glyoxylic acid analysis, the reaction mixture was filtered after the reaction to remove proteins and precipitates. Derivation was carried out using 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNP) as previously described<sup>31</sup> before LC–MS analysis. The LC–MS program was identical to that used for nucleoside analysis.

For quantitative determination of the content of 5gmC nucleosides in genomic DNA, multiple reaction monitoring (MRM)-based liquid chromatographytandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was used. The LC-MS/MS analyses were performed using a UPLC system (1290 series, Agilent Technologies) coupled to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent 6495 QQQ, Agilent Technologies). An ACQUITY UPLC BEH amide column (1.7  $\mu$ m; 2.1 mm  $\times$  100 mm, Waters) was used for the liquid chromatography separation. The mobile phases (A, 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide in 100% water; B, 100% acetonitrile) were used for compound separation. The linear gradient elutes from 85 to 40% B (0-2 min), 40% B (2-4 min), 40 to 85% B (4-4.1 min), then stays at 85% B until 7 min for re-equilibrium. The flow rate was set to 0.6 ml/min. Optimized MRM transition parameters for each of the nucleosides 5gmC, 5mC and C, G were obtained using pure compound standards. 5gmC: 332.1/216.1 (quantifier transition, CE 24); 332.1/150.0 (qualifier transition, CE 44); 5mC: 242.1/126.1 (quantifier transition, CE 8); 242.1/54.3 (qualifier transition, CE 60); C: 228.1/112.1 (quantifier transition, CE 8); 228.1/41.3 (qualifier transition, CE 64); G: 268.1/152.1 (quantifier transition, CE 21); 268.1/135 (quantifier transition, CE 45). All compounds were measured on positive ESI mode. Then the retention time for each compound was individually determined by measuring the corresponding MRM transitions on the BEH amide column. For 5gmC: 2.04 min; 5mC: 1.26 min; C: 1.32 min; G: 1.37 min. The amount of each nucleoside was calculated according to the peak areas of quantifier MRM transitions: 5gmC (332.1/216.1), 5mC (242.1/126.1), C (228.1/112.1) and G (268.1/152.1) by interpolation from the standard curves.

GC–MS analysis. For the analysis of CO<sub>2</sub>, the CMD1-catalysed reaction was performed in sealed vials under N<sub>2</sub> atmosphere in a glove box, with pure O<sub>2</sub> air blown manually. The reaction products within the vials were directly subjected to GC–MS analysis using an Agilent 7890A GC, equipped with an Agilent J&W GC 113-3133 column (30 m × 320  $\mu$ m × 3  $\mu$ m) and a mass spectrometer 5975C as the detector. Helium was used as the carrier gas, at a flow rate of 1.5 ml/min. Oven temperature was initially 35 °C for 6 min, then gradually increased to 320 °C at 11 min.

**Determination of cellular content of vitamin C.** The method for vitamin C content quantification was based on a published protocol<sup>32</sup>, but with some modifications. The algal cells were cultured in TAP medium to mid-exponential phase. Cells  $(1 \times 10^7)$  were collected in a 1.5-ml centrifuge tube and washed with 1 ml water. The cell pellet was frozen in liquid nitrogen. Vitamin C was extracted by adding 300 µl extraction buffer (2 mM EDTA, 10 mM DTT) followed by vigorous shaking. Glass beads (100 µl, Sigma) were added and vortexed using a bead-beater at maximum speed for 30 s. The samples were centrifuged at 19,000g at 4°C for 30 min. The supernatant was collected and filtered into chromatographic vials using 4-mm hydrophilic PTFE syringe filters with a pore size of 0.22 µm (Microlab).

Vitamin C was quantified using LC–MS. It was separated chromatographically using a Q Exactive LC–MS system (Thermo Scientific) with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide Column (130Å, 1.7  $\mu$ m, 2.1 mm  $\times$  50 mm, Waters) with negative ion mode. The tray temperature of the autosampler was set at 4 °C and the column oven temperature at 30 °C. For the elution of vitamin C, the flow rate was set at 0.3 ml/min, and the mobile phase used was A = 25 mM NH<sub>4</sub>Ac + 25 mM NH<sub>3</sub>·H<sub>2</sub>O, B = ACN. The linear gradient eluted from 95% B for 2 min, 95% B to 40% B (2–6 min), then stayed at 40% B until 9 min for re-equilibrium. The amount of vitamin C was calculated according to the calibration curve. The cellular vitamin C concentration was calculated using the following formula (the average cell volume for *C. reinhardtii* is about 200 fl):

Cellular vitamin C concentration (mM)

$$= \frac{\text{vitamin C concentration of the extract }(\mu M) \times \text{extract volume}(\mu I)}{\text{cell volume}(fl) \times \text{amount of cells} \times 10^{-9}}$$

Structural determination of P nucleosides by NMR. Ten to forty micrograms of purified P1 and P2 nucleosides were dissolved in 50  $\mu$ l phosphate buffer (0.1 M in D2O, pD 7.4)^{33} and their NMR data were acquired on Bruker 600 MHz and 850 MHz spectrometers, both equipped with 5-mm cryogenic TCI probes. One-dimensional  $^1H$  NMR spectra and a set of 2D NMR spectra were recorded and processed as previously reported^{34} including  $^1H_{-}^{-1}H$  COSY,  $^1H_{-}^{-1}H$  TOCSY,  $^1H$  JRES (J-resolved spectroscopy),  $^1H_{-}^{-13}C$  HSQC and  $^1H_{-}^{-13}C$  HMBC 2D spectra. The  $^1H$  and  $^{13}C$  chemical shifts were referenced to methyl signals of TSP ( $\delta_{\rm H}$  0.000,  $\delta_{\rm C}$  0.00). For the stronger proton coupling systems and more complex split peaks, the accurate chemical shifts and coupling constants were simulated with NMR-Sim5.4^{35}.

Three-bond <sup>1</sup>H-to-<sup>1</sup>H J-coupling constants ( ${}^{3}J_{\text{H-H}}$ ) were calculated for those protons attached to the chiral carbons (C8 and C9) and their adjacent carbons (C7 and C10). For both nucleosides P1 and P2, such constants were calculated for all four possible configurations (that is, 8*R*, 9*R*; 8*S*, 9*S*; 8*R*, 9*S*; 8*S*, 9*R*) using the DFT approach after molecular geometries were fully optimized at the wb97x-d/6-311G (d,p) level. All calculations were carried out using the Gaussian 09

software package with the Fermi contact, diamagnetic spin-orbit, paramagnetic spin-orbit and spin-dipole terms being taken into consideration according to the Ramsey theory<sup>36</sup>.

*C. reinhardtii* strains and culture conditions. Wild-type strains (CC124 and CC125) were obtained from the *C. reinhardtii* Resource Center. The *npq4* mutant strain<sup>22</sup> was a kind gift from W. Yang. All strains were cultured mixotrophically in TAP medium on a rotary shaker at 25 °C and maintained at a light intensity of 20 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>. In the experiment, cells were transferred to Sueoka's high salt medium (HSM)<sup>37</sup> at 1 × 10<sup>5</sup> ml<sup>-1</sup> and exposed to light intensity as described in the main text and figure legends. For mRNA quantification, protein immunoblotting and bisulfite sequencing analysis, low light refers to ~20 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> and high light refers to ~300 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>. For the phenotype characterization, the cells were grown at ~300 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> to 1 × 10<sup>6</sup> ml<sup>-1</sup> and treated at low light (~50 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) or high light (~750 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) for at least 16 h.

For 5-aza-2'-deoxycytidine (5-aza, Sigma) treatment, CC125 cells at a cell density of  $1.2\times10^4$  ml $^{-1}$  were cultured in TAP medium in the presence of 400  $\mu M$  5-aza. On day 2 the medium was changed to TAP medium with fresh 400  $\mu M$  5-aza, and the cells were collected on day 4 for further analysis.

Gene editing in *C. reinhardtii* based on CRISPR–Cas9-mediated co-selection. The principle and flow chart of the gene-editing procedure are summarized in Extended Data Fig. 8a, b. In brief, a pPEI-His-SUMO-SpCas9 plasmid was transformed into *E. coli* strain Transetta (DE3) (TransGen Biotech). *Sp*Cas9 protein was bound to Ni-NTA beads and collected from the resin in elution buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, and 10% glycerol). The eluted sample was next loaded into a 5-ml HiTrap SP HP Sepharose column (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) and eluted with buffer A (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 1 mM DTT and 10% glycerol) with a linear gradient of 100 mM to 1 M KCl. The fractions containing *Sp*Cas9 were mixed and concentrated to 500  $\mu$ l with a centrifugal filter (30 kDa, Millipore) and further purification was performed by gel filtration on a Superdex 200 16/300 column (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) in GF buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT and 10% glycerol). The eluted *Sp*Cas9 sample was then filtered through a 0.2- $\mu$ m Whatman filter to remove possible bacterial contamination.

The single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) in *C. reinhardtii* were designed using CRISPR RGEN Tools (http://www.rgenome.net/cas-designer/). The sgRNA was prepared in vitro using the MEGAshortscript T7 kit (Ambion). The sgRNA sequences are: *MAA7*: CAUAGCGACCAUUUGCGUCC; *CMD1*: GGAACAUCU CGUCGCAUGCU; *VTC2*: UUUCCCGGCUACUGGCGUUU. Genotyping primers are as follows: *MAA7*-F: GCGTAATTCGGCTACTTTCAC; *MAA7*-R: TCTCAGCAAACACCCGTCATT; *CMD1*-primer1-F: TGCTATG GGCGTCTCGCTCAC; *CMD1*-primer2-F: CGTTTAACGACTGGAAGGCTGC; *CMD1*-primer1/2-R: TCGGCATGGATAGATGGTCAGAC; *CMD1*primer3-F: GCAAAATGAGTGTCGCCCTA; *CMD1*-primer3-R: TAGAAAACCACCTCCTGCCC; *VTC2*-F: GGAGCTTTTCGTCGATCAACA; *VTC2*-R: CGTCTGTCACTGCAACTACG.

For the transformation experiment, C. reinhardtii cells (CC125, mt+) were grown to a cell density of  $2 \times 10^6$  cells ml<sup>-1</sup> in TAP medium. For electroporation,  $2 \times 10^7$  cells were suspended in 1 ml Max Efficiency Transformation Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by suspension in the same reagent supplemented with 60 mM sorbitol. Purified SpCas9 (100 µg, 0.53 nmol) in storage buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, and 10% glycerol) was preincubated with the sgRNA for MAA7 and the sgRNA for the CMD1 gene (0.8 nmol each) at a 1:1.5:1.5 molar ratio at 37 °C for 15 min to assemble ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes. For co-transformation of C. reinhardtii, 250 µl of cell suspension  $(5 \times 10^{6} \text{ cells})$  was mixed with the preincubated RNP complexes. Cells were electroporated in a 4 mm cuvette (600 V, 50 µF, infinite resistance) using Gene Pulser Xcell (Bio-Rad) as described<sup>38</sup>. Immediately after electroporation, 600 µl TAP with 60 mM sorbitol was added. Cells were recovered overnight in 10 ml TAP with 60 mM sorbitol shaken at 110 r.p.m. under continuous low light and then plated onto TAP medium supplemented with 25  $\mu$ M 5-fluoroindole (5-FI) and 20% starch. The plates were incubated under 30 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>. The 5-FI resistance colonies appeared after 5-7 days and were picked up for genotype characterization. Backcross and random spore analysis. The selected cmd1 mutant clone was backcrossed with wild-type CC124 (mt-) to segregate the MAA7 mutation from other potential off-target genetic alterations. For gametogenesis, 20 ml of each type of vegetative cells was cultured to a concentration of  $2 \times 10^6$  ml<sup>-1</sup>. The cells were resuspended and cultured in M1 medium depleted of nitrogen under a light intensity of 120  $\mu$ mol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> for 18 h. Gametes were mixed in the dark for 2 h then 0.2 ml of the mixed cultures were spread onto a 4% agar TAP plate and exposed to light for 1 d and then stored at darkness for 5 d for maturation of zygotes. All the cells were collected and treated with 2% SDS for 2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were washed with TAP medium at least six times before being plated onto a TAP plate. After zygote germination, the cells were

diluted and plated onto a TAP plate again to isolate single clones for genotyping. Multiple independent *cmd1* clones obtained from two consecutive crosses were used for phenotype characterization.

For the random spore analysis, gametes on 4% agar plates were removed with a razor blade (the zygotes stick to the agar plate) and the remaining gametes were killed with chloroform. About 30 zygotes on a small piece of agar were transferred to the germination plate (1% TAP agar plate) and incubated under the light for 20 h. Then 0.1 ml TAP medium was added to the agar to release the daughter cells from the zygotes, and then the medium was spread onto the whole plate. After 5–8 days, meiotic products grew into visible colonies that were picked for subsequent analysis. The colonies were grown in 1 ml TAP medium in 24-well plates at low light for 2 days, and then diluted to  $OD_{620} = 0.1$  with TAP medium. Cells (3 µl) were spotted onto 1.5% agar plates and the plates were incubated in low light (20 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) or high light (1,000 µmol photons m<sup>-2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) for 66 h.

Gene complementation in *C. reinhardtii*. For the complemented expression of wild-type or mutant CMD1 in the *cmd1* strain, the *Hsp70A/Rbcs2* (*HSRB*) fusion promoter and *PsaD* terminator were used. An HA-tag coding sequence was fused at the C terminus-coding sequence of a cloned *CMD1* genomic fragment. The paromomycin resistance gene *AphVIII* was fused downstream as a selection marker. For the expression of *LHCSR3*, the *Hsp70A/Rbcs2* fusion promoter and *PsaD* terminator were added to the full-length genomic *LHCSR3.1* gene. In this construct, the *AphVIII* marker driven by *Hsp70A/Rbcs2* fusion promoter was included. The constructs were introduced into *cmd1* cells by electroporation using BTX Gemini SC2 Electroporation System in a 4-mm cuvette (600 V, 50 µE, infinite resistance). The transformants were screened for their resistance to 10 µg ml<sup>-1</sup> paromomycin and identified by western blot analysis with anti-HA (Cell Signaling Technology) and anti-LHCSR3 (Agrisera) antibodies.

**RNA preparation and gene-specific mRNA quantification.** Total RNA was extracted from *C. reinhardtii* using Trizol (Thermo) according to the instruction manual. To measure gene expression, quantitative RT–PCR was performed using CFXP6 Real-Time PCR with SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Tli RNaseH Plus, Takara). A gene encoding G-protein-subunit-like protein (GBLP) was used as the endogenous control.

The primers used were: *LHCSR3.1*-qRT-F (5'-CACAACACCTTGATGCGAGA TG-3'), *LHCSR3.1*-qRT-R (5'-CCGTGTCTTGTCAGTCCCTG-3'), *LHCSR3.2*qRT-F (5'-TGTGAGGCACTCTGGTGAAG-3'), *LHCSR3.2*-qRT-R (5'-CGCC TGTTGTCACCATCTTA-3'), *VTC2*-qRT-F (5' TGCTAAAGCTGCTGCC GACATTG-3'), *VTC2*-qRT-R (5' CACTGAGACACGTCGTACCTGAAC-3'), *GBLP*-qRT-F (5 '-CAAGTACACCATTGGCGAGC-3') and *GBLP*-qRT-R (5'-CTTGCAGTTGGTCAGGTTCC-3').

Western blot analysis. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 12,000g for 30 s, and resuspended in 60 µl SBA buffer (100 mM DTT, 100 mM Na<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub>) with 40 µl SBB buffer (30% sucrose, 5% SDS). The samples were vortexed for 20 min at room temperature and then subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles. After centrifugation, the supernatants were loaded onto a 10–12.5% SDS–PAGE gel and the proteins were blotted onto a nitrocellulose membrane. Membranes were blocked for 0.5 h with 5% milk in TBST and then incubated with anti-LHCSR3 polyclonal antibody (Agrisera) diluted 1:10,000 in TBST or anti-HA monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology), diluted 1:1,000 or anti- $\alpha$ -Tubulin monoclonal antibody (Sigma) diluted 1:1,000 for 1 h and then rinsed 3 times for 5 min before incubation with peroxidase-conjugated affinipure goat anti-mouse IgG (Jackson), both diluted 1:10,000 for 1 h. The blots were obtained using a CCD imager (Thermo).

Large-scale DNA preparation from *C. reinhardtii*. Total DNA was isolated using CTAB method as described<sup>39</sup> and was dissolved in nuclease-free water for further analysis.

Southern blotting of genomic DNA. Total DNA ( $10 \mu g$ ) was digested using SalI and NheI restriction enzymes and samples were separated by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel. After treating the gel in 0.2 N HCl for 10 min, denaturation buffer (1.5 M NaCl, 0.5 M NaOH) for 30 min, and neutralization solution (0.5 M Tris-HCl, 3 M NaCl, pH 6.8) for 30 min, the DNA in the gel was blotted onto nylon membrane by capillary transfer in  $20 \times$  SSC buffer. The Southern blotting probe fragment was prepared by PCR amplification from the *C. reinhardtii* genomic DNA using primers: *CMD1*-Southern-F (5'-GGCCAAACAACCGAGTCTTG-3') and *CMD1*-Southern-R (5'-CACAGCAACAACACCTCA-3'). Probe labelling and the detection of hybridization signal were performed using the DIG High Prime Labelling and Detection Starter Kit II (Roche) according to the instruction manual.

**Bisulfite sequencing (BS-seq) and TET bisulfite sequencing (TET BS-seq).** For bisulfite sequencing, genomic DNA was extracted and treated with the EZ DNA Methylation-Direct Kit (Zymo Research). The bisulfite-treated DNA was subjected to PCR amplification using Taq HS polymerase (TAKARA). The bisulfite primers were *LHCSR3*-BSF (5'-TGGGTTGGTTGATATAGTTTGATA-3'), and *LHCSR3*-BSR (5ʹ-AATCTCRCTAACTCCCCTATCT-3ʹ); *HSRB*-BSF (5ʹ-TGAAGT TATAGGATTGATTTGG-3ʹ), and *HSRB*-BSR (5ʹ-TACAAATACTCAAATACCC CAT-3ʹ). PCR products were then purified with a Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen) and cloned into a pClone007 Simple Vector (Tsingke). Individual clones were sequenced by standard Sanger sequencing. Data were analysed using the online tool QUMA [\(http://quma.cdb.riken.jp/](http://quma.cdb.riken.jp/)).

For TET BS-seq, genomic DNA was subjected to oxidation by recombinant human TET2CD before bisulfite treatment. In brief, 200 ng DNA was incubated with 10 µg hTET2CD in a total volume of 20 µl at 37 °C for 3 h in the presence of 50 mM HEPES (pH 8.2), 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM l-ascorbic acid, 1 mM 2-OG, 0.1 mM Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2, 1 mM DTT and 1 mM ATP. After that, TET-treated DNA was directly used for BS-seq as described above.

**Chlorophyll fluorescence measurements.** Chlorophyll fluorescence of *C. reinhardtii* cells was measured using a Dual-PAM-100 (Walz) with an emitter-detector unit ED-101US/MD. Wild-type and *cmd1* strains were cultured under 180 μmol photons m–2 s–1. Cells were then exposed to actinic light of 600 μmol photons m–2 s–1 to induce NPQ. Total NPQ was calculated as (*F*m – *F*m′)/*F*m′, in which *F*m is the maximum fluorescence resulting from the measuring during a brief, saturating flash of light, and *F*m′ is the maximum fluorescence measured in the light-adapted state. The photosynthetic electron transport rate was calculated as electron transport rate = (*F*m′ – *F*s)/*F*m′ × photon flux density (μmol photons m–2 s–1), in which *F*s is the steady-state fluorescence level.

**Luciferase assay.** For the construction of the luciferase reporter, we fused the *Hsp70A/Rbcs2* fusion promoter and *PsaD* terminator with the *renilla* coding sequence. Independently, the promoter region of *LHCSR3.1* (chromosome\_8: 1945381–1947449) was cloned to generate another reporter plasmid. The plasmids were treated with M.SssI methyltransferase (Zymo Research) to generate 5mC-plasmids. 5mC-plasmids were further treated with CMD1 to generate 5gmC-plasmids. For luciferase assay, these plasmids were linearized and introduced into wild-type CC125 cells by electroporation with BTX Gemini SC2 Electroporator in a 4-mm cuvette (600 V, 50 µF, infinite resistance). The cells were collected at different times and subjected to luciferase activity measurement with Renilla Luciferase Assay System (Promega). The luciferase activity was normalized to the corresponding chlorophyll fluorescence and then compared to the value of the mock control, which was set to 1. Each experiment was repeated three times. **Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay.** The ChIP assay was performed as described[40.](#page-7-9) An anti-HA monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) was used to pull down CMD1–HA, with mouse IgG used as a negative control. The pulldown complex was eluted and subjected to quantitative RT–PCR. Signals for individual genomic regions from anti-HA pulldown samples were normalized against IgG control samples and then to the corresponding signals of *cmd1* cells lacking CMD1–HA, which was set to 1. Primers used were as follows: F1: 5ʹ-TGTGT TTCCGACTTTGCCAG-3ʹ, R1: 5ʹ-GACACGACATCACACGACAG-3ʹ; F2: 5ʹ-CACTCCTCCCTCTCCTTGC-3ʹ, R2: 5ʹ-GAAGAAGAGGCGGTGGAGAG-3ʹ; F3: 5ʹ-GGTTGCAACACCCTAACGTT-3ʹ, R3: 5ʹ-CCCATGAAACCAAGCACC AA-3ʹ; F4: 5ʹ-CATACGGGGTCCCTACACTC-3ʹ, R4: 5ʹ-TGTCCAGTGAGAAGT AGCCG-3ʹ.

**Statistical analysis.** No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size, the experiments were not randomized and the investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. All values are expressed as mean ± s.e.m. and calculated from the average of at least two independent biological replicates. The statistical significance of differences was estimated by Student's *t*-tests, using GraphPad software. *P* < 0.05 was considered significant. All other statistical tests are described in the figure legends and Methods.

**Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing.** Genomic DNA was isolated as described above. For library preparation, genomic DNA spiked with unmethylated lambda DNA (~2% of the genomic DNA) was sheared by Covaris S220 to generate fragments (~300 bp in length). Then about 500 ng of sheared DNA was converted by bisulfite and purified with EZ DNA Methylation-Direct Kit (Zymo Research). DNA libraries were prepared using TruSeq DNA Methylation Kit (Illumina) and sequenced on Illunima NextSeq-500 platform with pair-end 150 bp mode. After quality control (FastQC Version 0.11.5) and adaptor trimming (Trimmomatic[41](#page-7-10) V0.36), the clean reads were aligned to the *C. reinhardtii* genome (V5.5, Phytozome) using BSMAP version v2.[942.](#page-7-11)

Raw methylation estimates were called for cytosines covered by at least twenty reads. Commonly detected cytosine sites were averaged across replicates to increase robustness. To determine the methylation status of genes, methylation ratios of cytosines within the promoter region (2 kb upstream of transcription start site (TSS)) were collected and compared between wild-type and *CMD1* mutant samples using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and corrected for false discovery rate (FDR) by the Benjamini–Hochberg method[43](#page-7-12). Fold changes, absolute differences and percentages of relative differences were also calculated. To control for false positives more stringently, we identified differentially methylated genes as those with FDR values below 0.001 combined with a 20% relative change and an absolute methylation change of 0.04 in the methylation level. Functional enrichment was analysed using Gene Ontology annotation and Fisher's exact test.

**RNA-seq analysis.** Total RNA was extracted from *C. reinhardtii* using Trizol (Thermo). RNA libraries were prepared using TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina) and sequenced using Illumina Hiseq Xten platform with pairend 150-bp mode. After quality control (FastQC Version 0.11.5) and adaptor trimming (Trimmomatic[41](#page-7-10) V0.36), the clean reads were aligned to the *C. reinhardtii* genome (V5.5, Phytozome) using TopHat2 (version v2.1.1[\)44](#page-7-13) with default settings. The transcription levels of annotated genes (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) were quantified and normalized using Cufflinks (v2.0.0)[45](#page-7-14) with default parameters. Differential analysis was performed using the quasi-likelihood *F*-test implemented in edgeR (v3.16.5[\)46](#page-7-15) and genes with *P* < 0.005 and at least 1.5-fold changes were considered to be differentially expressed.

Extended Data Fig. 7 | Characterization of reaction mechanism of CMD1. a, Mass spectrometry analysis of P1 nucleoside from reactions using various <sup>13</sup>C-labelled vitamin-C co-substrates. The use of [<sup>13</sup>C<sub>6</sub>]-VC led to a 3-D increase in the mass of P1, whereas no mass change was detected when [1-13C]-VC or [3-13C]-VC was used. This indicated that the glyceryl moiety was from C4-C6 of vitamin C. Data are representative of two independent experiments. b, Mass determination of the most abundant fragment ions generated by CID of P1. Arch arrows denote the relationship of ions featuring the loss of <sup>13</sup>C carbons (top three panels) and loss of <sup>12</sup>C carbons (bottom panel). The masses corresponding to the fragments containing <sup>13</sup>C atoms are indicated in red. These data indicate that <sup>13</sup>C6 of vitamin C ends up in the distal carbon of the side chain of P1 (C10 in Fig. 2c), and that <sup>13</sup>C from [5-<sup>13</sup>C]-VC ends up in C9. Data are representative of two independent experiments. c, Proposed mechanism of CMD1 catalysis. The catalysis starts with the coordination of Fe(11) to the conserved 2-His-1-carboxylate triad of the enzyme, leaving three sites on the metal that are occupied by water molecules (A). Deprotonated vitamin C displaces two bound water molecules and coordinates to Fe(II) with its C-1 carbonyl group and C-2 alkoxide (B). Hydrolysis of the bound vitamin C yields the ring-opened intermediate (C), which then tautomerizes to the  $\alpha$ -keto form (D). The remaining bound water molecule leaves when 5mC binds to the active site (E). The binding of O2 to the iron centre generates an Fe(III)-superoxo intermediate (F). The nucleophilic attack of the distal oxygen onto C-2 of 2-keto-L-gulonate yields an Fe(IV)-peroxo species (G). This species initiates oxidative decarboxylation of vitamin C to produce an Fe(Iv)-oxo species, which is coordinated with the C-1 carboxylate of

the resulting L-xylonic acid (H). The Fe(IV)-oxo species abstracts a hydrogen atom from 5mC to generate Fe(III)-hydroxide species and a 5mC radical (I). The C-2 hydroxyl group of the coordinated L-xylonic acid binds to the Fe(III) centre with loss of a bound water molecule (J). Homolysis of the C2-C3 bond of the coordinated L-xylonic acid and non-stereoselective attack of the 5mC radical lead to the formation of the product nucleobases P1 and P2 and Fe(II)-bound glyoxylic acid (K). Eventually, glyoxylate dissociates from the iron centre to complete the catalytic cycle. The side reaction that generates 5hmC can be explained by this reaction mechanism; the 5mC radical combines with a hydroxide group linked to Fe(III) (intermediate I), in a manner similar to reactions catalysed by TET dioxygenases. Notably, however, the generation of trace 5hmC is not dependent on 2-OG (Fig. 3a, Extended Data Fig. 6d), confirming that a different mechanism is involved. d, GC-MS analysis of the co-product CO2 from CMD1-catalysed reactions using <sup>13</sup>C-labelled vitamin C. The reactions were carried out in airtight vials and directly subjected to GC-MS analysis. The carbon atom of  ${\rm \check{C}O_2}$  is shown to come from the C1 of vitamin C. Data are representative of two independent experiments. e, Mass spectrometry analysis of the co-product glyoxylic acid upon DNP derivatization. As C4-C6 and C1 of vitamin C were transferred into base P and CO<sub>2</sub>, respectively, the remaining two carbons of vitamin C were converted into glyoxylic acid. This is in close agreement with the mass increases of the glyoxylic acid derivatives when using uniformly labelled  $(^{13}C_6)$  and singly labelled  $(3-^{13}C)$  vitamin C. The arrow indicates the peak of the DNP conjugate in the LC profiles. Data are representative of two independent experiments.

To further determine the absolute confguration of these two chiral centres (C8 and C9) in the THB residue in P1 and P2, the *J*-coupling constants of all four stereoisomers (8*R*, 9*R*; 8*S*, 9*S*; 8*R*, 9*S*; 8*S*, 9*R*) were calculated by DFT calculations using the GIAO method. This is because *J*-coupling constants are dependent on the dihedral angles of the planes in which protons are located.
