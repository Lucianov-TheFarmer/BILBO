**Highlight:** Combination of bioinformatics analysis and transgenic experiments identifies a negative regulator of leaf photosynthesis in rice.

#### Abstract

Recet

Improving photosynthesis is a major approach to increase crop yield potential. Here we report a negative transcription factor of photosynthesis, which can be manipulated to increase rice photosynthesis and plant biomass in the field. This transcription factor named *NEGATIVE REGULATOR OF PHOTOSYNTHESIS 1* (*NRP1*) (Os07g0471900) which was identified through a co-expression analysis using rice leaf RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data. The *NRP1* showed significantly negative correlation with the expression of many genes involved in photosynthesis. Knocking out *NPR1* led to greater photosynthesis and increased biomass in the field, while overexpression of *NRP1* decreased photosynthesis and biomass. Transcriptomic data analysis shows that *NRP1* can negatively regulate the expression of photosynthetic genes. Protein transactivation experiments show that *NRP1* is a transcription activator, implying that *NRP1* may indirectly regulate photosynthesis gene expression through an unknown regulator. This study shows that combination of bioinformatics analysis with transgenic testing can be used to identify new regulators to improve photosynthetic efficiency in crops.

**Keywords:** basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) protein, co-expression analysis, negative regulator of photosynthesis, disease resistance, biomass, rice (*Oryza sativa* L.)

#### Introduction

Improving photosynthetic efficiency can be used to improve plant biomass and crop yield (Long *et al.*, 2015; Zhu *et al.*, 2010). Many targets have been identified to improve photosynthesis for greater crop yield (Long *et al.*, 2015; Ort *et al.*, 2015), which are related to ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco) (Sharwood, 2017), the photorespiration pathway (Hagemann and Bauwe, 2016; Shen *et al.*, 2019; South *et al.*, 2019; Walker *et al.*, 2016), Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) cycle (Rosenthal *et al.*, 2011; Simkin *et al.*, 2015; Zhu *et al.*, 2007) and photoprotection (Kromdijk *et al.*, 2016; Zhu *et al.*, 2004). Given the high level of complexity of photosynthesis, systems modeling approach has been used to identify potential targets to engineer photosynthesis, followed by transgenic testing in model plants and crops to confirm their impacts (Long *et al.*, 2015).

The improvement of photosynthesis so far focuses on manipulation of photosynthetic machinery directly, while manipulation of transcription factors (TFs) which can regulate expression of many photosynthetic genes simultaneously is relatively less explored. This is in contrast to the application of TFs in other aspect of crop improvements. For example, TFs have been extensively studied with respect to their potential to improve stress resistance (Goossens et al., 2017; Hu and Xiong, 2014; Phukan et al., 2016; Todaka et al., 2015; Wang and Wang, 2015; Yang et al., 2010) and to improve canopy architecture and yield (Lu et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2018). Recently, the genetic regulation of photosynthesis have attracted more and more attention, e.g. genetic regulatory networks of photosynthesis have been developed through computational approaches (Yu et al., 2014). Concurrently, one transcription factor, i.e. HIGHER YIELD RICE (HYR), was identified to be a major regulator of photosynthesis, whose overexpression led to coordinately increased photosynthesis and rice yield (Ambavaram et al., 2014). Similarly, constitutive expression of ZmG2 and ZmGLK1 in rice enhanced photosynthetic capacity and grain yield in the field (Li et al., 2020). Besides, overexpression of mEmBP-1 in rice increased expression of many genes in photosynthesis, which resulted in increased leaf photosynthetic rate and grain yield (Perveen et al., 2020). Many TFs related to transcriptional regulation of photosynthesis are synthesized to develop a regulatory network (Wang et al., 2017a); genes involved in this network can be tested to identify those that can be used to improve photosynthetic efficiency in crops.

Recently, a new method was proposed which combines gene network, metabolic models and leaf photosynthesis models to identify candidate genes that may increase photosynthesis for a particular crop under different environment (Kannan *et al.*, 2019). Here we present a simplified approach, where we used co-expression analysis to identify potential TFs controlling photosynthesis. The identified TFs were further combined with transgenic experiments to test its function in regulation of photosynthetic efficiency. In particular, this study shows that a TF, named *NEGATIVE REGULATOR OF PHOTOSYNTHESIS 1* (*NRP1*), can concurrently reduce the expression of many photosynthetic genes. We show that knocking out of *NRP1* resulted in improved rice photosynthesis, biomass and expression of photosynthetic genes in the field, suggesting that it has potential to be used to manipulate photosynthesis using the CRISPR/Cas9 techniques (Bortesi and Fischer, 2015; Puchta, 2017; Scheben *et al.*, 2017; Yin *et al.*, 2017).

#### **Materials and Methods**

## Co-expression analysis of rice leaves and identification of NEGATIVE REGULATOR OF PHOTOSYNTHESIS 1 (NRP1)

RNA-seq data for co-expression analysis were downloaded from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) (SRA accession: SRP001787, SRP007388, SRP013556, SRP017256). The RNA-seq reads were aligned using the TopHat software (Trapnell *et al.*, 2009) with and gene expression levels represented by fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) value were obtained through the Cufflinks software (Trapnell *et al.*, 2010). Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) of each gene pairs was calculated with the R package "rsgcc" (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/rsgcc/index.html) (Ma and Wang, 2012). A gene pair was defined as co-expression pair when its absolute value of PCC was over 0.3 with p-value less than 0.05.

#### Gene Ontology (GO) Enrichment Analysis

GO enrichment analysis was conducted with "agriGO" online toolkit (http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/agriGOv2/index.php) (Tian *et al.*, 2017).

### Subcellular Localization of NRP1

The pA7 plasmid with CaMV 35S promoter and YFP was used in this study. NRP1 was fused in frame with yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) in pA7 plasmid (*35S::NRP1-YFP*). The plasmid which contains CaMV 35S promoter with nuclear localization signal (NLS) fused to a red fluorescent protein (*35S::NLS-RFP*) was obtained from Dr. Keran Zhai (Zhai *et al.*, 2019). For rice protoplasts extraction and transformation, we followed the protocol of an

earlier study (You *et al.*, 2016). In detail, for protoplasts extraction, shoots of 10-day-old seedlings Zhonghua11 were cut into ~0.5 mm segments and digested using enzyme solution (1.5% Cellulase RS, 0.75% Macerozyme R10, 0.6 M D-mannitol, 20 mM MES, 0.1% BSA, 1mM CaCl<sub>2</sub>) for four hours under darkness at 28 °C. Then rice protoplasts were suspended in MMG solution (0.5 M D-mannitol, 15 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 4 mM MES). For protoplast transformation, 10 µg plasmid of *35S::NRP1-YFP* and *35S::NLS-RFP*, respectively, were mixed with protoplasts gently. Then a PEG solution (40% PEG4000, 0.2 M D-mannitol. 0.1 M CaCl<sub>2</sub>) was added and mixed gently. After 10 minutes under darkness at room temperature, the solution was centrifuged and resuspended with washing solution (154 mM NaCl, 125 mM CaCl<sub>2</sub>, 5 mM KCl, 5 mM glucose, 2 mM MES) for three times. Before observation, the protoplasts were cultured at room temperature for about 18 hours under darkness. Fluorescence was observed using a Leica LSM880 confocal microscope.

#### Construction of the Phylogenetic Tree of NRP1

To construct the phylogenetic tree of NRP1, orthologs of NRP1 in representative species among viridiplantae were queried and chosen according to two criterion: (1) a species represents a main lineage, e.g., *M. pusilla* represents chlorophyte, and *M. polymorpha* represents the ancestral type of Embryophyte, (2) one lineage represents monocot and one represents dicot. We used Brassicaceae (including Arabidopsis thaliana) to represent dicot and Grass (including *Oryza sativa*) represents monocot. In total, 29 representative species were used in this study. The protein sequences of these representative species were downloaded from Phytozome (https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov/). Orthologs of NRP1 were predicted through the BLASTP software (Shirvev et al., 2007) (version 2.2.31+) with a Evalue threshold of 1e-5. Proteins sequences of NRP1 orthologs were aligned by the MUSCLE software (Edgar, 2004). Phylogenetic tree was constructed by the RaxML software (Stamatakis, 2006) with the PROTGAMMAILG model (General Time Reversible amino acid substitution model with assumption that variations in sites follow gamma distribution). Bootstrap scores were obtained based on 100,000 times sampling. LOC\_Os03g55220 (Os03g0759700) from O. sativa was used as outgroup since it showed the highest identity to NRP1.

#### Multiple Alignment Analysis of bHLH Domain of NRP1

To compare the bHLH domain of NRP1 with homologous bHLH proteins, we used the BLASTP tool to query homologous bHLH proteins with the bHLH domain sequence of NRP1 in the Rice Annotation Project (RAP) Database (https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/) and The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR) (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). The bHLH domain sequence of NRP1 was obtain from UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprot/Q0D6J9). Only the homologous bHLH proteins which DNA binding motif have been studied were kept. For multiple alignment analysis, we used the MUltiple Sequence Comparison by Log-Expectation (MUSCLE) (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/muscle/). For visualization of the multiple alignment result, we used the ESPript 3.0 web server (http://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/cgi-bin/ESPript.cgi) (Robert and Gouet, 2014).

#### Overexpression (OE) and Knockout (KO) Transgenic Plants

For the OE vector, the coding region of *NRP1* was amplified by gene specific primers (CDS-F: ATGGCCAGGGGGCGCCGCCGGC, CDS-R: TCGGTATAAGCAGCTTCTATAGG). Then *NRP1* segment was inserted in an OE vector (the vector containing maize ubiquitin promoter and NOS terminator which derived from pCAMBIA1300 Cambia, Australia) and fused with the c-Myc tag. For the KO vector using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, we followed a previous report (Lu *et al.*, 2017) to edit the *NRP1* gene. In detail, coding sequence of Cas9 was amplified from pX260 (Cong *et al.*, 2013) and inserted in a vector contain maize ubiquitin promoter (derived from pCAMBIA1300 Cambia, Australia). Then sgRNA with OsU6 promoter were also inserted into the vector. Targets of CRISPR/Cas9 were shown in Fig. 3. Finally, the OE and KO vectors were transformed into rice through the agrobacteriummediated methods (Hiei *et al.*, 1994).

### Western Blotting

To validate the overexpression of NRP1 in OE lines, we used the Myc-Tag Antibody (2272S, Cell Signaling Technology, Inc.) and Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (ab6721, abcam). To extract the protein, we grinded the leaf samples with liquid nitrogen, then we lysed the samples by adding 5% SDS solution and heating the samples to 95 °C for 5 min. After, the lysed samples were centrifuged and supernate was transferred to 1.5 ml tube. Then we performed western blot following the manufacture's protocol.

#### Growth Conditions in the Field

The rice grew at Songjiang breeding stating (30°56′ N, 121°8′ E) of the Shanghai Institutes of Plant Physiology and Ecology, Shanghai, China. During the growth period of rice, the average of highest and lowest atmosphere temperature of field within a day is 33.3±3.8 °C and 26.2±2.6 °C, respectively. For each line, seeds after germination were grown on square pots filled with soil in the phytotron, then 28-day-old seedlings were transplanted into field in 7×7 manner and plant spacing was 0.2 meter for both row and column. In this study, wild type (WT), three OE lines (OE-2, OE-3, OE-4) and three KO lines (KO-13, KO-14, KO-16) were grown in paddy field. In total, 9 plots were used. In these 9 plots, the WT, WT, OE-2, OE-3, OE-4, KO-13, KO-14, KO-16, WT, were planted sequentially. The first WT and the last WT were used to avoid border effect and not used for measurements. The experiments were done in two years at different field sites.

#### Gas Exchange Measurement and Data Analysis

High-efficiency all-weather photosynthetic measurement system (HAPS) was employed for gas exchange measurement followed a previous study (Chang *et al.*, 2017) with LI-6400 infrared analyzer (Li-Cor Inc., Lincoln, Nebraska, USA). Then maximal rate of ribulose-1,5bisphosphate (RuBP) carboxylation by Rubisco ( $V_{cmax}$ ) and the maximal photosynthetic electron transport rate ( $J_{max}$ ) were calculated through fitting Farquhar-von Caemmerer-Berry (FvCB) model (Farquhar *et al.*, 1980) using the R package "plantecophys" (Duursma, 2015).

### Gene Expression Analysis

For gene expression profiling, we took the leaf samples at the early heading stage (showed in Supplementary Fig. S1). Three replications of the top second leaves from the main tillers for WT, the OE lines (OE-2, OE-3, OE-4) and the KO lines (KO-13, KO-14, KO-16) were harvested in the field at about 9:00 a.m. Leaf samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately and then stored in -80 °C fridge before RNA extraction. For RNA extraction, we used the PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Life Technologies Corporation). We firstly grinded the leaf sample with liquid nitrogen, then followed the manufacture's protocol. The quality of purified RNA was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and the RNA samples with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) higher than 6.8 will be used for RNA library construction. RNA libraries were prepared based on standard Illumina protocols and sequenced using Illumina X Ten platform in paired-end 150-bp mode.

The quality of RNA-seq data (fastq files) was assessed by the FastQC software (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Then adaptor and reads with low quality from raw RNA-seq reads were trimmed using trim\_galore software (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim\_galore/) (Adaptor of read1: AGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCAC; Adaptor of read2: AGATCGGAAGAGCGTCGTGTAGGGAAAGAGTGT). Then RNA-seq analysis were performed by the STAR (Dobin *et al.*, 2013) software (version 2.5.3a) with the rice reference genome IRGSP-1.0 as well as GTF file (downloaded from Ensembl Plants http://plants.ensembl.org/). After generating genome index, the clean RNA-seq reads were aligned by STAR (Dobin *et al.*, 2013) with "--quantMode GeneCounts" option to count number reads per gene.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined by the R package "DESeq2" (Love *et al.*, 2014) with the read counts reported by STAR (Dobin *et al.*, 2013). DEGs were analyzed in each transgenic line comparing to WT. Only genes with the adjusted p-value below 0.05 were considered DEGs in each transgenic line. For more reliability, DEGs showed the same trend of expression changes in at least two lines were defined as reproducible DEGs and kept for further analysis.

#### Measurements of Agronomic Traits

To compare the agronomic traits (including plant height, panicle number, plant biomass and seed biomass), we randomly dug out ten rice plants with soil from the field at the harvesting stage. Then the rice plants were washed to clean the soil and the roots were cut off. After this, the plant height was measured from basal stem to the tip of panicle of the main tiller. The panicle number was counted at the same time. For sample drying, panicles were cut and dried at 37 °C for 7 days, the stem and leaves were dried at 110 °C for 1 hours then dried at 70 °C for 4 days or longer until the sample was fully dried. To measure plant biomass and seed biomass, the dried panicles, and stem and leaves were weighed together to measure plant biomass. Finally, the seeds were separated from the panicles to measure seed biomass.

#### Transactivation Assay of NRP1

Matchmaker Gold Yeast Two-Hybrid System (Clontech) was used to determine whether NRP1 contained transcriptional activation domain. NRP1 was fused with GAL4 DNA binding domain in pGBKT7 plasmid and transformed into yeast Y2HGold cells. We also used the empty pGBKT7 plasmid as a negative control. The positive yeast clone which grew on synthetically defined (SD) medium lacking Trp (SD/-Trp), was transfer to SD/-Trp medium with X- $\alpha$ -gal and Aureobasidin A (SD/-Trp/X- $\alpha$ -gal/AbA) for resistance examination of Aureobasidin A and activation of  $\alpha$ -galactosidase.

#### Statistical Analysis

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) and least significant difference (LSD) test were performed by the R software (version 3.3.3) (https://www.r-project.org/) with R package "agricolae" (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/agricolae/index.html). For visualization of the RNA-seq results with heatmap, we used the R package "pheatmap" (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/index.html).

#### Results

#### NRP1 shows negative correlation with expression of photosynthetic genes

Since transcription factors (TFs) often show similar expression patterns with its target genes (Li *et al.*, 2015; Serin *et al.*, 2016), we analyzed the co-expression gene pairs between transcription factors and other genes by calculating Pearson's correlation coefficient (PCC) in a set of RNA-seq data from rice leaves. For each TF, its significantly correlated genes were classified into positive ones and negative ones; then gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted for these genes separately. As a result, a transcription factor (Os07g0471900) (named *NEGATIVE REGULATOR OF PHOTOSYNTHESIS 1, NRP1*), was identified for its significantly negative correlation with genes enriched in thylakoid part (GO: 0044436, FDR = 0.000087), photosynthetic membrane (GO: 0034357, FDR = 0.00032) and chloroplast (GO: 0009507, FDR = 0.00041) (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Dataset S1).

We further conducted a detailed analysis of the expression pattern and correlation between the *NRP1* and genes involved in the photosynthesis pathway described in the KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). We found that nine photosynthetic genes showed significantly negative correlation with *NRP1*; these genes include delta subunit of ATP synthase, ferredoxin, components of photosystem I, and components of photosystem II (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table S1). Remarkably, only those genes negatively correlated with *NRP1* were enriched in photosynthesis (Fisher exact test, p = 0.00984); neither positively correlated genes (Fisher exact test, p = 0.7336) nor all correlated genes (Fisher exact test, p = 0.2775)

#### NRP1 codes for a nuclear protein with a novel bHLH domain

NRP1 belongs to the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) protein family, which has been shown to be TFs and are typically located in nucleus (Ji *et al.*, 2013; Luo *et al.*, 2013; Niu *et al.*, 2013). In order to verify cellular location of NRP1, we fused NRP1 with yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) to create NRP1-YFP and then co-transformed it into rice protoplasts with the plasmid which contains nuclear localization signal fused to a red fluorescent protein, i.e. NLS-RFP, as a nuclear marker. Results show that NRP1-YFP was co-localized with the NLS-RFP (Fig. 2A), which indicates that NRP1 is a nuclear protein.

We identified the orthologous proteins of NRP1 and then constructed its phylogenetic tree (Supplementary Fig. S2). In most dicot species, there is only one single copy while in monocot species multiple copies of NPR orthologs exist (Supplementary Fig. S2). So far, no functional study of these orthologs proteins was found.

To evaluate the potential function of NRP1, we further characterized the sequence property of bHLH domain of NRP1. Most bHLH proteins can bind to the E-box (CANNTG) or G-box (CACGTG) DNA motif, and the DNA binding motif of bHLH proteins was determined by 4 amino acid residues within the basic region of bHLH domain, which typically are His/Lys-6, Glu-10, Arg-13, Arg-14 (H/K<sub>6</sub>-E<sub>10</sub>-R<sub>13</sub>-R<sub>14</sub>, were marked with blue in Fig. 2B) (Carretero-Paulet *et al.*, 2010; Feller *et al.*, 2011; Pires and Dolan, 2010). Interestingly, these four amino acid residues of NRP1 were Ser-6, Ser-10, Pro-13, Lys-14 (S<sub>6</sub>-S<sub>10</sub>-P<sub>13</sub>-K<sub>14</sub>) rather than H/K<sub>6</sub>-E<sub>10</sub>-R<sub>13</sub>-R<sub>14</sub> (Fig. 2B). Besides, we compared the sequence of bHLH domain of NRP1 with the homologous bHLH proteins which DNA binding motif have been studied in rice (Cordeiro *et al.*, 2016; Todaka *et al.*, 2012; Zhang *et al.*, 2017) and *Arabidopsis thaliana* (Gaillochet *et al.*, 2017; Huq *et al.*, 2004; Liu *et al.*, 2015; Lou *et al.*, 2014; Martinez-Garcia *et al.*, 2000; Nagel *et al.*, 2014; Pedmale *et al.*, 2016; Wei *et al.*, 2018), and the S<sub>6</sub>-S<sub>10</sub>-P<sub>13</sub>-K<sub>14</sub> of NRP1 are different from these homologous bHLH proteins (Fig. 2B), which suggests that NRP1 contain a novel bHLH domain and may bind to an unknown DNA motif rather than the G-box or E-box motif.

#### KO lines of NRP1 improved leaf photosynthesis without apparent growth defects

We further used transgenic approaches to evaluate potential regulatory role of *NRP1* in photosynthesis. We generated overexpression (OE) lines of *NRP1* and knockout (KO) lines of *NRP1* using CRISPR/Cas9 technology with rice variety Zhonghua11. We selected three OE lines (OE-2, OE-3, OE-4) with different NRP1 protein levels for further experiments (Fig. 3A, 3B). For KO lines, two target sites were designed and three lines (KO-13, KO-14, KO-16) with frameshift mutation in *NRP1* were randomly selected for detailed experiments (Fig. 3A, 3C; Supplementary Fig. S6).

Gas exchange measurements were used to evaluate leaf photosynthetic rates (*A*) of the WT, OE and KO lines in field condition during early heading stage (Supplementary Fig. S1). We measured *A* under different photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) (light response curve) (Fig. 4A) and different intercellular CO<sub>2</sub> concentrations (CO<sub>2</sub> response curve) (Fig. 4B). We found that *A* was significantly higher in KO lines comparing to WT and OE lines under 400 - 2000 PPFD (Fig. 4A). To compare the parameters of CO<sub>2</sub> response curve (Fig. 4B), we fitted the Farquhar-von Caemmerer-Berry model (Farquhar *et al.*, 1980) using the R package "Plantecophys" (Duursma, 2015). We found that maximal rate of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP) carboxylation by Rubisco ( $V_{cmax}$ ) was significantly decreased in OE lines compared to WT and KO lines (Table 1). Besides, the maximal photosynthetic electron transport rate ( $J_{max}$ ) was changed only in OE-3 line and KO-13 line (Table 1). These results suggest that NRP1 has negative effect on leaf photosynthesis in field condition, especially for  $V_{cmax}$ .

#### KO lines of NRP1 increased biomass while OE lines of NRP1 decreased biomass

As photosynthesis is considered to be the limited factor of yield potential (Long *et al.*, 2015; Zhu *et al.*, 2010), to test whether NRP1 could influence plant biomass, we surveyed the agronomic traits of WT, OE lines and KO lines in the harvest stage at both  $T_3$  and  $T_4$ generations of the transgenic lines (Table 2). We found all OE lines showed significantly lower plant biomass comparing to WT (Table 2). In addition, two KO lines (KO-14, KO-16) showed significantly higher plant biomass compared to WT, and all KO lines showed higher plant height and biomass comparing to OE lines (Table 2). These results indicate that NRP1 has negative effect on biomass accumulation. Compared to the significant difference between KO, OE and WT in plant biomass, the changes in the seed biomass between these lines were less significant, though there is still a general trend of increase in seed mass in the KO lines compared to WT and OE (Supplementary Table S1). Because NRP1 was identified by co-expression analysis (Fig. 1A, 1B) and belongs to bHLH TF family (Fig. 2B) which may regulate the gene expression, to verify whether these observed changes in the photosynthetic physiology and biomass were caused by changes in the photosynthetic gene expression in the *NRP1* OE and KO lines, we examined the transcriptomes of WT, OE and KO lines in field condition. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each transgenic line compared to WT were identified using an R package 'DESeq2' (Love et al., 2014). The list of DEGs was first independently identified for these three OE lines and three KO lines and then we only selected those DEGs which showed the same trend of expression changes in at least two lines for further analysis. The results show that 367 and 909 reproducible DEGs (Supplementary Dataset S1) were identified in OE lines and KO lines, respectively. GO enrichment analysis for these reproducible DEGs using the 'agriGO' online toolkit (Tian et al., 2017) identified the enriched GO terms (Supplementary Dataset S1). Consistent with higher leaf photosynthetic rate, the up-regulated genes in KO lines were indeed enriched in photosynthesis (Fig. 5A). In addition, the other enriched GO terms include photosynthetic membrane, thylakoid, photosystem and chloroplast part (Fig. 5A), which are related to photosynthesis.

To further examine how KO *NRP1* influenced different components of photosynthesis, we analyzed the up-regulated genes of photosynthetic pathways described in the KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) in KO lines. Surprisingly, the up-regulated genes in the KO lines were related to all major components of photosynthesis, including photosystem II, photosystem I, light-harvesting protein complex, ATP synthase, cytochrome b<sub>6</sub>/f complex, chlorophyll biosynthesis pathway and even enzymes involved in the Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) cycle (Fig. 5B). These results show that *NRP1* can negatively regulate expression of many genes involved in photosynthesis.

#### NRP1 contains a transcriptional activation domain

To gain more insight into how NRP1 regulates the expression of photosynthetic genes, we conducted a transactivation assay to test whether NRP1 directly or indirectly regulates photosynthetic genes. To do this, we fused the *NRP1* gene with a GAL4 DNA binding domain within the pGBKT7 plasmid and transformed to Y2HGold yeast cell. Results show that NRP1 can activate the expression of reporter genes in yeast (Fig. 5C), which suggest that NRP1 contains a transcriptional activation domain.

#### Discussion

In this study, we report that combination of bioinformatics analysis with transgenic testing can be used as a viable approach to identify new TFs controlling photosynthesis. Compared to the approach developed by (Kannan *et al.*, 2019), the bioinformatics method used here is rather simplified since it does not consider the complex interaction between enzymes in the photosynthetic light reactions and Calvin Benson cycle. Even however, here we show that, though with this simplification, we can use this approach to identify new regulators of photosynthesis, which can be used to improve leaf photosynthetic efficiency in crops. It is worth emphasizing here that though the TFs identified through this approach might not necessarily represent an optimal choice of TFs in the sense that they will not result in optimized enzyme distribution in photosynthetic machinery, as would potentially be the case for (Kannan *et al.*, 2019). Here we discuss the impacts of this newly identified negative regulator of photosynthesis, i.e. NRP1, on photosynthesis and plant performance. We also briefly comment on the potential mechanism underlying how NRP1 may influence photosynthesis.

# KO lines of NRP1 show higher photosynthesis and plant biomass, while OE lines show the opposite

Proteins of bHLH family like PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTORs (PIFs) are generally considered as negative regulators of photosynthesis (Castillon *et al.*, 2007; Leivar and Monte, 2014; Leivar and Quail, 2011; Wang *et al.*, 2017a). So far, it is not clear whether these negative regulators can be used as engineering targets for photosynthesis improvement. Considering the rapid advances of the CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing technology (Bortesi and Fischer, 2015; Puchta, 2017; Scheben *et al.*, 2017; Yin *et al.*, 2017), identification of negative regulators of photosynthesis represents a tempting option to manipulate photosynthesis. In this study, the *NRP1* was identified as a potential regulator of photosynthesis through the co-expression assay based on RNA-seq data, where we found that the expression of *NRP1* is negatively correlated with a number of genes related to photosynthesis (Fig. 1A, 1B). We further used transgenic experiments to show that KO *NPR1* can indeed increase photosynthesis while OE of *NRP1* decreased photosynthetic efficiency (Fig. 4A, 4B, Table 1). These results suggest that this co-expression analysis combined with transgenic experiments indeed can be used to identify regulators of photosynthesis.

In this study, we found that three OE lines showed significantly lower plant biomass and two KO lines (KO-14 and KO-16) showed significantly higher plant biomass, which suggest that NRP1 has a negative effect on biomass accumulation. CRISPR/Cas9 may induce off-target mutations (Endo *et al.*, 2015; Puchta, 2017; Scheben *et al.*, 2017; Zhang *et al.*, 2014). To minimize the chance of having this off-target effect causing the observed phenotype, we used two different target sites to generate KO-14 and KO-16 (Fig. 3C). Both lines showed significantly higher plant biomass compared to WT (Table 2), which indicates that the higher plant biomass of these KO lines were resulted from *NRP1* knockout rather than an off-target effect. In this study, the KO-13 did not increase plant biomass compared to WT. Two reasons might be related to this lack of increase in biomass in this line. During the transgenic process, tissue culture may also induce genomic and epigenetic changes (Miyao *et al.*, 2012; Stroud *et al.*, 2013). Second, plant biomass is complex trait (Xing and Zhang, 2010) and influenced by many factors including genomic and epigenetic changes described above.

Identifying those regulators that can not only increase photosynthesis, but also ultimately improve biomass accumulation and crop yield is a major goal of photosynthesis research. This is challenging since photosynthesis and yield are usually related to many different factors (Chang and Zhu, 2017). Here we show that, concurrent with the increase in photosynthesis in KO lines of *NRP1*, we also found an increase in biomass accumulation (Table 2), suggesting that knocking out of *NRP1* might be potentially used as a strategy to improve photosynthesis in rice. Here we caution that, though in this study, we did not notice apparent growth defect in the KO lines of *NRP1*; however, more extensive field testing in more field sites, in different weather conditions and in more germplasms is still needed to test the impact of *NPR* on photosynthesis and biomass production. The impacts of *NRP1* on many other rice breeding related traits also need to be evaluated before this potential in rice

## Higher photosynthetic $CO_2$ assimilation rate of KO lines may be due to simultaneous upregulation of multiple photosynthetic genes

A major focus of the current photosynthesis improvement is to identify limiting steps of photosynthesis and then to manipulate the limiting steps specifically (Long *et al.*, 2015). As discussed earlier, the bioinformatics approach used can not be used to identify the optimal choice of TF for photosynthesis improvement. However, considering that the limiting step of photosynthesis is in essence a systems property, *i.e.* the limiting step will shift when the

quantities or activities of proteins involved in photosynthesis change. In another word, if we can simultaneously increase the expression of multiple genes in photosynthesis, it is likely to increase photosynthetic efficiency since the fluxes through the limiting steps might be increased during the coordinated up-regulation.

The *NRP1* identified here can simultaneously and negatively regulate expression of multiple photosynthetic genes. As shown in the KO lines, the up-regulated genes are located in different components of photosynthesis (Fig. 5A, 5B). Among these genes, many have been shown earlier to be potential limiting steps of photosynthesis. Specifically, *PetC* codes the iron-sulfur subunit of cytochrome b<sub>6</sub>/f complex, which is closely related to leaf photosynthetic rate and plant biomass (Yamori *et al.*, 2016). *YGL8* (also called *YL-1* and *OsCRD1*) has important functions in chlorophyll biosynthesis (Kong *et al.*, 2016) and it can influence photosynthetic capacity (Wang *et al.*, 2017b) and chloroplast membrane stability (Sheng *et al.*, 2017). While *LYL1* is related to chlorophyll biosynthesis and is response to high light resistance (Zhou *et al.*, 2013). The simultaneous up-regulation of these different genes together contributed to the increased leaf photosynthetic rates (Fig. 4A, 4B).

Interestingly, *NRP1* does not seems to influence expression of the earlier reported genes controlling photosynthesis in rice, which include *SBPase* (Feng *et al.*, 2007), *PHD1* (Li *et al.*, 2011), *NAL1* (Takai *et al.*, 2013), *Ghd8* (also called *CAR8* (Adachi *et al.*, 2017)), and *HYR* (Ambavaram *et al.*, 2014). *CAR8* and *HYR* are TFs and *HYR* haves been shown to directly activate photosynthetic genes (Ambavaram *et al.*, 2014). *SBPase* and *PHD1* code enzymes, which involved in the CBB cycle (Feng *et al.*, 2007) and galactolipid biosynthesis (Li *et al.*, 2011), respectively, while *NAL1* regulates the growth of lateral leaf (Takai *et al.*, 2013). For our expression data, these genes were not DEGs according to our differentially expressed analysis results (Supplementary Dataset S1, Fig. S3). These suggests that *NRP1* might have influenced photosynthesis either directly through influencing those up-regulated photosynthetic genes (Fig. 5A, 5B) or through some unknown novel mechanisms.

# *NRP1* indirectly regulates the expression of photosynthetic genes through a yet unknown novel downstream factor

The *NRP1* identified in this study belongs to the bHLH protein family (Fig. 2B), which is one of the largest protein families of TFs and is found throughout plants (Carretero-Paulet *et al.*, 2010; Feller *et al.*, 2011; Li *et al.*, 2006) and animals (Bersten *et al.*, 2013; Cowles *et al.*, 2013; Fritzsch *et al.*, 2010). bHLH family includes many plant TFs, which are involved in

hormone signaling (Clouse, 2011; Fujita *et al.*, 2011; Kim and Wang, 2010), stress response (Hu *et al.*, 2017; Pireyre and Burow, 2015) and organ development (Pillitteri and Torii, 2012; Schiefelbein *et al.*, 2009; Wilson and Zhang, 2009). More relevant to this study, PIFs which belong to bHLH protein family repress the expression of photosynthetic genes and generally considered as the negative regulators of photosynthetic gene expression (Castillon *et al.*, 2007; Leivar and Monte, 2014; Leivar and Quail, 2011; Wang *et al.*, 2017a). In line with this notion, here we show that KO lines of *NRP1* showed up-regulation of the expression of photosynthetic genes (Fig. 5A, 5B).

Considering regulatory mechanisms of TFs, how did KO *NPR* result in up-regulated expression of photosynthetic genes (Fig. 5A, 5B)? There are two possibilities, i.e. (1) NRP1 may directly repress expression of photosynthesis gene; (2) NRP1 may activate downstream factors, which is in turn repressing photosynthetic gene expression. To test these two possibilities, we conducted a transactivation experiment (Fig. 5C). Results showed that NRP1 can activate the reporter genes in yeast system, which suggests that NRP1 indeed contains a transcriptional activation domain and may regulate its direct targets through activation, rather than repression. In additions, S<sub>6</sub>-S<sub>10</sub>-P<sub>13</sub>-K<sub>14</sub> within the basic region of NPR differ from other known bHLH TFs (Fig. 2B), which implies that *NPR* may regulate its direct targets through binding to the unknown DNA motif but not G-box or E-box, which were found preferentially in the promoters of photosynthesis genes (Kobayashi *et al.*, 2012; Toledo-Ortiz *et al.*, 2014; Wang *et al.*, 2017a). In another word, NRP1 may activate another unknown negative regulator of photosynthetic genes, rather than repress the expression of photosynthetic genes directly.

What might be the down-stream target of this *NPR* that mediated these changes in photosynthesis? Interestingly, in our experiment, we found that the down-regulated genes of KO lines were enriched in apoptosis (GO:0006915, FDR = 0.0078), programmed cell death (GO: 0012501, FDR = 0.0088), death (GO: 0016265, FDR = 0.01) and cell death (GO: 0008219, FDR = 0.01) (Supplementary Dataset S1) and the down-regulated apoptosis related genes were mainly disease resistance proteins (Supplementary Fig. S4, Supplementary Table S2). Further, we found 41 reproducible DEGs related to disease resistance, and 33 of them showed down-regulated in KO lines (Supplementary Fig. S4, Supplementary Table S2). In line with this observation, a number of earlier reports showed that activation of disease resistance can inhibit both photosynthetic  $CO_2$  assimilation and photosynthetic gene expression (Bilgin *et al.*, 2010; de Torres Zabala *et al.*, 2015; Lewis *et al.*, 2015; Su *et al.*,

2018), and also have negative effect on plant biomass accumulation (Goto *et al.*, 2015; Pajerowska-Mukhtar *et al.*, 2012) which was similar to the phenotype what we observed (Fig. 4, Table 1, Table 2). All these suggest that the downstream target of NRP1 might be a regulator related to disease resistance, which further negatively regulated photosynthetic gene expression (Supplementary Fig. S5). More studies are needed to either confirm or reject this hypothesis to gain more insight on the regulation of NRP1 on photosynthesis.
