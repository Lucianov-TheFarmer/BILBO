# Seed weight differences between wild and domesticated soybeans are associated with specific changes in gene expression

## Introduction

Soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr.] is a worldwide crop of economic importance, because of the high concentrations of protein and oil in its seeds. Besides its high nutrient value, as a legume it can fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiosis with microorganisms. Soybean was first domesticated in China approximately 5000 years ago (Carter et al. [2004\)](#page-8-0), and was then introduced to North America in 1765 (Hymowitz and Harlan [1983\)](#page-8-0). Although both cultivated and wild soybeans (Glycine soja Sieb. & Zucc) have twenty chromosomes (2n = 40), hybridize easily, present normal meiotic chromosome pairing, and produce viable fertile progeny, they are significantly different with respect to several morphological characteristics as result of divergent natural and artificial selection (Liu et al. [2007](#page-8-0)). Seed size is one of these phenotypic differences, and wild soybean has tiny black seeds rather than large yellow seeds (Kim et al. [2010\)](#page-8-0). In Arabidopsis thaliana, a handful of genes have been shown to determine seed size (Adamski et al. [2009](#page-8-0); Jofuku et al. [2005;](#page-8-0) Luo et al. [2005;](#page-8-0) Zhou et al. [2009\)](#page-9-0), such as AINTEGUMENATA (ANT), encoding a transcription factor of the AP2-domain family that can control plant organ cell number and organ size (Mizukami and Fischer [2000\)](#page-9-0). However, seed size is not simply controlled by a single gene in soybean, and quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping has been extensively conducted to explore the underlying genetic basis of this domestication-related trait (Hyten et al. [2004](#page-8-0); Liu et al. [2007\)](#page-8-0). Seed weight is often used as an indicator of average seed size, and seed size (evaluated as 100-kernel weight) has previously been associated with 26 QTLs, scattered across 18 of the 20 soybean linkage groups (Hyten et al. [2004\)](#page-8-0), further indicating its complexity.

To explore the differences in seed weight between cultivated and wild soybeans at the transcriptome level, we performed strand-specific RNA sequencing on seeds from thirteen soybean samples including six landraces and seven wild accessions. 520 differentially expressed genes were identified at seed development stage between cultivated and wild soybeans, and some were previously shown to be associated with seed development in Arabidopsis or soybean. Subsequently, we used real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)to validatethe expression pattern of these genes in seeds of cultivated and wild soybeans, which confirmed the same trend observed in our RNA-seq datasets. Long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) has also been known to play fundamental biological roles in plant development and physiology (Ariel et al. [2014](#page-8-0); Wang et al. [2017;](#page-9-0) Zhang et al. [2014\)](#page-9-0), and we characterized 1,251 long intergenic noncoding RNAs (lincRNA), 243 intronic RNAs and 81 antisense lncRNAs from soybean seeds in this study. Furthermore, expression profiling of these lncRNAs indicated that some of them were expressed in a sample-specific fashion in cultivated and wild soybeans. Taken together, the results from gene and lncRNA co-expression analysis implicate lncRNAs as potentially important for soybean seed development.

## Materials and methods

## Sample collection and measurement of seed weight

Soybean samples used in this study were field grown, and they blossomed on the same day in Nanchang city. Seeds were harvested thirteen days after fertilization for RNA sequencing because the previous study of (Du et al. [2017\)](#page-8-0) reported seed size differences between a landrace and one elite variety at this stage. Dry weights of 100 mature seeds from each of thirteen soybean accessions were measured separately, and analyzed with the two-sample independent t test.

## RNA isolation, RNA sequencing and real-time PCR assay

Total RNAs were isolated using Trizol reagent (Sigma) according to the manufacturer's protocol, and DNA contaminants were removed using TURBO DNA-free Kit (Ambion). Strand-specific RNA-seq libraries were constructed with a TruSeq Stranded mRNA kit (Illumina) in accordance with the manufacturer's instruction. RNA-seq library quality was assessed using a Fragment Analyzer (Advanced Analytical), and libraries that passed quality control were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 instrument with 150 nt pair-end reads. cDNA preparation and qRT-PCR were carried out as (Wang, et al. [2014](#page-9-0)). Details of the primers used in this study are listed in Table S6.

## Bioinformatic analysis of RNA sequencing

Adaptor and low quality sequences of raw reads were trimmed using trim\_galore with the following parameter: -stringency 6. Trimmed reads were mapped to the soybean reference genome (Wm82.a2.v1) using TopHat v2.0.14 with the following parameters: -N 6 -read-editdist 6 -G (Kim et al. [2013\)](#page-8-0). Mapped reads were filtered using SAMtools to remove improperly paired and ambiguously mapped reads with the following parameters: -F 4 -q 1 -f 2 (Li et al. [2009](#page-8-0)). Read counts for all reference genes were obtained with the R package <span id="page-2-0"></span>''GenomicAlignments'' using the above filtered mapped reads as input (Lawrence et al. [2013\)](#page-8-0). Differential expression (DE) analysis of reference genes was performed using the R package ''edgeR'' (Robinson et al. [2010\)](#page-9-0). Samples from cultivated or wild soybeans were treated as biological replicates and genes with adjusted p values less than 0.05 based on edgeR were selected as significantly differentially expressed between cultivated and wild soybeans.

Gene Ontology (GO) over-representation analysis of DE genes was performed using the web-based tool ''agriGO'' with ''Hypergenometric'' as the statistical test method and ''Hochberg FDR'' to correct for multiple testing (Du et al. [2010\)](#page-8-0).

Known soybean QTLs were obtained from SoyBase and converted to genomic regions according to the SoyBase genetic map (Grant et al. [2010](#page-8-0)). DEGs overlapping with these QTL genomic regions were identified as genes linked to QTLs.

#### Soybean lncRNA identification and co-expression analysis

De novo identified soybean lncRNAs were identified within the RNA-seq data using a pipeline including several publicly available tools. In summary, filtered mapped reads for each sample were assembled into long transcripts using Cufflinks v2.2.1 with the following parameter:–library-type fr-firststrand (Trapnell et al. [2012\)](#page-9-0). Assembled transcripts from individual samples were then merged and size selected ([200 nt) as reference-assembled long transcripts. Known gene transcripts were filtered out by comparing the genomic coordinates of assembled long transcripts against soybean known gene intervals. Only transcripts that did not overlap with known genes were kept and classified into three categories: intergenic transcripts, intronic transcripts and antisense transcripts. Potential unannotated protein coding transcripts from each category were further filtered out with two more steps: (1) BLASTX against known proteins (SWISS-PROT) and (2) identification of open reading frames (ORFs). To get a reliable lncRNA dataset, we remove low expression transcripts with the following criteria: raw count greater than 10 reads in at least 2 out of 13 samples or greater than 2 reads in at least 7 out of 13 samples.

Co-expression networks of genes and lincRNAs were reconstructed using the R package ''WGCNA'' with soft power set at ''12'' (Langfelder and Horvath [2008\)](#page-8-0). Coexpression modules/sub-networks were visualized with Cytoscape v3.0 (Shannon, et al. [2003\)](#page-9-0). GO over-representation analysis of genes in co-expression modules was performed using ''argiGO'' with the same settings as the above DE analysis.

#### Results

#### Measurement of seed weight in cultivated and wild soybeans from China

Seeds of six landraces and seven wild soybeans were collected from ten different provinces of China (Table S1) and were field grown in Nanchang city, Jiangxi province. Seed sizes of the six cultivated soybean varieties were obviously larger than for the seven wild accessions (Fig. 1a). We then measured 100-kernel weight from each accession, and seed weights of wild soybean accessions were remarkably lighter than landraces (Fig. 1b). The average 100-kernel weight of the six cultivated soybean seeds was 16.1506 g, which was significantly heavier than for the seven wild accessions whose average weight was 2.5705 g (Twosample Independent t test, p\0.01).

#### Differential gene expression analysis between cultivated and wild soybeans

To identify genes and potential regulatory networks associated with seed weight, we performed RNA sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform, and analyzed seed gene expression at transcriptome level. In total, nearly 400 million 151 nucleotide (nt) long paired-end reads were generated from thirteen samples with sequencing depth <span id="page-3-0"></span>ranging from 75 to 137-fold for all genic regions in the soybean genome (Table S1). High mapping rates, ranging from 88 to 93%, were obtained when mapping reads to the reference genome Williams 82 (Schmutz et al. [2010](#page-9-0)) in all 13 samples (Table S1). Multidimensional scaling (MDS) plot based on gene expression profiles of thirteen samples indicated different expression patterns between cultivated and wild soybeans (Fig. 2a). Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between cultivated and wild soybeans (considering individual samples from each group as replicates). 360 up- and 160 significantly down-regulated genes were identified by comparing the six cultivated soybeans to the seven wild soybeans (Fig. 2b and Table S2). Gene Ontology (GO) over-representation analysis of these DEGs indicated that ''translation'', ''cellular macromolecule biosynthesis'' and ''macromolecule biosynthetic process'' were the three biological process terms with the most significant enrichment. Next, we examined the correlation between our identified DEGs and seed weight by determining if their Arabidopsis homologs were associated with seed development. We found twelve of their homologs (Table S3) were known to be involved in Arabidopsis seed development (Meinke et al. [2008\)](#page-9-0). We then chose three of these genes (Glyma13g177800, Glyma11g139400 and Glyma09g042800) involved in different biological functions for investigation of their expression pattern by qRT-PCR (Fig. 3). Glyma13g177800's homolog encodes the cytoplasmic ribosomal protein S6; the Arabidopsis homolog of Glyma11g139400, TRANSLOCASE OF THE INNER MEMBRANE9 (TIM9) is essential for maintenance of mitochondrial function of early proper cells and endosperm-free nuclei (Deng, et al. [2014](#page-8-0)); Glyma09g042800's homolog encodes a LYR family Fe/S cluster biogenesis protein but its function is still unknown. Transcriptional levels of these three genes were significantly higher in cultivar seeds compared to wild soybeans, (Glyma13g177800: p\0.05; Glyma11g139400: p\0.05; Glyma09g042800: p\0.05, two-sample Independent t test), which was consistent with the trend observed in the RNA-seq data. In addition, we studied the expression pattern of Glyma13g150000 (Kim et al. [2009](#page-8-0)), a soybean gene previously reported to be involved in cell elongation, in our thirteen samples using qRT-PCR (Fig. 3). This gene was expressed at a much higher level in the six cultivated soybeans than in the seven wild soybeans (Two-sample Independent t test, p\0.01), supporting our findings from the RNA-seq analysis. Moreover, the association between these DEGs and reported soybean seed weight QTLs (Grant et al. [2010](#page-8-0)) was investigated. 53 (out of 360) upregulated and 22 (out of 160) down-regulated genes were in these QTL regions (Table S4), further indicating that DEGs identified here are good candidates for the future functional study of soybean seed weight.

#### Reconstruction of gene and lincRNA co-expression networks

To infer the potential functional importance of our soybean lincRNAs, we reconstructed co-expression networks based on the expression profiles of soybean reference genes and lincR-NAs identified from our RNA-seq dataset. Fifty-two co-expression modules, representing clusters of transcripts with highly correlated expression pattern across thirteen individual samples, were reconstructed (Table S5 and Figure S1). Between 1 and 296 lincRNAs were co-expressed with reference genes in different co-expression modules (Table S5). Many of these co-expression modules represented transcripts, including both genes and lincRNAs, with over- or under-expression in one or several individual samples (Figure S2). For example, transcripts in module ''black'' showed over-expression in wild soybean ''ys17'', while module ''indianred3'', ''lightpink2'', ''palevioletred1'', ''pink3'', ''sienna2'' and ''sienna4'' represented transcripts under-expressed in one specific soybean sample (Figure S2). We also found that one co-expression module ''salmon1'', containing 67 genes and 4 lincRNAs, showed overall over-expression in cultivated soybeans and under-expression in wild soybeans (Fig. [5](#page-7-0)a). Visualization of this co-expression module identified a lincRNA ''LINC.XLOC\_021359'' as one of the potential co-expression hubs (Fig. [5b](#page-7-0)). The GO over-representation of 67 genes in this co-expression module showed that ''polysaccharide biosynthetic process'', ''glucan biosynthesis'' and ''macromolecule biosynthetic process'' related biological process terms were significantly over-represented in these genes (Fig. [5](#page-7-0)c). With respect to the cellular component category, genes were significantly enriched for the term ''membrane'' (Fig. [5c](#page-7-0)). Altogether, results from this co-expression network analysis were not only helpful for investigating the molecular pathways highly associated with seed weight in cultivated and wild soybeans, but also provided valuable information for continued investigation of the functions of soybean lincRNAs.

### Discussion

Soybean is an important oil crop, and understanding the gene expression profile of soybean seeds is important for improving seed yield. In this study, we comprehensively analyzed transcriptomes from seeds of thirteen soybean accessions including six cultivars and seven wild soybeans, which exhibited significant differences in seed weight (Fig. [1b](#page-2-0), c). Five hundred and twenty DEGs containing 360 up-regulated and 160 down-regulated genes were identified between two species (Fig. [2b](#page-3-0) and Table S2), and function enrichment analysis showed that genes associated with protein synthesis were over-represented in these DEGs. This finding indicates that some DEGs identified here are also associated with differences in seed composition between wild and cultivated soybeans, in addition to seed weight. Enrichment of DEGs associated with protein synthesis functions might be the cause of seed protein content differences between cultivated and wild soybeans (Chen and Nelson [2004\)](#page-8-0). In addition, we found that twelve of them have homologs associated with seed development in Arabidopsis. The functions of these Arabidopsis homologs are quite diverse, and they are related to translation, mitochondrial function and so on (Table S3), implying that seed development encompasses wide-ranging processes. More importantly, a high proportion of DEGs (53 out of 360 up-regulated and 22 out of 160 down-regulated) were linked to known seed weight QTLs in soybean. All these results indicate that DEGs identified in this study are valuable resources for investigating seed size differences associated with domestication and future improvement of seed yield in soybean. Although DEGs were characterized as significantly differentially expressed between cultivated and wild soybeans, there was still expression variance within the individual cultivated or wild samples. For example, the expression level of Glyma13g177800 in soybean cultivated variety ''df3'' was no higher than in seven wild soybean accessions, although the overall trend was that this gene was significantly up-regulated in cultivated soybeans compared to wild soybeans (Fig. [3\)](#page-4-0). This observation suggests that there is still substantial genetic variation within both the cultivars and the wild accessions, indicating the complexity of underlying mechanisms for seed weight phenotype in soybean. However, another soybean gene Glyma13g150000, encoding a leucine-rich repeat receptor-like kinase, was reported to be highly expressed at a specific stage of soybean seed development and may be involved in the regulation of cell elongation (Kim et al. [2009](#page-8-0)). We found that it was expressed in all six cultivated soybean accessions at a higher level than in any of the seven wild soybean accessions. This indicates that Glyma13g150000 is a good candidate for future experiments to assess its function in controlling soybean seed weight.

#### Conclusions

There is a significant difference between cultivated and wild soybeans with respect to seed weight. Here, we identified 520 DEGs of soybean seeds between six landraces and seven wild accessions using transcriptomic analyses. 75 DEGs are associated with previously reported seed weight QTLs, and 12 DEGs have homologs involved <span id="page-8-0"></span>in Arabidopsis seed development. We also de novo identified soybean lncRNAs and reconstructed soybean gene and lncRNA co-expression networks, and some lncRNAs were found to be hubs of co-expression sub-modules with enriched functional roles, indicating their potential regulatory roles in soybean seed development. This study provides valuable resources for future functional studies of seed development, e.g., seed weight, which will be important for the genetic improvement of seed yield in soybean.
