#### Summary

Fruit ripening is governed by a complex regulatory network. Reversible histone methylation and demethylation regulate chromatin structure and gene expression. However, little is known about the involvement of histone demethylases in regulating fruit ripening.

Here, we found that the tomato *SLJMJ6* encodes a histone lysine demethylase that specifically demethylates H3K27 methylation. Overexpression of *SLJMJ6* accelerates tomato fruit ripening, which is associated with the upregulated expression of a large number of ripening-related genes.

Integrated analysis of RNA-seq and ChIP-seq identified 32 genes directly targeted by SIJMJ6 and transcriptionally upregulated with decreased H3K27m3 in *SlJMJ6*-overexpressed fruit. Numerous SIJMJ6-regulated genes are involved in transcription regulation, ethylene biosynthesis, cell wall degradation, and hormone signaling. Eleven ripening-related genes including *RIN*, *ACS4*, *ACO1*, *PL*, *TBG4*, and a DNA demethylase *DML2*, were confirmed to be directly regulated by SIJMJ6 through removing H3K27me3.

Our results demonstrate that SIJMJ6 is a ripening-prompting H3K27me3 demethylase that activates the expression of the ripening-related genes by modulating H3K27me3, thereby facilitating tomato fruit ripening. Our work also reveals a novel link between histone demethylation and DNA demethylation in regulating fruit ripening. To our knowledge, this is the first report of the involvement of a histone lysine demethylase in the regulation of fruit ripening.

**Keywords**: fruit ripening, JmjC-domain protein, H3K27 demethylase, histone demethylation, DNA demethylation, DML2, RIN, ACO

#### Introduction

Fruits, the seed-bearing structures of flowering plants, are important for providing humans with important nutrients such as carbohydrates, vitamins, dietary fiber and special functional active substances. Ripening is crucial for the development and maintenance for fruit quality. Fruit ripening undergoes drastic changes in color, texture, flavor, aroma components and carbohydrate contents. Fruit ripening is regulated by endogenous hormones and genetic regulators, which act in a network that determine expression of ripening-related genes (Gao *et al.*, 2018). A better understanding of the regulatory network controlling ripening is biological interesting and also economically important for developing strategies to improve nutritional and sensorial qualities of fruit and reduce post-harvest losses (Jiang *et al.*, 2018; Li *et al.*, 2019).

Transcription regulators play a vital role in regulating fruit ripening by activating or repressing the expression of ripening-related genes. Tomato is a good model system to study the mechanism underlying fruit ripening due to simple diploid genetics, well-annotated genome, ease of transformation, short life cycle and deep study foundation (Giovannoni *et al.*, 2017). Transcription factors-mediated regulatory network in tomato ripening has been well characterized. In tomato, the transcription factors RIPENING INHIBITOR (RIN), NON-RIPENING (NOR) and COLORLESS NON-RIPENING (CNR) in combination with ethylene signaling govern fruit ripening (Vrebalov *et al.*, 2002; Manning *et al.*, 2006; Giovannoni, 2007). In addition to master transcription factors, other transcription factors, such as APETALA2a (AP2a), FRUITFULL (FUL1 and FUL2) and GOLDEN-LIKE2 (GLK2), are also implicated in regulation of tomato fruit ripening (Karlova *et al.*, 2011; Bemer *et al.*, 2012; Nguyen *et al.*, 2014). Together, these transcription factors constitute an intertwined regulatory network to coordinately regulate fruit ripening.

Epigenetics refers to the reversible and heritable changes in gene expression that do not involve any alteration of DNA sequences. Epigenetic markers, including DNA methylation, histone modifications (methylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, glycosylation, sumoylation), and non-coding RNA play critical roles in the regulation of chromatin structure and dynamic gene expression (Berger *et al.*, 2009; Allis & Jenuwein, 2016). Emerging evidences have suggested an involvement of epigenetic modifications such as DNA methylation and histone acetylation in regulating fruit ripening and senescence (Giovannoni et al., 2017). In tomato, inhibition of DNA methyltransferase activity induces premature fruit ripening, and fruit development is associated with dynamic genome-wide DNA methylation changes, suggesting a possible role of DNA methylation in control of fruit maturation (Zhong *et al.*, 2013). Further work showed that either down-regulation of 4 putative DNA demethylases (DMLs) or knock-out of the Arabidopsis DNA demethylase ROS1 close homolog DML2 leads to inhibited fruit ripening and altered ripening-related gene expression (Liu et al., 2015; Lang et al., 2017). Moreover, silencing of histone modifiers, the RPD3/HDA1-type histone deacetylase genes SlHDA1 and SlHDA3, causes accelerated ripening process, whereas down-regulation of the HD2-type histone deacetylase gene SlHDT3 results in delayed fruit ripening and decreased carotenoid accumulation (Guo et al., 2017a; Guo et al., 2017b; Guo et al., 2018). In addition, the banana histone deacetylase MaHDA1 negatively regulates the expression of ripening-related genes by interacting with the transcription factor MaERF11 during fruit ripening (Han et al., 2016). These studies reveal the tip of the iceberg that epigenetic modifications are involved in the regulation of fruit ripening.

Histone methylation, which mainly occurs at specific lysine and arginine residues located on the N-terminal tails of the core histones, plays an essential role in chromatin configuration and gene expression regulation. Histone lysine residues can be mono-, di- or trimethylated, which are dynamically controlled by histone lysine methyltransferases and demethylases (Liu *et al.*, 2010). There are two known families of histone lysine demethylase, lyine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) and Jumonji C-terminal (JmjC) domain-containing demethylases (Shi *et al.*, 2004; Tsukada *et al.*, 2006). In plants, histone lysine methylation occurs mainly at lysine 4 (K4), lysine 9 (K9), lysine 27 (K27), lysine 36 (K36), lysine 79 (K79) of histone H3 (Liu *et al.*, 2010). The methylation of specific histone lysine residues is associated with gene activation or repression. Generally, the methylation of K4, K36 and K79 on histone H3 is related to active chromatin and positively regulates gene expression, while the methylation of K9 and K27 on histone H3 is associated with repressed gene expression. In model plants Arabidopsis and rice, reversible histone lysine

methylation and demethylation play essential roles in reprogramming of transcription in multiple developmental processes, including flowering (Sun & Zhou, 2008; Ning *et al.*, 2015; Yang *et al.*, 2016; Li *et al.*, 2018; Zheng *et al.*, 2019), shoot development (Cheng *et al.*, 2018), stem elongation (Chen *et al.*, 2013), leaf senescence (Liu *et al.*, 2019; Wang *et al.*, 2019). The ripening of fleshy fruits is a unique but economically important phase in the life cycle of horticultural crops. However, the involvement of histone methylation modification during this process remains largely unclear. Moreover, the histone methylation- mediated transcription regulatory network during fruit ripening has not been elucidated.

In this study, 25 histone demethylases (HDMs) were identified in the tomato genome, of which SIJMJ6, a member of the plant-specific KDM5/JAR2DI sub-family of JmjC domain-containing proteins, is an H3K27 demethylase with apparent demethylation activity for trimethyl at H3K27. Overexpression of SIJMJ6 accelerates tomato fruit ripening. We found that SIJMJ6 is involved in H3K27me3 demethylation in a number of ripening-related genes and activates the expression of these genes to promote fruit ripening in tomato. Notably, SIJMJ6 directly activates the expression of *DML2*, a key DNA demethylase which is required for fruit ripening. This is, to our knowledge, the first report of the involvement of histone lysine demethylase in the regulation of fruit ripening.

#### Materials and methods

#### Plant materials and growth conditions

Wild-type tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum* Mill. cv. Ailsa-Craig) and transgenic lines in the Ailsa-Craig background were grown in a greenhouse at 23°C under 16 h light and 8 h dark condition. Flowers were tagged at anthesis and fruit development and ripening was recorded as days post-anthesis (dpa). Fruits of wild-type and transgenic lines were sampled at 37 dpa and 40 dpa. Peel tissues were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and store at -80°C.

#### **RNA** extraction and quantitative **RT-PCR** (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was isolated from tomato peel tissues using a HiPure Plant RNA Mini Kit (Magen) and cleaned with DNase I (Takara). The DNA-free RNA was used as the template for reverse-transcription PCR. qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR® Premix Ex Taq TM II (Takara, Otsu, Japan) in an ABI7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Gene-specific primers were listed in **Table S1.** The tomato *Actin* gene (Solyc03g078400) was used as the internal control. Relative gene expression values were calculated using the  $2^{-\triangle \triangle Ct}$  method and were normalized using the internal reference gene *Actin*. Three independent biological replicates were used in the analysis. In this study, *Solanum lycopersicum* cultivar Henz1706 was used for qRT-PCR analysis for expression of histone demethylase genes.

#### Subcellular localization

The coding regions of HDMs were subcloned into pSAT6-EYFP vector. The fusion constructs (pSAT-HDM-EYFP) and the control vector (pSAT6-EYFP) were transferred into Arabidopsis mesophyll protoplasts using the polyethylene glycol transfection method. The transformed protoplasts were incubated at 22°C for 24-36 h. EYFP fluorescence in protoplasts was observed and captured by a laser confocal microscope (Leica SP8 STED 3X).

#### Plasmid construction and transgenic plant generation

The SIJMJ6 cDNA was subcloned into a pBI121-GFP vector using In Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Takara). The sequence was verified by further cloning and sequencing. The recombined pBI121-35S-SIJMJ6-GFP vector was transformed into AC using the stable *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*-mediated transformation method (Van Eck *et al.*, 2006). The transgenic tomato lines were selected through kanamycin resistance. Genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves of transgenic tomato plants in both T0 and T1 generations and confirmed by PCR amplification.

#### Fruit ripening characteristics

Fruits of wild-type and *SIJMJ6-OE* lines at 37 dpa and 40 dpa were harvested. Ethylene production rate was determined using a Hewlett-Packard 5890 series gas chromatograph equipped with a 25 m HP-PLOT Q capillary column (Agilent Technologies, USA) and a flame ionization detector. Ethylene concentration were calculated by comparing sample peak areas with ethylene standards of known concentration, and normalized for fruit weight (Chung *et al.*, 2010). The content of carotenoids in tomato was determined by spectrophotometry. Tomato peel tissue (2 g) was ground in liquid nitrogen and extracted in 20 ml of 60:40% (v/v) hexane: acetone three times. The extract was centrifuged at 4,000×g for 5 min. The absorbance of total pooled supernatants was measured at 450 nm with a Phillips PU 8720 scanning spectrophotometer. The total carotenoid concentration was calculated with following equation: total carotenoids mg ml<sup>-1</sup> = (OD<sub>450</sub>)/0.25. Water-soluble pectin and acid-soluble pectin were extracted according to the method of Duan *et al.* (2008). The concentrations of water-soluble pectin and acid-soluble pectin were determined using carbazole method using galacturonic acid as a standard.

#### Histone demethylation assay in vivo and in vitro

The in vivo histone demethylation assay was performed according to the method described by Lu *et al.* (2011) and Chen *et al.* (2013). Briefly, pBI121-35S -SIJMJ6-GFP recombined vector was transformed into *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* cells (strain GV3101) and then transfected the half of a 5-week-old *Nicotiana benthamiana* leaf. The other half of the leaf was not transfected and served as a control. The infiltrated leaves were used to isolate nuclei and be immunostained with anti-GFP (Abcam) at 1:200 dilution and anti-histone methylation-specific antibodies at 1:200 dilution, followed by Alexa Flour 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse (Invitrogen) and Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (Invitrogen) secondary antibodies at 1:200 dilution. The location of nuclei was visualized using 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining. The fluorescent signals were observed and captured under a laser confocal microscope (Leica). Nuclei location,

SIJMJ6-GFP protein and methylated histone were displayed in blue, green and red, respectively. For protein gel blot assay, histones were extracted from the leaves of wild-type and transgenic line at 7-month-old plants as described in the The EpiQuik<sup>TM</sup> Total Histone Extraction Kit (Epigentek). Antibodies used in western blot are list below.

An *in vitro* histone demethylation assay was performed according to the method described by Whetstine *et al.* (2009). Briefly, the full-length of SIJMJ6 was subcloned into the pGEX-4T-3 and then introduced into *Escherichia coli* Rosetta (DE3) for expression with induction by 1 mM IPTG (Sigma) at 16°C for 18 h. The fusion proteins SIJMJ6-GST were affinity-purified with Glutathione Sepharose 4B resin (GE Healthcare). 5 µg of purified proteins were incubated with 5 µg of calf thymus type II-A histones (Sigma) in 50 µl reaction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 50 µM Fe(NH<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>(SO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>6H<sub>2</sub>O, 1 mM  $\alpha$ -ketoglutarate, 2 mM ascorbate) at 37°C for 5h. The reaction system without SIJMJ6-GST was considered as the control. The reactions were inhibited by 10 µM EDTA and then analyzed by western blot with anti-histone methylation-specific antibodies list below.

Antibodies used in this study were as follows: anti-H3K27me3 (07-449, Millipore), anti-H3K27me2 (ab24684, Abcam), anti-H3K27me1 (A2361, ABclonal), anti-H3K36me3 (ab9050, Abcam), anti-H3K36me2 (ab9049, Abcam), anti-H3K36me1 (A2354, ABclonal), anti-H3K9me3 (ab8898, Abcam), anti-H3K9me2 (07-441, Millipore), anti-H3K9me1 (A2358, ABclonal), anti-H3K4me3 (07-473, Millipore), anti-H3K4me2 (07-442, Millipore), anti-H3K4me1 (A2355, ABclonal), anti-H3 (ab1791, Abcam) and anti-GFP (ab290, Abcam).

#### **RNA-seq analysis**

Total RNAs from wild-type and *SIJMJ6-OE* fruits at 37 dpa were extracted with the HiPure Plant RNA Mini Kit (Magen). Three biological replicates were used for each sample. The mRNA was isolated using oligo-dTs coupled with magnetic beads, fragmented into 300 bp and reverse-transcribed with random primers into First-strand cDNA. Then Second-strand cDNA was synthesized using DNA polymerase I and RNase H. The synthesized double cDNA were subjected

to purification, end repair, dA-tailing, ligation to Illumina adapters and agarose gel electrophoresis. The 200 bp fragment were excised, amplified and sequenced on Illumina HiSeq<sup>™</sup> 4000 by Gene Denovo Co. (Guangzhou, China)

For the assembly library, raw reads were filtered to obtain clean data by removing those containing adaptors, reads with > 10% unknown nucleotides and reads with the percentage of Q-value ( $\leq$ 20) base higher than 50%. The clean data were mapped to the tomato reference genome (version SL3.0) using HISAT2 software and reconstructed using StringTie software. Subsequently, fragments were assigned to genes by feature Counts and gene expression abundance was represented by RPKM value. Genes with > 2.5-fold changes with statistical significance (FDR < 0.05) were considered as differentially expressed genes by DESeq2 software identification.

Gene functional classification was carried out by the DAVID functional annotation clustering tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/gene2gene.jsp) based on DEGs, using a threshold of P < 0.05. The functional clusters enrichment analysis was calculated by comparing the whole tomato genome.

#### Chromatin immunoprecipitation

ChIP was performed as described by Gendrel *et al.* (2005) with minor modifications. Briefly, 5 g of peel from wild-type and *SlJMJ6-OE* fruits at 37 dpa were sliced into pieces, cross-linked in 1% formaldehyde and stopped by adding a final concentration of 0.125 M glycine. Subsequently, the fixated sample were rinsed with water, frozen and ground in liquid nitrogen for chromatin isolation. Anti-GFP (Abcam) and anti-H3K27me3 (Millipore) were used for immunoprecipitation into the chromatin. Immunoprecipitated DNA was used for ChIP-seq DNA library preparation or ChIP-qPCR analysis.

#### **ChIP-seq** analysis

A ChIP-seq library was constructed using the NEBNext DNA Library Prep Master Mix Set for Illumina (E6040S; New England BioLabs). ChIP-Seq libraries were sequenced on the Illumina

NovaSeq 6000 platform. Sequence reads were aligned to the *Solanum lycopersicum* genome (version SL3.0) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner's Smith-Waterman Alignment (BWA-SW) (Li & Durbin, 2010). The aligned reads were applied to Model-based Analysis of ChIP-seq (MACS) (Zhang *et al.*, 2008) and identified as enriched regions (peaks) with default parameters. For the annotation of genomic distribution of peaks across gene structure, we divided the genome into five fragment: promoter (the 2 kb region upstream of the transcription start site and the 100 bp region downstream of the TSS), exon, intron, TTS (the 100 bp region upstream of the transcription end site and the 1 kb downstream of the TES) and Intergenic (outside these regions). To identify binding motif, the 200-bp extension of sequencing peaks towards upstream and downstream were extracted and applied to HOMER-findMotifGenome.pl software. Subsequently, the motifs predicted by software were matched to the known motifs in databases for elicitation. Differential methylation of H3K27me3 between wild-type and *SIJMJ6-OE* fruits were detected using the ChIPDiff program with a 2-fold change as described in Lu *et al.* (2011).

#### **ChIP-qPCR** analysis

For ChIP-qPCR, the chromatin of tomato sample was sonicated to an average fragment length of 500 bp fragment. 10% of sonicated chromatin was used as the input control and 90% were immunoprecipitated with equal amounts of antibodies including anti H3K27me3 (07-449, Millipore), anti-GFP (ab290, Abcam) and IgG (12-370, Millipore). The abundance of immunoprecipitated chromatin was determined by RT-qPCR using the primers given in **Table S1** and calculated to the relative enrichment compared with the input. The data are presented as mean  $\pm$  SD of three biological replicates.

#### Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as the mean  $\pm$  SD. Differences among different treatments were compared using SPSS version 7.5 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

The raw and processed RNA-seq and ChIP-seq data have been deposited in the NCBI GEO repository (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) with the accession number GSE 142714.

#### Results

#### Identification and characterization of histone demethylases in tomato

To identify the *HDM* genes in tomato genome, the sequences of *Arabidopsis thaliana* HDMs (https://www.arabidopsis.org/index.jsp) were used as queries to search against the new tomato reference genome (https://solgenomics.net/). As a result, 25 *HDMs* were identified which unevenly distributed in tomato chromosomes (**Table S2**). Phylogenetic analysis displayed that the tomato HDMs are grouped into 2 clades, the LSD1 and JmjC domain-containing (JMJ) HDMs (**Fig. 1**). The LSD1 family comprises 5 members (SILSD1-5), while the JMJ family has 20 members (designated as SIJMJ1 to SIJMJ17, and SIJMJC1 to SIJMJC3) which can be further categorized into 5 subfamilies, including KDM4/JHDM3, KDM5/JAR2D1, JMJD6, KDM3/JHDM2 and JMJC/DOG (**Table S2**). All 10 HDMs in KDM4/JHDM3, JMJD6 and JMJC/DOG subfamilies displayed high similarity to their homologues in Arabidopsis (**Fig. 1**).

We further determined the diversity of the domain architectures of the tomato HDMs. The LSD1-type HDMs are characterized with a peculiar structure domain, harboring a SWIRM domain in the N terminus and an amino oxidase domain in the C terminus, respectively. In contrast, the JMJ-type HDMs possess one of the ZnF\_C2H2 zinc finger, zf-c5hc2, FYRN/FYRC, PHD, F-box, zf-4cxxcr1, RING, WRC and Cupin8 metalloenzyme domains, respectively (Fig. S1). Various combinations of the highly conserved motifs in the HDMs possibly endow them with diverse functions in epigenetic modifications.

We next examined the subcellular localization of the tomato HDMs. The coding regions of the

HDMs fused with yellow florescence protein (YFP) were transiently transformed into Arabidopsis protoplasts. As a result, the subcellular localization of 20 HDMs was successfully determined. For the JMJ-family HDMs, only SIJMJC2 is distributed in the cytoplasm, whereas all the other JMJs are all localized in the nucleus (**Fig. S2**). The LSD1 family members SILSD2, SILSD3 and SILSD4 are all located in the nucleus (**Fig. S2**). The different subcellular localizations suggested that these HDMs may play versatile roles in tomato development.

#### Expression profile of tomato histone demethylase genes during development and ripening

To examine the possible role of tomato HDMs in fruit ripening, we investigated their gene expression patterns during fruit development and ripening stages by RT-qPCR assay. The fruits at various stages of development and ripening including 10 days post anthesis (dpa), 20 dpa, 30 dpa, mature green (MG), break stage (Br), turning stage (Tu), pink stage (Pi) and red ripe stage (RR) were harvested for analysis. Interestingly, the expression of the *LSD1* family genes increases significantly during late ripening stages, especially at the red ripe stage (**Fig. S3**). Furthermore, the *JMJ* genes exhibited differential expression patterns during fruit ripening. The transcription levels of *SIJMJ1*, *SIJMJ2*, *SIJMJ4*, *SIJMJ4*, *SIJMJ6*, *SIJMJ7*, *SIJMJ8*, *SIJMJ10*, *SIJMJ12*, *SIJMJ16*, and *SIJMJ17* were enormously upregulated during the early ripening stage, reached the turning stage, and then decreased during the late ripening stage (**Fig. 2**), implying that these genes might play a role in the regulation of fruit ripening, whereas the expression of *SIJMJ9*, *SIJMJ14*, *SIJMJC3* genes showed no significant changes during fruit ripening (**Fig. 2**).

#### SIJMJ6 promotes tomato fruit ripening

*SIJMJ6* was selected to further investigate the biological function of the HDMs in tomato fruit ripening attributing to its high expression level during fruit ripening (**Fig. 2**). The transgenic plants (*35S:SIJMJ6*, also referred as *SIJMJ6-OE*) overexpressing *SIJMJ6* driven by the 35S promoter

were generated in tomato cultivar *Ailsa Craig* (AC), and 22 independent transgenic lines were obtained. Two stable transgenic lines, *J6-34* and *J6-35*, expressing high levels of *SLJMJ6* gene and SIJMJ6-GFP fusion protein were selected for further analysis (Fig. 3a, b).

The wild-type and transgenic lines were grown in the same conditions in a green house. Phenotypic analysis showed that the fruit ripening stage of the two *SLJMJ6-OE* lines J6-34 and J6-35 was earlier than that of the wild-type (**Fig. 3c**). To verify the role of SlJMJ6 in fruit ripening, we further detected the physiological traits of *SLJMJ-OE* fruits. The ethylene production rate, carotenoid content and soluble pectin level of *SLJMJ6-OE* fruits were significantly higher than those of wild-type at 37 dpa and 40 dpa (**Fig. 3d-g**). Together, these results indicated that SlJMJ6 may play a role in promoting tomato fruit ripening.

#### SIJMJ6 specifically demethylases H3K27me2/3 in tomato

To determine the histone demethylase activity of SIJMJ6 in *vivo*, we transiently expressed SIJMJ6-GFP in tobacco (*Nicotiana benthamiana*) leaves as described (Liu *et al.*, 2019). In SIJMJ6-GPF expressing cells, H3K27me2/3 signals were substantially reduced (**Fig. 4a**), whereas the levels of mono-, di-, or trimethylation at H3K9, H3K4, and H3K36 were not obviously altered (**Fig. S4a**), suggesting that SIJMJ6 might act as a histone demethylase specific catalyzing H3K27me2/3.

To further confirm that SIJMJ6 has H3K27 demethylase activity in tomato, we compared the global H3K27 mono-, di-, and trimethylation levels between the wild-type and the *SlJMJ6-OE* transgenic lines J6-34 and J6-35. Consistently, a decrease of H3K27 di- and tri-methylation but not monomethylation was detected in the two transgenic lines compared with the wild-type (**Fig. 4b, c**).

We also examined the H3K27 demethylase activity of SIJMJ6 *in vitro* as described (Whetstine *et al.*, 2006). As shown in **Fig. 4d, e**, the recombinant SIJMJ6-GST protein reduced the levels of H3K27m2 and H3K27me3, but showed no obvious effect on the level of H3K27me1 *in vitro*. In addition, the methylation levels at H3K9, H3K4, and H3K36 were not obviously affected by

SIJMJ6 (Fig. 4d, e; Fig. S4b, c). Taken together, these results demonstrated that SIJMJ6 specifically demethylases H3K27me2/3 in tomato.

#### SIJMJ6 regulates the expression of ripening-related genes

The early fruit ripening phenotype of *SIJMJ6-OE* transgenic plants prompts us to identify the genes regulated by SIJMJ6. RNA-seq analysis was performed to compare the transcriptomes of the wild-type and the *SIJMJ6-OE* line *J6-34* fruits at 37 dpa. At this stage, the wild-type fruits were at the mature green stage whereas the *SIJMJ6-OE* fruits were at the break stage. Fold change >2.5 and FDR < 0.05 were set as the significant threshold for differentially expressed genes. As a result, 828 genes were upregulated whereas 2028 genes were downregulated in *SIJMJ6-OE* fruits compared with wild-type (**Fig 5a; Data set S1**).

Gene functional classification analysis showed that the upregulated genes in *SLJMJ6-OE* fruits are highly enriched in signal transduction as well as biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (**Fig. 5b**), whereas the downregulated genes are enriched in transmembrane and metabolic pathways (**Fig. 5c**). Moreover, a large number of genes involved in ripening-related transcription regulation, ethylene biosynthesis and signal transduction, cell wall degradation, carotenoid biosynthesis, flavor volatile biosynthesis were significantly upregulated in *SLJMJ6-OE* fruits compared with wild-type (**Table 1**).

The reliability of RNA-seq data was further confirmed by RT-qPCR assays. The expression of 12 fruit ripening-related genes including *ACS2*, *E4*, *FUL1*, *PSY1*, *GGPPS2*, *CHS2*, *BCAT1*, *LoxC*, *SGR1*, *EXP1*, *PG2* and *MAN4* was verified to be upregulated in *SlJMJ6-OE* fruits compared with wild-type (**Fig. 5d**). These results suggested that SlJMJ6 is involved in regulating the transcription of ripening-related genes.

#### Genome-wide identification of SIJMJ6-associated genes during tomato fruit ripening

To further investigate the regulatory roles of SIJMJ6 in fruit ripening, chromatin

immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) was performed to capture the genome-wide direct targets of SIJMJ6. Fruits from *SIJMJ6-OE* transgenic plants that were used for RNA-seq assays were harvested for chromatin extraction. An anti-GFP antibody was used for immunoprecipitation, and an anti-IgG antibody was used as a negative control. 7904, 7608 and 7777 SIJMJ6 binding genes were obtained from three biological replicates, respectively, and 5403 overlapping genes were regarded as the SIJMJ6-binding genes (**Fig. 6a; Data set S2**). Bioinformatics analysis revealed that SIJMJ6 binds to various genomic regions including promoters, intergenic areas, transcriptional starting sites (TTS), exons and introns, of which the DNA-binding sites of SIJMJ6 were predominantly located within the promoters and intergenic regions (**Fig. 6b**).

To gain more insights into the DNA-binding properties of SIJMJ6 in tomato fruits, *de novo* motif prediction was performed using the Multiple Em for Motif Elicitation (MEME) program (Machanick & Bailey, 2011). The results showed that the predominant motif in abundance is TGGGC(C/T) (36.35%), followed by GGCC(T/C) (12.97%), AGGCC(T/C) (8.80%) and GG(G/C)CC (7.88%) (**Fig. 6c**). These motifs are specific binding sites of some types of transcription factors such as Zinc helix-loop-helix and TCP. It is suggested that SIJMJ6 may be recruited to the targets through a subset of specific DNA binding transcription factors. Moreover, integrated analysis of ChIP-seq and RNA-seq displayed that 24.63% (204 of 828) of the genes upregulated in *SIJMJ6-OE* are bound by SIJMJ6 (**Data set S3**), suggesting that SIJMJ6 may directly bind and activate these genes during fruit ripening.

# H3K27me3 demethylation correlates with upregulated expression of key ripening-related genes in *SlJMJ6-OE* tomato fruit

H3K27me3 is generally related to gene repression and it is critically important for plant development (Holec & Berger, 2012; Gan *et al.*, 2015). Our data revealed that SIJMJ6 has H3K27me2/3 demethylase activity (**Fig. 4**). We further investigated whether JMJ6 regulates the expression of the target genes by changing their H3K27me3 levels. We performed a ChIP-seq

using the anti-H3K27me3 antibody to analyze the genome-wide H3K27me3 profile in wild-type and *SIJMJ6-OE* fruits at the same stage as those used in RNA-seq. In the *SIJMJ6-OE* fruit, H3K27me3 levels at 2096 genes were decreased at least 2-fold compared with the wild-type fruit (**Data Set S4**). SIJMJ6-mediated H3K27me3 removal sites were distributed in different regions of annotated genes, including promoters, exons, introns, TTS, and intergenic regions, of which intergenic regions accounted for 81.24% because they constitute a large proportion of the genome (**Fig. 7a**). Meta-gene analysis also showed decreased H3K27me3 levels in *SIJMJ6-OE* fruits, especially at transcription start site (TSS) regions (**Fig. 7b, c**).

Among the 204 SIJMJ6-bound and activated genes, 32 genes exhibited decreased H3K27me3 levels in the *SlJMJ6-OE* fruits compared with the wild-type (**Fig. 8a; Data Set S5**), suggesting that SIJMJ6 may directly promote their expression by removing H3K27me3. Gene functional classification analysis showed that these SlJMJ6-associated and regulated genes are highly enriched in metabolic pathways, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, lyase, ethylene biosynthesis and so on (**Fig. 8b**).

Interestingly, these 32 SIJMJ6-bound and activated genes with decreased H3K27me3 include a large subset of genes functionally related to fruit ripening, such as RIPENING INHIBITOR (*RIN*), ethylene-responsive transcription factor 1 (*DREB2A*), 1-aminocyclopropane 1-carboxylate synthase-4 (*ACS4*), 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate oxidase 1 (*ACO1*), ACC oxidase related (*E8*), pectate lyase (*PL*), beta-galactosidase 4 (*TBG4*), gibberellin 20-oxidase-3 (*GA2OX1*), GDSL esterase/lipase (*AAE*), ECERIFERUM 3 (*CER3*) and alcohol acyl transferase (*AAT1*). These genes are functionally associated with transcriptional regulation, ethylene biosynthesis and signaling, cell wall degradation, lipid degradation and GA biosynthesis and signaling, which are of importance for fruit ripening. Gene browse view of the genes regulated and bound by SIJMJ6 clearly showed that the upregulation of these ripening-related genes in *SLJMJ6-OE* fruits is associated with decreased levels of H3K27me3 (**Fig. 9a-d**). These data were further validated by ChIP-qPCR and RT-qRCR analyses (**Fig. 9a-d**). Taken together, our findings indicated that SIJMJ6 is a positive regulator of fruit ripening and directly activates the transcription of a subset of ripening-related genes by decreasing their H3K27me3 levels.

SIJMJ6 directly activates the expression of DNA demethylase DML2 during fruit ripening

Previous work reported that tomato DML2-mediated DNA demethylation is central to the control of ripening (Liu *et al.*, 2015). Loss of function of *DML2* results in ripening inhibition via hypermethylation and repression of the expression of genes involved in key biochemical processes (Liu *et al.*, 2015; Lang *et al.*, 2017). RNA-seq data displayed an increase of *DML2* transcription in *SlJMJ6-OE* fruits compared with wild-type (**Fig. 10a**), which was further confirmed by RT-qPCR (**Fig. 10b**). ChIP-seq and ChIP-qPCR showed that *DML2* was directly bound by SlJMJ6 (**Fig. 10c**). Moreover, a decrease of histone H3K27me3 level was detected in *SlJMJ6-OE* fruits compared with wild-type (**Fig. 10d**). Collectively, these data suggest that SlJMJ6 directly activates *DML2* expression via removal of histone H3K27me3.

#### Discussion

Histone lysine methylation is an important epigenetic mark (Holec & Berger, 2012), which is dynamically controlled by histone methyltransferases and demethylases (Liu *et al.*, 2010). In Arabidopsis and rice, H3K4 and H3K9 demethylases-mediated modification of histone lysine methylation have been widely reported (Liu *et al.*, 2010; Lu *et al.*, 2011; Crevillen *et al.*, 2014; Gan *et al.*, 2014; Cui *et al.*, 2016; Zheng *et al.*, 2019). Here, we identified a JmjC domain-containing protein, SIJMJ6, which specifically demethylates histone H3K27m2/3 in tomato (**Fig. 4a, b**). H3K27 demethylase are involved in floral transition (Lu *et al.*, 2011; Gan *et al.*, 2014; Yang *et al.*, 2016; Li *et al.*, 2018; Zheng *et al.*, 2019), shoot development (Cheng *et al.*, 2018), leaf senescence (Wang *et al.*, 2019), biotic stress response (Li *et al.*, 2013) in plants. In this work, overexpression of *SIJMJ6* accelerates tomato fruit ripening (**Fig. 3a**), which is related to upregulated expression of a large number of ripening-related genes, involving in transcription regulation, ethylene biosynthesis and signaling, cell wall degradation, carotenoid biosynthesis, flavor volatile biosynthesis (**Fig. 5; Table 1**). Therefore, histone H3K27 demethylase SIJMJ6

plays a positive regulatory role in tomato fruit ripening. However, due to the limitations of overexpression approaches, the other tomato *SLJMJs* that exhibit high expression levels during fruit development may also play roles in regulating ripening-related genes. Further work by constructing *jmj6* mutants via CRISPR-Cas9 to validate its role in regulating fruit ripening is needed.

Chromatin status is of importance for regulating gene transcription (Liu et al., 2010). The JmjC domain-containing histone demethylases play important roles in the removal of methyl groups from methylated histone at lysine sites, thereby modifying chromatin status and regulating gene expression in plants (Liu et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2019). In this study, we found that SIJMJ6 directly removes H3K27me3 at the loci of a large number of ripening-related regulatory or functional genes and activates their transcription (Fig. 9; Fig. S5). RIN is considered to be a master regulator of fruit ripening (Vrebalov et al., 2002; Osorio et al., 2011; Fujisawa et al., 2012), which targets a large number of genes related to ripening processes, such as ethylene synthesis and signaling, and cell wall modification in tomato (Ito et al., 2008; Fujisawa et al., 2011; Martel et al., 2011; Fujisawa et al., 2012; Qin et al., 2012; Fujisawa et al., 2013). ChIP-seq and ChIP-qPCR analysis revealed that SIJMJ6 can directly bind to the gene body of RIN and demethylate H3K27me3 from its chromatin, thereby upregulating expression of *RIN* (Fig. 9a). Ethylene plays a crucial role in climacteric fruit ripening by acting as a signal to initiate ripening-associated processes. ACS4 and ACO1 are responsible for ethylene synthesis during tomato fruit ripening (Alexander & Grierson, 2002). Here, we found that ACS4 and ACO1 are direct targets of SIJMJ6 and SIJMJ6 activates the expression of ACS4 and ACO1 by removing H3K27me3 during fruit ripening. PL (Solyc03g111690) (Uluisik et al., 2016) and β-galactosidase 4 (TBG4) (Eda et al., 2017) play roles in tomato fruit softening. In this study, PL and TBG4 were confirmed to be directly bound by SIJMJ6. Significantly decreased H3K27me3 levels at both TBG4 and PL (Solyc03g111690) loci are in consistent with their upregulated expression in SIJMJ6-OE fruits compared with wild-type. In addition, SIJMJ6 directly associates with the chromatin regions of some other RIN-targeted genes (Fig. S5) related to transcriptional regulation, ethylene and GA biosynthesis and signaling, and lipid degradation (Martel et al., 2011; Fujisawa et al., 2012; Qin et

*al.*, 2012; Fujisawa *et al.*, 2013), and decreases their H3K27me3 levels and activates their expression (**Fig. S5**). Therefore, these results suggest that, in addition to direct regulation on the ripening-related functional genes, SIJMJ6 also may indirectly regulate the expression of ripening-related genes by activating the master regulator *RIN* during tomato fruit ripening (**Fig. 11**).

DNA methylation and H3K27me3 are both key repression marks for gene expression. The cross-talk between DNA methylation and the H3K27me3 histone mark have been reported to be involved in mammal development and carcinogenesis (Bayliss *et al.*, 2016; Zhang *et al.*, 2019; Chen *et al.*, 2019). Recently, it has been shown that the fruit-induced DEMETER-like DNA demethylase gene *DML2* positively regulates fruit ripening in tomato by activating the expression of a subset of key fruit-ripening factors, such as *RIN*, *NOR*, *CNR* via decreasing the level of DNA methylation (Liu *et al.*, 2015; Lang *et al.*, 2017). Here, we showed that the expression of *DML2* is significantly upregulated in *SLJMJ6-OE* fruits compared with wild-type. Moreover, SLJMJ6 directly targets *DML2* and activates its expression via removing H3K27me3 methylation. SLJMJ6-mediated *DML2* activation may facilitate demethylation of fruit ripening regulators and encoding enzymes, such as *RIN* and *ACS4*, and their gene transcription. Our finding uncovered a novel crosstalk of histone modification and DNA methylation in regulating gene expression and plant development (**Fig. 11**). Further genome-wide association analysis of H3K27me3 and DNA methylation patterns will help to elucidate their crosstalk in regulating fruit ripening.

In conclusion, we investigate the function of an H3K27me3 demethylase SIJMJ6 in tomato fruits, which provides new insight into the involvement of epigenetic regulation in fruit ripening. The genome-wide H3K27me3 profiling and transcriptomic analysis indicated that SIJMJ6 contributes to the activation of ripening-related regulators, such as *RIN*, and functional genes, such as *ACS4*, *ACO1*, *PL*, *TBG4* by removing the repressive H3K27me3 mark, therefore promoting fruit ripening in tomato. In addition, crosstalk between SIJMJ6-mediated H3K27me3 modification and DML2-mediated DNA methylation may orchestrates the regulation of tomato fruit ripening (**Fig. 11**). To our knowledge, this is the first report that a histone lysine demethylase is implicated in regulating fruit ripening.

**Fig. 11** A proposed model to explain the role of SIJMJ6 in regulating fruit ripening in tomato. During fruit ripening, SIJMJ6 directly associates with the chromatin regions of the master regulators of fruit ripening such as *RIN*, removes H3K27me3 methylation and activates their expression. The activated expression of *RIN* further upregulates the expression of ripening-related genes, such as *ACS4*, *ACO1*, *PL* and *TBG4*, thereby prompting fruit ripening. SIJMJ6 also positively regulates fruit ripening by directly activating the ripening-related genes via removal of H3K27me3 in the loci of these genes. In addition, SIJMJ6 activates the expression of *DML2*, a DNA demethylase, via removing H3K27me3 in the gene locus, which could result in DNA demethylation and upregulated expression of ripening-related regulators, such as *PSY1*, *ACS4*, *ACO1*, *PL* and *TBG4*. The black arrows indicate what were confirmed in our work while the blue arrows indicate what have been report in previous studies (Ito et al., 2008; Fujisawa et al., 2011; Martel et al., 2011; Fujisawa et al., 2012; Qin et al., 2015; Lang et al., 2017).
