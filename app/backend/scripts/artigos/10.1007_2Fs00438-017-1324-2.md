# **Comparative transcript profling of fertile and sterile fower buds from multiple‑allele‑inherited male sterility in Chinese cabbage (***Brassica campestris* **L. ssp.** *pekinensis***)**

**Xue Zhou1 · Zhiyong Liu1 · Ruiqin Ji1 · Hui Feng1**

Received: 22 September 2016 / Accepted: 4 May 2017 © Springer-Verlag Berlin Heidelberg 2017

**Abstract** We studied the underlying causes of multipleallele-inherited male sterility in Chinese cabbage (*Brassica campestris* L. ssp. *pekinensis*) by identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) related to pollen sterility between fertile and sterile fower buds. In this work, we verifed the stages of sterility microscopically and then performed transcriptome analysis of mRNA isolated from fertile and sterile buds using Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform sequencing. Approximately 80% of ~229 million high-quality paired-end reads were uniquely mapped to the reference genome. In sterile buds, 699 genes were signifcantly upregulated and 4096 genes were down-regulated. Among the DEGs, 28 pollen cell wall-related genes, 54 transcription factor genes, 45 phytohormone-related genes, 20 anther and pollen-related genes, 212 specifcally expressed transcripts, and 417 DEGs located on linkage group A07 were identifed. Six transcription factor genes *BrAMS*, *BrMS1*, *BrbHLH089*, *BrbHLH091*, *BrAtMYB103*, and *BrANAC025* were identifed as putative sterility-related genes. The weak auxin signal that is regulated by *BrABP1* may be one of the key factors causing pollen sterility observed here. Moreover, several signifcantly enriched GO terms such as "cell wall organization or biogenesis" (GO:0071554), "intrinsic to membrane" (GO:0031224), "integral to membrane" (GO:0016021), "hydrolase activity, acting on ester bonds" (GO:0016788), and one signifcantly enriched pathway "starch and sucrose metabolism" (ath00500) were identifed in this work. qRT-PCR, PCR, and in situ hybridization experiments validated our RNA-seq transcriptome analysis as accurate and reliable. This study will lay the foundation for elucidating the molecular mechanism(s) that underly sterility and provide valuable information for studying multiple-allele-inherited male sterility in the Chinese cabbage line 'AB01'.

**Keywords** *Brassica campestris* L. ssp. *pekinensis* · Male sterility · Flower buds · RNA-seq · DEGs

# **Introduction**

Chinese cabbage is a typically allogamous plant with bisexual fowers and signifcant heterosis. Male sterility is widely and commonly used in many crop plant species, because it eliminates the need for hand emasculation in the production of hybrid seeds (Bino [1985\)](#page-21-0). The application of male sterile lines is an economical method to improve cross-breeding in Chinese cabbage, which can generate stronger plants that develop more rapidly and are higher yielding (Lippman and Zamir [2007](#page-21-1)). There are two types of male sterility in Chinese cabbage: genic male sterility (GMS) and cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS) (Vandermeer [1987](#page-22-0)). GMS has some disadvantages over CMS, one of which is the diffculty and time required to transfer the male sterility trait to other lines; however, compared with CMS, GMS has many advantages, such as stability and complete sterility, no negative cytoplasmic effects, and extensive distribution of fertility restorer genes.

The ideal percentage of male sterile plants should be 100% in male sterile lines. Our lab obtained four genetically stable lines consisting of 100% male sterile plants by crossing male sterile plants with male fertile plants between AB genome lines, and proposed a genetic hypothesis of genic-multiple-allele male sterility (Feng et al. [1995,](#page-21-2) [1996](#page-21-3)). The model indicated that male sterility was controlled by three alleles at a single locus: the "*Ms<sup>f</sup>* " allele for fertility restoration, the "*Ms*" allele for male sterility, and the "*ms*" allele for male fertility. Among these three alleles, the dominant:recessive relationship was *Ms<sup>f</sup>* > *Ms* > *ms*. Based on this model, a temporary maintainer line was used in a cross with the homozygous sterile line to obtain a male sterile line with 100% male sterile plants (Fig. S1). Following the genetic hypothesis, the male sterile allele has been transferred into different ecotypes of Chinese cabbage (Feng et al. [2007;](#page-21-4) Wang et al. [2005\)](#page-22-1). Using these male sterile materials, several linked molecular markers have successfully been identifed (Feng et al. [2009](#page-21-5); Liu et al. [2010](#page-21-6)). Based on genetic linkage analysis, the *Ms<sup>f</sup>* gene used in our study is located on linkage group A07 (Wang et al. [2010](#page-22-2)). However, the molecular mechanism underlying multipleallele-inherited male sterility in Chinese cabbage remains to be determined.

Understanding the basic processes of anther and pollen development is critical for sexual reproduction and selective breeding. The processes of anther cell division and differentiation, as well as pollen cell wall development to anther dehiscence, involve a diverse range of gene interactions (Goldberg et al. [1993;](#page-21-7) Ma [2005;](#page-22-3) McCormick [2004](#page-22-4); Scott et al. [2004;](#page-22-5) Wilson and Zhang [2009](#page-22-6)). Recently, a signifcant progress has been made from the use of male sterile mutants in the model plant *A*. *thaliana*. The *SPO-ROCYTELESS* (*SPL*)/*NOZZLE* (*NZZ*) gene is required for the early stage cell division and differentiation in the anther (Liu et al. [2009](#page-21-8); Schiefthaler et al. [1999\)](#page-22-7). Early tapetal initiation is controlled by downstream genes, including *EXTRA SPOROGENOUS CELLS* (*EXS*)/*EXCESSMICRO SPOROCYTES1* (*EMS1*) (Canales et al. [2002;](#page-21-9) Zhao et al. [2002](#page-23-0)) and *TAPETUM DETERMINANT 1* (*TPD1*) (Yang et al. [2003](#page-22-8)). *SOMATIC EMBYOGENESIS RECEPTOR*-*LIKE KINASE1* (*SERK1*) and *SERK2* also have redundant functions early in tapetum formation (Albrecht et al. [2005](#page-20-0); Colcombet et al. [2005](#page-21-10)). Several transcription factor genes that affect the development of the tapetum have been identifed. These include: *DYSFUNCTIONAL TAPETUM1* (*DYT1*), *TAPETAL DEVELOPMENT AND FUNCTION 1* (*TDF1*), *ABORTED MICROSPORES* (*AMS*), *MALE STE-RILITY 1* (*MS1*), and *MYB103/80* (Sorensen et al. [2003](#page-22-9); Yang et al. [2007](#page-22-10); Zhang et al. [2006](#page-22-11), [2007](#page-23-1); Zhu et al. [2008](#page-23-2)). Among these genes, *DYTI* and *TDF1* play important roles in the early tapetal development and pollen wall formation, respectively. *AMS*, *MS1*, and *MYB103*/*80* are essential for post-meiotic tapetal function and pollen development. In addition to these genes, *MALE STERILITY2* (*MS2*), *FACE-LESS POLLEN*-*1* (*DEX1*), *DEFECTIVE IN EXINE PAT-TERN FORMATION* (*FLP1*), and *NO EXINE FORMA-TION* (*NEF1*) are also involved in the later stages of pollen development. Thus, a better understanding of the processes involved in anther and pollen development may provide clues to the molecular mechanisms that control GMS in Chinese cabbage.

In addition, phytohormones are also known to have important functions in tapetel and pollen development in fowering plants. The previous studies have shown that auxin, as the frst plant hormone identifed, plays a critical role in pollen development (Cecchetti et al. [2008\)](#page-21-11). Gibberellin appears to be necessary for normal tapetal development, and possibly infuences the availability of carbohydrates to the tapetum (Plackett et al. [2011](#page-22-12)). Abscisic acid regulates the separation of tapetum cells from microspores during the early anther development in *Brassica napus* (Zhu et al. [2010\)](#page-23-3). Moreover, abscisic acid insensitivity also can lead to male sterility in *Arabidopsis* plants (Brocard-Gifford et al. [2004](#page-21-12)). Ethylene appears to have a role in the development of the tapetum (Hirano et al. [2008](#page-21-13)), and tapetum-specifc expression of a mutated ethylene receptor gene is a potential strategy for inducing male sterility (Takada et al. [2006\)](#page-22-13). Nevertheless, the effects of phytohormones on male sterility in the Chinese cabbage line 'AB01' have not been reported.

With the rapid development and drastic cost reduction of next-generation high-throughput DNA sequencing, sequence-based assays of transcriptomes, namely RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), have become a comprehensive and accurate tool for determining patters of gene expression. In recent years, RNA-seq technology has been applied to many horticultural plants for various purposes, including *A*. *thaliana* (Chen et al. [2010;](#page-21-14) Torti et al. [2012\)](#page-22-14), cucumber (Zhang et al. [2014\)](#page-23-4), *Zea mays* (Lu et al. [2013\)](#page-21-15), *Brassica rapa* (Tong et al. [2013\)](#page-22-15), and even for fertility studies in *B. napus* (An et al. [2014;](#page-20-1) Yan et al. [2013\)](#page-22-16). However, to our knowledge, the genome-wide transcriptional profles of fertile and sterile fower buds of the genic male sterile Chinese cabbage line 'AB01' by RNA-seq technology have not yet been reported.

In this study, RNA extracted from fertile and sterile fower buds of 'AB01', a GMS line of *Brassica campestris* L. ssp. *pekinensis*, was sequenced using an Illumina high-throughput DNA sequencing instrument. The purpose of this work was to identify the differences in the transcription profles between fertile and sterile fower buds, and to identify the key DEGs involved and their related functions. These results will lay the foundation for elucidating the molecular mechanism(s) underlying sterility, and make a meaningful contribution to the breeding of multiple-allele-inherited male sterile cultivars in Chinese cabbage.

# **Materials and methods**

#### **Plant material**

Fertile and sterile fower buds of the GMS Chinese cabbage line 'AB01' were used as the study materials. The GMS AB line produces a 1:1 ratio of sterile (AB01-1, *MsMs*) to fertile (AB01-2, *Msf Ms*) progeny plants, and it is considered to be a stable system. Fertile and sterile plants were cultivated in the same experimental plot at Shenyang Agriculture University (Shenyang, Liaoning Province, China). During fowering, different sizes of fertile and sterile fower buds were chosen for paraffn embedding and sectioning, and RNA in situ hybridization. Young leaves of fertile and sterile plants were sampled for DNA extraction. Fertile fower buds between 2.00 and 3.50 mm in length and sterile fower buds between 1.50 and 3.00 mm were selected from three different plants for transcriptome profling; these sizes represent the important period for pollen sterility in the male sterile plants (Figs. [2b](#page-4-0), [3](#page-5-0)f–h, f1–h1). The samples were then combined, wrapped in foil, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for future study. Each treatment had three biological replicates.

### **Paraffn sectioning and RNA in situ hybridization**

The different development stages of fertile and sterile fower buds were fxed for >24 h in FAA fxatives, and dehydrated through an ethanol series to 50% for long-term storage. The fxed tissues were then continuously dehydrated and cleared, respectively, using ethanol and xylene in volume ratios of 3:1, 1:1, and 1:3. After the buds were in paraffn, they were removed from the mold and embedded in 60 °C paraffn wax solution which was allowed to solidify at room temperature. Paraffn sections (10 μm) were obtained using an automatic microtome (YD-335, Jinhua, China). The sections were stained with safranin and fast green, and observed with a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Images of the anthers at different stages were captured with a Nikon DS-Ri1 camera (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Sequence-specifc oligonucleotide primers, which were designed by Primer Premier software (version 5.0) (Supplementary Table S1), were used to amplify the probe fragments by PCR. Probes were labeled in vitro with digoxigenin-UTP using the SP6 or T7 RNA polymerase transcription kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). In situ pre-hybridization, hybridization, antibody conjugates, and detection were performed as previously described (Leblanc-Fournier et al. [2008\)](#page-21-16).

#### **RNA preparation**

Total RNA was isolated from fertile and sterile fower buds using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA quality and DNA contamination were monitored by electrophoresis on 1% agarose gels (Fig. S2). A Nano-Photometer spectrophotometer (Implen, Westlake Village, CA, USA) was used to check RNA purity. RNA concentration was measured using the Qubit RNA Assay Kit and a Qubit 2.0 Flurometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Finally, RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clare, CA, USA) (Fig. S3).

#### **cDNA library construction and RNA‑seq**

For each sample, a total of 3 μg RNA were used for cDNA library preparation. Sequencing libraries were generated using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's recommendations. Two index codes were added to attribute sequence to each sample. Briefy, oligo-dT paramagnetic beads were used to purify mRNA from total RNA. Fragmentation was carried out using divalent cations at elevated temperature in an Illumina proprietary fragmentation buffer. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random oligonucleotides and RNase H−. Second-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. An end-repair step was performed to convert overhangs into blunt ends. After adenylation of the 3′ ends of the cDNA fragments, Illumina PE adapters were added to the ends by ligation. To obtain cDNA fragments of 150–200 bp in length, the library fragments were purifed with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, CA, USA). DNA fragments with adaptor molecules ligated on both ends were selectively enriched using the Illumina PCR Primer Cocktail in a 10-cycle amplifcation reaction. Finally, the PCR products were purifed using the AMPure XP system and the library quality was assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. The library preparations were then sequenced on an Illumina Hiseq 2000 platform and 100-bp paired-end reads were generated. The raw RNA-seq data have been deposited at GEO [\(https://](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/\)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under accession number GSE95242 ([https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE95242) cgi?acc=[GSE95242\)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE95242).

#### **Quantifcation of gene expression levels**

The Reads Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped reads (RPKM) considers the infuence of sequencing depth and gene length for the read count, and is the most commonly used method for estimating gene expression levels (Mortazavi et al. 2008). HTSeq v0.5.4p3 was used to count the numbers of reads that mapped to each gene (Anders 2010). The RPKM for each gene was then calculated based on the length of the gene and the number of reads that mapped to the gene.

#### Differential gene expression analysis

The DESeq R package (1.10.1) was used to analyze differential gene expression of two biological replicates per condition, which provided a statistical basis for determining differential expression from the digital gene expression data (Anders and Huber 2012). The resulting P values were adjusted using the approach of Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) for controlling the false discovery fate. Genes found to have an adjusted *p* value <0.05 by DESeq were considered to be differentially expressed.

#### GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of the DEGs

Gene Ontology (GO), enrichment analysis of DEGs, was implemented with the GOseq R package (Young et al. 2010), in which the gene length bias was corrected. GO terms were significantly enriched in DEGs with corrected p values <0.05. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is a database resource for describing high-level functions and utilities of a biological system, such as the cell, the ecosystem, and the organism, from molecular-level information, especially large-scale molecular data sets generated by genome sequencing and other experimental technologies (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). In this study, KOBAS software (Mao et al. 2005) was used to test for statistical enrichment of differentially expression genes in KEGG pathways.

#### Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) validation

Twenty DEGs were selected for validation of the RNA-seq data using qRT-PCR. Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to synthesize the first-strand cDNA. The primer pairs for the selected genes were designed with Primer 5.0 software (Premier Biosoft, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and synthesized by Sangon. Primer sequences are given in Supplementary Table S2. qRT-PCR assays were performed in triplicate with Super-Real PreMix Plus (SYBR Green) on a Bio-rad iCycler IQ (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), using the following program: 95 °C for 15 min; followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; with a final extension step of 72 °C for 30 min. Relative gene expression was calculated using the  $2^{-\Delta\Delta C_t}$  method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001), and analyzed using Bio-Rad IQ5 software.

#### DNA extraction and polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Genomic DNA was extracted from individual young leaves from fertile and sterile plants using DNAsecure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). Genomic DNA concentration was detected using agarose gels and adjusted to 50 ng/ μL. PCR primers were designed to amplify the full length of the gene Bra002004 and its promoter sequence. PCR primer sequences were listed in Supplementary Table S3. PCR was performed using a 10-µL reaction volume containing 6.1 µL of ddH<sub>2</sub>O, 1 µL of 10× LA PCR Buffer II (Mg<sup>2</sup>+plus), 0.8  $\mu$ L of 2.5-mM dNTP Mixture, 0.1  $\mu$ L of TaKaRa LA Taq, 1 µL of 0.5-µM forward and reverse primers, and 1 µL of template DNA. PCR assays were performed on a Bio-Rad iCycler thermal cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), using the following program: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 2 min, and a final incubation step of 72 °C for 5 min and 4 °C thereafter. The PCR products were examined on 2% agarose gels and detected using ethidium bromide staining. The PCR products were purified using a Gel Extraction Kit (CWBIO, Beijing, China). Subsequently, the purified PCR products were introduced into pGEM@-T Easy Vector (Promega, Madision, WI, USA) and transformed into TOP10 competent cell (CWBIO, Beijing, China). The recombinant plasmids were sequenced by GeneWize Life Sciences (GeneWize, Tianjin, China). The sequences were aligned using the DNAMAN software.

# Phenotypic characterization of fertile and sterile flower buds

Sterile flowers from multiple-allele-inherited male sterile Chinese cabbage plants are readily identifiable, because they are smaller than the fertile flowers. In addition, the sterile buds are not as full as the fertile buds (Fig. 1). During early development of the flower buds, the pistil appears normal, but the sterile stamens begin to develop abnormally when the bud size reaches 1.50 mm; from 1.50 to 3.00 mm, the sterile anthers degrade gradually and produce no pollen (Fig. 2b); there were no pollen grains observed when compared with the fertile flower buds (Fig. 2).

To accurately determine the period when male sterility occurred, paraffin sections of fertile and sterile anthers were observed at different stages of development. As shown in Fig. 3a, e, a1, e1, there were no obvious differences in anther cellular morphology of the epidermis, endothecium, middle layer, tapetum, and microsporocytes between fertile and sterile anthers. #### **Analysis of gene expression levels**

To obtain statistical evidence of the difference in gene expression between fertile and sterile buds, reads per kilobase per million reads (RPKM) was used to normalize the gene expression levels. The relative expression level of a gene is considered to be directly represented by the transcript abundance level, and when the transcript abundance is high, this means that the relative gene expression level is also high. We used all of the uniquely mapped reads to calculate the RPKM value for each gene (Supplementary Table S4). As shown in Table [2](#page-6-1), the most abundant RPKM interval was 0–1 (average 14,258, 32.89% of the total genes in fertile buds; and 15,330, 35.36% of total genes in sterile buds), followed by 3–15 (average 9201, 21.26% of the total genes in fertile buds; and 8236, 19.00% of genes in sterile buds), and 15–60 (average 11,211, 25.86% of genes in fertile buds; and 11,490, 26.50% of genes in sterile buds).

#### **Analysis of DEGs located on linkage group A07**

Based on the genetic linkage analysis, the *Ms<sup>f</sup>* gene was located on linkage group A07 (Wang et al. [2010\)](#page-22-2). Therefore, the DEGs at this locus may play an important role in the molecular mechanism(s) underlying sterility in Chinese cabbage. We obtained a total of 417 DEGs (including 50 novel genes) were located on linkage group A07, including 353 (including 55 fertile bud-specifc genes) were found to be down-regulated and 64 (including 5 sterile bud-specifc genes) were up-regulated in sterile buds (Supplementary Table S8). 168 DEGs from above were further classifed into 15 functional categories, such as hydrolase activity, transferase activity, ion binding, oxidoreductase activity, heterocyclic compound binding, and carbohydrate derivative binding, etc. In addition, 109 DEGs had no functional categories, and the other 140 (including 38 novel genes) encode previously unknown proteins. Moreover, we obtained a total of 42 DEGs that may be related to anther and pollen development in Chinese cabbage, including 26 transcription factor genes, 6 pollen cell wall-related genes, and 10 protein kinaserelated genes (Table [4\)](#page-8-0). These results may lay the foundation for future study of multiple-allele-inherited male sterile cultivars in Chinese cabbage.

#### **Analysis of pollen cell wall‑related genes**

The formation and modifcation of the pollen cell wall is extremely important for normal pollen development. Both layers of the pollen cell wall are important for pollen activation, hydration, germination, and pollen tube growth (Owen and Makaroff [1995\)](#page-22-21). Thus, a large number of genes, representing many gene families, are considered to be involved in cell wall synthesis and regulation; these include genes in the methyltransferase, pectinesterase, polygalacturonase, pectate lyase, glycosyl hydrolase, and fructosidase families (Huang et al. [2009a\)](#page-21-19). Based on gene annotations in *A*. *thaliana*, we obtained 86 DEGs that are associated with pollen cell wall formation and modifcation (Supplementary Table S9). Among these DEGs, 28 are newly discovered and 58 overlap with the results of Dong et al. ([2013\)](#page-21-20). Within the group of 28 newly discovered DEGs, we found nine pectinesterase genes, 13 polygalacturonase genes, one pectate lyase genes, one glycosyl hydrolase, and four beta-galactosidase genes, and most of which were highly and specifcally expressed in fertile buds (Table [5](#page-9-0)). In addition, the pollen cell wall-related genes might participate in the processes leading to GMS in Chinese cabbage.

# **Analysis of differentially expressed phytohormone genes**

In plants, hormones function to coordinate growth and development at the supracellular level. The importance of hormones in tapetum and pollen development has been demonstrated previously, and the proper phytohormone contents and signals are required for pollen development in different kinds of plants (Sawhney and Shukla [1994](#page-22-22)). In our study, some DEGs related to phytohormone synthesis and signaling were identifed (Table [7\)](#page-12-0). We found a total of ten auxin-related genes that were down-regulated in sterile buds, including four Aux/IAA-related genes, fve auxin-responsive protein genes, and one auxin-binding protein gene. We found fve gibberellin-related DEGs that were down-regulated, such as *gibberellin 20*-*oxidase 2* (*GA20OX2*) and *GA20OX3*, and the other two were upregulated in sterile buds. In addition, 13 ethylene-related DEGs were identifed, seven of which were down-regulated, whereas the other six genes were up-regulated in sterile buds. Fifteen abscisic acid-related DEGs were highly and specifcally expressed in sterile buds; however, the other two genes showed an inverse pattern of expression. Accordingly, this group of DEGs that were analyzed may provide new ways to study the molecular mechanism(s) that determine sterility in Chinese cabbage.

To facilitate the global analysis of gene expression, the genes that showed signifcant differential expression were assigned to different functional categories through the GOseq R package, and the annotations were integrated manually using GO classifcation ([http://www.geneon](http://www.geneontology.org)[tology.org\)](http://www.geneontology.org). In our study, 4795 DEGs from the two samples were further classifed into 65 functional subcategories (corrected *p* values <0.05) in the GO classifcation. Of these, 27, 26, and 12 GO terms were signifcantly enriched in the biological process, molecular function, and cellular component categories, respectively (Supplementary Table S11). The most signifcantly enriched GO categories (30 GO terms) and the detailed comments on the up- and down-regulated DEGs are shown in Figs. [5](#page-14-0) and [6.](#page-15-0) Under biological process, "cell wall organization or biogenesis" (GO:0071554) was the most signifcantly enriched category; for the cellular component ontology, a large proportion of DEGs were related to the "intrinsic to membrane" (GO:0031224) and "integral to membrane" (GO:0016021) categories. In the molecular function ontology, "hydrolase activity, acting on ester bonds" (GO:0016788) was signifcantly enriched. The DEGs in the different functional categories may provide a valuable resource for the study of genic male sterility in Chinese cabbage.

# **Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway mapping**

To identify metabolic pathways in which the DEGs are involved and enriched, pathway-based analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database [\(http://www.](http://www.genome.ad.jp/kegg/) [genome.ad.jp/kegg/](http://www.genome.ad.jp/kegg/)), and the DEGs were classifed using Marioni's method (Mao et al. # **Discussion**

In fowering plants, the stamen is the male reproductive organ, and it plays a signifcant role in plant breeding. The major events of pollen development have been classifed into 14 stages, and changes in any of these may result in male sterility (Sanders et al. [1999](#page-22-23)). Based on the aborted stages in pollen development, plant male sterility can be classifed into several types such as (1) degradation of the pollen mother cells, (2) early or delayed degeneration of tapetal cells, (3) critical chemical changes in pollen wall development, and (4) failure of the anther to dehisce (Li et al. [2006;](#page-21-21) Nonomura et al. [2004;](#page-22-24) Shi et al. [2011](#page-22-25), [2012](#page-22-26); Steiner-Lange et al. [2003](#page-22-27);

In this study, the morphologies of fertile and sterile fower buds in Chinese cabbage were compared, and we found that there were few differences between them other than in the stamen (Fig. [2\)](#page-4-0). We speculate that abnormal development of both the MMCs and the tapetum could be the important factors that determine pollen sterility in Chinese cabbage line 'AB01'.

In our RNA-seq analysis, 229 million high-quality paired-ends reads were obtained, and 18,368 annotated transcripts were identifed. After removing some DEGs with lower fold-change, we obtained 699 up-regulated and 4096 down-regulated genes with an absolute value of log2 (fold-change) ≥2 in sterile buds. As in our previous study, (Dong et al. [2013](#page-21-20)), we identifed three classes of DEGs such as those encoding cell wall-related enzymes, major transcription factor family genes, and putative genes related to pollen development; both overlapping and newly discovered DEGs were very important to the study of GMS in Chinese cabbage line 'AB01'. Moreover, we also analyzed several classes of DEGs including a large number of SETs, different types of phytohormone-related genes, and carbohydrate nutrient metabolism genes that had not been discussed in the previous study. In addition, the DEGs located on linkage group A07, which may be related to anther and pollen development, were analyzed as well. All of these analyses identifed important factors that might cause male sterility in Chinese cabbage line 'AB01'. These results indicate that although fower bud development is a complicated process that involves many genes, changes in the expression of a small fraction of these genes could transform a given trait and produce male sterility.

Normal development of the pollen cell wall is crucial for the formation of mature pollen grains. Pectin methylesterases (PMEs) constitute a large gene family in *Arabidopsis*; PMEs are involved in cell wall loosening, and have been implicated in various aspects of pollen formation and pollen tube elongation (Liu et al. [2011;](#page-21-22) Tian et al. [2006](#page-22-29)). The *A. thaliana QUARTET I* (*QRT1*) gene encodes a PME that regulates the separation of pollen tetrads (Francis et al. [2006](#page-21-23)). *VANGUARD 1* (*VGD1*) also encodes a PEM-homologous protein of 595 amino acids that can enhance the growth of pollen tubes (Jiang et al. [2005\)](#page-21-24). Pectin lyases (PLs) are enzymes that are very important for pollen tube emergence, growth, and degradation the cell walls in stylar tissues to facilitate pollen tube penetration (Wu et al. [1996\)](#page-22-30). Forty-six PL genes are present in the *Brassica* genome, and eleven of them are pollen-specifc (Jiang et al. [2013\)](#page-21-25). In addition, some pollen-related polygalacturonases (PGs), including *QRT3, ARABIDOPSIS DEHISCENCE ZONE POLYGALAC-TURONASE1* (*ADPG1*), and *ADPG2,* play specifc roles in tetrad separation, pollen maturation, and pollen tube growth in *A*. *thaliana* (Ogawa et al. [2009\)](#page-22-31). Importantly, three pollen-specifc PG genes, *Brassica campestris Male Fertility 2* (*BcMF2*), *BcMF6,* and *BcMF9,* are identifed with pollen wall development in Chinese cabbage (Huang et al. [2009a](#page-21-19), [b;](#page-21-26) Zhang et al. [2008\)](#page-23-7). In our study, 28 DEGs involved in cell wall synthesis and regulation were newly discovered (Table [5\)](#page-9-0), most of which are highly and specifcally expressed in fertile buds. These genes may be very important in the study of anther and pollen development in Chinese cabbage.

Transcription factors can regulate tissue-specifc gene expression, which is another key factor in the study of pollen development. Based on the previous research on male sterile mutants in *A*. *thaliana*, *AMS* is a basic helixloop-helix (*bHLH*) protein that is required for tapetal and microspore development. Abortion of the microspores in the absence of the *AMS* gene product implicates a role for this gene as an activator in the control of pollen mitosis. Moreover, the *AMS* gene is recessive in nature, and, when mutated, results in tapetal degeneration and microspore abortion, as well as a reduction in flament elongation (Sorensen et al. [2003\)](#page-22-9). Here, *Bra002004* (*BrAMS*) is highly homologous to the *AMS* gene (*AT2G16910*) from *Arabidopsis,* and it was signifcantly highly expressed in fertile buds. Moreover, we found that promoter region difference may cause the gene *Bra002004*'s differential expression at the transcription level. To verify the accuracy of our work, we selected this gene that maps to linkage group A07 for an in situ experiment. The results showed that specifc hybridization signals were detected in the tapetum, tetrad, and pollen in fertile buds. Because *AMS* plays a crucial role in tapetal development and the transcriptional regulation of microspore development within the developing anther (Sorensen et al. [2003\)](#page-22-9), *Bra002004* may be one of the causes of pollen sterility in the GMS line 'AB01' used in this work.

In addition, *MS1* encodes a Plant Homeodomain (PHD) transcription factor that shows tightly regulated expression in the tapetum as the callose breaks down, up until the free microspore stage (Yang et al. [2007](#page-22-10)). *MS1* is a critical sporophytic controlling factor for anther and pollen development, and also plays a key role in regulating late tapetal gene expression and pollen wall deposition (Ito et al. [2007](#page-21-27)). The *ms1* mutant also does not produce viable pollen (Ito and Shinozaki [2002](#page-21-28)). In our study, *Bra002401* (*BrMS1*) is highly homologous to *AT5G22260* (*MS1*) and also shows down-regulation in sterile buds, and it may be an important gene for the further study of processes that control pollen sterility in Chinese cabbage 'AB01'.

In addition to *AMS* and *MSI*, there are some other transcription factors associated with tapetum and pollen development. Transcription factor genes *bHLH089* and *bHLH091* are highly expressed in the tapetum of the *Arabidopsis* anther, and single mutants at each of the two genes may cause abnormal tapetum morphology, delay callose degeneration, and abort pollen development (Zhu et al. [2015\)](#page-23-8). The transcription factor *AtMYB103* is required for anther development by regulating tapetum development, callose dissolution, and exine formation in *A*. *thaliana* (Zhang et al. [2007\)](#page-23-1). At the same time, *ANAC025* may also be associated with specifying tapetum and pollen wall development (Xu et al. [2014\)](#page-22-32). In our study, *BrbHLH089* (*Bra015480*), *BrbHLH091* (*Bra018269*), *BrAtMYB103* (*Bra027668*), and *BrANAC025* (*Bra027101*) are the homologs of the four *Arabidopsis* transcription factor genes in Chinese cabbage, and all of them show down-regulated expression in sterile buds, indicating that these transcription factors may be involved in pollen sterility in Chinese cabbage.

Many phytohormones, including auxins, gibberellins, ethylene, and abscisic acid, may affect plant male fertility, and some signaling or phytohormone biosynthetic mutants often lead to male sterility (Cecchetti et al. [2008](#page-21-11); Kieber et al. [1993](#page-21-29); Singh et al. [2002;](#page-22-33) Zhu et al. [2010](#page-23-3)). Numerous studies have shown the critical roles of auxin in plant pollen development (Cecchetti et al. [2008;](#page-21-11) Feng et al. [2006](#page-21-30)). The early auxin-responsive genes have been broadly grouped into three major classes: the auxin/indoleacetic acid (Aux/IAA), GH3, and small auxin-up RNA (SAUR) gene families (Guilfoyle [1999\)](#page-21-31). Both Aux/IAA and auxin response factors are the key proteins in auxin signaling pathways, and have essential roles in foral development (Benjamins et al. [2001;](#page-21-32) Liscum and Reed [2002\)](#page-21-33). In this work, *BrABP1* (*Bra000850*) was found to be down-regulated in sterile buds, indicating that the auxin signal level was weak. At the same time, four Aux/IAA-related genes and fve auxin responsive protein genes were shown to be down-regulated as well. A previous study has shown that auxin mainly accumulates after the tetrad period (Feng et al. [2006\)](#page-21-30). Hence, we speculate that the weak auxin signal may affect normal pollen development.

Because of cross-talk between the phytohormone pathways, a weak auxin signal can also affect the synthesis and reaction pathways of other hormones. For example, *gibberellin 20*-*oxidase* (*GA 20*-*oxidase*) is a potential control point in the regulation of GA biosynthesis. In this study, both *BrGA20OX2* (*Bra028706*) and *BrGA20OX3* (*Bra022565*) were down-regulated in sterile buds. In addition, many ethylene- and abscisic acid-related genes that showed either up- or down-regulation were identifed also, and these may interfere with hormone reaction pathways in plants. To sum up, a weak auxin signal may be one of the key factors in the occurrence of male sterility in Chinese cabbage.

Carbohydrates are not only important for sustaining anther and pollen development, but also act as signals infuencing that development (Clement et al. [1996](#page-21-34)). Pollen grains absorb soluble carbohydrates that are conveyed by the tapetum by crossing the tapetal cytoplasm to reach the locular fuid and microspores (Pacini [1996\)](#page-22-34). Accordingly, carbohydrate metabolism abnormalities can often affect male sterility (Clément and Audran [1999](#page-21-35); Dorion et al. [1996\)](#page-21-36). It has been reported that perturbed carbohydrate metabolism can cause unusual starch storage in the endothecium and also contribute to other symptoms such as tapetal hypertrophy and poor microspore wall formation (Mamun et al. [2006](#page-22-35)). Starch and sucrose are important components of carbohydrates; in this study, starch and sucrose metabolism (ath00500) was signifcantly enriched, and 48 DEGs involved in the pathway were obtained, including two that were up-regulated and 46 that were down-regulated in sterile buds. Abnormal starch and sucrose metabolism may affect pollen development, and the impact of this pathway on male sterility in Chinese cabbage warrants further investigation.

In conclusion, we obtained 28 pollen cell wall-related genes, 54 differentially expressed transcription factor genes, 45 phytohormone-related genes, and 20 anther- and pollen-related genes as potential candidates for the male sterile phenotype. We also analyzed the functional classifcations of 212 SETs and 417 DEGs that map to linkage group A07. Among these genes, *BrAMS*, *BrMS1*, *Brb-HLH089*, *BrbHLH091*, *BrAtMYB103*, and *BrANAC025* were identifed as putative sterility-related genes. The weak auxin signal that is regulated by *BrABP1* could be one of the key factors responsible for pollen sterility here. These data will generate a useful resource for studying the molecular genetic mechanism(s) underlying multipleallele-inherited male sterility in the Chinese cabbage line 'AB01'.
