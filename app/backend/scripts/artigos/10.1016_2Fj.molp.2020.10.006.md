Running title: AtPRMT3 regulates pre-ribosome assembly to repress nucleolar stress

**Short summary**: AtPRMT3 cooperates with the ribosomal protein RPS2B and facilitates the dynamic assembly of the 90S/SSU processome during ribosome biogenesis, thereby repressing nucleolar stress in plant growth and development.

#### Abstract

Ribosome biogenesis, taking place mainly in the nucleolus, involves coordinated expression of pre-ribosomal RNAs (pre-rRNAs) and ribosomal proteins, pre-rRNA processing, and subunit assembly with the aid of numerous assembly factors. Our previous study showed that the Arabidopsis thaliana protein arginine methyltransferase AtPRMT3 regulates pre-rRNA processing; however, the molecular mechanism remains unknown. Here we report that AtPRMT3 interacts with Ribosomal Protein S2 (RPS2) facilitating the 90S/Small Subunit (SSU) processome processing and repressing nucleolar stress. We isolated an intragenic suppressor of atprmt3-2 bearing the entire N-terminus but lacking an intact enzymatic activity domain of AtPRMT3, which rescued the developmental defects of atprmt3-2. RPS2 was further identified as an interacting partner of AtPRMT3, and loss-of-function rps2a2b mutants were reminiscent of atprmt3 phenotypically, including pleiotropic developmental defects and aberrant pre-rRNA processing. RPS2B bound directly to pre-rRNAs in the nucleus, and such binding was enhanced in *atprmt3-2*. Consistently, multiple components of 90S/SSU processome were more enriched by RPS2B in atprmt3-2, which causes early pre-rRNA processing defects and nucleolar stress. Our study uncovered a novel mechanism by which AtPRMT3 cooperates with RPS2B to facilitate the dynamic assembly/disassembly of the 90S/SSU processome during ribosome biogenesis and repress nucleolar stress.

**Keywords**: AtPRMT3, RPS2, ribosome assembly, pre-rRNA processing, 90S/SSU processome, nucleolar stress

### Introduction

Protein arginine methyltransferase 3 (PRMT3) is a conserved type I protein arginine methyltransferase catalyzing the formation of asymmetric dimethylarginine (Ahmad and Cao, 2012; Bedford and Clarke, 2009; Liu et al., 2010; Tang et al., 1998). Deletion of the PRMT3 homolog in fission yeast, affects the balance in the 40S:60S free ribosomal subunits ratio without disturbing precursor ribosomal RNA (pre-rRNA) processing (Bachand and Silver, 2004). In mice, PRMT3-deficient embryos are small in size, but the levels of 40S, 60S, 80S monosome and polyribosomes are unaffected (Swiercz et al., 2007). In yeast and mammals, PRMT3 methylates Ribosomal Protein S2 (RPS2) (Bachand and Silver, 2004; Swiercz et al., 2007; Swiercz et al., 2005); however, the RMT3-RPS2 interaction, rather than the methyltransferase activity of RMT3, is essential for ribosomal small subunit maturation (Perreault et al., 2009). AtPRMT3 is required for ribosome biogenesis at the level of pre-rRNA processing, where it balances the two alternative pre-rRNA processing pathways (Hang et al., 2014), which differ in the order of 5' external transcribed spacer (ETS) removal and internal transcribed spacer (ITS1) cleavage (Hang et al., 2014; Sáez-Vásquez and Delseny, 2019; Weis et al., 2015a).

The membrane-less nucleolus is a primary location for ribosome biogenesis, where rDNA transcription, rRNA processing, and ribosome assembly occur (Woolford and Baserga, 2013). Ribosome assembly is a fundamental and highly sophisticated process involving not only ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) and ribosomal proteins, but also many assembly factors and small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) (Henras et al., 2008; Lafontaine, 2015; Woolford and Baserga, 2013). In the early stage of pre-rRNA processing, the primary rRNA transcripts are assembled co-transcriptionally with ribosomal proteins and assembly factors to form the 90S/SSU processome (Dragon et al., 2002; Grandi et al., 2002; Osheim et al., 2004). The U3 small nucleolar ribonucleoprotein complex (snoRNP) is an essential component of the 90S/SSU processome, which is required for nucleolar processing of 18S rRNA precursors

(pre-18S rRNA) (Dragon et al., 2002; Watkins and Bohnsack, 2012). Base pairing of the U3 snoRNA with the 5' ETS and pre-18S rRNA helps the 90S/SSU processome mediate early pre-rRNA folding and cleavage within the 5' ETS and ITS1 (Dutca et al., 2011; Marmier-Gourrier et al., 2011; Sharma and Tollervey, 1999). Cleavage of 35S rRNA transcripts at site A2 in yeast splits the 90S/SSU processome into pre-40S and pre-60S particles (Thomson et al., 2013). In higher plants, U3 snoRNP was first characterized as the Nuclear Factor D (NF-D) complex in cauliflower inflorescences (Sáez-Vásquez et al., 2004a, 2004b) and later as BoU3 (B. oleracea U3) complex from *Brassica oleracea* (Samaha et al., 2010). The NF-D/BoU3 complex recruited by A123B [A(1), A(2), A(3), and B motifs] contributes for the P site cleavage in 5' ETS region (Caparros-Ruiz et al., 1997; Sáez-Vásquez et al., 2004a, 2004b; Samaha et al., 2010). Ribosomal proteins are indispensable for ribosome biogenesis, shuttling into the nucleus to incorporate into pre-ribosomes coordinately and sequentially (de la Cruz et al., 2015). For example, RPS2 in fission yeast participates in pre-rRNA processing and nuclear export of pre-40S subunits (Perreault et al., 2008). Defects of any components involved in the ribosome biogenesis usually result in disordered structure of the nucleolus, which can induce nucleolar stress at the cellular level (Boulon et al., 2010; Ohbayashi and Sugiyama, 2017; Zhang and Lu, 2009).

Ribosome biogenesis is seamlessly orchestrated to ensure normal growth and development. Defects of ribosome biogenesis lead to severe biological consequences, such as ribosomopathies in humans (Narla and Ebert, 2010). In *Arabidopsis*, some null mutants of ribosome-related genes are embryo lethal or show pleotropic developmental defects (Byrne, 2009; Horiguchi et al., 2011; Weis et al., 2015a). For example, factors involved in SSU maturation such as SLOW WALKER 1 (SWA1, a homolog of yeast UTP15) (Shi et al., 2005), PERIODIC TRYPTOPHAN PROTEIN 2 (PWP2) (Missbach et al., 2013), and YAO (a homolog of yeast Ribosomal RNA Processing protein 9, RRP9) (Li et al., 2010) are essential for gametophyte development in *Arabidopsis*. Plant ribosome biogenesis is affected by different kinds of factors, such as the RRP7 (Micol-Ponce et al., 2018), NUCLEOLIN (Pontvianne et

al., 2007; Kojima, et al., 2007), histone deacetylase HD2B and HD2C (Chen et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2018), the snoRNA *HIDDEN TREASURE 2* (*HID2*) (Zhu et al., 2016), ribosomal protein RPL4A (Rosado et al., 2010), and the putative methyltransferase ROOT INITIATION DEFECTIVE 2 (RID2) (Ohbayashi et al., 2011). Mutants of these factors produce common phenotypes of ribosomal proteins and related factors, including retarded growth, and narrow and pointed leaves.

In this study, we reported the isolation of an intragenic suppressor of *atprmt3-2*, which produces a truncated AtPRMT3 lacking an intact enzymatic domain at the C-terminus and show that the N-terminus of AtPRMT3 is required for its biological functions. Then we identified the *Arabidopsis* RPS2 family members as *in vivo* physical interaction partners of AtPRMT3. The *rps2a2b* mutants displayed similar developmental defects to those of *atprmt3-2* mutants, such as pointed first true leaves, shorter primary roots, retarded growth, and aberrant pre-rRNA processing. RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays showed that AtPRMT3 affected the association of RPS2B to pre-rRNAs in the nucleus. Dysfunction of AtPRMT3 resulted in enhanced association between RPS2B and the 90S/SSU processome and nucleolar stress evidenced by enlarged nucleolar size in *atprmt3-2* mutants. Our findings revealed a novel mechanism by which AtPRMT3 and RPS2B interact to regulate the dynamic assembly of the 90S/SSU processome to promote ribosome biogenesis and repress nucleolar stress in plant growth and development.

### Results

#### The enzymatic domain of AtPRMT3 is dispensable for its biological function

AtPRMT3 is required for proper pre-rRNA processing but the underlying molecular mechanism remains elusive (Hang et al., 2014). To address this question, we performed a genetic screen for suppressors of the *atprmt3-2* mutant, which is a null mutant with a T-DNA insertion in the fifth intron (Hang et al., 2014). Seeds of *atprmt3-2* were mutagenized with ethylmethanesulfonate (EMS), and four dominant suppressors, namely m15, m21, m206, and b3, were isolated to rescue the pleiotropic phenotypes of *atprmt3-2* (Supplemental Figure 1A). By whole genome re-sequencing, m15, m21 and m206 exhibited the same intragenic G-to-A nucleotide mutation ahead of the T-DNA insertion site of *atprmt3-2*; this mutation converts tryptophan-366 into a stop codon (W366\*) and is referred to *atprmt3-R1* hereafter (Figure 1A and Supplemental Figure 1B). Further by sanger sequencing, we identified a new intragenic C-to-T mutation converting Glutamine-353 into a stop codon (Q353\*) in b3, which is referred to atprmt3-R2 hereafter (Figure 1A). RT-PCR indicated that expression of N-terminus of AtPRMT3, other than the full-length transcript, existed in both atprmt3-2 atprmt3-R1 and atprmt3-2 atprmt3-R2 resembling to atprmt3-2 (Supplemental Figure 1C). Interestingly, immunoblotting with anti-AtPRMT3 suggested that truncated N-terminal part of AtPRMT3 was successfully translated in these two dominant suppressors atprmt3-2 atprmt3-R1 and atprmt3-2 atprmt3-R2 (Figure 1B).

The intragenic mutation (W366\*) in *atprmt3-R1* is located in Double E domain, which is essential for PRMT enzymatic activity, and conserved in different species (Supplemental Figure 1D). In *atprmt3-R1* and *atprmt3-R2*, the enzymatic domain of AtPRMT3 is disturbed. To further verify that the enzymatic activity is dispensable for the biological function of AtPRMT3, we introduced the E374Q point mutation in the double E domain (Figure 1C); this mutation completely abolished the *in vitro* enzymatic activity of AtPRMT3 against calf thymus core histones (Supplemental

Figure 2A) and myelin basic protein (Supplemental Figure 2B). Consistently, AtPRMT3<sup>E374Q</sup> restored the developmental defects (Figure 1D; Supplemental Figure 2C and 2D) and aberrant pre-rRNA processing of *atprmt3-2* (Supplemental Figure 3C), suggesting that the enzymatic domain in its C-terminus is not essential for the biological function of AtPRMT3. Furthermore, we found that a shorter N terminal region (amino acids 1-171) containing the C2H2-zinc finger of AtPRMT3 (AtPRMT3<sup>N</sup>) (Figure 1C), is sufficient to restore the abnormal developmental phenotypes (Figure 1D; Supplemental Figure 3C). These data show that the N-terminal region containing the C2H2-zinc finger of AtPRMT3, rather than enzymatic activity, is sufficient for its biological functions, which might function through mediating protein interactions.

#### AtPRMT3 physically interacts with RPS2 family proteins

The RPS2 (uS5) protein family members (Ban et al., 2014) were enriched by AtPRMT3 (Figure 2A and Supplemental Table 1). As structural components of cytoplasmic 80S ribosome, RPS2 proteins are conserved in the eukaryotes (Supplemental Figure 5). The Arabidopsis RPS2 family comprises six genes: RPS2A (AT1G58380), three tandem RPS2B (AT1G58684, AT1G58983, and AT1G59359, which are adjacent to RPS2A) genes, RPS2C (AT2G41840), and RPS2D (AT3G57490) (Supplemental Figure 6A). These genes produce the RPS2A/B/C/D proteins, respectively. These four RPS2 proteins share the same domain structures, with an N-terminal arginine/glycine-rich (RGG/RG) motif and a C-terminal Ribosomal\_S5 domain (Supplemental Figure 6B). Moreover, their amino acid sequences are highly conserved: RPS2B, RPS2C, and RPS2D share 99%, 92%, and 95% identity with RPS2A, respectively (Supplemental Figure 6C).

The physical associations between AtPRMT3 and each RPS2 protein were further validated by *in vitro* pull-down (Figure 2B) and *in vivo* co-IP assays (Figure 2C and Supplemental Figure 4B). Besides, AtPRMT3<sup>N</sup> was sufficient for protein interactions with RPS2 both *in vitro* (Supplemental Figure 4C) and *in vivo* (Figure 2D). When the nuclei were treated by harsher lysis conditions (stronger sonication and higher concentration of detergent) to release more proteins, we found that besides the major cytoplasmic localization, AtPRMT3 also localized in the nucleus (Supplemental Figure 4D). AtPRMT3 and RPS2 interaction can occur in both cytoplasm and nucleus (Supplemental Figure 4E), which was consistent with the bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay (Supplemental Figure 4F).

# Disruption of *RPS2A2B* produced developmental and pre-rRNA processing defects resembling those of the *atprmt3* mutants

To check whether RPS2 family members play important function in plant growth and development, we screened T-DNA insertion mutants or created genetic mutants by CRISPR-Cas9 approach. It is obvious that neither T-DNA insertion allele *rps2c-1* for *RPS2C* (Supplemental Figure 7E and 7F), nor gene-editing alleles *rps2d-1* and *rps2d-2* for *RPS2D* (Supplemental Figure 7G) exhibit pointed first true leaves like *atprmt3-2* (Supplemental Figure 8H). The three identical genes encoding RPS2B are physically close to *RPS2A* on chromosome 1 (Supplemental Figure 6A). Besides, RPS2A and RPS2B show a high degree of sequence identity, differing by only one amino acid (Supplemental Figure 6C). To determine the biological function of *RPS2*, CRISPR-Cas9 technology was applied to knock out *RPS2A* and the three *RPS2B* genes simultaneously, using a common guide RNA (Supplemental Figure 7A). The resulting *rps2a2b-1* mutants were confirmed by DNA sequencing (Supplemental Figure 7B) and immunoblotting (Supplemental Figure 8A).

Intriguingly, the *rps2a2b-1* mutants exhibited pleiotropic developmental defects resembling those of *atprmt3-2*, such as short primary roots (Figure 3A and 3B), pointed first true leaves, disturbed leaf vein patterns (Figure 3C and Supplemental Figure 8B), growth retardation including delayed germination (Supplemental Figure

8C), delayed cotyledon greening (Supplemental Figure 8D). The developmental defects of rps2a2b-1 could be rescued by a complementation construct expressing *RPS2B*, but not by the one expressing *RPS2A* (Figure 3A–3C and Supplemental Figure 8B–8D). Since RPS2A and RPS2B differs by only one amino acid, we predict that the different complementation results of *RPS2A* and *RPS2B* may mainly due to their different expression levels (Supplemental Figure 8A). Consistently, the mRNA levels of *RPS2A* were lower than those of *RPS2B* in Col-0 (Supplemental Figure 8E). To further validate this hypothesis, we used the promoter of *RPS2B* to drive the expression of *RPS2A* and found that *RPS2B<sub>pro</sub>::RPS2A* could rescue the developmental defects of rps2a2b-1 (Supplemental Figure 8F and 8G). In addition, a single mutant of rps2a-1 caused by T-DNA insertion did not show any obvious developmental defects (Supplemental Figure 7C and 7D; Supplemental Figure 8H). The results suggest that phenotypes of rps2a2b-1 are mainly contributed by rps2b mutations.

To determine the molecular function of RPS2A2B in ribosome biogenesis, we used northern blot assays to determine the pre-rRNA processing status in *rps2a2b-1* mutants. Steady state levels of pre-rRNA intermediates can be detected by probes as previously described (Hang et al., 2014), and as illustrated (Supplemental Figure 3A and 3B). Compared with wild-type Col-0, the *rps2a2b-1* mutants accumulated more 33S(P')/32S, and 27SA2 (Figure 3D and Supplemental Figure 9A), the markers for the "5' ETS-first" processing manner. By contrast, P-A3, the marker for "ITS1-first" pre-rRNA processing manner, was decreased in *rps2a2b-1* (Supplemental Figure 9B). This unbalanced pre-rRNA processing pattern in *rps2a2b-1* may further result in delayed processing of 18S-A3 in pre-40S SSU, and 27SA and 27SB in pre-60S LSU (Large Subunit) (Figure 3D and Supplemental Figure 9). These pre-rRNA processing defects of *rps2a2b-1* were similar to those of *atprmt3-2* and were also rescued by *RPS2B*, but not by *RPS2A* (Figure 3D).

To further decipher the genetic relationship between *AtPRMT3* and *RPS2A2B* in ribosome biogenesis, genetic crosses were carried out between *atprmt3-2* and

*rps2a2b-1*. However, we failed to obtain the *atprmt3rps2a2b* triple mutants. Examination of siliques of *atprmt3(+/-)rps2a2b(-/-)* plants revealed the presence of aborted ovules (Supplemental Figure 10A). These defective ovules were retarded at the globular embryo stage (Supplemental Figure 10B). Therefore, the similar developmental and molecular phenotypes of *rps2a2b-1* and *atprmt3-2*, as well as the embryonic lethal phenotypes of *atprmt3 rps2a2b* triple mutants suggested that RPS2B and AtPRMT3 may function coordinately and redundantly in ribosome biogenesis to promote plant growth and development. Nevertheless, we still could not exclude the possibility of the extra ribosomal activities of RPS2 here, which will be determined in the future.

# The genes involved in ribosome biogenesis were over expressed in *atprmt3* and *rps2a2b* mutants

To gain further insights into the biological function of AtPRMT3 and RPS2A2B, we performed the transcriptomic analysis with Col-0, *atprmt3-2*, and *rps2a2b-1* by high-throughput sequencing. We obtained 16~20 million clean paired-end reads from each sample, and over 15 million reads of each sample were aligned to the Arabidopsis genome (Supplemental Table 3). We identified 2,415 up-regulated genes and 1,329 down-regulated genes in *atprmt3-2* compared with Col-0 (Supplemental Figure 11A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the up-regulated genes showed significant enrichment in functions related to RNA methylation ( $P = 2.11 \times$  $10^{-31}$ ), translation ( $P = 7.74 \times 10^{-24}$ ), peptide biosynthetic process ( $P = 1.54 \times 10^{-23}$ ), and ribosome biogenesis ( $P = 1.21 \times 10^{-8}$ ) (Supplemental Figure 11C). Also, we identified 3.026 up-regulated genes and 1.142 down-regulated genes in rps2a2b-1 compared with Col-0 (Supplemental Figure 11B). The up-regulated genes in *atprmt3-2* and *rps2a2b-1* showed significant overlap ( $P < 10^{-100}$ ) (Figure 4A) and GO analysis revealed that the 1,587 overlapping up-regulated genes were strongly enriched in genes related to ribosome functions such as translation, peptide biosynthetic process, ribosome biogenesis, ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis and ribosomal small subunit biogenesis (Figure 4B and 4E), implying that RPS2B and

AtPRMT3 have redundant functions in ribosome biogenesis. The down-regulated genes in *atprmt3-2* and *rps2a2b-1* also showed significant overlap ( $P < 10^{-100}$ ) (Figure 4C) and GO analysis revealed that the 587 overlapping down-regulated genes were involved in many biological process (Figure 4D).

The U3 snoRNP contains the U3 snoRNA and core proteins, including Nucleolar protein 1 (NOP1)/FIBRILLARIN (FIB), NOP56, NOP58, Small nuclear protein 13 (Snu13), and RRP9 (Watkins and Bohnsack, 2012). Notably, genes related to the U3 snoRNP were up-regulated in both *atprmt3-2* and *rps2a2b-1* mutants (Supplemental Figure 11D and 11E), as further validated by RT-qPCR (Supplemental Figure 11F). Consistent with their mRNA levels, the protein levels of FIB and NOP56 were up-regulated in *atprmt3-2* and *rps2a2b-1* (Supplemental Figure 11H), reflecting the over accumulation of the U3 snoRNP core proteins in both mutants. Moreover, snoRNAs such as U3 and snoR30 were also over-accumulated in *atprmt3-2* and *rps2a2b-1* mutants, consistent with the aberrant pre-rRNA processing in these mutants (Supplemental Figure 11G).

#### **Disruption of AtPRMT3 resulted in enlarged nucleolus**

As a major cellular compartment for ribosome biogenesis, the nucleolus is a membrane-less dynamic structure with changing size, shape, and position in the nucleus (Shaw and Brown, 2012). The nucleolus encapsulates various ribonucleoproteins and RNAs that can achieve self-organization or self-assembly, potentially through liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) (Sawyer et al., 2019; Yao et al., 2019). Aberrant ribosome biogenesis is usually correlated with disorganization of the nucleolus and results in nucleolar stress, which is often manifested by varied size of the nucleolus in ribosome-related mutants, such as enlarged nucleus in the *apum23* (Abbasi et al., 2010) and *rid2* (Ohbayashi et al., 2011), or reduced size of nucleolus in *apum24* under sugar treatment (Maekawa et al., 2017).

To determine whether the state of nucleolus was affected without AtPRMT3, we visualized nucleolus using GFP-FIB1 in Col-0 and *atprmt3-2* and measured the area nucleolus indicated by GFP-FIB1 relative to that of the nucleus indicated by DAPI

staining (Figure 5A). Notably, *atprmt3-2* plants had a higher frequency of larger nucleolus compared with those in Col-0 (Figure 5B), we also identified that the nucleolus of *rps2a2b-1* showed enlarged state, similar to those of *atprmt3-2* (Supplemental Figure 12), suggesting that nucleolar stress occurred when either AtPRMT3 or RPS2A2B was disrupted.

#### Enhanced association between RPS2B and pre-rRNA in the atprmt3 mutants

The pre-rRNA processing defects of *rps2a2b-1* mutants started from the primary transcripts of 33S(P') and 32S in the nucleolus, indicating the important function of RPS2B in early stages of rRNA biogenesis. Indeed, subcellular fractionation assay supported the nuclear localization of RPS2B-YFP (Supplemental Figure 13A). Moreover, immunofluorescence in *RPS2B-YFP*/Col-0 further uncovered that RPS2B-YFP can be localized in both nucleoplasm and nucleolus (Supplemental Figure 13B), where ribosome biogenesis actively occurs in living cells. We further applied immunofluorescence assay in the nuclei of Col-0 and found that endogenous nuclear RPS2A2B distributed in both nucleoplasm and nucleolus (Supplemental Figure 14). This is consistent with the published data in which RPS2 proteins were characterized in the nucleolus proteome using plant cell culture (Palm et al., 2016; Pendle et al., 2005) and plants (Montacie et al., 2017). Upper evidence indicates that RPS2B participates in ribosome biogenesis at a relatively early stage starting from the nucleolus.

To determine whether RPS2B could regulate the processing step by binding to pre-rRNAs directly, we performed RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) using GFP-Trap in the nuclear fractions of Col-0, *RPS2B-YFP/*Col-0, and *RPS2B-YFP/atprmt3-2* seedlings. We found that the pre-rRNA fragments were specifically enriched in samples with *RPS2B-YFP*, but not in the wild-type Col-0 control. As the negative control, U5 snRNA did not co-purify with RPS2B-YFP (Figure 6A). These results suggest that RPS2B binds to pre-rRNAs *in vivo*. Intriguingly, we found that the signal enriched by RPS2B-YFP was higher in *atprmt3-2* mutants than in Col-0 (Figure 6A),

indicating an enhanced association between RPS2B and pre-rRNA in *atprmt3-2* mutants.

#### Retarded processing dynamics of the 90S/SSU processome in *atprmt3* mutants

Ribosome biogenesis is a complicated and highly orchestrated process requiring numerous assembly factors as well as ribosomal proteins (Henras et al., 2008; Lafontaine, 2015). Here, enhanced interaction between RPS2B and pre-rRNAs in *atprmt3* mutants inspired us to compare the functional RPS2B-associated pre-ribosome complex in Col-0 and *atprmt3-2*. We first enriched RPS2B-YFP proteins and their associated complexes from the nuclear extracts of *RPS2B-YFP/*Col-0 and *RPS2B-YFP/atprmt3-2* seedlings, with wild-type Col-0 as a negative control (Supplemental Figure 13C). RPS2B-YFP associated proteins were characterized by LC-MS/MS analysis, using spectrum counts to quantify protein abundance. We normalized the counts to those of RPS2B to estimate the relative abundance of proteins associated with RPS2B in different samples. GO analysis by using Metascape revealed that these RPS2B-associated proteins mainly function in ribosome biogenesis (Supplemental Figure 13D) (Zhou et al., 2019).

Among the RPS2B-associated proteins, intriguingly, we identified a large set of *Arabidopsis* homologs of assembly factors in yeast that comprise the 90S/SSU processome (Figure 6B, Upper, and Supplemental Table 4). The 90S/SSU processome contains the 5' ETS particle, which is an early assembly intermediate formed on the 5' ETS, including U THREE PROTEIN A (UTPA), UTPB, UTPC, U3 snoRNP, the M PHASEPHOSPHOPROTEIN 10 (MPP10) complex, and other assembly factors that join the complex sequentially (Barandun et al., 2018; Klinge and Woolford, 2018). Extensive studies have revealed that these factors assemble in a stepwise and dynamic manner (Chaker-Margot et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2016). Compared with Col-0, the *atprmt3* mutants showed more normalized spectral counts for the 90S/SSU processome components, including UTPA (SWA1/UTP15 and UTP4), UTPB (UTP6, PWP2), DOM34 INTERACTING PROTEIN 2 (DIP2/UTP12), U3 snoRNP (FIB1, FIB2, NOP56, NOP58 and RRP9/YAO), MPP10 (IMP4) as well as some complexes

including Nucleolin, H/ACA snoRNP, UTPC and other assembly factors (Figure 6B, Upper). Enhanced association between RPS2B and the 90S/SSU processome in *atprmt3* mutants suggests that AtPRMT3 functions as an important ribosome biogenesis factor in the dynamic assembly of the 90S/SSU processome. To validate the RPS2B interactome results, co-IP products of *RPS2B-YFP* from nuclear extracts were immunoblotted with antibodies against U3 snoRNP core proteins, such as FIB and NOP56. Consistent with the MS results, the association between RPS2B and snoRNP increased in *atprmt3-2* mutants (Figure 6C).

To further verify the state of the 90S/SSU processome in *atprmt3* mutants, we performed IP-MS using antibodies against FIB, to identify and compare the FIB-associated components in Col-0 and *atprmt3-2*. Consistent with the RPS2B-IP MS results, the interactions between FIB and the 90S/SSU processome were also enhanced in *atprmt3-2* mutants (Figure 6B, Lower). Then, the enhanced interactions between FIB and NOP56 as well as H/ACA ribonucleoprotein complex subunit CBF5 were further validated by co-IP (Figure 6D). Moreover, gel filtration analysis uncovered that more FIB and NOP56 were distributed in the high molecular weight fractions in *atprmt3-2* than in Col-0, implying that the core complex of snoRNP (FIB and NOP56) was retained in the larger complex (Figure 6E). These results suggest that AtPRMT3 promotes the dynamic assembly/disassembly of the 90S/SSU processome at the early pre-rRNA processing stages to regulate ribosome biogenesis.

#### Discussion

Previously, we showed that AtPRMT3 is essential for proper pre-rRNA processing (Hang et al., 2014), but how AtPRMT3 functions in this pathway remains unclear. Here, we revealed that AtPRMT3-RPS2B interaction in *Arabidopsis* promotes ribosome biogenesis by fine-tuning the dynamic assembly/disassembly of 90S/SSU processome in the nucleus, thereby repressing nucleolar stress during plant growth and development.

PRMTs usually catalyze arginine methylation of different substrates to regulate diverse biological processes (Ahmad and Cao, 2012; Bedford and Clarke, 2009). However, in this study, we identified an intragenic mutant and demonstrated that AtPRMT3 functions through protein-protein interaction rather than its enzymatic activity similar to that in yeast (Perreault et al., 2009). The PRMT3–RPS2 interaction is conserved in unicellular fission yeast (Bachand and Silver, 2004) and multicellular organisms, such as mouse (Swiercz et al., 2007), and human cells (Swiercz et al., 2005). However, its underlying biological function remains elusive. In this work, we found that RPS2B physically interacts with AtPRMT3 in Arabidopsis and their mutants share similar biological and molecular defects, indicating that AtPRMT3 and RPS2B are involved in the same pathway. Moreover, the PRMT3-RPS2 interaction in vivo involving only the N-terminal C2H2-zinc finger domain of AtPRMT3 was sufficient for its function. Earlier work in human cells suggested that PRMT3 may protect RPS2 from ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis (Choi et al., 2008). RPS2B has a highly basic N-terminal RGG/RG domain; these RNA binding domains are found in many proteins involved in phase separation (Chong et al., 2018). Due to the highly conserved function of PRMT3, we hypothesized that AtPRMT3 might function as a chaperone to prevent non-specific interactions or aggregation, and to help RPS2 efficiently incorporate into the pre-ribosome, as shown for the chaperones in yeast (Pillet et al., 2017).

Alternative pre-rRNA processing pathways differ in the order of 5' ETS removal and ITS1 cleavage (Hang et al., 2014; Hang et al., 2018; Sáez-Vásquez and Delseny,

2019; Tomecki et al., 2017; Weis et al., 2015a; Weis et al., 2015b), and these processing pathways are dependent on the stepwise and dynamic assembly of the pre-ribosomal complexes (Cheng et al., 2020; Du et al., 2020; Klinge and Woolford, 2018). Here we characterized a large set of Arabidopsis homologs for complexes of UTPA, UTPB, U3 snoRNP, MPP10 and UTPC, which co-purified with FIB and RPS2B in the nucleus, demonstrating the existence of the 90S/SSU complex in higher plants (Sáez-Vásquez and Delseny, 2019). Here we propose a working model that AtPRMT3-RPS2B interaction is essential for proper ribosome biogenesis, especially the dynamic assembly of the 90S/SSU processome in the nucleolus, to promote cellular function, plant growth and development (Figure 7). AtPRMT3 may regulate the availability of RPS2B to incorporate into the pre-ribosome to promote dynamic assembly/disassembly of the 90S/SSU processome. Disruption of the AtPRMT3-RPS2B interaction may delay the dynamic assembly/disassembly of 90S/SSU processome on 35S(P) transcript and affects downstream balance of "5' ETS-first" and "ITS1-first" processing manners, which exhibits earlier removal of the 5' ETS than the cleavage in ITS1. Aberrant ribosome biogenesis further lead to nucleolar stress, resulting in aberrant cellular function, and finally causing growth and developmental defects (Figure 7).

Defects in ribosome biogenesis are usually accompanied by changes in the composition and organization of the nucleolus, resulting in nucleolar stress (Matos-Perdomo and Machin, 2019). Mechanisms underlying nucleolar stress is complex. In animals, nucleolus stress can trigger the binding of specific ribosomal proteins to the ubiquitin E3 ligase MDM2, which inhibits MDM2-mediated p53 ubiquitination, leading to the activation of p53 (Zhang and Lu, 2009). Recent work has been reported that ANAC082 might serve as a nucleolar stress mediator in *Arabidopsis* (Ohbayashi et al., 2017), while detailed mechanism remains elusive in plants. It will be informative to determine whether and how ANAC082 is activated by disruption of AtPRMT3-RPS2B interaction to mediate the nucleolar stress responses, which may account for the growth and developmental defects of *atprmt3-2* and

*rps2a2b-1*. In addition, the membrane-less nucleolus exhibits liquid-liquid phase separation characteristics (Sawyer et al., 2019), and serves as a central compartment sensing stress and coordinating stress responses (Boulon et al., 2010; Zhang and Lu, 2009). Nucleolar stress can also be induced by environmental stresses such as extreme temperature (Boulon et al., 2010). Interestingly, the extreme temperature like cold or heat stress can severely affect ribosome biogenesis in rice, thereby restricting plants growth and yield product (Hang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2016). However, it is still unknown whether plant nucleolus acts as the temperature sensor to quickly adjust cellular ribosome biogenesis and metabolism for better acclimation to ambient temperature fluctuations. Since AtPRMT3-RPS2B interaction promotes ribosome biogenesis and repress nucleolar stress, it will be intriguing to determine the functions of AtPRMT3 and RPS2B in extreme temperature responses in the future.

#### Methods

#### **Plant Materials**

The *atprmt3-2* (WISCDSLOX391A01) mutant was characterized previously (Hang et al., 2014). The *rps2a-1* (SALKseq\_48220.1), *rps2c-1* (SALK\_020959C) mutants were obtained from the *Arabidopsis* Biological Resource Center. The *rps2a2b-1* mutant was created by CRISPR-Cas9 (Wang et al., 2015). The *rps2d-1* and *rps2d-2* mutants were created by CRISPR-Cas9 as well (Tsutsui and Higashiyama, 2017).

#### EMS Mutagenesis, Genomic DNA Sequencing, and Data Analysis

0.1-gram *atprmt3-2* seeds were mutagenized with 0.3% EMS and 0.01% Tween-20 for 15 h, and then planted on soil. M2 seeds were collected from individual M1 plants. M2 generations were screened for the rescued phenotypes of *atprmt3* mutants. The suppressors were backcrossed to *atprmt3-2* for at least three times before further genetic analysis. Seedlings showing wild-type and mutant phenotypes were pooled separately in F2 generation for whole-genome sequencing using the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform performed by Berry Genomics company. The reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (Bolger et al., 2014) and mapped to the reference Arabidopsis thaliana Genome (TAIR10) using BWA-MEM (Li, 2013; Li and Durbin, 2009). Alignments were then sorted with samtools (Li et al., 2009) and duplicates were marked with PicardTools (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard). SNPs called with samtools/bcftools were then filtered with the mapping quality  $\geq 30$  (Li, 2011). SNP index for each allele at segregating bi-allelic SNPs in 100-kb sliding windows by (by 10 kb) was summed for the various sequencing pools and allele frequencies were calculated. Finally, the difference in allele frequency (SNP index) between the mutant pool and wild-type pool was calculated for all pairwise comparison and plotted across the 5 chromosomes.

#### **Northern Blot**

Northern blot was conducted as previously described (Hang et al., 2014; Hang et al.,

2015). The probes in this study are listed in Supplemental Table 2 or as previously described (Hang et al., 2014).

#### **Subcellular Fractionation**

Subcellular fractionation was conducted as previously described with minor modification (Hang et al., 2014). Briefly, 1 g seedlings were ground thoroughly in 2 mL Honda buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH7.4, 2.5% Ficoll 400, 5% dextran T40,0.4 M sucrose, 10 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 1 mM DTT, protease inhibitors) at 4 °C, then they were filtered through 60 µm mesh nylon netting. Triton X-100 was added to a final concentration of 0.5%, and incubated on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged at 1,500 g for 5 min. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 13,000g for 5 min, and kept as cytoplasm fraction. The pellet were resuspended by suspension buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 0.25 M sucrose, 10 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 1% Triton X-100, protease inhibitors) and centrifuged at 1,500 g for 5 min, and this step was repeated for two or three times to remove the green debris until the pellet turns white. Finally, the pellet was suspended in nuclear lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 1% SDS, 1 mM DTT, protease inhibitors) and gently mixed for 1 h at 4 °C, and then sonicated to release more nuclear proteins. The nuclear fraction was centrifuged at 13,000 g for 5 min to remove debris.

#### **Circular RT-PCR assay**

The circular RT-PCR assay was performed as described before (Hang et al., 2014; Hang et al., 2015) with minor modification that SuperScript<sup>™</sup> II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, 18064022) was applied for reverse transcription step.

## Generation of Complementation Lines for *RPS2A<sub>pro</sub>::RPS2A-GFP-HIS*, *RPS2B<sub>pro</sub>::RPS2B-GFP-HIS*, and *RPS2B<sub>pro</sub>::RPS2A-GFP-HIS* in *rps2a2b-1*

The genomic fragments of *RPS2A* (905-bp upstream of the start codon and the 968-bp genomic region without the stop codon) and *RPS2B* (717-bp upstream of the start codon and the 1196-bp genomic region without the stop codon) were amplified by

primers HX1739/HX1740 and HX1735/HX1705, respectively. Both fragments were cloned into the pENTR-D-TOPO vector (Life Technologies, K2400-20), to generate (TOPO- $RPS2A_{Pro}$ :: RPS2A) and XF2958 (TOPO- $RPS2B_{Pro}$ :: RPS2B), XF2957 respectively. After digestion with the restriction enzyme Mlu I (NEB, R0198S), fragments containing RPS2A and RPS2B were cloned into the destination vector pMDC107 using Gateway LR Clonase II (Life Technologies, 11791-100) to generate DNA constructs XF2066  $(RPS2A_{Pro}::RPS2A-GFP-6 \times HIS)$ and XF2967  $(RPS2B_{Pro}::RPS2B-GFP-6\times HIS)$ , respectively. Similarly, the gene body of RPS2A driven by the promoter of RPS2B was cloned to pMDC107, and named as  $RPS2B_{pro}$ :: RPS2A-GFP-6×HIS. The constructs were then introduced into the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 and transformed into rps2a2b-1 mutants to generate the complementation lines RPS2A-GFP, RPS2B-GFP for and *RPS2B*<sub>pro</sub>::*RPS2A-GFP*. All primers are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

#### Generation of 35Spro:: RPS2B-YFP-HA in Col-0 and atprmt3-2 mutants

The cDNA fragment of *RPS2B* was amplified by primers HX4160/HX4161 and cloned into *pEASY-T1* (TRANSGEN BIOTECH, CT101-01) to generate the construct XF2935. Using XF2935 as the template, the full length coding sequence (CDS) of RPS2B was amplified by primers HX1703/HX1705 and subcloned into the target vector *pEG101* by Gateway cloning system (Earley et al., 2006), to generate the construct XF2967 (*35S<sub>pro</sub>::RPS2B-YFP-HA*). The construct was introduced into the *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* strain EHA105 and then into Col-0 to generate *RPS2B-YFP/*Col-0. The transgenic plant was crossed with *atprmt3-2* and progeny were screened to identify homozygous *RPS2B-YFP/atprmt3-2*. All primers are in Supplemental Table 2.

Generation of transgenic lines of  $AtPRMT3_{pro}$ :: $AtPRMT3^{E374Q}$ -GFP-HIS,  $UBQ10_{pro}$ :: $AtPRMT3^{WT}$ -HA and  $UBQ10_{pro}$ :: $AtPRMT3^{N}$ -HA in atprmt3-2

The point mutation construct XF2891 ( $AtPRMT3_{pro}$ :: $AtPRMT3^{E374Q}$ -GFP-HIS) was created using primers HX2708/HX2709 to perform site-directed mutagenesis of XF1630 ( $AtPRMT3_{pro}$ :: $AtPRMT3^{WT}$ -GFP-HIS) (Hang et al., 2014). The CDS regions of AtPRMT3 and its N terminus (1-171 amino acids) amplified by primers HX6075/HX6076 and HX6075/HX7568, respectively, were cloned into the XF1724 vector to generate the constructs XF3379 ( $UBQ10_{pro}$ :: $AtPRMT3^{WT}$ -HA) and XF3500 ( $UBQ10_{pro}$ :: $AtPRMT3^{N}$ -HA). These constructs were introduced into the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 and then into atprmt3-2 to generate AtPRMT3^{WT}-GFP, AtPRMT3^{E374Q}-GFP, AtPRMT3<sup>WT</sup>-HA and AtPRMT3^N-HA, respectively. All primers are in Supplemental Table 2.

#### **Proteomic Analysis**

The products immunoprecipitated by the indicated antibodies were separated by SDS-PAGE, and protein bands (excluding the antibody heavy and light chains) were digested by trypsin and recovered from the gel. The samples were analyzed by LC-MS using Nano LC-MS/MS (Dionex Ultimate 3000 RLSCnano System) interfaced with QExactive HF. The data were analyzed with MaxQuant software (1.3.0.5), using the TAIR10 database.

#### Phylogenetic Analysis and Sequence Alignment of RPS2 proteins

Database searching was performed at TAIR10 (<u>www.arabidopsis.org/Blast/index.jsp</u>) and NCBI (<u>www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</u>). Multiple alignments of protein sequences were performed with Clustal X (Larkin et al., 2007) and then manually edited with the GeneDoc program. Evolutionary analyses were conducted with the neighbor-joining method in MEGA6 (Tamura et al., 2013).

#### **Histological Analysis of Seedlings**

Histological analysis for cotyledons and the first leaves of 24-day-old seedlings were performed as described previously (Hang et al., 2014), with ClearSee solutions (Kurihara et al., 2015). Images were taken with an Olympus SZX12 microscope.

# Recombinant Protein Expression, *in Vitro* Methyltransferase Assay, and Pull-down Assay

Specific CDS regions of RPS2A, RPS2B, RPS2C, and RPS2D were cloned into XF0510, to generate the constructs XF2939 (HIS-MBP-RPS2A), XF2942 (HIS-MBP-RPS2B), XF2945 (HIS-MBP-RPS2C) and XF2948 (HIS-MBP-RPS2D), respectively. XF2895 (GST- $AtPRMT3^{E374Q}$ ) was created by site-directed mutagenesis of XF193 (GST-AtPRMT3) (Hang et al., 2014) using primers HX2708/HX2709. The CDS of AtPRMT3<sup>N</sup> (a.a.1-171) was amplified by primers HX1172/HX1173 and cloned into XF760 to generate XF2901 (GST-AtPRMT3<sup>N</sup>). Recombinant proteins were expressed in the *Escherichia coli* strain BL21 (RIL). Methyltransferase activity assays were performed as previously described (Hang et al., 2014). For *in vitro* pull-down assays, 5 µg of HIS-MBP and HIS-MBP-RPS2 proteins were first immobilized to Ni-charged Profinity IMAC Resin as the bait proteins, followed by blocking with 5% BSA at 4°C for 2 hours in BW buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl and 0.1% (m/v) CA-630). Then each immobilized bait protein was incubated with 5 µg of GST-AtPRMT3, GST-AtPRMT3<sup>N</sup> or GST (as preys) at room temperature for 30 minutes, and finally being washed with BW buffer 5 times. Immunoblotting was performed with anti-GST antiserum.

#### **Antibodies Used in Immunoblotting**

GST-RPS2B-N (amino acids 1-50) and HIS-MBP-RPS2B-C (amino acids 51-284) were used as antigens to produce RPS2B-N mouse antibody and RPS2B-C rabbit antibody, respectively. HIS-MBP-NOP56 (NP\_176007.1) and HIS-SUMO-CBF5/NAP57 (NP\_191274.1) were used to generate CBF5 and NOP56 mouse antiserum, respectively. Rabbit antibodies of FIB (NP\_568772.3) were made at Abclonal Company, using antigen of amino acids 51-308 for FIB1. The specificities of NOP56 and FIB antibodies were validated as shown in Supplemental Figure 15. The commercial antibodies used include anti-histone H3 (Abcam, ab1791), anti-PEPC (Agrisera, AS09458), and anti-HSC70 (Enzo, ADI-SPA-818-D).

#### Subcellular Localization of RPS2B and nucleolus/nucleus area measurement

For the subcellular localization assay of RPS2B-YFP in vivo in Supplemental Figure 13B, 10-day-old transgenic seedlings of 35S<sub>pro</sub>::RPS2B-YFP-HA were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Scientific, 28908) in  $1 \times PBS$  buffer. Then nuclei were isolated and immunofluorescence was performed as described previously (Bey et al., 2018). The primary antibody against FIB and the secondary Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) (Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor 555, Thermo-Fisher, A-31572) were used. For the subcellular localization assay of endogenous RPS2A2B in Supplemental Figure 14B, 12-day-old seedlings of Col-0 were fixed, and nuclei were isolated for immunofluorescence as described above with rabbit anti-RPS2B-C. For area measurements of nucleoli and nuclei in Figure 5A, GFP-FIB1/Col-0 and GFP-FIB1/atprmt3-2 lines containing the same transgenic line of CXF0332 (35Spro::GFP-FIB1) were used. For area measurements of nucleoli and nuclei in Supplemental Figure 12A, Col-0, *atprmt3-2*, and *rps2a2b-1* were used. The 1<sup>st</sup> pair of true leaves of 28-day-old seedlings were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Scientific, 28908) in  $1 \times PBS$  buffer. Then 4', 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was added at a final concentration of 1 µg/ml to stain for DNA as described before (Abbasi et al., 2010). Images were taken with a Zeiss LSM510 inverted confocal microscope equipped with a Plan-Apochromat  $100 \times 1.4$  Oil DIC objective, a Zeiss Axio Observer Z1 lenses and an Axiocam 506 mono CCD camera (Carl Zeiss, Thornwood, NY). The nucleolar and nuclear areas were measured by IMAGE J software.

#### **Embryo Microscopy**

The embryos were incubated in Hoyer's solution (chloralhydrate:distilledwater:glycerol = 8:3:1, m:v:v) for 45 min at 4°C, then cleared at room temperature until the embryos were clear. The embryos were photographed with a BX51 microscope (Olympus) in the differential interference contrast mode.

#### **Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BiFC)**

The coding sequence of AtPRMT3 (amplified by primers H538 and H540) and RPS2B (amplified using primers H541 and H542) were cloned into pXY106 and pXY104 vectors (Zhang, et al. 2015; Zhang, et al. 2020), respectively. Positive constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101, which were further subjected to co-infiltrate into leaves of 4-week-old tobacco (*N. benthamiana*) for the two proteins to be tested. After 36-48 h, YFP signals were analyzed by Olympus BX53 Digital Fluorescence Microscope.

#### **RIP RT-PCR**

Two grams of 12-day-old seedlings were incubated in ice-cold  $1 \times PBS$  buffer and cross-linked twice at 800 mJ/cm<sup>2</sup> in a Hoefer UVC 500 ML ultraviolet Crosslinker (GE). The nuclei were extracted as previously described (Bowler et al., 2004). RIP experiment was performed as described (Chen et al., 2018). GFP-Trap beads (ChromoTek, gtma-10) were used for IP. Finally, the immunoprecipated RNA was reverse transcribed with random primers (TransGen, AH301-02). Gene specific primers (Supplemental Table 2) were used for RT-PCR to detect the target transcripts.

### **RNA Sequencing and Analysis**

Total RNA from 12-day-old seedlings was subjected to polyA selection and strand-specific RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the dUTP method (Levin et al., 2010). All clean reads were aligned and normalized as previously described (Zhao et al., 2019). Genes with FDR (False Discovery Rate) values less than 0.05 and fold-change over 1.5 were identified as differentially expressed genes using edgeR (Robinson et al., 2010). Gene ontology (GO) was analyzed using GOstats (Falcon and Gentleman, 2006) and the of *P*-value cutoff was set to 0.05.

**Figure 7. Working model of AtPRMT3-RPS2B interaction in pre-ribosome assembly and nucleolar stress.** In wild type, AtPRMT3-RPS2B interaction may keep functional or sufficient RPS2B, together with other ribosomal proteins and tremendous assembly factors, to promote dynamic ribosome assembly/disassembly at the early stage in nucleolus. When the interaction disappeared as AtPRMT3 is disrupted in *atprmt3-2*, functional RPS2B in the nucleus may be affected or insufficient, resulting in delayed assembly/disassembly of 90S/SSU processome exhibiting higher association with RPS2B and many assembly factors. Aberrant ribosome biogenesis in *atprmt3-2* further triggers nucleolar stress displaying enlarged nucleolus, which finally affect normal cellular function, plant growth and development.
