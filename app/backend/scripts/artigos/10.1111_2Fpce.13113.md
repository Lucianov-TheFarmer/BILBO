#### **ABSTRACT**

We studied physiological mechanisms of photoavoidance and photoprotection of a dwarf rice mutant with erect leaves, *d1*, in which the *RGA1* gene, which encodes the Gα subunit of the heterotrimeric G protein, is non-functional. *d1* leaves exhibit lower leaf temperature and higher Photochemical Reflectance Index (PRI) relative to wild-type (WT), indicative of increased photoavoidance and more efficient light harvesting. RNA-seq analysis of flag leaves revealed that mRNA levels of genes encoding HSPs, enzymes associated with chlorophyll breakdown, and ROS scavengers were down-regulated in *d1*. By contrast, genes encoding proteins associated with light harvesting, PSII, cyclic electron transport, PSI, and chlorophyll biosynthesis were up-regulated in *d1*. Consistent with these observations, when WT and *d1* plants were experimentally subjected to the same light intensity, *d1* plants exhibited a greater capacity to dissipate excess irradiance (increased non-photochemical quenching) relative to WT. The increased capacity in *d1* for both photoavoidance and photoprotection reduced sustained photoinhibitory damage, as revealed by a higher Fv/Fm. We therefore propose RGA1 as a regulator of photoavoidance and photoprotection mechanisms in rice and highlight the prospect of exploiting modulation of heterotrimeric G protein signaling to increase these characteristics and improve the yield of cereals in the event of abiotic stress.

*Key-words*: *d1*, RGA1, heterotrimeric G protein, leaf temperature, photoavoidance, photoprotection, PSII, *Oryza sativa*, rice.

#### **Brief Summary Statement:**

Reduced photoinhibition via increased photoavoidance and photoprotection are agronomic traits of interest that impact both stress tolerance and yield. We have shown that null mutation of *RGA1*, encoding the canonical Gα subunit of the rice heterotrimeric G protein, improves photoavoidance and photoprotection and modulates the expression of related genes. This study highlights the potential of manipulation of G-protein signaling for agronomic purposes.

### **INTRODUCTION**

Light is the source of energy for photosynthesis, but even moderate irradiance causes some photoinhibitory damage (Ewart 1896). Photoinhibition is caused by the over-excitation of Photosystem II (PSII) reaction centers (Powles 1984) and the over-production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) as a byproduct of photosynthesis (Murata *et al.* 2011). Plants minimize this damage through a series of mechanisms that protect PSII from photoinhibition (Demmig-Adams & Adams III 1992), repair the damage caused to PSII (Tikkanen *et al.* 2014), and detoxify ROS to minimize photooxidative damage (Asada 2006). Improving photosynthetic efficiency and capacity to reduce photoinhibitory damage have been suggested as a strategy to increase yield in crops (Murchie *et al.* 2015; Murchie & Niyogi 2011; Zhu *et al.* 2010).

Environmental stress aggravates photoinhibitory damage, and photoprotective mechanisms promote tolerance to multiple stress factors by reducing the degree of photoinhibition (Murata *et al.* 2007; Takahashi & Murata 2008). Stress tolerance determines yield stability across environmental conditions (Tollenaar & Lee 2002). Rice is one of the crops for which increased yield stability would substantially benefit food security.

There is a recognized variation among rice cultivars in susceptibility to photoinhibition (Black *et al.* 1995), and breeding strategies may have indirectly selected for more efficient light harvesting (Sinclair & Sheehy 1999) and photoprotection (Murchie *et al.* 2015; Murchie & Niyogi, 2011). As an example, two rice hybrids selected for increased yield exhibit decreased midday photoinhibition (Wang *et al.* 2005).

Plants have evolved both molecular and architectural traits to avoid and tolerate photoinhibitory damage. Often, the study of these photoprotective mechanisms focuses on non-photochemical quenching (NPQ), while photoavoidance provides another adaptive mechanism against photoinhibitory damage and is determined by the architecture of the plant, including leaf angle (Falster & Westoby 2003; Liu *et al.* 2003). Compared to plants with horizontal leaf arrangement, plants with erect leaf disposition are more efficient in reducing excessive light interception at midday (Sinclair & Sheehy 1999). Daikoku (*d1*), a spontaneous rice dwarf mutant carrying the *d1* mutation displays such an erect leaf phenotype (Fujisawa *et al.* 1999).

*d1* was among the first rice mutants to be identified by segregation analysis (Akemine 1925) and was later identified as a mutation in the gene encoding RGA1, the canonical αsubunit of the heterotrimeric G protein in rice (Ashikari *et al.* 1999). RGA1 encodes a 380 amino acid protein, and the *d1-1* mutant with a two bp deletion in *RGA1* in cultivar Taichung 65 (T65), studied here, results in a protein null phenotype (Oki *et al.* 2009). Heterotrimeric G proteins are molecular switches, transducing signals from receptor molecules to effector molecules. In their inactive form, G proteins comprise a trimer consisting of a Gα subunit with bound guanosine diphosphate (GDP), Gβ and Gγ. In the active form, GTP-bound Gα dissociates from Gβγ, and both Gα and Gβγ can activate downstream effectors. Once the GTPase activity of Gα hydrolyzes GTP, the Gαβγ trimer reassembles (Assmann 2002; Jones & Assmann 2004; Oldham *et al.* 2008). The rice genome contains a single canonical G

gene: *RGA1* (Ishikawa *et al.* 1995), a single G gene: *RGB1* (Ishikawa *et al.* 1996), four G subunit genes: *RGG1*, *RGG2*, *GS3*, and *DEP1*, and a fifth related G gene or pseudogene *OsGGC2* (Botella 2012). Recently, extra-large G proteins (XLGs) have been shown to bind Gβγ in *Arabidopsis thaliana*, adding another layer of complexity to the G protein paradigm in plants (Chakravorty *et al.* 2015; Maruta *et al.* 2015). Rice has three annotated candidate *XLG* genes (*XLG1*, *XLG2*, and *XLG3*; Maruta *et al.* 2015) and a likely fourth candidate *XLG* based on sequence homology.

Heterotrimeric G proteins in plants were initially posited to act in light signaling downstream of phytochrome (Bowler *et al.* 1994; Neuhaus *et al.* 1993; Okamoto *et al.* 2001). This role was later questioned based on the absence of a hypocotyl elongation phenotype in single and double mutants of Gα and Gβ in Arabidopsis upon differential red and far-red illumination (Jones *et al.* 2003). However, more recently, G proteins have been implicated in phytochrome A (PHYA) mediated cell death, in a process resulting from hyper-accumulation of ROS in the chloroplast, which is alleviated in the Arabidopsis Gα loss of function mutant, *gpa1* (Wei *et al.* 2008). Although the process causing reduced levels of ROS in the chloroplasts of Arabidopsis Gα mutants is not well understood, this phenotype led us to hypothesize that Gα may regulate the capacity of plants to tolerate excessive incoming radiation energy. Increased photoprotection would reduce the production of ROS following overexcitation of PSII and thereby reduce photoinhibitory damage to PSII (Murata *et al.* 2007; Nishiyama *et al.* 2011). The 50% reduction in chlorophyll content in Arabidopsis Gα overexpression lines (Okamoto *et al.* 2001) and the dark green phenotype typical of the Gα loss of function mutant, *d1*, in rice (Fujisawa *et al.* 1999) are consistent with altered rates of chlorophyll synthesis and/or light dependent bleaching of pigments (photooxidation) following photoinhibitory damage (Kok *et al.* 1965). Moreover, the chloroplast protein, Thylakoid formation 1 (THF1) in Arabidopsis interacts with the orthologous heterotrimeric G protein α subunit, GPA1 (Huang *et al.* 2006). THF1, also designated Psb29, is a highly conserved component of PSII (Keren *et al.* 2005) . More recently, Arabidopsis RGS1, which promotes the GTPase activity of Gα (Chen *et al.* 2003), has been proposed to detect changes in light intensity through free RGS1, GαGTP, and *G*βγ (Liao *et al.* 2017). Based on these observations, we hypothesized that heterotrimeric G proteins could play a role in regulating photoinhibition, photoavoidance, and photoprotection.

In this study, we dissected the different processes contributing to light use efficiency, photoinhibition and photoprotection in wild type rice, and their dependence on RGA1, combining transcriptome analysis of flag leaves of WT and the *RGA1* mutant, *d1-1*, with gas exchange, chlorophyll fluorescence, thermal imaging and light spectrometry. We discuss the implications of our results for improved yield stability and stress tolerance in rice.

### **MATERIALS AND METHODS**

### **Plant Growth Conditions**

Wild-type (WT) and *d1-1* mutant plants (henceforth referred to as *d1*) were grown in a greenhouse. Plants were grown individually in US 2 gallon pots containing Metro-mix 360 potting mixture (Sun Gro Horticulture Canada Ltd). Growth temperatures averaged 30˚C during the day with peaks of 35˚C at midday; night temperature was 25˚C. The day:night cycle was 16:8 with light supplied as natural daylight supplemented with 1000W metal halide lamps (Philips Lighting Co., Somerset, NJ) for the duration of the light cycle. Light intensity averaged 500 μmol m-2 s -1 Photosynthetic Photon Flux Density (PPFD). Plants were grown for 100 days before use in experiments.

### **Thermal imaging**

Infra-red (IR) images were obtained using a FLIR T620 thermal imaging camera (FLIR Systems, USA). Emissivity was set at 0.95. The setup consisted of a black sheet in the background with plants set at a 250 cm distance from the camera. Images were taken over a 15 minute timespan at midday. Images were processed using the Flir Quick Report software with suitable scale and palette ranges. Leaf temperature was analyzed at three different points in the middle of the flag leaf of the primary tiller from thermal images of each one of the six available plants per genotype, resulting in a total of 18 measurements per genotype.

#### **Determination of light reflection from leaves, absorptance and chlorophyll content,**

We determined reflectance and transmission from and through seven leaves of seven WT and *d1* plants each, using a Perkin Elmer Lambda 95 UV/VIS Spectrophotometer in the range between 450 and 700 nm. Measurements of reflectance were employed for the calculation of PRI and NDVI, and measurements of reflectance and transmission were both used to calculate the ratio of absorptance (A; A = 1 - (R + T)).

The activation of photoprotective mechanisms and decreased light use efficiency (LUE) in response to excessive irradiance are associated with a decrease in the photochemical or physiological reflectance index (PRI). The activation of photoprotective mechanisms as described by this index indicates the de-epoxidated state of the xanthophyll cycle (accumulation of zeaxanthin), which is a photoprotective mechanism (Gamon *et al.* 1992; Gamon *et al.* 1997; Nichol *et al.* 2006). This index is also indicative of light use efficiency based on the premise that the activation of photoprotective mechanisms is negatively correlated with the efficiency of light use (Garbulsky *et al.* 2014). PRI was calculated from measurements of reflectance (R) following Gamon *et al.* (1997) as:

(Gamon *et al.*, 1997)

#### PRI = (R<sup>531</sup> − R570)/(R<sup>531</sup> + R570)

Total chlorophyll concentration and chlorophyll a/b ratios were calculated spectrophotometrically following Porra *et al.* (1898), after extraction from fresh flag leaf tissue in 80% (v/v) acetone. Additionally, a proxy for chlorophyll content, the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), was calculated from measurements of reflectance (R) following Sims & Gamon (2002) as:

NDVI = (R750-R705)/(R750+R705)

### **Gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence measurements**

For gas exchange measurements, flag leaves were placed inside the 2 cm<sup>2</sup> leaf chamber of a portable gas exchange system (LI-COR 6400 with an integrated 6400-40 leaf chamber fluorometer, Li-COR, Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA) and measured at the point of maximal width. Flow rate in the chamber was adjusted to 300 μmol air s-1 . Steady-state measurements of the different physiological parameters were performed at either 500 μmol m-2 s -1 PPFD or at light saturation, which was 1500 μmol m-2 s -1 PPFD as determined from A/I curves (see methods section on chlorophyll fluorescence measurements). Light was provided by the Li-COR red/blue LED system with blue light comprising 10% of the total photon flux.

Light response curves (A/I) were first obtained by determining photosynthetic rates in flag leaves over a range of irradiances sequentially stepped down from high to low (2,000, 1,500, 1,000, 750, 500, 400, 300, 200, 100 and 0 μmol m-2 s -1 PPFD). Dual Peltier devices on the sides of the sensor head kept block temperature constant at 30°C, avoiding leaf heating at higher irradiances. Light-saturated photosynthetic capacity (Amax) and dark respiration (Rd) were calculated by fitting the data to the model of Hanson et al. (1987). Apparent quantum yield (Φ) was estimated as the first derivative of the fitted equation at PPFD = 0, and the light compensation point (Lcp), i.e. the fluence rate at which photosynthesis equals respiration, was calculated as the x-intercept of the model (Hanson 1987).

Chlorophyll fluorescence measurements were performed to quantify photoprotective and photoinhibitory mechanisms following standard protocols (Kautsky 1960), using the 6400-40 fluorometer (Li-COR, Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA) while maintaining the leaf chamber temperature constant at 30°C. For this, we adapted the leaf tissue to the dark for 20 minutes. The minimal level of fluorescence (Fo) was then measured by applying a modulated red LED light (630 nm, 1 mol m-2 s -1 PPFD). Individual leaves were then subjected to a short (0.8 seconds) super-saturating intensity light pulse at 7,000 mol m-2 s -1 PPFD. This protocol allows the precise calculation of the maximal fluorescence of the dark-adapted state (Fm). Next, leaf tissue was adapted for 20 min to either the light intensity representative of midday illumination (500 mol m-2 s -1 PPFD) or a high light intensity at which photosynthesis is approaching saturation, and photoinhibitory damage typically becomes severe (1,500 mol m-2 s -1 PPFD) as determined from A/I curves from 0 to 1,500 mol m-2 s -1 (described above). Under the imposed illumination intensity (500 or 1500 mol m-2 s -1 ), the fluorescence signal initially decreases slowly with the onset of CO<sup>2</sup> assimilation. The steady state level of fluorescence in the light was then measured (Fs), with a second supersaturating light pulse (0.8 seconds x 7,000 mol m-2 s -1 PPFD) to obtain a measure of maximal steady-state fluorescence (Fm') after which the background light was turned off, and a pulse of far-red light from LEDs (740 nm, 8 mol m-2 s -1 ) was simultaneously applied for 3 seconds to allow the re-oxidation of PSII; this protocol allows measurement of the minimal level of fluorescence in the light (F0').

Based on these measurements, the following parameters were calculated:

i) Photochemical quenching (qP, Maxwell *et al.* 1994) [qP = (Fm' − Fs')/ Fm' − F0')];

ii) non-photochemical quenching (NPQ, Bilger & Björkman, 1990) [NPQ = (F<sup>m</sup> −

Fm')/ Fm'];

iii) maximum quantum efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm, Krause & Weis 1991) [Fv/F<sup>m</sup> = (F<sup>m</sup> − F0)/ Fm];

iv) The photochemical efficiency of photosystem II (ΦPSII, Genty *et al.* 1989)[ΦPSII = (Fm' – Fs)/ Fm'].

The quantum yield of CO<sup>2</sup> fixation, CO2, i.e. the ratio of net CO<sup>2</sup> fixation to PPFD absorbed (Oberhuber *et al.* 1993), was calculated as:

$$\Phi_{CO2} = (A - A_{dark}) / (I \times \alpha_{leaf})$$

where A is the assimilation rate at the light intensity of interest and Adark is the dark assimilation rate (respiration). Leaf absorptance (leaf) is calculated as:

leaf = (blue × B + red × (100 − B))/ 100

Measurements of reflectance (R) and transmission (T) were both used to calculate the fraction of leaf absorptance (A; A = 1 − (R + T)). blue is the fraction of leaf absorptance at 460 nm (0.93 for WT and 0.94 for *d1*), red is leaf absorptance at 640 nm (0.87 for WT and 0.91 for *d1*) is the percentage of the light which is blue light (10%) as provided by the Licor 6400 LED system (Supporting Information Fig. S1).

We used the derived values of leaf absorptance, leaf, in the calculation of the electron transport rate (ETR) or the actual flux of photons driving PSII. ETR was calculated over the same range of light intensities as for the A/I curves, based on the equation:

$$ETR = (F_{m'} - F_{s}/F_{m'}) \times f \times I \times \alpha_{leaf}$$

where *f* is the proportion of absorbed light used by PSII and is assumed to be 0.5 for C<sup>3</sup> plants, and I is incident photon flux density.

Statistical analysis and graphical representation were conducted using Rgui<sup>23</sup>

### **RNA extraction and cDNA library preparation**

Two biological replicates of rice leaf blades, sampled from the flag leaf at the region of maximum width, were collected from two-month-old WT and *d1* plants grown as described above and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted from each sample using the NucleoSpin RNA Plant kit (Cat. # 740949, Macherey-Nagel). After examination of the quality (Bioanalyzer, Agilent) and quantity (NanoDrop 2000, ThermoFisher) of each RNA sample, aliquots of total RNA were submitted to the Genomics Core Facility at Penn State University for cDNA library preparation and next-generation sequencing on a Hiseq 2500 (Illumina). Approximately 40 million 150 bp single-end sequencing reads were obtained for each library.

## **RNA-Seq analysis**

Raw sequencing reads were examined using FastQC, and adapter sequences were trimmed from both ends of the raw reads using Trimmomatic (Bolger *et al.* 2014). Low-quality base calls (Q < 30) were also trimmed from both the 5' and 3' ends of the reads. Reads from each of the four libraries were then mapped independently to the rice genome (IRGSP-1.0) using Tophat2 (Kim *et al.* 2013), with a GTF (Gene Transfer File) annotation file supplied as an option. Transcript abundance and differential gene expression were calculated using Cufflinks (Trapnell *et al.* 2012). Gene expression levels were normalized using fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (FPKM). After confirming high correlation in transcriptome expression levels between biological replicates (see Supporting Information Fig. S2), data from the replicates were merged using Cuffmerge. Cuffdiff was used to calculate *q* values (FDR adjusted p-values) to identify differentially expressed (DE) genes between WT and *d1*, defined as those with *q* value less than 0.05 and with FPKM greater than 1 in either of datasets. DE genes with *q* values less than 0.05 were further divided into genes up-regulated in *d1* as compared to WT control (Supporting Information Table S1) and genes down-regulated in *d1* as compared to WT control (Supporting Information Table S2).

### **Gene Ontology**

Gene Ontology analyses were performed using PANTHER (Protein Analysis Through Evolutionary Relationships, [http://pantherdb.org;](http://pantherdb.org/) Mi *et al.* 2016). The Statistical Overrepresentation Test was applied to analyze the enrichment in each set of up- or downregulated DE genes. Both GO complete and GO Slim terms were used. Bonferroni correction was applied to correct for multiple testing. Fold enrichment was used to rank the different GO terms. We also performed the GO Statistical Overrepresentation Test using agriGO and SEACOMPARE analysis (Du *et al.* 2010) to cross-compare GO Statistical Overrepresentation between the genes up- and down-regulated in *d1*, to further confirm the results obtained with the PANTHER tool.

## **qPCR Analysis**

A third set of independent total RNA samples of WT and *d1* leaves at the same stage as for library preparation was isolated using the RNA plant kit (Cat. # 740949, Macherey-Nagel). One microgram of each RNA sample was used for first-strand cDNA synthesis using the SuperScript III first-strand synthesis system (Cat. #: 18080051, ThermoFisher). Rice *Actin1* (Os03g0718100) was used as the internal control. qRT-PCR analysis was performed using a Bio-Rad IQ5 Real-time PCR Detection Systems with SYBR Green Supermix (Cat. #. 1708880, Bio-Rad). ### **Results**

# **The erect architecture and higher reflectance of** *d1* **allows maintenance of lower leaf temperatures**

WT is a Taichung 65 variety (T65), a well-known rice variety of Taiwan where average daily temperatures between March and September typically range from 25 °C to 30 °C (Lur *et al.* 2009). *d1* plants exhibited phenotypes under our growth conditions consistent with those previously described in the literature (Fujisawa *et al.* 1999), including an erect leaf disposition and dwarf stature (Fig. 1a). From the analysis of thermal images taken at midday, the leaves of WT plants exhibited an elevated temperature (*p* = 2.271\*10-14), compared to *d1* leaves (Fig. 1b and 1c), consistent with previous observations on younger plants (Ferrero-Serrano & Assmann 2016). In comparison to WT, *d*1 showed a higher reflectance (*p* = 8.766 × 10-8 ; t-test) at 800 nm, Fig. 2a) in the infrared (IR, wavelengths greater than 750 nm), suggesting a greater capacity to reflect incoming light energy in that range and so reduce the heat loads associated with those wavelengths.

## *d1* **plants with erect leaf architecture exhibit a higher capacity for photoavoidance and lower degree of photoinhibitory damage to PSII**

The spectral characteristics of light reflectance from WT and *d1* leaves (Fig. 2a) result in a higher NDVI in *d1* plants (Fig. 2b; p = 1.236\*10-7 ; t-test) indicative of a higher chlorophyll content, which is consistent with the darker green color of *d1* leaves (Fig. 1). Chlorophyll quantitation from leaf tissue confirmed the increased chlorophyll content in *d1* leaves (Fig. 2c; p = 0.006), and a lower chlorophyll a/b ratio (Fig. 2d; p = 0.046).

Chlorophyll content is positively correlated with both light use efficiency (LUE) and with the photochemical reflectance index (PRI), a term commonly used to measure relative de-epoxidation state in the xanthophyll cycle: (Gamon *et al.* 1992; Guo & Trotter 2004). *d1* plants indeed exhibit a higher PRI relative to WT as determined by reflectance at 531 and 570 nm (Fig. 3a and 3b; p = 1.665\*10-10), indicative of a lower relative de-epoxidation state in the xanthophyll cycle and increased LUE in *d1*.

Photoinhibition was characterized as PSII efficiency, also known as maximum quantum yield (Fv/Fm). *d1* plants exhibited a slight but statistically significant higher Fv/F<sup>m</sup> than WT plants, indicating a lower degree of photoinhibitory damage (Fig. 4a; *p* = 0.037).

## *d1* **plants exhibit a higher capacity for photoprotection when** *d1* **and WT are exposed to the same light intensities**

The quantification of Hanson parameters (Hanson 1987) from A/I curves (Fig. 4b) confirmed that WT and *d1* exhibit similar photosynthetic rates throughout the range of light intensities, including in the region of the curve where photosynthesis is approaching light saturation (Asat; WT = 22.760.73; *d1* = 23.000.44). WT and *d1* also have similar dark respiration rates (Rd; WT = -1.270.47; *d1* = -1.160.26), light compensation points (Lcp; WT=27.238.99; *d1*=26.185.34), and apparent quantum yields (Φ; WT=0.490.06; *d1*=0.470.05) (all parameters calculated from the data of Fig. 4b). As light intensity increases, electron transport rate (ETR) increases for both genotypes, but WT reaches a higher ETR at PPFDs as light intensity increases (Fig. 4c). The proportion of absorbed photons used in PSII photochemistry calculated from both gas exchange data (CO2; Supporting Information Fig. S3) and chlorophyll fluorescence (PSII; Fig. 4d) showed no significant differences between WT and *d1* at either 500 μmol m-2 s -1 (*p* = 0.059 for PSII and *p* = 0.321 for CO2) or 1500 μmol m-2 s -1 (*p* = 0.197 for PSII and *p* = 0.321 for CO2) PPFD. While the proportion of PSII reaction centers that are open, qP, was not significantly different between the two genotypes at 500 μmol m-2 s -1 PPFD (Fig. 4e, *p* = 0.806), at saturating light intensity (1500 μmol m-2 s -1 PPFD), there were more reaction centers open in *d1* (Fig. 4e, *p* = 0.03601). *d1* plants exhibit greater non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) at both 500 and 1500 μmol m-2 s -1 PPFD (Fig. 4f; *p* = 0.008 and *p* <0.01).

### *d1* **mutation and differentially expressed genes**

RNA-seq data confirmed that there is a 2-bp deletion in the second-to-last exon of *RGA1* as previously described (Oki *et al.* 2009). **DISCUSSION**

## *d1* **plants with erect leaf architecture exhibit a greater capacity to avoid photoinhibitory damage**

Excessive irradiance and leaf temperatures increase photoinhibitory damage (Demmig-Adams & Adams III 1992; Greer *et al.* 1986). An efficient strategy for plants to increase light harvesting efficiency over the daily time course is through the avoidance of excessive light absorption at midday (photoavoidance) when both irradiance and the associated heat loads are higher, while maximizing the absorption of limited radiation early in the morning and late in the afternoon.

Previous observations on the relationship between leaf angle and leaf temperature showed that increased leaf angles, as in *d1* plants (Fig. 1a), are effective in reducing leaf temperature (Medina *et al.* 1978). As observed in Figures 1b and 1c, and reported previously for younger plants (Ferrero-Serrano & Assmann 2016), *d1* plants exhibit a lower leaf temperature at midday than WT plants. Most of the thermal radiation absorbed by leaves, as for any object, is in the infrared region of the spectrum (IR, wavelengths greater than 750 nm). Lower leaf temperature in *d1* has been shown to be responsible for decreased transpirational water loss relative to WT, providing *d1* mutants with increased drought tolerance (Ferrero-Serrano & Assmann 2016). The greater reflectance of light in the IR by *d1* leaves (Fig. 2a) suggests a greater capacity to avoid elevated leaf temperatures. Genes coding for Hsp90 family chaperones and chaperonin class proteins were over-represented in the set of genes with transcript abundance lower in *d1* than in WT (PC00028 and PC00073; Supporting Information S11), consistent with the well-known up-regulation of Hsp transcripts under high-temperature stress (Lindquist 1986; Xu *et al.* 2012)*.* 

Perhaps the most universal mechanism of response to the amount of radiation intercepted by plants is qE. qE or flexible NPQ is a rapidly inducible and reversible component of NPQ with a photoprotective function under fluctuating light intensities. Typically, qE increases at midday, when light intensities are higher and plants are more susceptible to photoinhibitory damage (Ishida *et al.* 2013). Two processes are necessary for qE: the formation of a pH gradient across the thylakoid membrane resulting in the alkalization of the stroma (Horton *et al.* 1996), and the reversible formation of zeaxanthin by de-epoxidation of violaxanthin such that the excess absorbed energy is thermally dissipated (Demmig-Adams & Adams III 1996). Species with a horizontal leaf disposition exhibit an increased de-epoxidation state of the xanthophyll cycle and a decrease in LUE at midday (Lovelock & Clough 1992), as the requirements for energy dissipation increase. For instance, by forcing the leaves of field-grown rice into a horizontal position, photoinhibition increases (Chen *et al.* 2003).

Increased light use efficiency (LUE) favors sustained productivity in crops (Charbonnier *et al.* 2017). When plants experience excessive radiation, and increase the deepoxidation state of the xanthophyll cycle and consequently decrease LUE, they show a lower PRI (Evain *et al.* 2004; Nichol *et al.* 2006). Because *d1* plants exhibit an architecture associated with avoidance of excessive light intensities at midday, we predicted a lower xanthophyll de-epoxidation state of *d1* plants relative to WT, which has a more horizontal leaf disposition (Fig. 1). To test this hypothesis, we evaluated the photochemical reflectance index, PRI (Gamon *et al.* 1992; Peñuelas *et al.* 1995) of WT and *d1* leaves at midday. The results shown in Figures 3a and 3b confirmed a higher PRI for *d1* plants than for WT, indicative of a lower de-epoxidation state of the xanthophyll cycle and increased LUE. It has been previously shown that rice cultivars with more horizontal leaf orientation suffer greater levels of photoinhibition even though the de-epoxidation state of the xanthophyll cycle was increased as compared to cultivars with erect leaf disposition (Murchie *et al.* 1999). Optimal light harvesting by means of upright leaf disposition may prove more efficient than the activation of flexible quenching (qE) to avoid photoinhibitory damage. For example, newly developed super-rice hybrids selected based on their erect leaf architecture exhibit a lower degree of photoinhibition than traditional hybrid rice (Wang *et al.* 2005).

*d1* **exhibits a lower de-epoxidation state of the xanthophyll cycle and higher chlorophyll content resulting in more efficient light harvesting.**

GO analysis of up-regulated transcripts in *d1* using PANTHER (Mi *et al.* 2016) revealed enrichment in genes involved in response to radiation and light stimulus (GO:0009314 and GO:0009416.Supporting Information Table S4). This enrichment was confirmed using agriGO (Du *et al.* 2010; GO:0006721 and GO:0006633). Particularly, the over-enrichment of genes involved in light harvesting (GO:0009765, Supporting Information Table S4) indicates increased mRNA levels of genes involved in the absorption and transfer of light energy. Over-excitation of PSII reaction centers when subjected to supra-optimal irradiance is deleterious, and LHCII proteins undergo degradation upon increasing light intensities (Forsberg *et al.* 2005; Kouril *et al.* 2013; Lindahl *et al.* 1995; Yang *et al.* 1998). LHCII contains three major proteins (Lhcb1, Lhcb 2 and Lhcb 3) and three minor proteins (Lhcb4, Lhcb5, and Lhcb 6; Liu *et al.* 2004). The expression of all LHCII subunit-encoding mRNAs was greater in *d1* than in WT (significantly so for Lhcb2, Lhcb3, Lhcb5 and Lhcb6; Fig. 5; Supporting Information Table S16), consistent with the minimal exposure of *d1* to supraoptimal irradiance. Conversely, the expression of genes coding for early light-induced proteins (ELIPs) was lower in *d1* than in WT plants (Suppl. Table 5). ELIPs are pigmentbinding proteins (Krol *et al.* 1995) that belong to the extended LHC protein superfamily associated with PSII (Adamska & Kloppstech 1991). While the expression of LHC mRNAs is known to decrease as light intensity increases, expression of ELIPs increases proportionally with light intensity (Adamska *et al.* 1992; Meyer & Kloppstech 1984). Although the function of ELIPs is under debate (Montane & Kloppstech 2000; Rossini *et al.* 2006), the lower levels of expression of genes encoding ELIP and the higher expression of LHC genes in *d1* relative to WT leaves again suggest lower exposure to photoinhibitory irradiances in *d1*. We observed the same trend in PSI antenna system (LHCI) genes *Lhca1*, *Lhca2*, *Lhca3,* and *Lhca4* (Jansson 1999), which were all up-regulated in *d1*, although only Lhca2 was significantly so (Fig. 5, Supporting Information Table S17).

Carotenoids in light harvesting complexes play a key role in protecting the photosynthetic machinery from overexcitation. While the PRI index indicates the lower activation of qE in *d1* as a result of more efficient light harvesting, this is not to say that if subjected to excessive irradiances *d1* has a lower *capacity* for qE quenching. Accordingly, *BETA-CAROTENE HYDROXYLASE 3* (*BCH3*), encoding a beta-carotene hydroxylase enzyme that regulates the synthesis of zeaxanthin from beta-carotene, was up-regulated in *d1.*  Furthermore*,* both *ZEAXANTHIN EPOXIDASE* (*ZEP*) and *VIOLAXANTHIN DE-EPOXIDASE* (*VDE*), enzymes regulating the interconversion of zeaxanthin to violaxanthin, were also up-regulated in *d1*, although the differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 6, Supporting Information Table S18).

Chlorophyll content of rice leaves is an important agronomic trait and has long been used to predict yield (Peng *et al.* 1996). The fact that *d1* plants have an increased chlorophyll content as shown by both NDVI (Fig. 2a,b) and chlorophyll quantification (Fig. 2c) suggests that under steady state conditions *d1* leaves, due to their erect architecture, are exposed to a lower light intensities at midday: shade leaves typically increase chlorophyll content as a strategy to increase light capture (Boardman 1977; Ort *et al.* 2011). Additionally, *d1* exhibited lower chlorophyll a/b ratios, also typical of plants growing under lower light intensities (Anderson 1986). Consistent with these observations, up-regulated genes in *d1* were enriched in genes associated with the chlorophyll biosynthetic process (GO:0015995; Supporting Information Table S4). For instance, the gene encoding Glucokinase 1 (*GLK1*), which is required for chloroplast development and responsible for synchronizing the expression of chlorophyll biosynthetic enzymes in varying environmental conditions (Waters *et al.* 2009) as well as the mRNAs of several genes encoding key regulators of chlorophyll biosynthesis were up-regulated in *d1* (Fig. 7; Supporting Information S19), including *MAGNESIUM CHELATASE SUBUNIT H* (*CHLH*), *MAGNESIUM CHELATASE SUBUNIT I* (*CHLI*), *PROTOCHLOROPHYLLIDE OXIDOREDUCTASE-A* (*PORA*) and *CHLOROPHYLL-A OXYGENASE* (*CAO*).

Excessive illumination promotes chlorophyll degradation, as a result of excess photon absorption, photoinhibitory damage and excessive ROS production. The decreased capacity of WT to avoid excessive irradiance is correlated with the down-regulation in *d1* of mRNA encoding several key enzymes that promote chlorophyll degradation. The *Stay-Green Rice* gene (*SGR*) exhibits a higher expression in rice leaves as light intensity increases (Rong *et al.* 2013). SGR has been proposed to be a positive regulator of chlorophyll degradation (Hörtensteiner 2009; Park *et al.* 2007) in response to excessive illumination, in a process mediated by PSII protein THF1 (Huang *et al.* 2013), a known interactor of G in Arabidopsis. The observed higher levels of *SGR* in *WT* than in *d1* are consistent with a higher degree of photoinhibitory-mediated chlorophyll degradation in WT. Similarly, the gene encoding chlorophyllase (*CHL*), the enzyme responsible for the degradation of chlorophyll A, and the gene encoding red chlorophyll catabolite reductase (*RCCR*), the enzyme responsible for the reduction of red chlorophyll catabolite, were expressed at lower levels in *d1* as compared to WT (Fig. 7, Supporting Information Table S20)*.*

These conclusions drawn from mRNA-seq results are consistent with our physiological measurements and sustain the conclusion that *d1* plants, with erect leaf architecture and more efficient light harvesting, exhibit a lower de-epoxidation state of the xanthophyll cycle and higher chlorophyll content.

# *d1* **plants exhibit increased transcript levels of genes involved in light harvesting and photosynthesis**

The set of genes showing increased mRNA levels in *d1* exhibited an over-representation of genes encoding for proteins located in PSII (GO:0009523). The excitation energy collected from LHCII is passed into the core PSII reaction center core protein D1/PsbA, which binds all the redox-active cofactors involved in the energy conversion process in PSII (Nanba & Satoh 1987). In consonance with our observations for LHCII (Fig. 5; Supporting Information Table S16), mRNAs encoding many components of PSII exhibited a significantly increased level in *d1* compared to WT (Fig. 5; Supporting Information Table S21), which suggests both a larger pool of PSII reaction centers to match a larger LHCII antenna and a lower degree of photoinhibition in *d1* relative to WT. The mRNAs encoding PsbO and PsbP were more abundant (although only PsbP was found to be significantly so) in *d1* than in WT while *PsbQ* was the only LHCII-PSII annotated mRNA found at lower levels in *d1* than WT (Fig. 5; Supporting Information Table 21). In *Nicotiana tabacum* plants in which the levels of PsbP and PsbQ were down-regulated by RNAi, *PsbP* but not *PsbQ* RNAi lines were hypersensitive to light (Ifuku *et al.* 2005), so PsbQ function may not be relevant to the light intensity responses studied here. All annotated PSII mRNAs encoding low molecular mass proteins with an important role in PSII assembly, stability, and repair of PSII, were abundant in *d1* relative to WT (Fig. 5; Supporting Information Table S21). Chlorophyll fluorescence revealed a higher potential quantum yield (Fv/Fm) of *d1*, which indicates a larger number of PSII reaction centers and lower degree of photoinhibitory damage under steady-state conditions (Fig. 4a).

While photoinhibitory damage to PSII is reversible, damage to PSI is believed to be essentially irreversible (Tikkanen *et al.* 2014). An increased rate of cyclic electron transport around PSI contributes to the protection of PSI (Essemine *et al.* 2017; Huang *et al.* 2016; Yamori & Shikanai 2016). Transcriptome analysis supports a higher rate of cyclic relative to linear electron transport in *d1* (Fig. 5, Supporting Information Table S22). There are two pathways for cyclic electron transport: PGR5- and NDH-dependent pathways. *PGR5* was found to be non-significantly up-regulated in *d1*, but *PGRL1A* and *PGRL1B*, essential components of the PGR5 dependent pathway, were significantly up-regulated in *d1*. Similarly, genes coding for proteins regulating the NDH-dependent pathway (*NDF6*, *NDH-0*, and *NDF1*) were also up-regulated in *d1*. WT favors linear electron transport compared to *d1* plants, as shown from the measurements of ETR (Kalaji *et al.* 2017; Fig. 4c). Consistently, the gene coding for the enzyme ferredoxin-NADP<sup>+</sup> oxidoreductase (*FNR*), responsible for the final step of linear electron transport and NADPH production (Mulo 2011), was downregulated in *d1* (Fig. 5, Supporting Information Table S22), which taken together with the upregulation of cyclic electron transport coding genes, suggest a prioritization of cyclic vs. linear electron flow in *d*1, increasing its photoprotective capacity.

Damage to PSI occurs when the photoavoidance and photoprotective mechanisms fail to prevent electron flow from PSII exceeding the capacity of PSI acceptors and represents the ultimate consequence of photoinhibitory damage. GO analysis revealed that PSI cellular components were enriched in the set of genes up-regulated in *d1* using both the PANTHER (Mi *et al.* 2016, GO:0009538, Supporting Information Table S8) and agriGO analysis tools (Du *et al.* 2010; GO:0009522). All annotated PSI subunit-encoding mRNAs were present in *d1* at levels close to double those of WT (Fig. 5, Supporting Information Table S23).

## *d1* **plants exhibit a greater photoprotective capacity than WT in the absence of photoavoidance when subjected to the same light intensity**

So far, we have described a greater photoavoidance and capacity for photoprotection of *d1* plants under growth conditions based on data from thermal images, reflectance, and RNAseq. It was also of interest to ascertain what physiological processes occur when individual leaves of *d1* and WT are subjected to the same light intensities. In other words, in the absence of photoavoidance mechanisms such as differential leaf angles, do WT and *d1* still exhibit differential photoprotective characteristics? Accordingly, we studied the changes in photosynthetic rates when light intensity was manipulated in light response curves (A/I curves). From the analysis of A/I responses, no differences were found between WT and *d1* (Fig. 4b)*.* In other words, when experimentally subjected to the same light intensities both genotypes exhibited similar rates of carbon assimilation. To assess the capacity of WT and *d1*  for photochemical and non-photochemical quenching under the identical amount of light applied to the leaf, we employed chlorophyll fluorescence measurements (Kalaji *et al.* 2017; Krause & Weis 1984; Maxwell & Johnson 2000) at both the average steady-state illumination at midday, 500 µmol m<sup>2</sup> s -1 and at what was determined to be a saturating light intensity for photosynthesis from the A/I curves (1,500 µmol m<sup>2</sup> s -1 ). Analysis of the response of ETR to light intensity unveiled an increased capacity for ETR of WT under saturating light intensities (Fig. 4c). This is not surprising as the capacity for electron transport in sun plants is greater than in shade plants (Boardman 1977). Higher growth rates (as in WT) are associated with higher respiration rates (Lambers & Poorter 1992). We observed a decreased capacity for ETR (Fig. 4c) but similar assimilation rates as we increased light intensity (Fig. 4b), in *d1* relative to WT. We hypothesize that this may be explained by a lower light enhanced mitochondrial respiration (LEDR) in *d1*. We observed similar rates of respiration in the dark (Rd), between WT and *d1*, from the analysis of A/I curves (Fig. 4c). However, LEDR is a process resulting in increased respiration rates upon illumination (Atkin *et al.* 2000). A photoavoidance mechanism such as the one we describe in *d1* would explain its lower LEDR relative to WT. We support this hypothesis by the observed enrichment in the set of genes down-regulated in *d1* of mRNAs encoding enzymes of the TCA cycle (R-OSA-71403; Supporting Information S13). As a result, we observe similar A/I curves between WT and *d1* (Fig. 4b).

Similar quantum yields (ΦPSII) found in WT and *d1* both at steady state and saturating light intensities (Fig. 4d and Supporting Information Fig. S3), suggest that the genotypes do not differ significantly in the proportion of photons used in photochemistry when subjected to identical irradiances. While using the same proportion of photons in photochemistry compared to WT, *d1* exhibits a larger proportion of PSII reaction centers that are still open at high light intensities compared to WT, as suggested by its higher qP (Fig. 4e). In other words, a larger quantum flux would be necessary to reach the same proportion of filled reaction centers in *d1* relative to WT. From Fig. 4d is trivial to infer 1-qP, which is also known as the excitation pressure of PSII (Maxwell *et al.* 1994). The larger value of this parameter in *d1* at 1,500 µmol m<sup>2</sup> s -1 PPFD indicates a lower relative reduction state of the pool of plastoquinone Q<sup>a</sup> compared to WT. The lower excitation pressure of *d1* may be explained by its higher capacity for NPQ (photoprotection capacity, Fig. 4f) when leaves are subjected to the same incoming irradiance, indicating that *d1* has a greater capacity to reverse (quench) within minutes moderate photoinhibitory damage (qE + qT). Consistent with a greater capacity for rapid quenching of excess energy (NPQ), and lower excitation pressure (1-qP) of *d1*, the higher potential quantum yield (Fv/Fm) of *d1* indicates a larger number of PSII reaction centers and lower degree of photoinhibitory damage (Fig. 4a).

# **Increased light harvesting efficiency and increased capacity to dissipate excitation energy may contribute to decreased ROS production in** *d1*

ROS as signaling molecules control numerous processes including stress tolerance, growth, pathogen defense, programmed cell death, and stomatal behavior. Loss-of-function mutations of *GPA1*, the canonical Gα in Arabidopsis, result in a phenotype with reduced ROS accumulation in the chloroplast (Wei *et al.* 2008) and guard cells (Zhang *et al.* 2011). In rice the *d1* mutant exhibits a reduced cell death phenotype (Steffens & Sauter 2009) and reduced ROS accumulation, so RGA1 also has been proposed to regulate ROS levels (Suharsono *et al.* 2002). ROS is an unavoidable byproduct of photosynthesis. Under high light intensities, photoinhibitory damage to PSII proteins occurs, but there is also an increase in the production of ROS. This overproduction of ROS aggravates photoinhibitory damage as it causes the inhibition of *de novo* synthesis of new PSII proteins, reducing the rate of repair of photoinhibitory damage (Nishiyama *et al.* 2011).

We hypothesize that due to the more efficient light harvesting in *d1*, there is a lower production of ROS as a byproduct of photosynthesis. This hypothesis is consistent with the lower expression of mRNAs encoding diverse ROS scavengers seen in *d1* relative to WT; WT exhibits an over-representation compared to *d1* of transcripts in the category "Detoxification of Reactive Oxygen Species" (R-OSA-3299685; Supporting Information Table S13 & S24). As one example, flavonols reduce light-induced oxidative damage (Agati & Tattini 2010; Guidi *et al.* 2016). GO analysis revealed in WT an over-representation of genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis (Fig. 8; GO:0009813, Supporting Information Tables S5 and S25). This is consistent with the increased light intensity naturally encountered by WT leaves at midday and overproduction of ROS, increasing the requirement for ROS detoxification mechanisms. Interestingly, in Arabidopsis, G interacts with Pirin1, a regulator of flavonol levels in plants (Lapik & Kaufman 2003).

Also supporting the idea of WT having a higher production of ROS, the expression of several genes coding for lipoxygenases (LOXs) was found to be lower in *d1* than in WT (Supporting Information Table S26). This is consistent with an increased lipid peroxidation in WT, a process that contributes to the photoinhibition process under excessive illumination (Chan *et al.* 2012). Similarly, a diverse array of ROS scavengers was differentially expressed between WT and *d1* with 85% of them being down-regulated in *d1* (Supporting Information Table S24).

## **Implications**

Heterotrimeric G proteins control plant growth and cell proliferation (Ullah 2001; Ishida *et al*. 2014; Bommert *et al*. 2013), and the dwarf phenotype of *d1* rice exemplifies this. Gα in rice also plays a regulatory role in tolerance of biotic (Suharsono *et al*. 2002) as well as multiple abiotic stress conditions, including drought, salinity, heat, and cold (Ferrero-Serrano & Assmann 2016; Jangam *et al.* 2016; Yadav *et al*. 2013). Arabidopsis *GPA1* null mutants are also stress tolerant (Chakraborty *et al*. 2015) and under-produce ROS relative to WT (Zhang *et al*. 2011). How G proteins regulate tolerance of such a wide range of stress conditions remains elusive, but all these stresses have in common an associated increase in photoinhibitory damage and overproduction of ROS. ROS production from environmental stress conditions suppresses the repair of PSII (Murata *et al*. 2007; Nishiyama *et al.* 2011), aggravating the degree of photoinhibitory damage and consequently reducing stress tolerance.

The dwarf and erect phenotype of *d1* (Oki *et al.* 2009) thus provides the benefits of a decrease in photoinhibitory damage and an increase in tolerance of other stresses, albeit at the cost of a decrease in growth plasticity, reduced potential for avoidance of shading by elongation growth, and decreased total light interception. Plant plasticity is under genetic control (Paxman 1956; Price *et al.* 2003), and heterotrimeric G-proteins were also previously proposed to modulate plasticity based on research in Arabidopsis (Assmann 2004; Nilson & Assmann 2010).

## **Conclusion**

Leaf angle is a trait with important consequences for light interception (Falster & Westoby 2003; Murchie *et al.* 1999). Breeding for upright leaf disposition has been long recognized to be important in rice (Murchie *et al.* 1999; Sinclair & Sheehy 1999) as well as in other major cereals such as wheat (Innes & Blackwell 1983) and maize (Lambert & Johnson 1978). Its importance is confirmed by the positive correlation found between erect leaf architecture and yield (Ariyanayagam 2010; Sakamoto 2005).

Increased photoavoidance and photoprotection in *d1* are agronomic traits of interest (Murchie 2017). It has been suggested that wheat genotypes with erect leaf disposition and consequent more efficient daily light harvesting and lower incident irradiance peaks at midday, can fix a larger daily amount of carbon dioxide relative to genotypes with lax leaf posture (Austin *et al.* 1976). Here we show that the *d1* plants are less sensitive to photoinhibition relative to WT. Conversely, the increased sensitivity to photoinhibitory damage found in WT, while not severe in the absence of stress, may be sufficient to confer reduced tolerance in combination with other stresses.

. The role of RGA1 in modulating photoinhibition is supported by transcriptome differences, revealing the up-regulation in the *d1* mutant of chlorophyll biosynthesis, LHCII-PSII, cyclic electron transport and LHCI-PSI genes, indicating a lower degree of photoinhibitory damage, vs. the up-regulation in WT of transcripts encoding HSPs and proteins associated with chlorophyll degradation, and ROS scavenging. Chlorophyll fluorescence confirmed an increased rate of energy dissipation in *d1* (NPQ), and increased variable fluorescence (Fv/Fm), indicating a higher potential quantum yield and lower degree of photoinhibitory damage. We speculate that decreased ROS production in *d1* (Steffens & Sauter 2009), as in Arabidopsis G null mutants (Wei *et al.* 2008; Zhang *et al.* 2011), contributes to this phenomenon. We thus propose the canonical heterotrimeric Gα protein as a key negative regulator of photoavoidance and photoprotection, parameters known to impact the yield potential of cereals and their tolerance to abiotic stress. The exact cellular mechanisms by which heterotrimeric G proteins evoke changes in photosynthetic parameters will be an important topic for future research.
