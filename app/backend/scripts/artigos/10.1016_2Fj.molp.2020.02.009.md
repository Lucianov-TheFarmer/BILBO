**Short Summary:** Rice tillering is a major determinant of rice architecture and grain yield. This study shows that RNA-directed DNA methylation at Miniature Inverted-repeat Transposable Elements (MITEs) positively and negatively regulates D14 and OsMIR156 family members, respectively, thereby contributing to the regulation of rice tillering. 

### **ABSTRACT**

Tillering is a major determinant of rice plant architecture and grain yield. Here we report that depletion of rice OsNRPD1a and OsNRPD1b, two orthologues of the largest subunit of RNA polymerase IV, leads to a high-tillering phenotype, in addition to dwarfism and smaller panicles. OsNRPD1a and OsNRPD1b are required for the production of 24-nt siRNAs that direct DNA methylation at transposable elements (TEs) including Miniature Inverted-repeat TEs (MITEs). Interestingly, many genes are regulated either positively or negatively by TE methylation. Among them, OsMIR156d and OsMIR156j, which promote rice tillering, are repressed by CHH methylation at two MITEs in the promoters. By contrast, D14, which suppresses rice tillering, is activated by CHH methylation at a MITE in its downstream. Our findings reveal control of rice tillering by RdDM at MITEs and provide potential targets for agronomic trait enhancement by epigenome editing.

### **INTRODUCTION**

Cytosine DNA methylation is a conserved epigenetic mechanism that controls transposable elements (TEs) and regulates gene expression, and thus is crucial for the maintenance of genome stability, plant development and responses to stresses (Law and Jacobsen, 2010; Zhang et al., 2018). In plants, DNA methylation occurs in CG, CHG and CHH (H represents A, T or C) sequence contexts and is established through an RNA-directed DNA Methylation (RdDM) pathway (Law and Jacobsen, 2010; Matzke et al., 2009).

RdDM entails the transcription of target sequences by a plant-specific RNA polymerase Pol IV (Herr et al., 2005; Kanno et al., 2005; Li et al., 2015; Onodera et al., 2005). Pol IV transcripts are used as templates to generate double-stranded RNAs (dsRNAs) by RNA-dependent RNA polymerase 2 (RDR2) (Haag et al., 2012; Xie et al., 2004). dsRNAs are then processed by Dicer-like 3 (DCL3) into 24-nucleotide (nt) small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) (Blevins et al., 2015; Qi et al., 2005; Xie et al., 2004; Zhai et al., 2015), which are loaded into ARGONAUTE 4 (AGO4) (Li et al., 2006; Qi et al., 2006; Ye et al., 2012; Zilberman et al., 2003). Through base-pairing between 24-nt siRNAs and scaffold transcripts produced by another plant-specific RNA polymerase Pol V (Wierzbicki et al., 2008), siRNA/AGO4 complexes direct DNA methyltransferase DOMAIN REARRANGED METHYLTRANSFERASE 2 (DRM2) to target loci for de novo

DNA methylation (Cao and Jacobsen, 2002; Wierzbicki et al., 2008; Zhong et al., 2014).

In Arabidopsis thaliana, the majority of RdDM targets are TEs and repetitive sequences (Cokus et al., 2008; Lister et al., 2008; Stroud et al., 2013). TEs and repeats are concentrated in pericentromeric regions, where genes are rare (Arabidopsis Genome, 2000). This may partly explain why the Arabidopsis mutants for the key RdDM components do not manifest obvious developmental abnormalities (Cao and Jacobsen, 2002; Herr et al., 2005; Pontier et al., 2005; Xie et al., 2004). However, in the staple crop rice (Oryza sativa), TEs compose at least 35% of its genome and frequently reside in the euchromatic regions (International Rice Genome Sequencing, 2005; Tan et al., 2016; Wei et al., 2014; Zemach et al., 2010). This may increase the likelihood that RdDM at TEs regulates gene expression and development. Supporting this notion, knocking down OsDCL3a that produces 24-nt siRNAs, or knocking out OsDRM2 that is required for most of the CHH methylation in rice, leads to severe developmental defects including dwarfism and smaller panicle size (Tan et al., 2016; Wei et al., 2014). The osdcl3a knockdown lines also have enlarged leaf angle (Wei et al., 2014), whereas the osdrm2 mutants are sterile (Tan et al., 2016).

# **Depletion of OsNRPD1 Causes Global Loss of 24-nt siRNAs and CHH Methylation**

We focused on how OsNRPD1a/b regulate rice tillering. Since the double OsNRPD1a/b knockout lines were found to exhibit embryonic lethality, the OsNRPD1a/b RNAi line, osnrpd1-1, was used for collecting shoot bases, where the formation and outgrowth of tiller buds occur. We profiled small RNAs (sRNAs) in the shoot bases of WT and osnrpd1-1 by performing sRNA sequencing (Supplemental Table 1 and 2). The abundance of 24-nt siRNAs, which are dispersed in the chromosomes, was greatly reduced in osnrpd1-1 (Figure 2A, 2B

and Supplemental Figure 3). Consistent with previous findings(Wei et al., 2014), the majority of 24-nt siRNA clusters were derived from TEs, which include MITEs, other types of DNA TEs, long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons, and non-LTR retrotransposons that are further classified into long interspersed elements (LINEs) and short interspersed elements (SINEs) (Figure 2C). The levels of 24-nt siRNAs derived from all types of TEs were reduced in osnrpd1-1 (Figure 2D). More than half of TE-derived siRNA clusters was enriched at MITEs (Figure 2C). Considering that MITEs are preferentially inserted in gene-rich regions (Bureau and Wessler, 1994), we then analyzed the genomic distribution of siRNA clusters around protein-coding genes (PCGs). In WT, siRNA clusters tended to overaccumulate in the upstream region of transcription start sites (TSS) and the downstream region of transcription terminal sites (TTS), showing a distribution

pattern similar to that of MITEs, but not other types of TEs (Figure 2E). However, this strong correlation was lost in osnrpd1-1 (Figure 2E).

We next examined genome-wide cytosine methylation in the shoot base of WT and osnrpd1-1 by performing whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (BS-seq) (Supplemental Table 1 and 3). Our results showed that global CG and CHG methylation levels remained unchanged but CHH methylation level was significantly reduced in osnrpd1-1 (Figure 2F). In total, we identified 13,839 CHH

hypomethylated differentially methylated regions (hypo-DMRs) and 512 CHH hypermethylated-DMRs (hyper-DMRs) in osnrpd1-1 (Figure 2G and 2H). CG and CHG hypo-DMRs and hyper-DMRs in osnrpd1-1 were also identified but the numbers are much smaller (Figure 2G). Distribution analysis of CHH hypo-DMRs in osnrpd1-1 revealed that they were highly enriched at MITEs (Figure 2I). Furthermore, 54% of CHH hypo-DMRs were located in the genic regions (Figure 2J). When the genic regions were broken down into promoter (1kb upstream of TSS), 5' UTR, 3' UTR, CDS, intron and downstream region (1kb downstream of TTS), we found that the percentages of CHH hypo-DMRs in different types of regions were 48%, 2%, 3%, 5%, 16% and 25%, respectively (Figure 2J). The near absence of DMRs in exons may be explained by strong selection pressure against TE insertions in exons (Lisch, 2013). We also analyzed the average methylation levels along CHH hypo-DMR-associated PCGs in three cytosine contexts. No obvious difference of CG methylation was observed between WT and osnrpd1-1 (Figure 2K). The levels of CHG methylation in the promoter and downstream regions were decreased in osnrpd1-1 (Figure 2L). However, the levels of CHH methylation in the promoter, gene body and downstream regions were all decreased in osnrpd1-1 (Figure 2M).

Together, our results demonstrate that knocking down OsNRPD1a/b greatly impairs RdDM in the rice shoot base.

# **Subsets of the CHH hypo-DMR-associated Genes Are Positively or Negatively Regulated by RdDM**

To assess the effects of CHH methylation on gene expression, we performed mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq) using the shoot bases of WT and osnrpd1-1 (Supplemental Table1 and 4). There were 7,147 CHH hypo-DMR-associated genes and 5,895 were detected to be expressed in either WT or osnrpd1-1 (FPKM>=0.5). Among them, 94 genes were upregulated; to our surprise, 177 genes were significantly downregulated in osnrpd1-1 (fold change>=2; q-value < 0.05; Figure 3A and 3B). This suggests that subsets of the CHH hypo-DMR-associated genes are either positively or negatively regulated by RdDM.

To assess whether the location of CHH hypo-DMRs determines the effects of CHH hypomethylation on gene expression, we analyzed the distribution of CHH hypo-DMRs in different regions of upregulated and downregulated genes. We found that in upregulated and downregulated genes, the percentages of genes with hypo-DMRs in their promoter, intron, 5' UTR, 3' UTR or downstream region were not significantly different (Figure 3C), suggesting that the positive or negative effect of CHH methylation on gene expression is not simply determined by the location of CHH hypo-DMRs.

### **Their Promoters**

To elucidate the detailed mechanisms by which CHH methylation controls rice tillering, we studied the regulation of individual genes by CHH methylation and the contribution of such regulation to rice tillering.

Members of the OsMIR156 family produce miRNAs that repress SQUAMOSA–PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN-LIKE (SPL) family genes and thereby promote tillering (Chen et al., 2015; Jiao et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2015; Luo et al., 2012; Miura et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2015; Xie et al., 2006). There are 12 members in the OsMIR156 gene family (Supplemental Figure 4A). OsMIR156a to OsMIR156j encode the same mature miRNA OsmiR156a-j, while OsMIR156k and OsMIR156l encode mature OsmiR156k and OsmiR156l, respectively.

We found two CHH hypo-DMRs in the putative promoter regions of OsMIR156j (Figure 4A) and OsMIR156d (Supplemental Figure 4B), respectively. Each CHH hypo-DMR is associated with two MITEs (MITE #1 and #2). Locus-specific bisulfite sequencing analyses confirmed that DNA methylation, especially CHG and CHH methylation at OsMIR156d MITE #2, OsMIR156j MITE #1 and #2 was reduced significantly in the double OsNRPD1a/b knockout line (Supplemental Figure 4C). These results led us to examine changes in the expression levels of OsMIR156d and OsMIR156j primary transcripts, pri-OsmiR156d and pri-OsmiR156j in osnrpd1-1. mRNA-seq data showed that

pri-OsmiR156d and pri-OsmiR156j were both upregulated in osnrpd1-1 (Supplemental Figure 4D). Interestingly, among 10 members that encode OsmiR156a-j, only OsMIR156d and OsMIR156j were abundantly expressed in the shoot base (Supplemental Figure 4D). The upregulation of these two pri-miRNAs in osnrpd1-1/2 and osnrpd1ab double knockout lines was confirmed by RT-qPCR (Figure 4B and Supplemental Figure 4E). Moreover, the level of mature OsmiR156a-j, but not OsmiR156k and OsmiR156l, was greatly increased in osnrpd1-1 and osnrpd1ab double knockout lines as determined by sRNA sequencing (Figure 4C) and northern blot (Figure 4D and Supplemental Figure 4F). Consistent with the overaccumulation of OsmiR156a-j, the expression of Ideal Plant Architecture 1 (IPA1, also known as OsSPL14), a tillering repressor targeted by OsmiR156a-j, was repressed in osnrpd1-1/2 (Figure 4E). These results together suggest that defects of CHH methylation at MITEs in the putative promoter regions of OsMIR156d and OsMIR156j cause the overaccumulation of OsmiR156a-j and the repression of IPA1. Dysregulation of the OsmiR156/IPA1 module contributes to the high-tillering phenotype of the mutant lines for OsNRPD1a/b.

miR156 are differentially expressed in different tissues and at different developmental stages in Arabidopsis and rice (Wang et al., 2009; Wu et al., 2009; Xie et al., 2006; Xu et al., 2018). To demonstrate that the expression of OsmiR156

is regulated by DNA methylation under physiological conditions, we examined whether CHH methylation at MITEs in the promoter regions of OsMIR156d and OsMIR156j negatively correlates with expression of OsmiR156a-j in rice tissues. We detected pri-miR156d, pri-miR156j and mature OsmiR156a-j in leaf blade, leaf sheath and shoot base of 4-week-old rice seedlings. Our results showed that the levels of pri-miR156d, pri-miR156 and OsmiR156a-j were lowest in the shoot base (Figure 5A and 5B). As expected, the expression level of IPA1 was highest in shoot base (Figure 5A). We next performed locus-specific bisulfite sequencing and found that the levels of CHH DNA methylation at two MITEs in the promoters of OsMIR156d and OsMIR156j were highest in the shoot base (Figure 5C-5D and Supplemental Figure 4G-4H), correlating well with the repression of OsMIR156d and OsMIR156j expression in the shoot base (Figure 5B). These data support the notion that CHH methylation at MITEs in the promoter regions of OsMIR156d and OsMIR156j negatively controls their expression.

To further demonstrate that DNA methylation represses the expression of OsmiR156d and OsmiR156j, we treated WT rice seedlings with the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5'-Azacytidine(Christman, 2002). 5'-Azacytidine treatment inhibited CG, CHG and CHH methylation at MITEs in the promoters of OsmiR156d and OsmiR156j (Supplemental Figure 5A). The levels of pri-OsmiR156d, pri-OsmiR156j and OsmiR156a-j were upregulated by

5'-Azacytidine treatment in a dose-dependent manner (Supplemental Figure 5B

and 5C). Concomitantly, the level of IPA1 was downregulated (Supplemental Figure 5B). These data reinforce that OsmiR156d and OsmiR156j expression are regulated by DNA methylation.

## **D14 Expression Is Activated by RdDM at a MITE Located in Its Downstream**

DWARF14 (D14) encodes the receptor of strigolactone (SL) and represses the outgrowth of tillers in rice (Arite et al., 2009a; Gao et al., 2009a; Hamiaux et al., 2012; Jiang et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2009a; Nakamura et al., 2013; Yao et al., 2018; Yao et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2013). D14 has two forms of transcripts differing in 3'-UTR sequences, referred to as D14-Short (D14-S) and D14-Long (D14-L), respectively (Figure 6A). There are two MITEs (MITE #1 and #2) located within or downstream of D14 (Figure 6A). We found a CHH hypo-DMR overlapping with MITE #1 (Figure 6A). Locus-specific bisulfite sequencing confirmed that DNA methylation in all sequence contexts at MITE #1 was dramatically reduced in the osnrpd1ab double knockout line, while that at MITE #2 had a mild reduction (Supplemental Figure 6A). Our mRNA-seq data revealed that both D14-S and D14-L were downregulated in osnrpd1-1 (Figure 6A). Decreased expression of both transcripts in osnrpd1-1/2 and osnrpd1ab double knockout lines was confirmed by RT-qPCR (Figure 6B and Supplemental Figure 6B). D14 depletion can lead to feedback upregulation of DWARF10 (D10), a key enzyme in

catalyzing the biogenesis of SL, to generate optimal level of active SLs (Arite et al., 2007). Consistent with it, D10 was upregulated in osnrpd1-1/2 (Figure 6C), further indicating aberrant SL signaling in osnrpd1-1/2. These results suggest that D14 is positively regulated by RdDM at a nearby MITE. To determine that the regulation of D14 by RdDM occurs at the transcriptional

level, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) combined with qPCR (ChIP-qPCR) using a Pol II-specific antibody and immunoglobulin G (IgG) as a control. We found that Pol II enrichment was significantly reduced at the promoter and gene body of D14 in osnrpd1-1 (Figure 6D), suggesting transcriptional promotion of D14 by CHH methylation.

To further demonstrate the importance of CHH hypo-DMR overlapping with MITE #1, we generated transgenic lines that are devoid of MITE #1 using CRISPR/Cas9 technology (Ma et al., 2015b). Transgenic lines devoid of MITE #2, which is not associated with CHH hypo-DMRs, were generated in parallel for comparison (Figure 6E). Compared with WT, only the MITE #1 deletion lines, but not the MITE #2 deletion lines, had reduced D14 expression (Figure 6F) and exhibited high-tillering and dwarf phenotypes (Figure 6G-6I). Our results strongly support that RdDM at a MITE promotes D14 expression.

Proteins that are involved in strigolactone biosynthesis and signal tranduction have also been found to suppress lateral bud outgrowth and rice tillering (Arite et al., 2007; Arite et al., 2009b; Gao et al., 2009b; Ishikawa et al., 2005; Jiang et al., 2013; Lin et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2009b; Zhou et al., 2013; Zou et al., 2006). Interestingly, MITE #1 nested in the downstream region of D14 was hypomethylated and D14 expression was downregulated in osnrpd1-1/2. Deletion of MITE #1 caused downregulation of D14 and an increase in rice tillering, demonstrating that RdDM also controls rice tillering through the strigolactone pathway. In agriculture, breeding new elite cultivars with improved agronomic traits, such as cultivars with low tiller numbers, is still a challenge. Our findings shed light on epigenetic regulation of the expression of OsMIR156d/j and D14, making them valuable targets for agronomic trait enhancement by the emerging

epigenome editing (Kungulovski and Jeltsch, 2016).

DNA methylation, especially DNA methylation in gene promoters, typically represses gene expression. However, in Arabidopsis, DNA methylation in the promoter of ROS1, a DNA glycosylase that initiates active DNA demethylation, activates ROS1 expression (Lei et al., 2015; Williams et al., 2015). In hexaploid winter wheat, DNA hypermethylation in the TEs located in an intron of the gene VERNALIZATION1 (VRN1) during vernalization coincides with and may contribute to the induction of VRN1 by cold (Khan et al., 2013). RNA-seq on osdcl3a (Wei et al., 2014), osdrm2 (Tan et al., 2016), and osnrpd1a/b (this study) suggest that RdDM has a positive role in gene expression. This raise an important question as to how DNA methylation promotes gene transcription. In Arabidopsis, a DNA methylation reader complex, which comprises SUVH1, SUVH3 and three DNAJ-domain containing proteins, mediates the activation of genes that are already mildly expressed, antagonizing the repressive effect of DNA methylation that spreads to genes from neighboring TEs (Harris et al., 2018; Li et al., 2016; Xiao et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2019). Future investigations are necessary to determine whether the SUVH1/3 protein complex generally reads DNA methylation and activates gene expression in different plants and elucidate additional mechanisms responsible for the activation of gene expression by DNA methylation.

### **METHODS**

### **Rice Plant Growth Conditions and Generation of Transgenic Plants**

For observation and statistical analysis of the agronomic traits, rice plants were cultivated in the paddy field under natural growth conditions at the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology (IGDB, Beijing). Urea was used as the N source with 2 kg N/100 m<sup>2</sup> . The rice plants were transplanted with a density of 3 rows × 8 plants and the spacing between rice plants was 20 cm. For molecular studies, including RT-qPCR, high-throughput sequencing, northern blot, locus-specific bisulfite sequencing and ChIP-qPCR, rice plants were grown in a greenhouse at 70% humidity, under long-day conditions with daily cycles of 14 h of light at 30 °C and 10 h of dark at 25 °C. Shoot bases or leaves of 4-week old seedlings (the beginning of the tillering stage) were collected and used for the molecular studies.

All the transgenic plants generated in this study are in the Nipponbare background. After confirmation by sanger sequencing, the IR is introduced into pCam23ACT:OCS, a binary vector containing the rice Actin1 promoter, to generate the RNAi construct. **Small RNA Sequencing and Analysis** 

Total RNAs were purified from the shoot base of 4-week-old seedlings using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026). Large RNAs were selectively precipitated using 5% PEG8000 (Sigma, P5413) and 0.5 M NaCl (Sigma, S3014). sRNAs were recovered from the supernatant by ethanol precipitation and then separated by electrophoresis using a 15% denaturing PAGE gel. sRNAs that are 18-28 nt long were excised from the gel and purified for library construction as described(Mi et al., 2008). Briefly, purified sRNAs were firstly ligated with the 3' adaptors. After size selection, the products were further ligated with the 5' adaptors. The sRNAs with adapters served as templates for reverse transcriptions by SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, 18080093) to generate the cDNAs. Next, the cDNAs were amplified by PCR. The libraries were single-end sequenced in 50-bp on an Illumina HiSeq2500 platform. For data analysis, the adapters were removed using the FASTX-Tool kit (hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx\_toolkit/). Clean sRNA reads were mapped to the rice genome (RGAP7.0) with Bowtie(Langmead et al., 2009) allowing no mismatches. SAM output files were converted to BAM files using samtools for sRNA clustering analysis and visualization of the alignment of sRNAs using Gbrowser. For data analysis, rice genome annotation was downloaded from the Rice Genome Annotation Project (http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/) in the version of RGAP 7. TEs were annotated by using the RepeatMasker (http://www.repeatmasker.org/) program with default settings. MITEs annotations were downloaded from the Plant MITE Database (http://pmite.hzau.edu.cn/).

### **RNA Sequencing and Analysis**

Total RNAs were purified from the shoot base of 4-week-old seedlings using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN, 74904). Library construction and Illumina sequencing were performed by Bionova Biotech (Beijing, China). Sequencing reads were mapped to the rice genome (RGAP7.0) using TopHat (Trapnell et al., 2009). Uniquely mapped reads with up to 2 mismatches were retained. Read counts of each gene were calculated using Featurecount (Version 1.6.0). Gene expression levels were normalized into Fragments Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2(Love et al., 2014) with default settings. Genes with >2 fold changes in gene expression levels and q value < 0.05 (corrected from p-values by the Benjamini-Hochberg testing) were considered as DEGs.
