Here we set out to characterize first fruit inhibition (FFI), *i.e.* the inhibitory effect of the first fruit on subsequent development of younger ovaries during pollination-induced and parthenocarpic fruit set. After the first fertilized ovaries set fruit, younger fertilized ovaries remained in a temporary state of inhibition. Such ovaries preserved their size and green color, and if the older fruit were removed within a one-week reversibility window, they set fruit. The FFI effect was documented in both fertilized and parthenocarpic ovaries. We compared the gene expression profiles of pollinated ovaries (committed to set fruit) with respect to those affected by FFI, and to non-pollinated ovaries (undergoing senescence). The combined results are discussed with respect to current models of fruit set and specifically with regard to FFI. Moreover, these metabolome and transcriptome data provide a valuable resource for studying ovary development and fruit set.

### Introduction

Fruit set is an important developmental process that affects fruit yield, however its molecular and physiological regulation is not fully understood. The natural trigger for ovary development and fruit set is pollination and subsequent fertilization, that provide a hormonal stimulus for initial fruit growth. For example, the germinating orchid pollen releases signals, presumably ACC and auxin, that induce ACC synthase genes in floral organs, causing anther and petal abscission and ovary growth (Bui and O'Neill, 1998). Following fertilization, an interplay of auxin, gibberellins (GA) and cytokinins is required to attain fruit set. In Arabidopsis, auxin released by the fertilized ovule induces GA synthesis to further stimulate ovary growth (Dorcey et al., 2009). In fleshy fruit such as tomato, GA-auxin interactions operate as well (Srivastava and Handa, 2005; Ruan et al., 2012; Pattison et al., 2014). Regulators of the GA and IAA pathways in the ovary, such as DELLA (Marti et al., 2007; Dorcey et al., 2009), and IAA9 (Wang et al., 2009) restrain ovary development in the absence of pollination. Fruit set is enabled upon pollination by releasing such repression, and mutations in these genes caused parthenocarpic fruit set. In cucumber, brassinolide was implicated in fruit set (Fu et al., 2008), in addition to GA, IAA and cytokinin (Kim et al., 1992). In response to the above stimuli, the ovary undergoes rapid cell divisions and expansion. Expression studies of young cucumber ovaries correlated cell division and expansion

with expression of specific kinesin genes (Yang et al., 2013), and roles for cyclins and CDK transcripts were reported by Fu et al. (2010).

Transcriptomic studies of early fruit development have been carried out in a few fleshy fruit such as apple (Lee et al., 2007; Janssen et al., 2008), tomato (Lemaire-Chamley et al., 2005; Pascual et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2009) and grape (Schlosser et al., 2008). In cucumber, time-dependent transcript profiles of developing fruits were studied by Ando and Grumet (2010) and Ando et al. (2012) in relation to the cytological and cellular phases of ovary development. Li et al. (2014) analyzed the ovary transcriptome of cucumber at 2 dpa in a parthenocarpic and a non parthenocarpic variety, supporting the role of several hormones in fruit set. Wang et al. (2009) compared the transcript and metabolic profiles of young tomato fruit and discussed the power of integrating the two datasets for a systems-level understanding of fruit set. In cucumber, no comprehensive metabolomic studies have been reported so far. However, Hu et al. (2009) analyzed sugar metabolism in developing cucumber fruits (2-20 dpa). Classical biochemical studies reviewed by Schaffer et al. (1996) and Miao and Zhang (2016) described sugar synthesis, transport and conversion related to cucurbit fruit set, where the young fruit acts as a sink that attracts raffinose family oligosaccharides (RFO), and degrades them in order to support growth.

In the absence of pollination, the ovary is committed to rapid senescence (Llop-Tous et al., 2000; Shinozaki et al., 2016). For example, a non-pollinated *Pisum* ovary remains receptive for two days and eventually succumbs to ethylene-controlled programmed cell death (PCD), and such receptivity window can be extended for two more days by gibberellin application (Orzaez and Granell, 1997). Parthenocarpic genotypes, extensively used in greenhouse cucumbers, do not require pollination to set fruit and offer a convenient tool to study other levels of control over fruit set, separating the effect of pollination and fertilization from other variables and signals. While in several species parthenocarpic mutants were defective in hormone-related genes, the molecular basis of parthenocarpy in cucumber is still unknown.

It is evident that not every fertilized ovary sets fruit. During evolution, plants developed ways to optimize their reproductive success by limiting the simultaneous formation of too many competing fruits; however, in cucurbits, such mechanisms can severely limit agricultural yield. The environmental and internal stimuli used to control fruit set at the whole-plant level are yet-unknown. Numerous studies in different crop species have modeled fruit set as affected by fruit position on the plant, timing relative to other fruits, and assimilate supply (Chapman et al., 1979; Pechan and Morgan, 1985).

The inhibition of subsequent female flower maturation and fruit development by the first fruit that set (First Fruit Inhibition, FFI) is an intriguing developmental phenomenon observed daily by cucurbit breeders and growers, but its quantitative description and elucidation are surprisingly scarce (McCollum, 1934; Schapendonk and Brouwer, 1984; Zhang et al., 2015). FFI could potentially be explained by competition on assimilates among organs. The rate of cucumber photosynthesis increases dramatically upon fruit set (Pharr et al., 1985) regardless of fruit number, but later supply becomes limiting: Marcelis (1993) demonstrated, in fruit removal experiments, that competition over dry matter between developing fruit limits fruit number and size. On the other hand, "fruit dominance effects", *i.e.*, inhibition of younger tomato fruit even before assimilates become limiting, suggests an active inhibitory signal and a developmental basis for FFI (Bertin, 1995). Baldet et al. (2006) showed that flower removal developmentally affects the remaining flowers in the tomato inflorescence, by modifying cell cycle gene expression and increasing ovary cell number, thus affecting their future sink strength and fruit size. Bangerth (1989) proposed that auxin exported by an earlier tomato fruit interferes with auxin export from subsequent fruit; preventing such export delays younger fruit development. The two theories (competition versus dominance) are difficult to separate experimentally and even conceptually: sink strength is developmentally and hormonally controlled, and sugars not only provide energy but also act as signals. In cucumber, a first indication for the importance of trehalose phosphate in fruit set was recently provided by Zhang et al. (2015), who addressed FFI by comparing sugar levels in the first and second fruit.

In this study we provide a phenotypic and molecular description of fruit set and the FFI phenomenon in cucumber, and demonstrate their dynamic and reversible nature. We focus on the early events occurring at 1 dpa (earlier than previous studies), and present a metabolomic profiling of cucumber ovaries, in combination with transcript profiling. We performed a thorough comparison of parthenocarpic and non parthenocarpic genotypes, and within the parthenocarpic genotype, pollination dependent and independent fruit set. The combined molecular and metabolic changes observed provide new insights and suggest interesting gene targets for the study of cucumber fruit set. In addition, our data offer a permanent resource for researchers interested in ovary development and fruit set in cucurbits, as well as comparative studies in other crop species.

### Results

Gynoecious varieties, in which female flowers form sequentially on each node, provide a convenient system in which to study the developmental fate of a series of ovaries along a shoot. We observed that under a given set of conditions, such fates are rather predictable. In the cucumber gynoecious varieties `235` (parthenocarpic line) and 'Elem female', coded "W2" (non-parthenocarpic), a single female flower reaches anthesis every day at each node along the main stem. In the non-parthenocarpic line, ovaries that are not pollinated undergo rapid senescence. In case the ovary is pollinated, it may either set fruit or remain inhibited by older fruit, and fate is determined according to its position relative to other ovaries. In parthenocarpic genotypes, pollinated as well as non-pollinated ovaries will set fruit (seeded or seedless, respectively), depending on the presence of inhibitory fruit.

To describe the fate of sequential ovaries along the main stem of the two varieties, forty plants of each treatment were grown in replicates of five plants, and were sequentially pollinated, or bagged to prevent pollination, for 10 days. A smaller number of plants (10) were used for the non-pollinated treatment of the non-parthenocarpic variety, to verify that it did not set parthenocarpic fruit.

We recorded, for each node (numbered from the first pollinated/bagged node at the onset of the experiment), the status of each ovary, that underwent one of the following fates: fruit set (F, determined as dark-green fruit >10 cm); FS – partially developed dark green fruit, 5-10 cm; I – ovaries that did not develop, and remained inhibited and pale-green; N- ovaries that turned yellow and in some cases became necrotic. Figure 1A depicts these states, and Figure 1B-F plots the number of plants whose ovaries displayed a specific fate at each sequential node.

The two genotypes behaved similarly when fertilized. We observed that the first ovaries to develop and be pollinated at the first two nodes set fruit (F) at very high frequency, and within 10 days, fruit length reached 20 cm (Fig. 2B, C). In nodes 3-4, some fruit have set but growth has stopped (FS), and size remained below 10 cm. Most of the pollinated ovaries in subsequent nodes remained in a "stand-by state" (I): they did not develop further but did not degenerate and remained green for ~7 days before they turned yellow and senesced. Further up the stem, inhibited ovaries degenerated faster (N). Finally, at the upper nodes, degeneration even occurred prior to anthesis.

Interestingly, the pattern of ovary fate was different in the non-pollinated plants of parthenocarpic line 235 (Fig. 2D). Parthenocarpic fruit set appeared stronger, with more frequent fruit set in node 3 compare to pollinated plants. In the upper nodes, we did not record partially developed fruits (FS) and only few inhibited ovaries (I) as compared to the pollinated plants. Thus, inhibition by previous parthenocarpic fruit resulted in quicker senescence and degeneration of the next parthenocarpic ovaries, whereas in fertilized plants, inhibited ovaries were common (Fig. 2C). Possible reasons for such difference are discussed below. The "negative control" group, *i.e.*, non-pollinated ovaries at nearly every node (Fig. 2E). The difference in fate-frequency between pollination-driven and parthenocarpic fruit set, over all nodes, was statistically significant (Fig. 2F).

### Reversibility of the first-fruit-inhibited state

We next addressed the question whether the putative inhibitory stimulus is transient, and could be reversed by fruit removal. After scoring ovary fate for 10 nodes (Fig. 1), all the fully developed fruit (those scored "F") were removed, and we monitored the possible recovery of partially-developed fruit (FS) and inhibited ovaries (I) in nodes 4-8. As shown in Figure 2A, up to 50-65% of the inhibited ovaries resumed growth, the ratio varying according to the node and the genotype. In the parthenocarpic genotype, recovery in node 4 was weaker than in the non parthenocarpic genotype and started mostly in node 5.

In a second experiment, we attempted to measure more accurately the "reversibility window", *i.e.*, the period after pollination during which the inhibited ovary can still recover following removal of the older fruit. For this purpose, we used 50 plants of the Elem Female (W2) genotype at one-month age. We pollinated the plants and allowed them develop 1-2 fruit for one week. We next pollinated a single ovary (which we expected to be inhibited) per plant. Plants were divided into groups, in each group the inhibitory fruit were removed at an increasing interval from the second pollination, leaving the ovary in the inhibited state for 1-7 days post pollination before removing the inhibitory fruit. Figure 2B plots the proportion of inhibited ovaries that resumed growth, remained inhibited, or visibly degenerated. Between 1-4 days after pollination, 60-80% of the ovaries resume growth after fruit removal, with an apparent optimum at 3 days post pollination; after 6 days recovery ratios drop and after 7 days ovaries fail to recover at all, demarcating a definite reversibility window.

As a positive control, we pollinated ovaries in a similar position in the absence of older fruit, and observed full fruit set. As a negative control, we did not remove the older fruit and accordingly recorded no fruit set.

### Transcript profiling of cucumber ovaries

Transcriptional changes in developing ovaries could shed light on the mechanisms underlying such development. To gain an extensive view of gene expression, we employed RNAseq.We compared anthesis ovaries (coded "A") of the gynoecious, non-parthenocarpic cucumber Elem Female, with 1 dpa pollinated ovaries that were likely to set fruit ("F+1"), and ovaries that were not pollinated and were determined to abort ("S+1"). To experimentally generate an intermediate-strength first-fruit inhibition effect, that will cause reversible FFI, we fertilized two ovaries on each plant and let them grow for one week. Afterwards, we fertilized younger ovaries and collected the inhibited ("f+1") sample after 24 hrs.

In total, twelve cDNA preparations (triplicates of the above four stages) were sequenced from individual ovaries. The raw data produced by Illumina paired-end (PE) sequencing of the cDNA samples ranged from 15 to 19.8 million reads (supplementary Table S1). After filtering, clean reads were aligned to the cucumber genome. We could map the majority of reads (77.36% - 85.12%) in each sample. In total, over 17,800 expressed genes were detected in each sample out of the predicted 26,682 genes in the cucumber genome (Huang et al., 2009). A single inhibited ovary sample (f+1) had to be removed due to bad sequence quality. In order to test the consistency of biological replicates, we used principal component analysis (PCA) on normalized expression levels (Fig. S1A). We observed good separation of the samples according to the stage and treatment. Clustering analysis, based on Pearson correlations, was another way to observe the overall relations between the transcript profiles of our samples (Fig. S1B). The replicates of each stage/treatment clustered separately, suggesting the robustness of the data. Similar to PCA results, A, F+1 and f+1 samples clustered closer to each other, whereas S+1 was more distant. Closer relationships existed between A and f+1 samples, compared to F+1. This could suggest that the f+1 inhibited stage lags behind and remains more similar to anthesis day ovaries, compared to ovaries that set fruit. Inhibited ovaries are closer to non-pollinated ovaries in PCA dimension 1, but well differentiated from them in dimension 2: non pollinated ovaries are probably committed to the senescence pathway already at 1 dpa, and represent the most distant cluster.

Following the above overview, we turned our attention to examining differentially expressed genes (DEG), which distinguish the fates of cucumber ovaries. A two-fold threshold and a p value<0.05 were set as cut-off for this purpose. We made four pair-wise comparisons: F+1, S+1 and f+1 ovaries were each compared to anthesis day ovaries; in addition, we compared 1 dpa fertilized ovaries in the absence and in the presence of inhibitory fruit (f+1 vs F+1). We thus identified 301, 325, 114 and 244 DEG in the F+1 vs A, S+1 vs A, f+1 vs A and f+1 vs F+1 comparisons, respectively (Fig. 3A). When we looked at the intersections of the lists of DEGs we found a relatively low number of shared genes between the F+1, S+1 and f+1 ovaries, indicating that the transcriptional responses in the three conditions are rather distinct and involve different sets of genes (Fig. 3B). The number of DEG represent less than 2% of the total number of expressed genes that were detected, suggesting that transcriptomic shifts at one dpa are still small and therefore likely to be relatively specific. The limited differences detected between f+1 and anthesis-stage ovaries were in accordance with the PCA results and suggested a relative stasis of development in inhibited ovaries and their transcriptional resemblance to anthesis-day ovaries.

Interestingly, in ovaries committed to set fruit, a larger portion of the genes, 246, were downregulated, compared to 55 up-regulated DEG (Fig. 3A). The other pair-wise comparisons also suggest that transcript down-regulation is more often associated with growth and development, whereas in inhibited or senescent ovaries, more genes were up-regulated. Several of these changes were confirmed in targeted qRT-PCR analysis. Figure 4 provides examples of a few such differential transcripts.

Assignment of DEG to functional categories and pathways. In order to classify the transcripts to functional classes, we performed a Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the differential transcripts. We tested for enrichment of specific GO classes at each development stage, compared to their representation in the cucumber genome (Fig. 5). We also performed KEGG pathway analysis (http://www.kegg.com) that assigns genes to biochemical pathways. Finally, we used Mercator (Lohse et al., 2014) as an additional annotation tool to assign genes to functional categories. Table S3 enlists the DEG according to their differential expression pattern, and provides their sequence homology revealed by BLAST, whereas Table S4 is based on Mercator annotation outputs (see below).

**Transcript changes during fruit set (F+1 ovaries).** Interestingly, all of the GO categories that were enriched in F+1 ovaries were defense-related, and contained genes which were down-regulated relative to anthesis day levels (Fig. 5). These categories included *toxin catabolic* 

process (GO:0009407) and defense response (GO:0006952), as well as responses to ROS, karrikin (fire-related signals) and wounding. KEGG analysis indicated changes in the pathways of glutathione metabolism (csv00480) in pollinated ovaries (F+1), specifically in defense-related glutathione-Stransferases. An example of a defense-related transcript is chitinase (Csa1M267230; Fig. 4) that decreased in fruit setting ovaries while increasing in inhibited ones. Mercator annotations pointed at biotic stress, receptor kinases, protein degradation, glutathione S-transferase and transcriptional regulation, as the down-regulated classes. Interestingly, some of these categories are typically represented at the initiation of developmental plant cell death (dPCD, Van Durme and Nowack, 2016). Growth processes are often antagonistic to defense processes, and fruit set could thus require down-regulation of defense and dPCD-related genes (Wang et al., 2009). An apparent exception involves a putative defensin-like transcript (Csa7M239060) that, in contrast to other defense-related genes, was up-regulated in the fertilized ovaries. Defensins are small cysteine-rich proteins that play an important role in the innate immune system of plants (Aerts et al., 2008), but certain family members have acquired roles in plant development and reproduction (Dresselhaus and Marton, 2009), e.q., they could function as pollen attractants secreted from ovules (Okuda et al., 2009; Takeuchi and Higashiyama, 2012).

We further examined the list of individual DEG (Table S3). Contrary to our expectation, invertase, sucrose synthase and alpha galactosidase transcripts, often related to sink strength, were not induced, while a raffinose synthase transcript (Csa4M167980) was specifically up-regulated in fertilized ovaries, and not in non-fertilized or inhibited ovaries.

Several phytohormones play key roles in ovary development. Previous studies have indicated significant accumulation of auxins, GAs, and cytokinins following fertilization (Gillaspy et al., 1993; Wang et al., 2009). Our data indicated numerous DEG related to phytohormone pathways (Table 1). For example, we observed up-regulation of zeatin O-xylosyltransferase (Csa4M415930; Fig. 4) which catalyzes the formation of a putative storage conjugate, O-xylosylzeatin, from zeatin, (Moore, 1989), and cytochrome P450-87A3 (Csa1M044890), a likely enzyme in brassinolide synthesis. Four ethylene-responsive transcription factors were down-regulated (Csa6M133770, Csa6M486790, Csa3M018320 and Csa2M177210), which could reflect a decrease in ethylene levels and could relieve suppression of fruit set, along with two ABA related transcripts.

Transcript changes at the onset of senescence (S+1 ovaries). In non-parthenocarpic genotypes without fertilization (S+1), the ovary is irreversibly directed towards senescence. Compared to the other developmental stages analyzed, a large number of DEG, 182, was up-regulated relative to anthesis levels (Fig. 3). Enrichment analysis (Fig. 5) indicated an enrichment of the jasmonic acid biosynthesis category (GO:0009695), that includes five up-regulated, and two down-regulated genes of the lipoxygenase family. These genes are also included in several other categories such as lipid oxidation, membrane disassembly and oxylipin synthesis. Plant lipoxygenases catalyze the hydroperoxidation of lipids for developmental and signaling purposes, *e.g.*, jasmonic acid synthesis and control of senescence (Blee, 2002; Yang et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2015), hence they are also enlisted among hormone-related DEG in Table 1. Accordingly, KEGG analysis also indicated an upregulation of linoleic acid metabolism (csv00591). Three additional defense-related GO categories, defense response to other organism, defense response to bacterium and response to wounding were enriched. It thus appears that S+1 ovaries are activating a transcriptional program in preparation for dPCD, being committed to senescence later in development. Specific examples (Tables S3) include a cucumber ortholog of a senescence-associated gene, DIN1 Csa6M507370 (Fig. 4), and a senescence associated NAC factor (Csa5M606310). This transcriptional set-up is in contrast to fertilized ovaries, where most of the dPCD-related genes were down-regulated. According to the annotation categories of Mercator software (Table S4), several protein synthesis and cell division genes were down-regulated, and many hormone-related transcripts, and transcription factors, were either upor down-regulated.

Among the genes related to *cell division and growth*, Csa3M824990, an ortholog of *Cup-shaped cotyledon 2* from *Arabidopsis*, suggested to inhibit cell division (Sieber et al., 2007) was up-regulated. Six ribosome proteins transcripts and two histone H2A transcripts decreased in level. Transcripts of *cell wall modifying enzymes* were down-regulated, *e.g.*, polygalacturonase (Csa7M414430), pectinesterase (Csa7M414390), while others increased, *e.g.*, expansin (Csa3M910670) and βgalactosidase (Csa1M587980).

*Sugar metabolic transcripts.* A Sucrose synthase family-member, Csa5M322500, was up-regulated in S+1 ovaries, and a cell wall invertase, Csa7M450770, was down-regulated. In parallel, an invertase inhibitor (Csa6M080370) was up-regulated.

*Hormone metabolism and signaling.* S+1 ovaries had 22 differentially expressed genes involved in biosynthesis, signaling and transport of auxin, gibberellin, cytokinin, JA, ABA and ethylene (Table 1). The first three hormones promote fruit set, whereas ethylene is considered a negative regulator (Martinez et al., 2013). An aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) oxidase (Csa6M511860), acting

in ethylene synthesis, was up-regulated, together with amember of the ERF family (Csa6M055940) of ethylene responsive transcription factors, while another member was down-regulated. GA20ox2 (Csa3M179110) and GA3OX1 (Csa7M434970) related to GA biosynthesis, and Csa3M778430, a cytokinin-activating enzyme, were down-regulated, probably leading to reduced cytokinin and gibberellin levels. Auxin-related genes included up- and down-regulated DEG.

**Effect of first fruit inhibition on gene expression (f+1 ovaries).** Ovaries that were fertilized in the presence of inhibitory fruit (f+1) were compared to anthesis ovaries: only 36 DEG, compared to 246 in non-inhibited ovaries, were down-regulated, and 78 were up-regulated (compared to 56 in non-inhibited ones). We also compared f+1 to F+1, and saw that the inhibited state has 185 up-regulated and 59 down-regulated transcripts, relative to the non-inhibited state (Fig. 3A).

GO analysis indicated that many genes "related to pollen growth" were up-regulated by FFI, and part of them was assigned to the *pectin catabolic process* (GO:0045490) and *cell wall modification* (GO:0042545) categories; *carbohydrate metabolism* was enriched as well. Sixteen *cell-wall enzymes* were apparent also following Mercator annotation (Table S4). Others were related to carbohydrate metabolism. It thus seems that the f+1 ovaries undergo an initial remodelling of the cell wall, which can be either preparatory for fruit set or ovary abortion, depending on the presence or removal of older fruit. Many of the above transcripts were related (according to BLAST searches) to pollen development and pollen tube germination. This group included five pectin esterases, pectin esterase inhibitor (Csa4M003750), a pectate lyase (Csa5M604340) and two polygalacturonases (Csa3M838140 and Csa7M037450), and two pollen allergens (Csa6M519560 and Csa6M425710).

In addition, expression of SWEET8 bidirectional *sugar transporters* that mediate uptake and efflux of sugar across the plasma membrane, Csa4M622870 and Csa1M001300, increased in f+1. In *Arabidopsis*, AtSWEET8 specifically feeds the pollen (Eom et al., 2015). A trehalose 6-phosphate phosphatase transcript, TPP1 (Csa6M500540) specifically increased over ten-fold in inhibited ovaries over anthesis day levels (Fig. 4). This could implicate a trehalose phosphate signal in mediating FFI. All the above genes were not induced in non-inhibited ovaries.

We observed significant differences in expression of *hormone related transcripts* between the inhibited and non-inhibited ovaries (Table 1). For example, inhibited ovaries had lower expression of an isopentenyltransferase gene (Csa7M392940) that catalyzes cytokinin biosynthesis, zeatin O-xylosyltransferase (Csa4M415930; Fig.4) that catalyzes the formation of O-xylosylzeatin from zeatin, and an auxin transporter (Csa4M337340). In contrast, inhibited ovaries had elevated levels of other

auxin transporters (Csa3M000170, Csa2M264590), indole-3-acetic amino synthetase (Csa6M493310), and Csa6M358680, involved in cytokinin biosynthesis. Ethylene related transcripts were higher in the inhibited ovaries, including transcription factors (Csa6M091830, Csa3M018320, Csa5M165850 and Csa4M370550), ACC oxidase 3 (Csa6M511860) and ACC synthase (Csa4M049610). Other transcripts up-regulated by FFI included a lipoxygenase (Csa4M288610), Jasmonate O-methyltransferase (Csa3M859730) that catalyzes bioactive methyl jasmonate formation, and GA2ox1 (Csa4M075200) that degrades active gibberellins. The latter transcript exhibits a striking 10-fold accumulation in inhibited ovaries, compared to non-inhibited ones, verified by RT-qPCR (Fig. 4), suggesting that active gibberellin is a key player in fruit set and its deactivation could be important during FFI.

### Metabolomic profile of developing ovaries

In order to identify changes in the level of primary metabolites between ovaries undergoing different developmental fates, we performed gas-chromatography mass-spectrometry. In line Elem Female, we compared pre-anthesis (A-1) and anthesis-day ovaries (A) with pollinated ovaries determined to set fruit, after 5 hrs and 1 day (F+ 5h, F+1), as well as ovaries that were bagged and determined to senesce. In addition, we compared similar ovaries in the parthenocarpic line 235; here, however, the bagged ovaries are determined to set a parthenocarpic fruit (P+1). A parallel set of samples (designated with lower case letters f+1, p+1, etc.) was prepared from plants experiencing FFI, where inhibitory fruit were allowed to set. A subset of these samples (from 'Elem Female') had been used for the RNAseq experiment.

We identified and calculated the relative quantity of 87 polar metabolites, of which 88% could be annotated with high confidence against the Golm-Metabolome-Database. The metabolites were divided into the following categories: sugar derivatives (27%), amino acid and  $\alpha$ -amino acid (29%), organic compound (5%), fatty acid (6%) dicarboxylic and carboxylic acid (7%) nucleic acid (2%), others (12%) and unknown (12%). Figure S2 depicts a hierarchical clustering (HC) plot of relative metabolite levels. It shows clear separation between the profiles of the two genotypes. The level of most metabolites is higher in the parthenocarpic line, compared to those of the non-parthenocarpic one. Within each line, the metabolite profiles differ according to developmental fate, and the changes are apparent already after 24 hrs post anthesis. For example, there is a general increase in the level of most metabolites between A-1 (a day before anthesis) and anthesis, and a further increase after fertilization; whereas in ovaries committed to senescence (S+1) the levels decreased.

Moreover, the separation between FFI-inhibited and non-inhibited ovaries is also apparent, with generally lower levels of metabolites in f+1 ovaries.

In Figure 6A, the number of differentially abundant metabolites (defined as having 2-fold higher or lower amounts compared to anthesis-day ovaries) is shown separately for each genotype. In the non-parthenocarpic variety, we observed substantial changes in the metabolite profile (26 metabolites increased and a single one decreased) one day after fertilization, marking the initiation of fruit set. When the ovary was not fertilized, and committed to senesce, only four metabolites accumulated. In the parthenocarpic line, accumulation of metabolites was higher and faster, and no metabolites decreased. Of note, the metabolic profile of fertilized 'line 235' ovaries (F+1) and those committed to set a parthenocarpic fruit without fertilization (P+1) are strikingly different.

In the first-fruit inhibited ovaries, metabolite levels were lower than in their non-inhibited counterparts (Fig. 6B), and the effect was genotype-specific. In the non-parthenocarpic line, between 15 and 32 compounds show decreased level, relative to the non-inhibited ones, and only 0-2 compounds had higher levels. In the parthenocarpic line, however, the metabolic profile was less affected by inhibition. On anthesis, a large number of metabolites (36) did have lower levels in inhibited *vs*. non-inhibited ovaries, but afterwards, inhibited ovaries "caught up", and most metabolites were at similar levels as those of non-inhibited ovaries.

When we plotted the number of differentially expressed metabolites in the inhibited ovaries compared to the inhibited anthesis-day levels (Fig. 6C), we realized that in both genotypes, the effect of fertilization is also apparent in the inhibited ovaries, even though they do not set fruit: a large number of metabolites (45) increased in the non parthenocarpic line starting at 1 dpa, and an even larger proportion increased in the parthenocarpic line; such high figures demonstrate the responsiveness of ovaries to a "fruit set stimulus", even when actual fruit set is suspended. Changes in specific metabolites (Table 2) are described in the next paragraphs.

**Primary metabolite changes in response to fertilization (F+5h** *vs.* **A** , **F+1** *vs.* **A).** The nonparthenocarpic variety responded more slowly to pollination. After 5 hrs, the level of a single compound, galactinol, increased, while in the parthenocarpic line, 23 metabolites increased in level. One day after fertilization (F+1), metabolic profiles changed extensively in both varieties. Changes in sugar levels were prominent, with higher level of trehalose, glucose, glucose-6-P and fructose-6-P in both varieties, while fructose and sucrose levels were unaltered. Other sugars accumulated specifically in one genotype and not in the other. Many amino acids, namely Trp, Phe, Tyr, Hyp, Asn,

Leu, Gln, Gly, His and Ala increased in both varieties, whereas Ser, Thr, Glu, Met and Cys increased specifically in one genotype. The polyamine putrescine was down-regulated at F+5 hrs in the non-parthenocarpic variety, but increased in the parthenocarpic variety.

**Primary metabolite changes in non-fertilized ovaries (S+1 vs. A).** In ovaries committed to senescence, only mild changes in the primary metabolite profile were observed at 1 dpa. The level of four metabolites, putrescine, glucose, cysteine and unannotated metabolite increased, and one non-annotated metabolite decreased. Accumulation of putrescine and cysteine was noticed specifically in this stage.

**Parthenocarpic versus pollination-induced fruit set in the parthenocarpic line (F+1 vs P+1).** Table 2 clearly demonstrates that, in the same genotype, the ovary metabolome of parthenocarpic fruit set is completely different from pollination-induced fruit set already at 1 dpa. The latter is characterized by an increase in 36 primary metabolites (Fig. 6), while in the absence of pollination only six metabolites increased: glucose, 1,6-anhydro-beta-D-glucose, Asn, His, Val and citrulline.

**FFI effect on metabolic profile.** In the non-parthenocarpic genotype, FFI modified the metabolome at each developmental stage, decreasing the level of most metabolites (Figs. 5, 6; Table 2). The level of only two metabolites, maleic acid and uracil, increased at 1 dpa in the inhibited ovaries.

In the parthenocarpic variety the influence of inhibition was strong at anthesis, but the other stages "recovered" and only few metabolites remained down-regulated at the later stages: ornithine, citrulline, Gly, raffinose and trehalose. Cys, Asn, spermidine and putrescine increased in the inhibited ovaries.

# Pathway analysis and Integration of transcriptional and metabolic changes in early ovary development

Alterations in related metabolites could indicate the activation of specific metabolic pathways. The KEGG database (http://www.kegg.com) was used for pathway analysis. "Galactose metabolism" is the most significantly modified pathway in our study, with nine metabolites affected: galactinol, raffinose, sucrose, fructose, glucose, glucose-6P, myo-inositol, mannose and glycerol. Galactinol levels increased five hours after pollination in both varieties, and raffinose (produced from galactinol

and sucrose) also increased significantly in the parthenocarpic variety. Glucose, which is a degradation product of sucrose and an important developmental signal (Gomez et al., 2010; Ruan et al., 2010, 2012; Gupta et al., 2015), increased significantly in both varieties at 1 dpa, while sucrose and fructose levels did not change. Glucose undergoes phosphorylation by hexokinase, for the production of glucose-6-phosphate, whose levels also increased at 1 dpa. Thus, glucose and glucose-6-P could play a role in the cucumber fruit set. Another significantly modified pathway, obviously related to the former, is "starch and sucrose metabolism". In this pathway, two disaccharides,  $\alpha$  trehalose and maltose, increased after fertilization; they could be degraded to glucose, that accumulated at 1 dpa.

We next turned our attention to the integration of the transcriptional and metabolic changes in the non-parthenocarpic line, where both transcriptome and metabolic data were collected from the same samples. We first classified all cucumber protein sequences into functional categories ("bins") using the annotation tool Mercator (Lohse et al., 2014) and then assigned the metabolites to each category on the basis of the Mapman mapping file for the cucumber genome (Ramsak et al., 2014). A representation of the global changes for the most important gene categories, along with their associated metabolites, can be seen in Table S4. The general picture emerging from such analysis is that most of the metabolites undergo smaller variations with respect to their associated transcripts. Despite this, some observations can be made about the possible presence (or absence) of transcriptional regulation in some metabolic classes. For example, hexacosanoic, octadecanoic and palmitic acid had marginal variations in F+1, S+1 and f+1 ovaries with respect to anthesis, while some of their associated transcripts (fatty acyl-CoA reductase, phosphodiesterase and phospolipase) were differentially expressed in F+1 ovaries. In amino acid metabolism, we detected significant increases only in F+1 ovaries (Asp, Gln, Gly, His, Leu, Phe, Ser, Tyr, citrulline and hydroxyproline), with three down-regulated genes in F+1 potentially involved in glutamine/glutamate biosynthesis and degradation (Csa2M404730, Csa5M100000, Csa4M377160).

The levels of numerous sugars increased upon cucumber fruit set, as opposed to non-fertilized (S+1) or FFI (f+1) ovaries (Fig. 7). The strong accumulation of glucose and its phosphorylated derivatives (Glucose-6-P and Fructose-6-P) in F+1 ovaries may be the result of the down regulation of phosphofructokinase at this stage (Csa3M435010). We observed a strong up-regulation of beta-galactosidase genes (Csa1M650110, Csa7M011860, Csa5M150400) in f+1 ovaries, correlated with the up-regulation of several cell wall remodeling genes. In these ovaries we also detected an increase of apha-trehalose which was paralleled by an up-regulation of alpha-trehalose phosphate phosphatase (Csa6M500540). Also intriguing was the transient rise, 5 hrs post-pollination, in

raffinose (in the parthenocarpic line), a major transported sugar in cucurbits (Schaffer et al. 1996), and galactinol, a galactose donor for raffinose synthesis (in both lines). One could explain the rise in raffinose in pollinated ovaries by the uptake of raffinose and its temporary accumulation before it degrades. However, the observed increase in a putative *raffinose synthase* transcript upon pollination could indicate that raffinose is actually synthesized in developing ovaries. Handley et al. (1983) reported the presence of raffinose in developing seeds and suggested that it could be synthesized *in situ*.

### DISCUSSION

### An integrated picture of early-stage fruit set

Control over fruit set is developmentally complex. While the requirement for pollination in order to set a fruit is well established and the underlying hormone signals have been identified, the fate of pollinated ovaries depends also on poorly understood signals and their integration at a whole plant level. In the last years, a few studies addressed cucumber fruit development (Ando et al., 2012; Li et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2015). We focused on earlier developmental stages of fruit set, comparing fertilized ovaries embarking on fruit set with ovaries committed to senesce, and ovaries inhibited by earlier fruit. In both parthenocarpic and non-parthenocarpic cucumber lines, we observed extensive metabolic and transcriptional reprogramming already 24 hr post pollination. The profiles of the different ovary fates were strikingly different, and they were affected extensively by first fruit inhibition (FFI).

Our study combines transcriptomic and primary metabolic profiles of early-stage fruit set in cucumber. Previous studies that addressed cucumber fruit set either did not analyze the earlier post pollination stages and FFI, or did not perform transcriptome and metabolome studies (Ando et al., 2012; Li et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2015). With respect to parthenocarpic set, our study compares it to pollination-induced set within the same cucumber genotype. The data, stored in public databases, represent a permanent resource for the study ovary development and fruit set in cucurbits, and possibly also other crops.

The data obtained yield insightful results. First, they provide an initial hint on the possible roles of specific molecules (metabolites, transcripts), that can lead to hypothesis-driven, mechanistic experiments. Secondly, taking a broad picture of a process could generate interesting insights into its

dynamics. In Figure 7 we draw a model of the salient features and processes that characterize the developmental fates observed in this study.

### Contrasting patterns in fruit set and senescence initiation

When we compared the transcript and primary metabolite profiles of pollinated *vs.* senescencecommitted ovaries, we learned that fruit set initiation (F+1) is marked by decrease in the level of many transcripts, some of which are related to developmental programmed cell death (dPCD), while the onset of senescence is marked by an increase in many transcripts, some of which are related to dPCD-processes as well (Tables S3, S4; Fig. 7). Moreover, S+1 ovaries undergo an early decrease in transcripts related to cell division and growth. Interestingly, primary metabolites show an inverse trend, where most compounds increased at fruit set, and decreased or did not change in response to senescence and FFI stimuli. This could suggest that transcript changes that control fruit set often represent a relief of inhibition, while metabolic changes reflect the increased import and/or synthesis required in order to support growth. In tomato, Wang et al. (2009) compared pre-anthesis, anthesis and 4 dpa ovaries, and noted that the earlier stage involves changes in transcription factors and other regulatory genes, and after anthesis, cell division, growth and metabolism related transcripts follow. In our study, distinct sets of transcription factors (and other DEG) were differentially expressed in each developmental stage, with little overlap between programs.

Another general trend was the down-regulation of defense-related genes upon pollination. By contrast, lack of pollination, which directs non-parthenocarpic ovaries towards senescence, was accompanied by an increase in defense related transcripts, among which lipoxygenases were prominent. Plant lipoxygenase catalyzes the hydroperoxidation of lipids for developmental and signaling purposes (Porta and Rocha-Sosa, 2002); lipoxygenases and their downstream product jasmonic acid are also emerging as key players in senescence (Blee, 2002; Yang et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2015).

Defense related transcripts are often detected in plant reproductive organs (Kuboyama, 1998; Swanson et al., 2005; Tung et al., 2005). It has been suggested that pollen tube penetration through the transmitting tissue of the style is analogous to pathogen invasion (Clarke, 1981). Thus, decrease in defense processes may be essential for pollen tube "permissive invasion" of style and ovary tissues. Alternatively, defense genes could be required to protect the stigma and other sensitive,

sugar rich tissues from microbes, and expression levels could be highest at anthesis and decrease later, once their protective role has been accomplished.

### Multiple hormones and T6P signals could interact in the regulation of fruit set

Many hormone-related transcripts were differentially expressed in ovaries of divergent developmental fates (Table 1, Fig. 7). In general, our observations agree with current models of hormonal control of fruit set, where ovaries are arrested by hormone-response repressors and auxin, cytokinin and gibberellin relieve such repression (Ruan et al., 2012). For example, transcripts involved in gibberellin degradation and cytokinin conjugation increased in FFI ovaries (Fig. 4). Ethylene transcription factors were down-regulated, presumably to relieve an ethylene inhibitory signal. The numerous lipoxygenase transcripts with differential expression could also be acting via their jasmonate product (Table 1). Otherwise, mixed patterns of expression of auxin, cytokinin and gibberellin-related genes detected in our study support the complex cross-talk reported between these hormones in developing cucumber fruit by Li et al. (2014). In tomato, Wang et al. (2009) reported auxin, ethylene and polyamine-related transcripts as the most prominent hormone-related DEG in fruit set. In our study, the polyamine putrescine accumulated in the parthenocarpic line ovaries, suggesting an involvement in fruit set. Fos et al. (2003) reported that exogenous application of polyamines to unfertilized ovaries induced partial parthenocarpy in tomato; in other experiments, a putrescine inhibitor inhibited fruit set (Cohen et al., 1982) and interacted with several other hormones (Anwar et al., 2015).

We observed that the disaccharide trehalose transiently increases upon pollination; it is produced from its phosphorylated precursor, trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P) by trehalose-6-phosphate phosphatase (TPP). T6P is an emerging signal molecule that signals sucrose availability, shapes source-sink relationships and positively regulates organ growth and development (Gomez et al., 2010; Yadav et al., 2014; Figueroa and Lunn, 2016). Recently, Zhang et al. (2015) implicated a role for T6P in cucumber fruit set, since it increased upon pollination in the first fruit and not in the second. In our study, a TPP transcript was sharply up-regulated in inhibited ovaries (Table S3; Fig. 4). A second TPP appears to be down-regulated by FFI. Plants possess numerous TPP family members with differential expression patterns that could reflect functional divergence (Vandesteene et al., 2012), and their possible involvement in cucumber fruit set deserves further investigation.

### Difference between pollination-driven and parthenocarpic fruit set

There were many differences between the primary metabolite profiles of the parthenocarpic and non-parthenocarpic genotypes, but at this stage it would be impossible to assign such differences to the parthenocarpic trait. Genetic mapping of parthenocarpy in cucumber has been reported recently (Sun et al., 2006; Lietzow et al., 2016) and may in future lead to the unraveling of its molecular basis. In general, the parthenocarpic line 235 had a stronger fruit set response, and it accumulated many more metabolites 5 and 24 hrs after pollination compared to Elem Female. More physiologically relevant is the comparison between pollination-induced and parthenocarpic ovaries (F+1 vs P+1) within the same genotype, line 235. Pollinated ovaries of line 235 resisted FFI and remained viable whereas parthenocarpic ones underwent accelerated senescence, exposed to the same treatment (Fig. 1). Moreover, while parthenocarpic and pollination-induced fruit look and taste similar, the early ovary metabolome of parthenocarpic fruit set is completely different from pollination induced fruit set as early as 1 dpa. The latter is characterized by an increase in 36 primary metabolites, whereas in the absence of pollination only six metabolites increased. Tazuke et al. (2003) demonstrated that pollination decreases fruit abortion and allows more fruit to set compared to parthenocarpic fruit of the same genotype. This was not the case in our experiment, where parthenocarpic line 235 set more parthenocarpic fruit than pollinated fruit, but it will be interesting to test the competition between the two types of fruit on the same plant. In apple and cherry, pollinated fruit dominated and inhibited parthenocarpic fruit on the same tree (Goldwin and Schwabe, 1975; Retamales and Bukovac, 1986).

### Possible mechanisms of FFI

Inhibition of fruit set by earlier fruit (FFI) is a dynamic process that likely has profound implications for fruit size and yield (Stephenson et al., 1988; Bangerth, 1989; Zhang et al., 2015). Inhibition may be caused by competition for available assimilates, active regulation by phytohormones, or a combination of the two. The first fruit could act as a stronger sink compared to subsequent ones; in a study by Zhang et al. (2015) activity of alkaline alpha-galactosidase that breaks down imported oligosaccharides, was significantly higher in the peduncle of the first fruit than in the second fruit, indicating that the first fruit was indeed a stronger sink. In crops such as sweet pepper, cucumber and tomato, fruit compete strongly with each other for available assimilates, and the strength of the sink depends on the number of fruit that set and their developmental stage (Hall and Brady, 1977; Ali and Kelly, 1992; Marcelis, 1993).

In the present study we have described and quantified FFI in two gynoecious cucumber genotypes, and demonstrated its predictable yet reversible nature. Under our culture conditions, following the first two fruit that set on the main stem, several pollinated ovaries remain in "standby" mode, *i.e.* they did not grow but remained green and did not degenerate. However, upon removal of the inhibitory fruit within a one-week window, the inhibited ovaries resumed growth. One could consider FFI ovaries as similar to non-pollinated ones, albeit being on a slower route towards senescence. However, both the transcript and metabolic profiles suggest that it is a distinct stage, with little overlap with other stages, and perhaps more similar to anthesis day than to senescing or fruit setting ovaries (Fig. 3, Fig. S1A). The general observation, that inhibited ovaries still respond to pollination by reprogramming their metabolome, is interesting (Fig. 6C). It demonstrates the separate effects of FFI and pollination, and suggests that the ovary perceives and successfully integrates the two contrasting inputs, pollination and inhibition.

DEG that specifically respond to FFI are particularly interesting, including transcription factors, TPP and hormone related genes that could take part in generating or conveying the FFI signal (Tables S3,S4; Fig. 4). An unexpected class of transcripts specifically up-regulated in inhibited ovaries were related to pollen development and pollen tube germination, including pollen allergens, sugar transporters required to nourish the growing pollen tube, and pectin-degrading enzymes required for style penetration. All these genes were not induced in fertilized, non-inhibited ovaries.

This result raises the question whether the pollen tube could be delayed or disoriented in the inhibited ovary hence preventing fertilization. In such a case, pollen tube transcripts that are abundant at anthesis and disappear after successful fertilization may persist and be detected in the inhibited ovary. Pollen navigation depends on secretory cells along the pathway (Herrero, 2000) and could involve arabinogalactan proteins (AGP). Interestingly, expression of a galactosyl transferase transcript involved in AGP glycosylation increased at 1 dpa. In such a case, temporal changes in guidance mechanism could underlie the FFI phenomenon. In order to test this speculative hypothesis, future experiments should follow pollination dynamics in non-inhibited and inhibited ovaries and upon removal of the inhibitory fruit, in order to ascertain whether fertilization is indeed delayed under FFI.

## **Experimental Procedures**

### **Plant material**

We used two gynoecious cucumber genotypes, breeding lines `235` (parthenocarpic) and 'Elem female' (non parthenocarpic). For physiological experiments and ovary sampling, plants were grown in the net-house, in groups of five plants per 200 L container with potting mixture, or individually in 10 L pots. Female flowers at the 12–15<sup>th</sup> node of the main stem were closed one day before anthesis to prevent pollination and induce senescence in Elem Female, induce parthenocarpic fruit set in line 235, or hand pollinated at anthesis day to induce normal fruit set. To induce first fruit inhibition (FFI), two female flowers on each plant were fertilized (or allowed to set a parthenocarpic fruit in the nopollination treatment of line 235) and fruit were allowed to grow for 10 days, before analyzing the younger ovaries that developed on the same shoot. For each developmental stage, nine biological replicates consisting of single ovaries were collected, frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C.

### RNA extraction, cDNA preparation and qRT-PCR

RNA was prepared from 50 mg of frozen cucumber ovary tissue using the RiboEx (GeneAll, cat. no 301-902) Tri-Reagent protocol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized with a Random primer using qScript Flex cDNA Kit (Quanta). Polymerase chain reactions were carried out in Fast Real-Time PCR System, Applied Biosystems, Singapore. Each 10 µl reaction mixture contained 2 µl each of 10 nM forward and reverse primers, 5 µl of SYBR Green mix (Applied Biosystems) and 1 µl of diluted cDNA and standard cycling conditions were applied. Each qPCR experiment was conducted in biological triplicates consisting of three different RNA samples, and two to three technical replicates. The primers used for qPCR are given in Table S2. To standardize relative transcript quantity (RQ) between samples, four different housekeeping genes were initially trialed. Tubulin exhibited the most constant levels of expression among all ovary stages assayed and was chosen for RQ calculations.

### **RNA Seq experiment**

EXPRESS Tag sequencing was performed as described by Rallapalli et al. (2014). Briefly, total RNA (3 µg) was used to generate first-strand cDNA using an oligo(dT) primer comprising the P7 sequence Illumina (http://www.illumina.com/). Double-strand cDNA was synthesized as described previously

(Okayama and Berg, 1982). Purified cDNA was subjected to Covaris shearing (http://covarisinc.com/) (intensity 5, duty cycle 20%, cycles/burst 200, duration 90 sec). End repairing and A-tailing of cDNA was performed as described by Illumina. Y-shaped adapters were ligated to A-tailed DNA and subjected to size selection on agarose gels. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Genome Analyzer IIx. The Illumina sequence library was quality filtered using FASTX-Toolkit 0.0.13 (http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx\_toolkit/index.html) with parameters -q20 and -p50. The sub-library was artefact-filtered using the FASTX Toolkit. The cucumber reference genome (Huang et al., 2009) and its annotation file were downloaded from

ftp://www.icugi.org/pub/genome/cucumber/Chinese\_long/v2/. STAR (v2.5.0a) (Dobin et al., 2013) was used to construct indices and align the reads to the reference genome. The RNA-seq data has been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE108683. HTSeq v0.6.1p1 (Anders and Huber, 2010) was used to count the number of reads mapped to each gene. Differential expression analysis of conditions was performed using the DESeq2 R package (Love et al., 2014). A corrected P-value of 0.05 and log2 (fold change) of 1 were set as the threshold for detecting significant differentially expressed genes.

### Gene Ontology and KEGG enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was implemented using the GOseq R package (Young et al., 2010) in which gene length bias was corrected. GO terms with corrected P values of <0.05 were considered significantly enriched by DEGs. We also classified all protein sequences into functional categories ("bins") using the annotation tool Mercator (Lohse et al., 2014). The ClusterProfiler R software package (Yu et al., 2012) was used to examine the statistical enrichment of DEGS in The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways (Kanehisa and Goto, 2000; Kanehisa et al., 2016). Pathways with a q-value of <=0.1 were considered.

### **Metabolome analysis**

Metabolites were extracted from cucumber ovaries using the MTBE method described by Giavalisco et al., (2011). Vacuum-dried 150 µl polar phases were used for gas-chromatography massspectrometry (GC-MS) analysis following derivatization of metabolites (Lisec et al. 2006). GC-MS data were obtained using an Agilent 7683 series auto-sample (Agilent Technologies, http://www.home.agilent.com), coupled to an Agilent 6890 gas-chromatograph-Leco Pegasus two time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Leco; http://www.leco.com). Chromatograms were exported from

Leco CHROMATOF software (version 3.34) to R software. Ion extraction, peak detection, retentiontime alignment and library searching were obtained using the TargetSearch package from Bioconductor (Cuadros-Inostroza et al., 2009). Annotation of polar metabolites was performed by matching mass spectra and retention times to a library of standards (Golm Metabolomic Database; (Kopka et al., 2005), using the Target-search package (Cuadros-Inostroza et al., 2009). Daynormalization and sample median-normalization were conducted, and the resulting data matrix was log2 transformed. Raw metabolomic data were imported into R-software environment (http://www.r-project.org) in order to carry out statistical analysis and graphical representations. To determine significant (p < 0.05) differences between the different treatments and their control, a one-way ANOVA (Student's *t* test, ANOVA, Bonferroni-correction) was performed. For a comparison of the transcriptomic and metabolomics data, we assigned the metabolites to biological categories of the Mapman mapping file for the cucumber genome (Ramsak et al., 2014).
