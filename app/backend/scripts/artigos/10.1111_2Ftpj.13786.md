Running head: Gene imprinting in early embryo of maize

#### SUMMARY

Gene imprinting is a widely observed epigenetic phenomenon in maize endosperm; however, whether it also occurs in maize embryo remains controversial. Here, we used high throughput RNA sequencing on laser capture microdissection (LCM) and manually dissected maize embryos from reciprocal crosses between inbred lines B73 and Mo17 at six time points (3 to 13 days after pollination) to analyze allelic gene expression patterns. Co-expression analysis revealed sequential gene activation during maize embryo development. Gene imprinting was observed in maize embryos and a greater number of imprinted genes were identified at early embryo stages. Sixty-four strongly imprinted genes were identified (at the threshold of 9:1) on 5 DAP to 13 DAP manually dissected embryos (more imprinted genes on 5 DAP). Forty-one strongly imprinted genes on 3DAP). Furthermore, of the 56 genes that were completely imprinted (at the threshold of 99:1), 36 were not previously identified as imprinted genes in endosperms and nor in embryos. *In situ* hybridization

demonstrated that most of the imprinted genes were expressed abundantly in maize embryotic tissue. Our results shed lights on early maize embryo development and provide evidence supporting that gene imprinting occurs in maize embryos.

#### **INTRODUCTION**

In plant seeds, the embryo transmits genetic information to the next generation and the endosperm provides nutrients for embryo development and subsequent seed germination (Mei *et al.*, 2015; Sabelli and Larkins, 2009). A plant embryo develops from the fusion of one sperm and one egg, and an endosperm is from the fusion of a second sperm with two polar nuclei (Chaudhury *et al.*, 2001; Dumas and Mogensen, 1993). Embryo development in maize includes three stages: first, the oblate spheroidal embryo expands with increasing number of cells and activated gene expression (Mechin *et al.*, 2007); second, simultaneous differentiation of the coleoptile primordium, shoot meristem, and scutellum primordium occurs (Feng *et al.*, 2003); third, the embryo thickens and the scutellum enlarges rapidly toward the front, back and sides, forming the upper and lower longitudinal grooves (Feng *et al.*, 2003). Afterward, the embryo acquires nutrients including starch and oil to develop into a mature embryo.

Previous studies have explored gene expression patterns during embryo and endosperm development in maize. Gene expression in endosperm at almost every developmental stage has been analyzed (Chen *et al.*, 2014; Gutierrez-Marcos *et al.*, 2004; Meyer *et al.*, 2007; Waters *et al.*, 2013; Xin *et al.*, 2013; Zhan *et al.*, 2015; Zhang *et al.*, 2011). For example, bases on the analysis of RNA-seq data of maize endosperm from 6 DAP (days after pollination) to 38 DAP, 22362 genes were found to be expressed in endosperm, of which 437 were exclusively expressed in endosperm (Chen *et al.*, 2014). The number of expressed genes fluctuates during endosperm development: a large number of genes are expressed from 6 DAP to 10 DAP, and a smaller number of genes are expressed from 12 DAP to 16 DAP. After 16 This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved. DAP, the number of expressed genes gradually increases and reaches a maximum on 38 DAP (Chen *et al.*, 2014). As early stage maize embryos are often too small to be isolated, large scale gene expression studies have been limited to 8 DAP and later (Chen *et al.*, 2014; Lu *et al.*, 2013; Raissig *et al.*, 2013; Zhan *et al.*, 2015). In one RNA-seq analysis of maize embryos from 10 DAP to 38 DAP, 22686 genes were found to be expressed in the embryos, of which 536 were exclusively expressed in the embryos. In contrast to endosperm, the number of expressed genes in maize embryo is generally stable (Chen *et al.*, 2014). In another study of gene expression in maize embryos on 8 DAP, 2235 genes were found to be expressed only in the embryos, and 9.9% of the 2014 transcription factor (TF) genes expressed in the embryos were exclusively expressed in the embryos (Zhan *et al.*, 2015). Despite these data, large scale gene expression analysis of early embryo development, especially earlier than 8 DAP, is still lacking.

Gene imprinting is a widely observed epigenetic phenomenon in mammals and plants (Barrera-Figueroa et al., 2011; Huh and Rim, 2013; Jiang and Kohler, 2012; Kohler et al., 2012; Pignatta and Gehring, 2012; Wang et al., 2011). It refers to the parent-of-origin-specific expression of certain genes in various tissues, most commonly in endosperm. In maize, gene imprinting was mainly found in endosperm (Waters et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2014), i.e. Fie1, Fie2, Nrp1, Mez1, Meg1 and Peg1 (Raissig et al., 2011). In 1970, the imprinted gene R1 was first discovered in maize, and it was identified as a maternally expressed gene (MEG) based on the observation that fully colored kernels were produced when both the R and B alleles were inherited from the maternal parent but not from the paternal parent (Gehring, 2013; Kermicle, 1970; Selinger and Chandler, 2001). This gene encodes an MYC-like TF, which regulates anthocyanin biosynthesis in maize endosperm (Kermicle and Alleman, 1990). In addition, the paternally expressed gene (PEG) *GRMZM2G091819* has been found to play a critical role in endosperm development (Bernardi et al., 2012). In Arabidopsis, the MEG FH5 has been found to participate in endosperm cellularization (Gerald et al., 2009). These examples suggest that imprinted genes may participate

in endosperm development. So far, only a few putative imprinted genes have been detected in maize embryos, and most of them have also been found to be imprinted genes in endosperm, i.e. GRMZM2G003909, GRMZM2G006732 and GRMZM2G009465 (Chen et al., 2014; Gehring et al., 2011; Jahnke and Scholten, 2009; Waters et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2014). Thus, some investigators suspected that these putative imprinted genes in maize embryo might be derived from embryo samples contaminated by endosperm or ESR (embryo surrounding region) (Chen et al., 2014; Xin et al., 2013; Zhan et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2011). Therefore, whether gene imprinting occurs in maize embryo remains elusive. Most imprinted genes have been identified at one single time point of endosperm development. For example, 69 MEGs and 108 PEGs were detected from 14 DAP endosperm (Waters et al., 2013), and 68 MEGs and 111 PEGs were detected from 10 DAP endosperm in maize (Zhang et al., 2011). The imprinted genes identified from the two time points of maize endosperm development share some genes in common. Because the embryo and endosperm at early stages are usually too small to be isolated, the earliest maize embryos that have been investigated for gene expression are 9 DAP embryos obtained by manual dissection (Lu et al., 2013) and 8 DAP embryos obtained by laser capture microdissection (LCM) (Zhan et al., 2015). A zygote forms between 36 and 48 hours after pollination, and lots of gene transcriptions are activated at this time frame. Thus, this post-pollination early period is also critical for maize embryo development (Garcia-Aguilar and Gillmor, 2015). Beginning on 14 DAP, dramatic gene expression changes occur and embryo development enters the stage of starch and protein synthesis (Mei et al., 2015). Therefore, gene expression analyses of embryos between 3 DAP to 13 DAP are essential for understanding the underlying molecular mechanisms of early kernel development in maize. Here, we used embryos from reciprocal crosses between maize inbred lines, B73

and Mo17 to conduct gene expression analyses in a time course of early embryo development (3 DAP-13 DAP) in maize. We characterized the sequential gene activations during maize embryo development and identified 64 strongly imprinted

genes (at the threshold of 9:1) from manually dissected embryos (5 DAP to 13 DAP), including 57 MEGs and 7 PEGs. In addition, 41 strongly imprinted genes (37 MEGs and 4 PEGs) were identified from 3 DAP and 5 DAP embryos obtained by LCM. Overall, we found 91 strongly imprinted genes in maize embryos, and 36 of them were not previously found in endosperm and embryos. Our results indicate that gene imprinting during embryo development may be more common than what previous research have suggested. Imprinted genes might play roles in early embryo development.

#### RESULTS

#### RNA sequencing analysis of embryo and endosperm samples

To understand the gene expression patterns of early embryo development in maize, we performed time-course RNA-seq analysis on the immature embryos ranging from 3 DAP to 13 DAP. The kernels from reciprocal crosses between B73 and Mo17 (BM: B73 × Mo17; MB: Mo17 × B73) were used, and embryos on 5 DAP, 7 DAP, 9 DAP, 11 DAP, and 13 DAP, and endosperm on 5 DAP (as control) were collected by manual dissection (Figure 1a); 3 DAP and 5 DAP embryos were harvested by LCM (Figure 1b, 1c). For RNA-Seq analyses, 26,237 embryos were collected by manual dissection (ten samples of BM and MB at each of the five DAP time points, respectively); 973 embryos were harvested by LCM (four samples of BM and MB at two time points); each sample included three biological replicates.

The Illumina Hiseq2500 sequencer generated 9.53M-26.80M (average 18M) clean reads from each biological replicate. Clean reads were aligned to the B73 reference genome and Mo17 reference genome (See materials and methods, Figure S1). The average-mapping ratio was nearly 89% and the unique mapping ratio was > 83% for samples obtained by manual dissection; these ratios were 80% and 75%, respectively for samples obtained via LCM (Table S1). gene expression data of the three biological replicates were above 0.97 for manually dissected samples (Figure S2a) and above 0.9 for LCM samples (Figure S2b), indicating that the gene expression data were highly reproducible. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed (Figure S2c) and the correlation clustering (Figure S2d) of the expression profiles were explored. Our data showed that the gene expression patterns were divided into three groups: group 1 (red circle) represents the early embryotic stage (reciprocal crosses on 5 DAP), group 2 (yellow circle) represents the intermediate developmental stage (reciprocal crosses on 7 DAP), and group 3 (blue circle) represents the late developmental stage (reciprocal crosses from 9 DAP to 13 DAP). These results indicate that the gene expression pattern changes at early stage (5DAP or 7DAP) embryo development may be greater than those at later stage (9DAP to 13DAP).

The MB embryos were significantly larger than the BM embryos (p < 0.001, student's *t*-test), especially on 9 DAP and 13 DAP; the average area of BM embryos was 0.115 ± 0.008 mm<sup>2</sup> and 2.460 ± 0.006 mm<sup>2</sup> on 9 and 13 DAP, respectively; the average area of MB embryos was 0.247 ± 0.042 mm<sup>2</sup> and 4.003 ± 0.083 mm<sup>2</sup>, respectively (Figure 1d).

## Gene expression patterns during early embryo development in maize.

To identify the differential gene expression changes during early embryo development, we performed three types of pairwise comparison at the five DAP time points (5, 7, 9, 11 and 13 DAP) on the manually dissected samples and identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using a 2-fold change and FDR < 0.05 as significance cutoffs (Figure 2a, 2b, 2d). Type I DEGs were identified by comparing the gene expression profiles at two adjacent time points within the same cross direction (BM or MB). In B73 × Mo17, the number of DEGs decreased rapidly as the embryos continuously developed, indicating reduced transcriptome changes in the later stages of maize embryo development. However, in Mo17 × B73, the number of DEGs This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

remained generally stable (Figure 2a), suggesting that the direction of cross may influence gene expression during maize embryo development substantially. Type II DEGs were identified by comparing the expression profile at each later time point (7, 9, 11, and 13 DAP) to the profile at the earliest time point (5 DAP) in each cross (Figure 2b). As expected, in both BM and MB, the number of type II DEGs increased as the embryos developed, indicating that overall gene expression may change as embryos continuously develop.

The K-mean clustering method was used to classify type I and type II DEGs. Both 7164 DEGs in BM and 8152 DEGs in MB were classified into 10 co-expression modules, respectively. As shown in Figure 2c, sequential gene activations occurred at the three developmental stages of maize embryo development in both crosses. Genes related to responses to water deprivation or desiccation were specifically expressed at the early stage (5 DAP), which is consistent with the water loss phenomenon occurring during embryo development. Genes related to transcriptional or TF regulations were expressed at both the early and intermediate developmental stages (5 DAP and 7 DAP). Genes related to energy metabolism such as the genes involved in "CTP synthase activity", "nucleoside–triphosphatase activity", and "ribonucleotide binding" were activated at the intermediate and late developmental stages (7 DAP to 13 DAP). Genes related to nutrient reservoir activity were only expressed at the late stage of embryo development (11 DAP and 13 DAP).

To detect genes whose expression could be significantly affected by cross direction, we identified type III DEGs by comparing the gene expression profiles of each of the two reciprocal crosses at the same time point (Figure 2d). The earliest time point (5 DAP) showed the highest number of DEGs compared with the subsequent developmental stages. The number of DEGs decreased continuously to reach the lowest number on 9 DAP, and then increased on 11 DAP and 13 DAP, suggesting that cross direction could affect gene expression at both early and late developmental stages of maize embryos. Venn diagram analyses of type III DEGs revealed that only three DEGs were overlapped at the five time points in BM and This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

none in MB, suggesting that cross direction-related gene expression may be developmental stage specific (Figure S3a). Category enrichment analyses of the type III DEGs showed that category terms such as "RNA", "S-assimilation", and "signaling" were only enriched at early stage DEGs (5 DAP), whereas category terms such as "transport" and "amino acid metabolism" were enriched at all developmental stages in Mo17 x B73 (Figure S3b). On the other hand, very few category terms were enriched significantly in B73 x Mo17.

To identify DEGs that were not affected by cross direction, we combined the Type I and Type II DEGs and compared the combined DEGs to the Type III DEGs (Figure 2e). After removing 3512 overlapping DEGs, the remaining 6020 DEGs were then considered as development-related genes. The 6020 DEGs were classified into six major clusters by the K-mean algorithm (Teoh *et al.*, 2013) (Figure S3c). Based on how gene expression changed over time of development, we further sorted the six clusters into three classes: steadily increasing (Cluster 1 and Cluster 2), steadily decreasing (Cluster 3 and Cluster 4), and fluctuating transcription (Cluster 5 and Cluster 6) (Figure S3c). GO enrichment analysis showed that genes related to fatty acid biosynthesis were significantly enriched in Cluster 1 (increasing expression as embryo development progressed), which is consistent with the accumulation of stored oil within the embryo (Zhang et al., 2011). Genes related to transcriptional regulation were specifically enriched in Cluster 3 (decreasing expression from 5 to 13 DAP), which is consistent with a previous study showing that overall gene expression decreases during embryo development and that the majority of genes are expressed during early embryo development (Smaczniak et al., 2012).

#### Identification of imprinted genes in maize embryos.

To identify genes that showed significant expression bias towards maternal or paternal origin of transcription, we developed a scatter plot to illustrate the relative transcription output from the maternal and paternal alleles of each gene with more than 10 allelic reads in the embryo transcriptome (Figure 3a). We used three different

thresholds to identify genes that were imprinted at three different levels (Supplemental Materials and Methods). As shown in Figure 3a, 3b, 118 imprinted genes (106 MEGs and 12 PEGs) were identified (indicated by blue boxes) using the moderate threshold (3:1). Among them, 64 genes (57 of 106 MEGs and 7 of 12 PEGs) were strongly imprinted (Figure 3a, 3b, Figure S4, indicated by two red boxes). In addition, 44 of the 64 strongly imprinted genes were identified as completely imprinted genes (39 MEGs and 5 PEGs), they are indicated by black arrows in the figures (Figure 3a, 3b, Figure S4.).

Previous reports have shown that most of the imprinted genes identified in maize embryos are also imprinted genes in the maize endosperm (Waters *et al.*, 2013; Zhang *et al.*, 2014). We then compared the imprinted genes in 5 DAP embryo with those in 5 DAP endosperm. As shown in Figure 3c, the MEGs significantly outnumbered the PEGs in the 5 DAP embryos. In contrast, the number of PEGs was greater than the number of MEGs in the 5 DAP endosperm. Of the 118 imprinted genes (using the moderate threshold 3:1) identified in the embryo samples, 88 were specifically imprinted in the embryos. Next, we classified the imprinted genes into five sets. Set I: MEGs imprinted in both embryos and endosperms; Set II: MEGs only imprinted in embryos but not in endosperms; Set III: MEGs only imprinted in endosperms but not in embryos; Set IV: PEGs imprinted in both embryos and endosperms; Set V: PEGs only imprinted in endosperms but not in embryos (Figure 3d, Table S3.). In these five sets, we found that parts of the genes were embryo or endosperm specific MEGs / PEGs.

To identify which genes were continuously imprinted during early maize embryo development, we selected the genes that were imprinted at least two adjacent time points. Of the 64 strongly imprinted genes, 20 (17 MEGs and 3 PEGs) were continuously imprinted at two or more developmental stages. As shown in Figure 3e, these continuously imprinted genes were usually imprinted at the early stage (5 DAP, the first time point), except for three genes (*GRMZM2G130580*, *GRMZM2G150680*, and *GRMZM2G020302*), which were imprinted on 9 DAP. In agreement with previous studies, two MEGs (*GRMZM2G062650*, *GRMZM2G370991*) and two PEGs (*GRMZM2G028366*, *GRMZM2G127160*), which were identified as imprinted genes at the five time points in our study, have also been found as imprinted genes on 8, 10, 12, and 14 DAP endosperm in maize (Lu *et al.*, 2013; Waters *et al.*, 2013; Zhan *et al.*, 2015; Zhang *et al.*, 2014; Zhang *et al.*, 2011). To confirm the imprinted genes identified by using the manually dissected samples and to explore whether more imprinted genes could be identified, we analyzed gene expression data from samples obtained via LCM at 3 DAP and 5 DAP (Figure 4a). We used the same analysis strategy as for the manually dissected samples, and 80 MEGs and 13 PEGs were identified as moderately imprinted at 3 DAP and 21 MEGs and 6 PEGs were moderately imprinted at 5 DAP. Among them, 29 MEGs and 3 PEGs at 3 DAP as well as 14 MEGs and 2 PEGs at 5 DAP were strongly imprinted (Figure 4b, 4c, Table S2), confirming that more imprinted genes were detected at early embryo development. Fragments of 400-800bp with more than one SNP difference between the two parents were selected. Among them, 12 MEGs were predominantly expressed from maternal alleles in reciprocal crosses; 6 PEGs were predominantly expressed from paternal alleles in reciprocal crosses. These results are consistent with the mRNA-seg data (Figure S6).

### Comparison of imprinted gene datasets of 5DAP embryo obtained by manual dissection versus by LCM

Our RNA-seq data from both manually dissected and LCM samples supported that many genes were imprinted during early embryo development. Notably, 57 strongly imprinted genes (51 MEGs and 6 PEGs, at the threshold of 9:1) were detected in manually dissected 5 DAP embryos. Moreover, 16 strongly imprinted genes (14 MEGs and 2 PEGs) were identified in 5 DAP embryos obtained by LCM. Only 11 genes were overlapped in the two imprinted gene sets. To further investigate the low overlapping rate between the two imprinted gene datasets, we examined the expression levels of the 46 non-overlapping genes (57 genes minus 11 genes) in the 5 DAP endosperm tissues and found that 89.1% (41 of 46) of the non-overlapping genes were expressed at low levels (FPKM<10) in the endosperm, indicating that the 46 imprinted genes detected in the embryo samples obtained by manual dissection might not derive from endosperm tissue contamination. In addition, 68.8% (11 of the 16 genes) of the imprinted genes detected in the LCM embryo samples were also found in the manually dissected samples. Of the 5 imprinted genes that were only identified in the LCM samples but not in the manually dissected samples, four were imprinted moderately (at threshold of 3:1) and one (GRMZM2G420743) had too low allelic reads to determine its M/P expression. These results showed that almost all imprinted genes detected in the LCM samples were also found in the manually dissected samples. Of the 46 non-overlapping genes in the manually dissected embryo samples, 46% (21 of 46) did show moderate imprinting in the LCM embryo samples; 50% (23 of 46) showed too insufficient allelic reads in at least two biological replicates in the LCM samples to determine their M/P expressions; 4% (2 of 46) were strongly imprinted only in the MB cross but showed low allelic reads in the BM cross (Table S4). The high proportion (50%) of the non-overlapping genes with low allelic reads may be associated with the following reasons: the sequencing data of the LCM samples were derived from the amplified library, while the data of the manually

dissected samples were from the non-amplified library. Before amplification, the FPKM of a gene should be similar in the datasets from the two sources. However, 2-round of amplification for the LCM samples may change the FPKM of the gene. Furthermore, compared with the highly expressed genes, the genes with low expression levels were more difficult to be amplified. Therefore, the genes with low expression levels could show lower FPKM in the LCM samples. As shown in Table.S4, 91.3 % (21 of 23) of the genes with low allelic reads in the LCM samples also had low FPKM value (<10 in at least two replicates) in the manually dissected samples. Therefore, we speculated that the systematic bias during the 2-round amplification for the LCM samples might cause the inconsistency in the datasets from the two sources, especially for the genes with low expression levels.

#### In situ hybridization in maize embryos.

To verify the expression pattern of imprinted genes in embryo, we randomly chose nine strongly imprinted genes for *in situ* hybridization. Six of the nine genes showed detectable signals (Figure 5a). The MEG GRMZM2G003909 encodes proline-rich protein 2 (Prp2) and is involved in plant cell wall organization. GRMZM2G003909 transcripts were detected in the entire embryo and the surrounding tissues on 3 DAP (Figure 5a). On 5 DAP, *GRMZM2G003909* transcripts accumulated specifically in the apical region of the embryo (Figure 5b). On 7 DAP and 11 DAP, the transcripts spread in the entire embryo except the suspensor (Figure 5c, 5d, 5e). Five imprinted genes displayed similar expression patterns in the 5 DAP embryos with different signal intensities (Figure 5f, 5g, 5h, 5i, 5j). GRMZM2G359952, a putative MADS-box TF, showed the strongest signal (Figure 5f) (Smaczniak et al., 2012), followed by a homolog of *Arabidopsis* BETA-EXPANSINS (ATEXPB2) GRMZM2G118873 (Figure 5g) (Pre et al., 2008). Because MADS-box TF plays essential roles in almost every developmental process and Arabidopsis EXPANSINS participates in cell expansion and organ development, the high expression of *GRMZM2G359952* and *GRMZM2G118873* in the apical region of the embryo implies

that both of the genes may play important roles in embryo growth and development. Similarly, *GRMZM2G099454*, a putative homolog of *Arabidopsis* basic chitinase involved in the ethylene/jasmonic acid mediated signaling pathway during systemic acquired resistance (Lee *et al.*, 2001), was highly expressed in the apical region of the 5DAP embryo (Figure 5h). *GRMZM2G050412* and *GRMZM5G898755* showed moderate expression in similar regions. The specific activation of gene expression in the apical region of embryo is consistent with the fact that rapid division and differentiation occur in this area. Thus, our data indicate that some of the imprinted genes may be mostly expressed in maize embryos.

#### DISCUSSION

### Transcriptomic signatures during early embryo development in maize.

Double fertilization is one of the fundamental processes in flowering plants that produce embryos and endosperms (Mei *et al.*, 2015; Sabelli and Larkins, 2009). Embryos transmit genetic information to offspring and endosperm provides nutrients for seed development and germination (Chaudhury *et al.*, 2001; Dumas and Mogensen, 1993). Large scale gene expression analyses on maize embryos have been focused on 8 DAP to 38 DAP (Chen *et al.*, 2014; Jahnke and Scholten, 2009; Lu *et al.*, 2013; Raissig *et al.*, 2013; Zhan *et al.*, 2015). However, gene expression patterns in early maize embryos (before 8 DAP) remain elusive. Here, we investigated the transcriptome of maize embryos from 3 DAP to 13 DAP (Figure 2c). Our data reveal sequential waves of gene activation during maize embryo development. On 5 DAP, genes related to responses to water deprivation were specifically expressed. TFs were specifically up-regulated on both 5 DAP and 7 DAP, and energy metabolism related genes were activated on 7 DAP and 13 DAP. Genes

related to nutrient reservoir activity were specifically up-regulated on 11 DAP and 13 DAP. Such sequential gene expression activation enables proper embryo development in maize.

### Cross direction influences gene expression during embryo development.

Previous studies have shown that cross direction plays an important role in heterotic and kernel development in maize (He et al., 2013; Meyer et al., 2007; Waters et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2011). In addition, cross direction has been shown to affect gene expression in the hybrid offspring (Waters et al., 2011; Xin et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2011). For example, in the B73 and oh43 cross, B73 held more specific-bias genes than oh43 in their hybrid offspring (Waters et al., 2013). Moreover, the size of the 301 x 005 embryo was significantly larger than that of the 005 x 301 embryo on 6 DAP (Meyer and Scholten, 2007). In our study, we also found that the embryo size of MB was dramatically larger than that of BM at all six time points (Figure 1d). The RNA-seq data showed that cross direction affected the transcription of a large number of genes (Figure 2, Figure 3 and Figure 4). The number of DEGs compared with adjacent time points in BM decreased over time, whereas in MB, the number of DEGs fluctuated (Figure 2a). Furthermore, 3604 DEGs were identified by comparing BM and MB expression profiles at the same time point, and 92 of the 3604 DEGs were cross direction specific (Figure 2e). The growing period from seed planting to seed maturity is approximately 15 days longer in B73 (125 days) than in Mo17 (110 days), and the time from pollination to seed maturity is about 10 days longer in B73 than in Mo17. Thus, the cross direction-mediated morphological and transcriptomic changes may be associated with faster growth of Mo17 and the maternal-biased contribution.

Imprinted genes display high bias in gene expression. In addition to the classic imprinted genes Fie1, Fie2, Nrp1, Mez1, Meg1, and Peg1 in maize endosperm (Raissig et al., 2011), additional imprinted genes have been detected at late stage of embryo and endosperm development (Das et al., 2009; Waters et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2014). Notably, the numbers of MEGs and PEGs in embryo and endosperm are quite different. Maize embryos have a greater number of MEGs than PEGs (Garcia-Aguilar and Gillmor, 2015; Jahnke and Scholten, 2009). In maize and Arabidopsis endosperm, PEGs are more abundant than MEGs (Nie et al., 2015; Waters et al., 2013; Xin et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2011). In this study, we identified 64 strongly imprinted genes in 5 DAP to 13 DAP maize embryos obtained by manual dissection, including 57 MEGs and 7 PEGs (Figure 3b). These finding are opposite to the observation in endosperm (PEGs > MEGs) (Figure 3c). In particular, 51 MEGs and 6 PEGs were found in 5 DAP embryos, accounting for 92% of all imprinted genes identified in the manually dissected embryo samples (Figure 3c). Similarly, 29 MEGs and 3 PEGs were identified at 3 DAP embryos obtained by LCM, accounting for 76% of all imprinted genes identified in the LCM samples (Figure 4c). These data suggest that more imprinted genes may exist in early embryo development in maize.

In addition to the imprinted genes identified previously in embryo and endosperm (Chen *et al.*, 2014; Xin *et al.*, 2013; Zhan *et al.*, 2015; Zhang *et al.*, 2011), we identified 36 strongly imprinted genes in the 3 to 13 DAP embryos and 14 strongly imprinted genes in the 5 DAP endosperm in maize (Table S6) in the current study. GO term analysis revealed that genes related to "sequence–specific DNA binding transcription factor activity", "ovule development", and "borate uptake transmembrane transporter activity" were highly enriched in the imprinted embryo MEGs, while genes related to maintenance of floral organ identity and glycogen biosynthetic process were highly enriched in the imprinted endosperm PEGs and MEGs (Figure S7a).

DNA methylation has been found to be associated with gene imprinting. Previous studies have shown that allele-specific DNA methylation or parent-of-origin-dependent differentially methylation is associated with imprinted genes in plants (Zhang et al., 2011; Ibarra et al., 2012; Rodrigues et al., 2013). In Arabidopsis, a few imprinted genes are regulated by the DNA methyl transferase MET1 or DNA demethylation glycosylase DEMETER (Hsieh et al., 2011; Kinoshita et al., 2004; Tiwari et al., 2008; Wolff et al., 2011). In maize, allele-specific bisulfite sequencing of endosperm DNA revealed DNA hypomethylation at specific maternal alleles and hypermethylation at the corresponding specific paternal alleles (Zhang et al., 2014). Recently, increasing number of studies have indicated that histone modification may also play essential roles in gene imprinting. For example, in mammals, a mutually exclusive relationship exists between allele-specific DNA methylation and histone modification (Fournier et al., 2002; Lindroth et al., 2008; Hon et al., 2012). Regulatory mechanisms underlying gene imprinting in embryos may be different from those in endosperms. In Arabidopsis endosperm, PRC2 regulates H3K27me3 trimethylation, which is involved in maintaining imprinted gene expression at some loci. In contrast, imprinted gene expression in embryos requires eliminating and then reestablishing imprinting marks between generations (Schuettengruber and Cavalli, 2009; Raissig et al., 2013). However, how exactly histone modification and DNA methylation influence gene imprinting in maize early embryos requires further investigation.

#### Evaluation of other tissue contamination in embryo samples.

A recent report on *Arabidopsis* found that complete separation of embryo, endosperm, and seed coat was barely achieved by manual dissection or sorting, and only 15 of 91 putative imprinted MEGs and 67 of 84 putative imprinted PEGs reported previously were actually true imprinted genes in *Arabidopsis* endosperm (Schon and Nodine, 2017). Maize is a typical monocotyledon. In contrast to *Arabidopsis*, maize early embryo and endosperm are located in a triangle shape embryo sac, which is

isolated from other tissues, such as megarchidium and seed coat. Thus, the putative MEGs and PEGs in the maize embryo may be caused by endosperm tissue contamination (Zhan *et al.*, 2015; Zhang *et al.*, 2011).

In our study, to avoid other tissue contamination, we washed each embryo sample at least twice before extracting RNA. Each 5 DAP embryo sac was divided into "embryo" and "endosperm" tissues (same as the corresponding control) to further analyze gene expression in the two types of tissue. We first compared the data of 5 DAP embryo versus the data of endosperm samples. The correlation between each replicate pair was above 0.97, indicating that each replicate pair may be highly similar. Moreover, we compared the previously published gene expression data of 6 DAP to 38 DAP endosperm-specific genes (Chen et al., 2014) with our gene expression data of embryo (3 to 13 DAP) and endosperm (5 DAP) samples. As shown in the Figure S7b, 80% of the endosperm-specific genes expressed in multiple period of endosperm were actually expressed at extremely low levels in our 3 DAP to 13 DAP embryo samples. Notably, a few endosperm-specific genes were highly expressed in our manually dissected embryo samples (top part, consistently highly expressed in manually dissected samples) and LCM samples (bottom part). These highly expressed genes were found in several periods of endosperm (6DAP to 38DAP), and their expression levels were even higher in our embryo samples than in our 5DAP endosperm samples. The abundant gene expression in our embryo samples may not be caused by endosperm contamination. The hybridization process may increase the gene expression to higher levels than the inbred process.

In addition, we further evaluated surrounding tissue contaminations according to the recent report (Schon and Nodine, 2017). We used the gene expression of each type of tissue of 8 DAP kernel (Zhan *et al.*, 2015) as the reference dataset. Because immature kernels develop rapidly, the dataset of 8 DAP kernel may not be the optimal reference for the 3 DAP and 5 DAP embryo data. Thus, we selected the six genes that have been used in RNA-*in situ* and confirmed to have high-level expressions in 5 DAP embryos. Our RNA-seq results showed that 5 of the 6 genes had the highest This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved. expression in our 5 DAP embryo samples (Figure S7c). We examined the expression of the six genes in the reference dataset of 8 DAP kernel. Unfortunately, these genes were highly expressed in NU, PC, PE, and PED, but not in EMB (Figure S7d). Therefore, the 8 DAP reference data were not suitable as a reference for the comparison of our 3 to 5 DAP embryo data. We then further compared our embryo data (7 to 13 DAP) with the data from every tissue of the 8 DAP kernel by LCM (Figure S7e). RNA from other tissues was not enriched in the embryo samples, suggesting that the embryo samples of the current study appear unlikely to contain substantial contaminations of other types of tissues.

Moreover, of the 36 strongly imprinting genes that were identified in the current study but not in previous studies, 20 were not imprinted in our 5 DAP endosperm samples. It indicate that the 20 imprinted genes may not be caused by endosperm tissue contamination. In addition, 41 genes were strongly imprinted in the 3 DAP and 5 DAP embryos obtained by LCM (Figure S7f), and LCM has been considered a reliable isolation method showing the least cross-tissue contamination (Schon and Nodine, 2017). In summary, we found extensive gene imprinting in early embryo of maize although we cannot completely rule out the possibility of endosperm tissue contamination.

#### **EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES**

#### Tissue collection and RNA preparation for sequencing.

The two inbred lines, B73 and Mo17, were grown at the China Agriculture University Experiment Station in Beijing; these two lines were used as the two parental lines for reciprocal crosses. Before pollination, bagged the ears and the tassels of B73 and Mo17 with kraft paper on the day prior to pollination. The next day, patted each paper bag to collect the pollen from one parental, which was used to pollinate the ear of the other parental. After 3 days, 5 days, 7 days, 9 days, 11 days,

and 13 days, picked up the ears of reciprocal crosses (BM and MB). At least ten ears were used as replicates for each cross. We collected the embryo (3 to 13 DAP) and endosperm (5 DAP) from at least three ears. In each replicate, RNA samples were isolated using a Quick RNA isolation Kit (Huayueyang Biotechnology of Beijing) and sent to Novogene for mRNA library construction and high-throughput sequencing using the Illumina HiSeq2500 platform. Library construction and sequencing were performed according to Illumina instructions. We obtained 3G data size for each replicate in the following analysis. As the transcriptome size of maize is nearly 60M, the sequence depth is about 50×, which is abundant for the following analysis.

#### Paraffin sections.

At 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, and 13 days after a reciprocal cross between B73 and Mo17, kernels were fixed, embedded, sectioned, and dewaxed as described (Ding *et al.*, 2015). The 10µm-thick sections were mounted in neutral resin, de-waxed, and stained with Hematoxylin staining solution for 5 min (Muhlhausen *et al.*, 2013). Images were collected under a light microscope (D72, Olympus, Japan).

#### Tissue preparation and laser microdissection.

After the reciprocal cross were performed, 3 DAP and 5 DAP kernels were fixed in Farmer's Fixative (3:1 ethanol: acetic acid) and dehydrated. Tissue embedding and sectioning were performed as previously described (Zhao *et al.*, 2016). Laser capture microdissection was performed using a Leica LMD 6500 (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Three biological replicates were performed for each tissue. Each replicate comprised around 5,000 embryonic cells collected from 50-100 maize embryos and obtain 3G data for the following analysis.

#### **RNA extraction and amplification after LCM.**

Total RNA was extracted using an Arcturus PicoPure RNA isolation Kit (Applied Biosystems). DNase I (Qiagen) treatment was performed to remove potential DNA contamination. The TargetAmp 2-Round AminoallyI-aRNA Amplification Kit 1.0 (Epicentre Biotechnologies) was used for RNA amplification according to the manufacturer's protocol. After two rounds of amplification, 45-65µg of amplified RNA was obtained for each sample.

#### Read mapping and gene expression analysis.

After removing the reads with adapters, any reads containing 'n' (>10%), and low quality reads (sQ≤5), the remaining clean reads were checked by FastQC (Andrews, 2010). Clean reads were first aligned to the B73 reference genome using the Tophat aligner (Trapnell et al., 2009) (with up to 4 mismatches). To avoid bias, the unmapped reads were remapped to the Mo17 reference genome, which was created in silico by substituting the B73 allele with the corresponding Mo17 allele according to the single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) between the two inbred lines (with up to 2 mismatches). We required as many reads as possible to be mapped to the reference sequence. Reads with no more than five best mapped positions were retained for further analysis. After combining the mapping results, read counts of annotated genes (filtered gene set version 5b.60) were summarized by HTSeq-count (Anders et al., 2014). If reads pair is counted in more than one gene, the read pair is counted as ambiguous and not counted for any genes. Normalized gene expression values (reads per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped) (FPKM) were estimated using Cufflinks software (V2.2.1) (Trapnell et al., 2012). Genes with an average expression level of FPKM > 1 in three biological replicates were identified as "expressed". The RNA-Seq data have been deposited at the Sequence Read Archive under accession number GSE95399.

#### Principal component analysis and hierarchical clustering.

Normalized gene expression values were transformed with log<sub>2</sub> (FPKM value + 1) and were then used to calculate the correlation coefficient among biological replicates. FRKM values from three biological replicates were averaged and the log<sub>2</sub> (mean FPMK value + 1) was used for principal component analyses (PCA) and hierarchical clustering. The PCA was calculated with the prcomp function in R with default settings (Ihaka and Gentleman, 1996). Hierarchical clustering was performed using the pheatmap package (Kolde, 2015) with default settings using spearman's correlation distance.

#### Identification of differentially expressed genes.

Differential expressed genes (DEGs) in pairwise comparisons were identified by the R package edgeR (Robinson *et al.*, 2010), which uses counts per gene from HTSeq-count as input and performs data normalization using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method. Genes with at least a 2-fold change in expression and an FDR of < 0.05 were considered expressed differentially.

#### GO term and functional category enrichment analysis.

A k-mean approach was used (R package pheatmap) to cluster all the DEGs identified through pairwise comparisons and GO term enrichment analysis was performed for each cluster. The R package pheatmap was used to draw the heat map of each cluster. The GO term enrichment analysis was conducted for genes included in each cluster using the R Package TopGO (Alexa and Rahnenfuhrer, 2010). Adrian Alexi's improved weighted scoring algorithm and Fisher's test were used to determine the significance of GO term enrichment. Significantly enriched GO terms were identified as those with a p-value < 0.01. The functional category of each gene set was annotated using MapMan (Thimm *et al.*, 2004). Fisher's exact test was used to

examine whether a functional category was significantly overrepresented and the p-value was adjusted using the Benjamin Hochberg method.

#### Measuring allelic expression and identification of imprinted genes.

SNPs were downloaded from the data of Zhang et al. (2014). For the false SNPs, reads were mapped to one parental allele in all samples. Positive SNPs were kept by at least 1% of the reads at that position in the pair of reciprocal hybrids (Waters et al., 2013). By integrating mapping results and positive SNPs, allelic expression was calculated using the R package of asSeq (Sun and Zhabotynsky, 2013) and HTSeq-count. asSeq was used to extract allele-specific reads and HTSeq-count was used to summarize the allele-specific reads of each gene. Genes that had more than 10 allelic reads for each direction of the cross were used to perform the chi-square (x2) test. According to genetic composition, the theoretical allelic ratio is 1 maternal: 1 paternal in embryo and 2 maternal: 1 paternal in endosperm. Moderately imprinted MEGs/PEGs were identified in the embryo if significant allelic bias ( $\chi 2 < 0.05$ ) existed and if >75% of transcripts came from the maternal/paternal allele. In endosperm, moderately imprinted genes had significant allelic bias ( $\chi 2 < 0.05$ ) and >80% of transcripts coming from the maternal allele for MEGs or >60% of the transcripts from the paternal allele for PEGs. Strongly imprinted MEGs/PEGs were genes with significant allelic bias ( $\chi 2 < 0.01$ ) and >90% of transcripts coming from the maternal/paternal allele. Completely imprinted MEGs/PEGs were genes with >99% of the transcripts derived from the maternal/paternal allele (Waters et al., 2013). Genes that were identified as imprinted genes were supported by at least two biological replicates.

#### Tissue-Enrichment Test.

Maternal contamination was estimated according to the description by Schon and Nodine (2017) with some modifications. We used previously published RNA-seq data (Zhan *et al.*, 2015) as the reference. The dataset were the global gene

expression profiles of different seed sub-compartments (obtained by laser capture micro-dissection, LCM) of maize inbred line B73 at 8 DAP. Tissue-enriched genes in the seed sub-compartments of AL (aleurone), BETL (basal endosperm transfer layer), CSE (central starchy endosperm), CZ (conducting zone), EMB (embryo), ESR (embryo-surrounding region), NU (nucellus), PC (placento-chalazal region), PE (pericarp), and PED (pedicel) were downloaded (Zhan *et al.*, 2015). Tissue-enriched gene was defined as having 8-fold higher expression levels in the target tissue than in the other sub-compartments. Gi represents a tissue-enriched gene in tissue i. To quantify Gi enrichment in a sample, the expression levels of Gi [E (Gi)] were normalized to the global population of gene not in E(Gi), or [! E(Gi)], in the sample. All normalized expression levels (FPKM) were converted to percentile rank. The Null hypothesis proposed that mean E(Gi) were not higher than mean! E(Gi) was tested using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test in the R. –log10 of the p-value from the test was considered as the tissue-enrichment score of Gi.

#### Validation of imprinted genes detected in embryo.

Two analyses methods were used to validate that our maize embryo tissues were not contaminated with extensive endosperm tissues. First, according to the standard of contamination in previous reports (Zhang *et al.*, 2011), we compared the MEGs in endosperm with the PEGs in embryo and found no overlapping genes between them, suggesting that there may be no extensive contamination of endosperm in the embryo.

Second, we randomly tested eighteen of the imprinted genes detected in our study using a PCR-sequencing method (*GRMZM2G370991*, *GRMZM2G014119*, *GRMZM2G062650*, *GRMZM2G044440*, *GRMZM2G118205*, *GRMZM2G061088*, *GRMZM2G000566*, *GRMZM2G118873*, *GRMZM2G044882*, *GRMZM2G003909*, *GRMZM2G039538*, *GRMZM2G127160*, *GRMZM5G898755*, *GRMZM2G374169*, *GRMZM2G028366*, *GRMZM2G093947*, *GRMZM2G006732*, *GRMZM5G830365*) (Xu *et al.*, 2014). Each gene fragment was amplified by different primers of four embryo

cDNA samples: BB (self-cross of B73), MM (self-cross of Mo17), BM, and MB of 5 DAP embryos (Figure S6). The primer information are listed in Table S5.

#### In situ hybridization.

Embryos at different developmental stages were fixed in 3.7 % FAA. Embedded, sectioned, and hybridized with digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled sense and antisense gene-specific probes as described (Zhang *et al.*, 2013). The primer pairs used for probe synthesis are listed in Table S5.

#### qRT-PCR analysis.

Fifteen genes were amplified (*GRMZM2G014119*-F/R, *GRMZM2G028366*-F/R, *GRMZM2G137329*-F/R, *GRMZM5G898755*-F/R, *GRMZM2G102356*-F/R, *GRMZM2G354579*-F/R, *GRMZM2G010669*-F/R, *GRMZM2G127160*-F/R, *GRMZM2G370991*-F/R, *GRMZM2G003909*-F/R, *GRMZM2G118205*-F/R, *GRMZM2G107076*-F/R, *GRMZM2G020302*-F/R, *GRMZM2G130580*-F/R, and *GRMZM2G150680*-F/R, Table S5). BM and MB embryos of 5 DAP, 7 DAP, 9 DAP, 11 DAP and 13 DAP were collected for RNA isolation with three biological replicates respectively.

RNA extractions were performed using a Quick RNA isolation Kit (Huayueyang Biotechnology of Beijing). RNA quantity and quality were measured on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies), and only samples yielding intact 18S and 28S ribosomal RNA profiles were used. Reversed transcription was performed using SuperScript® III First-Strand Synthesis System for RT-PCR (Invitrogen, Cat #18080051). Transcripts were quantified by SYBR green fluorescence (Takara, Cat # RR820A) using 7500 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Relative expression was quantified using actin gene as an internal reference.
