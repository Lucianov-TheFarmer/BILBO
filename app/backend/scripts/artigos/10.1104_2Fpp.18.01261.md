- 32 **ABSTRACT**
- 33

34 The Unfolded Protein Response (UPR) is activated in plants in response to endoplasmic 35 reticulum (ER) stress and plays an important role in mitigating stress damage. Multiple factors 36 act in the UPR including the membrane-associated transcription factor, bZIP17, and the 37 membrane-associated RNA splicing factor, INOSITOL REQUIRING ENZYME1 (IRE1). We 38 have analyzed an Arabidopsis (*Arabidopsis thaliana*) *ire1a ire1b bzip17* triple mutant, with 39 defects in stress signaling, and found that the mutant is also impaired in vegetative plant growth 40 under conditions without externally applied stress. This raised the possibility that the UPR 41 functions in plant development in the same manner as it does in responding to stress. bZIP17 is 42 mobilized to the nucleus in response to stress, and through the analysis of a mobilization-43 defective bZIP17 mutant, we found that to support normal plant development bZIP17 must be 44 capable of mobilization. Likewise, through the analysis of *ire1* mutants defective in either 45 protein kinase and ribonuclease activities, we found that both must be operative to promote 46 normal development. These findings demonstrate that the UPR, which is associated with stress 47 responses in plants, also functions under unstressed conditions to support normal development. 48

- 49 **INTRODUCTION**
- 50

51 The unfolded protein response (UPR) is elicited by the accumulation of misfolded 52 proteins in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), a condition defined as ER stress (Urano et al., 2000). 53 In general, the UPR in plants can be induced by adverse environmental conditions or by 54 treatment with ER stress agents, such as tunicamycin or dithiothreitol. However, ER stress can 55 also be induced in the absence of external stressors, such as under certain physiological or 56 developmental conditions in which the demand for protein folding exceeds the capacity of the 57 folding machinery. For example, ER stress is induced in animals when β-lymphocytes 58 differentiate into plasma cells and produce high levels of immune-gamma globulins (IgGs) 59 (Reimold et al., 2001). In plants, the UPR is provoked by the heavy demand in the anther tapetal 60 cells to synthesize and secrete materials comprising the pollen coat (Deng et al., 2016). 61 In response to ER stress, the conditions in the ER are communicated to the nucleus 62 through the UPR signaling pathway (Walter and Ron, 2011). This results in an upregulation of

63 genes involved in protein import, folding, export and quality control. Signaling is mediated by 64 signal transducers that constitute two arms of the UPR signaling pathway in plants (Howell, 65 2013; Bao and Howell, 2017). One arm involves membrane-associated transcription factors, 66 such as bZIP17 and -28, and the other arm involves an RNA splicing factor, INOSITOL 67 REQUIRING ENZYME1 (IRE1). In response to ER stress, bZIP17 and/or -28 are mobilized 68 and transported to the Golgi where they are processed by Golgi-resident proteases, which release 69 their transcription factor domains (bZIP17(p) and/or bZIP28(p)) into the cytoplasm for further 70 import into the nucleus. The other arm of the UPR signaling pathway involves IRE1, for which 71 there are two isoforms in Arabidopsis (*Arabidopsis thaliana*), IRE1a and IRE1b (Koizumi et al., 72 2001). In response to ER stress, IRE1 is activated and splices bZIP60 mRNA, creating a 73 frameshift such that the mRNA encodes a form of bZIP60(s) that is transportable to the nucleus 74 (Deng et al., 2011). bZIP17(p), bZIP28(p) and bZIP60(s) can homo- or heterodimerize to 75 activate stress response genes in the nucleus (Liu and Howell, 2010). IRE1 has another activity 76 independent of its bZIP60 RNA splicing function and that is to degrade other mRNAs in 77 response to stress through a process called Regulated IRE1-dependent RNA Degradation or 78 RIDD (Hollien and Weissman, 2006; Hollien et al., 2009). In Arabidopsis, RIDD primarily 79 attacks mRNA encoding secreted proteins that are being translated by ribosomes on the ER 80 (Mishiba et al., 2013). 81 This report largely focusses on the membrane-bound transcription factor bZIP17, which

82 functions in one branch of the UPR. Loss-of-function *bzip17* mutants have no observable growth 83 phenotype under normal conditions and have only a modest salt-sensitive root growth phenotype 84 when grown on 150 mM NaCl (Liu et al., 2007). The salt sensitivity of *bzip17* was 85 complemented by introduction of 35S: bZIP17 into the *bzip17* mutant background. 86 Overexpression of a constitutively active, truncated form of bZIP17 (35S:bZIP17ΔC) in a wild-87 type background produced seedlings that were growth inhibited, while overexpression of full 88 length bZIP17 (35S:bZIP17) had no effect (Liu et al., 2008). Thus, overexpression of an

89 activated form of bZIP17 in a wild-type background results in a marked phenotype, while the

90 loss-of-function mutation in bZIP17 has no effect under unstressed conditions and results in only

- 91 mild sensitivity to the presence of salt.
- 92 Kim et al. (2018) generated multiple mutants involving bZIP17 and observed 93 considerable growth inhibition in the double *bzip17 bzip28* mutant, from which they concluded

- 94 that bZIP17 plays a pivotal role in vegetative development, with functional redundancy to
- 95 bZIP28. In this report, we have extended those observations by knocking out both arms of the
- 96 UPR signaling pathway and demonstrating that bZIP17 has profound effects on vegetative
- 97 development when it occurs in conjunction with debilitating mutations in *IRE1a* and *IRE1b*,

98 particularly in *IRE1b*. These findings reveal that the UPR is active during normal plant

- 99 development and mutations that affect the UPR in response to stress also impact its role in
- 100 normal development. Thus, the UPR signaling pathway must be competent to respond to stress
- 101 in order to support plant development under unstressed conditions.

### 106 **Mutant analysis**

107 Single mutants in the RNA splicing arm of the UPR signaling pathway, such as *ire1a* or 108 *ire1b*, or in the membrane-associated transcription factor arm involving *bzip28* or *bzip17*, have 109 little effect on the root growth of seedlings under unstressed conditions (**Figure 1, A to D**). On 110 the other hand, various UPR multiple mutants such as the *ire1a ire1b* double mutant have 111 pronounced defects in root growth (**Figure 1, A and D)** (Deng et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2014). 112 Among the double mutants, *ire1b* in conjunction with membrane-associated transcription factor 113 mutants, such as *bzip17* or -*28*, slowed root growth (**Figure 1, B to D**) as was also shown for the 114 double *ire1b bzip28* mutant by Deng et al. (2013). Although *ire1a* in conjunction with *bzip17* or 115 *bzip28* seemed to have little effect on root growth, *bzip17* in combination with *ire1b* in the 116 double *bzip17 ire1b* mutant clearly retarded root growth*.* The root growth defects in the triple 117 *ire1a ire1b bzip17* mutant, which we call *17ab*, were far more severe, resulting in seedlings with 118 short, stubby roots (**Figure 1, B and D**). Even though the *ire1b bzip17* or *ire1b bzip28* double 119 mutants are inhibited in primary root growth, the mutations appear to stimulate lateral root 120 formation. Although the effects in double mutants were not as severe as in the triple mutant, the 121 mutations are not leaky. We verified this by genotyping and showing that the genes are 122 interrupted by T-DNA insertions (**Supplemental Fig. S1**), and we found in previous studies that 123 accumulation of full-length transcripts was undetectable in all three single-mutant lines (Liu et 124 al., 2007, 2007; Bao et al., 2018).

125 To better understand the role of *bZIP17* in plant development, we focused on the triple 126 mutant, *17ab*. We were not able to analyze the triple *ire1a ire1b bzip28* mutant because we have 127 been unable to recover it, and therefore deemed it not viable. The *17ab* triple mutant exhibits 128 profound effects on shoot growth as well as root growth in unstressed seedlings and plants 129 (**Figure 2, A, B and D**). The mutant plants are dwarf, with smaller, but otherwise normal rosette 130 leaves. The *17ab* plants produce inflorescences that appear morphologically normal, but are also 131 stunted (**Figure 2C**), and were late to flower in comparison to wild type (**Figure 2D**). Note the 132 increased number of rosette leaves when the *17ab* plants start bolting, an indicator of delayed 133 flowering (**Supplemental Fig. S2**).

134 In genetic terms, *bZIP60* is immediately downstream from *IRE1*, and its RNA transcript

135 is the principal target of IRE1's RNA splicing activity. Therefore, we determined whether the

136 double *bzip17 bzip60* or *bzip28 bzip60* mutants had root growth inhibition phenotypes

137 comparable to *17ab* (**Figure 3, A and B**). Two alleles of bZIP60 were tested because *bzip60-1*

138 is considered to be hypomorph, while *bzip60-3* is a null mutant (Bao et al., 2018). Neither allele

139 in conjunction with *bzip17* or *-28* in double mutants had any significant effect on root elongation

140 in seedlings (**Figure 3, A and B**) or on shoot phenotype (not shown). These findings argue that

141 *IRE1a* and *IRE1b* function in the support of root growth in ways other than the splicing of

- 142 bZIP60 mRNA.

## 144 **Complementation analysis**

145 We used vegetative shoot and root growth to further assess the effects of UPR mutants on 146 plant development (**Figure 4, A to C**). To do so, we conducted complementation experiments 147 by introducing YFP-tagged UPR transcription factors driven by the ocs-containing super 148 promoter (Li et al., 2001; Lee et al., 2007) into *17ab* to determine whether they could rescue the 149 mutant phenotype**.** We showed that the activated, tagged forms of these bZIP transcription 150 factors, YFP-bZIP17(p), YFP-bZIP28(p) and YFP-bZIP60(s), are successfully targeted to the 151 nucleus (**Supplemental Fig. S3**.) We found that the processed form of YFP-bZIP17(p) and its 152 unprocessed, full-length form, YFP-bZIP17(fl), rescued *17ab*'s dwarf shoot and short root 153 phenotypes, although the rescue only partially restored normal root growth (**Figure 4, A to C**). 154 To our surprise, we observed that the processed form of YFP-bZIP28(p) and the spliced form of 155 YFP-bZIP60(s) also rescued shoot growth and partially restored root growth in the triple mutant. 156 This finding indicates that when overexpressed, YFP-bZIP17, YFP-bZIP28(p) and YFP-157 bZIP60(s) can perform similar functions in promoting shoot and root growth. 158 An important issue to be addressed is whether bZIP17 has to be mobilized to the nucleus 159 to rescue *17ab*'s mutant phenotype under unstressed conditions. Given the perception that 160 bZIP17 and bZIP28 are inactive in unstressed cells, it is not clear where these factors act during 161 normal development. To test whether bZIP17 must be mobilized and transported to the nucleus 162 to support normal development, we attempted to rescue *17ab* with a mutant form of bZIP17 that 163 interferes with its mobilization. bZIP17 is processed in the Golgi by Regulated Intramembrane 164 Proteolysis (RIP) involving SITE-2-PROTEASE (S2P) (Che et al., 2010). S2P cleaves 165 transmembrane domains (TMDs), which are recognized as substrates for S2P proteolysis by the 166 presence of a helix-breaking glycine residue in the middle of the TMD (Fluhrer et al., 2012). In

167 related studies, the helix-breaking glycine in bZIP28 was replaced by an alanine to produce

bZIP28G329A 168 (Srivastava et al., 2012). The mutation allowed bZIP28 to exit the ER in response

- We found that YFP-bZIP17G372A 171 was expressed at levels comparable to YFP-bZIP17(fl)

As controls, we demonstrated that YFP-177 bZIP17(fl) relocated to the nucleus in response to stress conferred by treatment with 2 mM DTT (**Figure 5, A and B**). Thus, we interpret this to mean that YFP-bZIP17G372A 180 can be mobilized out of the ER 181 in response to stress, but that its pathway to the nucleus is blocked in the Golgi. Under 182 unstressed conditions we do not see evidence for the relocation of YFP-bZIP17(fl) or YFPbZIP17G372A 183 to the nucleus, although the resolution of the technique would not have detected the 184 movement of a small amount of these factors (**Supplemental Fig. S5**.)
- 185 IRE1 is a dual protein kinase/ribonuclease, and so we attempted to determine which of its 186 activities are required for normal development under non-stressed conditions by assessing the 187 capabilities of various IRE1 constructs to rescue shoot and root growth in *17ab*. To perform this 188 analysis, we tried to complement *17ab* with site-specific *IRE1b* mutants that inactivate various 189 IRE1 functions (Deng et al., 2013). Both D608N K610N and D628A interfere with the protein 190 kinase activity of IRE1, and N820A knocks out its ribonuclease activity. All three mutations 191 prevent the splicing of bZIP60 mRNA in response to stress (**Figure 6A**). We observed that none 192 of the mutants rescued the dwarf phenotypes of *17ab* (**Figure 6, B to D**), meaning that the 193 protein kinase and ribonuclease functions of IRE1 are needed to restore normal growth. The 194 protein kinase and ribonuclease activities contribute to the RNA splicing capabilities of IRE1, 195 and yet we showed above that *bZIP60*, whose RNA is the splicing target for IRE1, is not 196 required for normal development under unstressed conditions. We interpret this to mean that the 197 RIDD activity of IRE1, and not its bZIP60 splicing activity, is a major contributor to normal 198 development.

200 **RNAseq analysis** 

201 RNAseq analysis was used to diagnose the plant growth syndrome exhibited by the *17ab*

- 202 mutant. Genes upregulated in the mutant compared to wild type may represent genes with RNA
- 203 transcripts that are normally degraded by the RIDD activity of IRE1 in the wild type under
- 204 unstressed conditions (**Supplemental Table S1, Supplemental Fig. S6**). On the other hand, the
- 205 genes that are downregulated can be considered to represent the genes normally activated by
- 206 bZIP17, IRE1a or IRE1b, individually or collectively, under unstressed conditions
- 207 (**Supplemental Table S2, Supplemental Fig. S6**).

208 Of the 163 genes that are upregulated by more than two-fold in the comparison between 209 the *17ab* and *WT*, 74 or 45.4% encode proteins predicted to have signal peptides according to the 210 SignalP server (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/SignalP/). That is a considerable enrichment 211 over the 17.6% of genes, genome-wide, that encode proteins with putative signal peptides in 212 Arabidopsis (Initiative, 2000). These data strongly implicate IRE1's RIDD activity, which 213 preferentially attacks the mRNAs of secreted proteins (Mishiba et al., 2013). In terms of GO 214 categories (according to the GO term enrichment tool in TAIR, **Supplemental Fig. 7**) the set of 215 upregulated genes is enriched 2.83 fold for those that encode proteins predicted to be extracellular (p=1.89E-11 216 ) with the most prominent molecular functions being β-glucosidase (p=6.95E-7) and peroxidase activities (1.94E-6 217 ).

218 Only 27 genes were downregulated (discounting IRE1 and bZIP17) more than 2 fold, but 219 of these only 2 or 7.7% were predicted to have signal peptides, which is less than half of the 220 frequency of genes genome-wide in Arabidopsis that encode proteins with putative signal 221 peptides. As for biological process, there was a 27-fold enrichment in photosynthesis genes compared to genome-wide (p=7.81E-8 222 ) and none of the 27 genes represent canonical UPR genes. 223

- 225 **DISCUSSION**
- 226

227 It seems logical to assume that that the UPR would not have a role in normal 228 development when plants are not being subject to applied stress, because ER stress activates the 229 UPR and, therefore, the UPR would be expected to be inactive under unstressed conditions. In 230 fact, the plants in this study are being coddled, growing in the lab at room temperature under 231 well-watered conditions, quite without applied stress. These conditions would not be expected to 232 launch the UPR, to activate IRE1 and mobilize the membrane-associated transcription factors 233 bZIP17 and bZIP28. However, is it possible that even under these conditions, the UPR functions 234 at a low level, in a minor tissue or during a brief period of development in unstressed seedlings, 235 below the level of their usual means of detection.

236 In fact, there is evidence of low-level activity of the UPR under unstressed conditions in 237 Arabidopsis. Che et al. (2010) reported that bZIP17 was mobilized, transported through the 238 Golgi apparatus and trafficked to the nucleus in unstressed seedlings. In an immunoblot analysis 239 of extracts from unstressed Arabidopsis seedlings, they demonstrated the presence of three forms 240 of bZIP17. 1) A full-length, endo H-sensitive form located in the ER, 2) an endo H-insensitive 241 form located in the Golgi and 3) a truncated form located in the nucleus. The endo H-sensitive 242 form located in the ER was the most prominent form in unstressed seedlings, but in response to 243 stress the pattern changed and the truncated form in the nucleus became more prominent. 244 However, small amounts of the truncated and the endo H-insensitive forms were found in 245 unstressed cells, indicating low-level transport of bZIP17 to the Golgi and on to the nucleus in 246 unstressed seedlings. This could potentially be sufficient to activate the genes we identified by 247 RNAseq as possible bZIP17 targets under non-stressed conditions.

248 Our studies are consistent with the hypothesis that the UPR contributes to normal 249 development in Arabidopsis by operating at low levels or in limited tissues in much the same 250 manner as it does in responding to ER stress (**Figure 7).** That conclusion is reached because 251 components of the UPR have to be competent to function under stress conditions in order to 252 support normal development under unstressed conditions. We show that normal development of 253 17*ab* can be restored by transgenic expression of *IRE1b*, but it cannot be rescued by *IRE1b* 254 mutants with defects in the ribonuclease or protein kinase domains of the protein. Likewise, the 255 triple mutant can be rescued by transgenic expression of bZIP17. However, it cannot be rescued

- 256 by a mutant of bZIP17 with a mutation in its transmembrane domain that interferes with bZIP17
- 257 processing, i.e., proteolytic cleavage by S2P. In other words, if bZIP17 cannot be processed as it
- 258 is in response to stress, then it cannot rescue the growth defect in 17*ab*. Thus, the components of
- 259 the UPR signaling pathway have to be competent to respond to ER stress (i.e. able to be
- 260 activated) in order to support normal development in the absence of applied stress. A major
- 261 difference between the role of the UPR in normal development and its response to stress is the
- 262 apparent lack of involvement of bZIP60 in normal development. Single bZIP60 mutants or

263 double bZIP60 mutants in combination with either bZIP17 or bZIP28 appear to be normal under 264 our unstressed conditions and as reported by Kim et al. (2018).

265 An interesting sidelight is that 17*ab* can be rescued by transgenic expression of a 266 processed form of bZIP28(p) and a spliced form of bZIP60(s). bZIP17 and 28 are thought to 267 have overlapping functions, however, a triple mutant involving bZIP28 is not viable, while the 268 one involving bZIP17 is. The difference in viability could be matter of degree. According to the 269 eFP browser (http://bar.utoronto.ca), *bZIP28* (AT3G10800) is more highly expressed than 270 *bZIP17* (AT2G40950). A knock out in bZIP28 therefore might be more severe than a knockout 271 of bZIP17, just because the levels of bZIP17 may be inadequate to support growth in a bZIP28 272 mutant. Alternatively, there might be some vital gene that only bZIP28 can regulate and bZIP17 273 cannot. Unfortunately, the reciprocal experiment of rescuing a bZIP28-containing triple mutant 274 with bZIP17 cannot easily be performed since the bZIP28 triple mutant is not viable. The rescue 275 of 17*ab* by the spliced form of bZIP60(s) is curious, since the double *bzip60 bzip17* mutant does 276 not affect normal development. However, it should be pointed out that in a yeast two-hybrid 277 system bZIP17, -28 and -60 both homo and heterodimerize with each other (Liu and Howell, 278 2010) and have overlapping target genes. However, the difference between the knockout 279 mutants and the complementation experiments might be due to the difference in expression of 280 the genes or the differences in the degree of activation of these factors in unstressed plants.

281 To better understand the 17*ab* mutant syndrome, we conducted a transcriptome analysis 282 of the mutant under unstressed conditions. In comparison to wild type, the upregulated genes in 283 the mutant are highly enriched for secreted proteins. This is consistent with the possible role of 284 IRE1's RIDD activity in promoting normal development under unstressed conditions. As stated 285 above, we have shown that that IRE1's bZIP60 mRNA splicing is not required for normal 286 development, because the *bzip17 bzip60* double mutant is normal. Although the splicing of 287 bZIP60 mRNA is not required for normal development, the ribonuclease activity of IRE1 is 288 required. That was demonstrated by the inability to rescue 17*ab* with a RNase-inactive IRE1b. 289 The fact that it does not rescue argues that IRE1's RIDD function, but not its mRNA splicing 290 activity, is required for normal development. Thus, when the *IRE1* genes are knocked out in 291 17*ab*, RIDD-targeted mRNAs accumulate. RIDD targets are largely mRNAs that encode 292 secreted proteins, many of which traffic to membranes, membranous organelles and the cell 293 walls. Therefore, during normal development under unstressed conditions these mRNAs are

294 likely held in check by the RIDD activity of IRE1, and the degradation or turnover of these 295 RNAs are required for normal development.

296 This, however, does not explain the role of bZIP17 in normal development. bZIP17 is a 297 positive acting transcription factor that upregulates the expression of both canonical and non-298 canonical UPR genes in response to stress (Liu et al., 2007; Che et al., 2010, Kim et al., 2018). 299 Therefore, one would expect to see the downregulation of bZIP17 transcriptional targets in the 300 17*ab* mutant. There are not many genes substantially downregulated in 17*ab* compared to wild 301 type under unstressed conditions. However, those that are downregulated are not canonical UPR 302 genes. This is similar to the finding made by Kim et al. (2018) who showed in the *bzip17 bzip28* 303 double mutants that the downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) under unstressed 304 conditions are not the canonical UPR targets. Many of the DEGs in their study were associated 305 with auxin regulation or cell growth, while in our study many were associated with chloroplast 306 functions.

307 In other organisms such as *Caenorhabditis elegans*, a functioning UPR is required for 308 normal development (Shen et al., 2001). *C. elegans* has a single *IRE1* gene, and a knock down 309 or a knock out mutant of *IRE1* seems to have no effect on normal development. However, *C.*  310 *elegans* has a homolog of the ER Protein Kinase called PEK. Although the single *pek-1* mutant, 311 like *ire1*, has no noticeable effect on development, the *ire1 pek-1* double mutant arrests 312 development at the L2 larval stage. The arrested larvae show vacuolization of intestinal cells 313 prompting Shen et al. (2001) to suggest that the excessive demand for protein synthesis in 314 intestinal cells may induce endogenous ER stress. More recent studies in *C. elegans* indicate that 315 *IRE1* on its own, independent of *X-BOX BINDING PROTEIN1* (*XBP1*), is required under non-316 stressed conditions for neural development, in particular polymodal sensory neuron (PVD) 317 arborization (Salzberg et al., 2017). 318 The UPR is also required for normal development in Drosophila. One demonstration of

319 this requirement is the fact that compromises in the XBP arm of the UPR signaling pathway 320 contribute to retinal degeneration in Drosophila (Ryoo et al., 2007). In an effort to explain the 321 role of the UPR in normal Drosophila development, Sone et al. (2013) developed a high-gain 322 reporter to visualize the splicing of *XBP1* mRNA under normal physiological conditions. The 323 gain of the reporter was heightened by engineering it with a hydrophobic region to better 324 facilitate its interaction with the *XBP1* mRNA splicing machinery. With the high-gain reporter, they were able to detect *XBP1* RNA splicing in the brain, gut, Malpighian tubules, larval trachea
and in the male reproductive organ of Drosophila.

327 As described in the Introduction, the UPR transcription factor Xbp1 in mice is required 328 during normal development to generate plasma cells from B-lymphocytes (Reimold et al., 2001). 329 Also, mouse lymphoid chimeras generated by injecting XBP-17 embryonic stem cells into 330 mouse blastocysts developed normal spleens and lymph nodes, but had low levels of all 331 immunoglobin subtypes. In culture, Reimold et al. (2001) found that the levels of 332 immunoglobulins in the supernatants from XBP-1-deficient B cells were much lower than in 333 controls even though the cells could be activated to proliferate and to undergo cell-surface 334 activation-marker expression and class switch recombination. In general, ablation of IRE1 $\alpha$  or 335 XBP1 in animals causes a wide variety of disfunctions, such as severe abnormalities in exocrine 336 pancreatic acinar cells, plasma B cells, zymogenic Paneth cells and Chief cells in the 337 gastrointestinal tract (Hur et al., 2012).

In the other arm of the UPR signaling pathway, Activating Transcription Factor 6 338 339 (ATF6) in animals, a homolog of bZIP17 and-28 in plants, is a membrane-associated 340 transcription factor. ATF6 is mobilized from the ER in response to ER stress, translocated to the 341 Golgi and processed to release its transcription factor component, which is subsequently 342 imported into the nucleus (Ye et al., 2000; Shen et al., 2002; Nadanaka et al., 2006). ATF6 has 343 also been shown to function in normal animal development and has been implicated in brain, 344 muscle, cartilage, bone, uterine and lens development, as well as in adipogenesis (Hillary and 345 FitzGerald, 2018).

346 Thus, the UPR plays important developmental roles in various organisms under 347 conditions without the application of external stress. In some cases, the stress appears to be 348 endogenous, such as in cells that are heavy secretors. But in other cases, there is no apparent 349 cause for the induction of stress. The requirement for the UPR signaling factors to be competent 350 to respond to stress in order to support normal development suggests that the UPR signaling 351 factors are activated during normal development in a manner similar to their response to stress, 352 except at a low level. What low-level expression actually accomplishes has yet to be determined 353 but should be the subject of further study.

## 366 **Vector construction and plant transformation**

367 To generate the constructs for *17ab* mutant complementation with the bZIP transcriptional

### 389 **RNAseq and data analysis**

390 Wild-type and *17ab* seeds were germinated, and seedlings were grown vertically on 100 mm ×

- 391 100 mm square plates (Fisher Scientific) on half-strength MS medium for 10 days. Triplicate
- 392 samples were immediately ground, and total RNA was extracted using a RNeasy Plant Mini Kit
- 393 (QIAGEN, Cat. No. 74904). RNAseq libraries were prepared and subjected to paired end
- 394 sequencing with read length 250 bp. Sequences were aligned to the Arabidopsis TAIR10
- 395 genome, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using NOISeq. NOISeq is
- 396 an R package divided into three blocks: (1) count data quality control, (2) filtering of low-count
- 397 features, normalization and batch effect correction and (3) DEG analysis (Tarazona et al., 2015).
- 398 The method differs from other RNAseq analysis methods in that it is data-adaptive and
- 399 nonparametric. The method does not have a strong dependency on sequencing depth for
- 400 differential expression calls and because of that the method reduces the incidents of false
- 401 positives as the number of reads grows (Tarazona et al., 2011). We applied the method to our
- 402 data using the criterion of fold change ≥2 and a divergence probability ≥0.8, in which a
- 403 divergence probability for identical distributions is 0.
- 404
