# PRP8A and PRP8B spliceosome subunits act co-ordinately to control pollen tube attraction in Arabidopsis

**Key words:** Pre-mRNA PROCESSING factor 8, Splicing, post-transcriptional regulation, cell-cell signalling, Cysteine-rich proteins, pollen tube attraction and reception, sexual reproduction

## **Abstract**

Precise guided pollen tube growth by the female gametophyte is a pre-requisite for successful sexual reproduction in flowering plants. Cysteine-rich proteins (CRPs) secreted from the embryo sac are known pollen tube attractants perceived by pollen tube receptor-like kinases (RLK's). How pre-mRNA splicing facilitates this cell-to-cell communication is not understood. Here, we report novel function of Pre-mRNA PROCESSING factor 8 paralogs, PRP8A and PRP8B, as regulators of pollen tube attraction. Double mutant *prp8a prp8b* ovules cannot attract pollen tubes, and *prp8a prp8b* pollen tubes fail in sensing ovules attraction signals. Only 3% of ovuleexpressed genes were misregulated in *prp8a prp8b*. Combination of RNA-seq and MYB98/LURE1.2-YFP reporter revealed the expression of MYB98, LUREs and 49 other CRPs were downregulated suggesting loss of synergid cell fate. Differential Exon usage (DEU) and Intron-retention (IR) analysis revealed autoregulation of PPR8A/PRP8B splicing. *In vivo*, PRP8A coimmunoprecipitates with splicing enhancer AtSF3A1, suggesting involvement of PRP8A in 3'-splice site selection. Our data hint that PRP8A/PRP8B module exhibit spliceosome-autoregulation to facilitate pollen tube attraction via transcriptional regulation of MYB98, CRPs and LURE pollen tube attractants.

#### **Introduction**

In flowering plants, two sperm cells are delivered to the female gametophyte for fertilization via a pollen tube after a guided growth through transmitting tract and the micropyle (Palanivelu et al., 2006; Dresselhaus and Franklin-Tong., 2013; Higashiyama and Yang., 2017). Both phases of the pollen tube guidance rely on prelaid synergid cell secreted chemoattractants encoded by Cysteine-rich protein family (CRPs) and Defensin-like subfamily of CRPs (DEFL) including LUREs (Marton et al., 2005; Okuda et al., 2009; Takeuchi et al., 2012; Marton et al., 2012). Genes encoding LURE family of proteins are specifically expressed in the synergid cells and their expression is under the control of MYB98 transcription factor which is also synergid cell-specific (Kasahara et al., 2005). Intriguingly, the expression of MYB98 is by itself controlled by the central cell-specific RNA pol II component, Central Cell Guidance (CCG) and CCG Binding Protein 1 (CBP1), implying a cell-to-cell central cell mediated pollen tube guidance control (Li et al., 2015). Pollen tube attraction and fertilization is inhibited in *ccg* and *cbp1* suggesting indispensable role of central cell in pollen tube guidance (Li et al., 2015). Further, several other secreted CRPs are expressed specifically from the central cell, however their role is still unknown (Li et al., 2015). These relayed cell-to-cell signals between the two gametophytes are perceived by primary cell surface receptors at the micropyle and pollen tube tip including female receptors such as LORELEI, LORELEI-LIKE GPI-anchored protein 1 (LLG1) and FERONIA, and male receptors such as COBRA GPI-anchored, MALE DISCOVERER1 (MDIS1) and MDIS2 from LRR-VI subfamily , MDIS1-INTERACTING RECEPTOR LIKE KINASE 1 (MIK1), MIK2 from LRR-XI and LRR-XII subfamilies respectively), POLLEN RECEPTOR-LIKE KINASE 6 (PRK6) and palmotylated LOSS IN POLLEN TUBE GUIDANCE 1 (LIP1) and LIP2 LURE receptors (Higashiyama and Yang., 2017). Collectively, the dominant feature of pollen tube guidance seems to rely on protein secretion and thus endomembrane and unconventional protein secretion mechanisms likely to play a fundamental role in pollen tube attraction and guidance (Liu et al., 2015; Hafidh et al., 2016b).

In Arabidopsis, almost 90% of the genes are interrupted by introns and it is estimated that >60% of intron containing genes undergo alternative splicing (AS) (Berget et al., 1977; Syed et al., 2012; Marquez et al., 2012; Reddy et al., 2013). Most frequently, 51% of the AS events utilize alternative 5' or 3' splice sites or exon skipping, influencing alternate protein coding sequence or aberrant mRNA production (McGlincy et al., 2008; Kalyna et al., 2012; Syed et al., 2012; Filichkin et al., 2015). Pre-mRNA splicing is mediated by the spliceosome complex which catalyzes two sequential transesterification reactions to excise the interspaced intron (reviewed in Will and Luhrmann, 2011). The spliceosome is composed of multiple subunits, the U2 dependent spliceosome type from U1, U2, U5 and U4/U6 small nuclear RNPs (snRNPs) and numerous non-snRNP proteins. Few proteins remain as core components of the tri-snRNP complex, including Snu114, Brr2 and PRP8. PRP8a interacts with a preformed U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP complex to form a larger catalytically active spliceosomal B complex (Maeder et al. 2009, Bartels et al. 2002). To select an alternative splice site, splicing factors (SF) bind to exonic/intron enhancer or suppressor sequence elements to influence recruitment of the spliceosome complex resulting in alternative splicing (Staiger and Brown., 2013). Genetic screen of SF3a-U2 associated *ATROPOS* (Moll et al., 2008), U4/U6 LACHESIS (Gross-Hardt et al., 2007) and U5 associated CLOTHO/GAMETOPHYTIC FACTOR 1 (Liu et al., 2009), revealed the critical role of SF proteins in the development and competence of the female gametophyte for fertilization, but interestingly no effect has been reported on the male gametophyte (Tzafrir et al., 2004; Coury et al., 2007; Moll et al., 2008; Liu et al., 2009).

Arabidopsis genome encodes two copies of PRP8, PRP8A (AT1G80070) and PRP8B (AT4G38780). An *abnormal suspensor* 2 (*sus2*) mutant defective in PRP8A is embryo-lethal (Schwartz et al., 1994), whereas PRP8B function has not been investigated. A hypomorphic *prp8a*-6 allele was further identified to be involved in splicing of long noncoding antisense transcripts (COOLAIR), that are important for the regulation of FLOWERING LOCUS C activity and could complement *prmt5* mutant of the Nine Teen complex (NTC)/Prp19, a subcomplex of the spliceosome machinery (Marquardt et al. 2014; Deng et al., 2016).

Here, we report for the first time that double mutant *prp8a prp8b* has no striking female or male gametogenesis defects, but *prp8a prp8b* specifically impairs ovule competence for pollen tube attraction and the competence of the pollen tubes to navigate towards ovules. We propose that PRP8A/PRP8B associates with SF3A1 and function in defining the competence of both gametophytes to facilitate female-male communication.

#### **RESULTS**

## **PRP8B is Preferentially Expressed in Pollen**

PRP8 is well conserved in all domains of life including all land plants (Fig. 1A). In plants, the role of pre-mRNA splicing factor PRP8A in male or female gametophytes is unknown. Moreover, the role of PRP8B, a close paralog of PRP8A, has yet to be characterized (Fig. 1A). PRP8A shows 93% identity with PRP8B, however they exhibit polymorphisms at the N-terminal PROCN domain and at the C-terminal JAB domain as predicted by PFAM (http://pfam.xfam.org) and InterProScan (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro) EMBL-EBI databases (Fig. 1B). We initially identified PRP8A as a gene whose transcripts are mostly stored in tobacco pollen tubes as late as 24 h after pollen germination, and thus we hypothesised to have a likely late function during pollen tube growth (Hafidh et al., 2018). Comparison of publicly deposited expression datasets (Zimmerman et al., 2004; Honys D, Twell D., 2004; Winter et al., 2007; Borges et al., 2008; Qin et al., 2009; Loraine et al., 2013, ) revealed over 30-fold elevated expression of PRP8B in pollen and semi *in vivo* pollen tubes over PRP8A (Fig. 1C, Fig. S1A). Whereas PRP8A dominate the expression during embryogenesis and seed development (Fig. S1A) (Belmonte, M. F. et al., 2013). Real time RT-qPCR verified PRP8B dominant expression in pollen and a broader expression of PRP8A (Fig. 1D). In the embryo sac, both genes were classified as expressed in synergid, egg cell and the central cell (Wuest et al., 2010). In agreement with public expression datasets, RT-qPCR verified negligible expression of PRP8B at post globular stage of embryo development relative to PRP8A (Fig. 1D).

To investigate localization of both subunits, we constructed PRP8Apro-PRP8A::GFP and PRP8Bpro-PRP8B::GFP and monitored their localization. Signal of PRP8A-GFP was detected in the nuclei of all female gametic cells and cells of the integuments in mature ovules, whereas PRP8B could only be reliably detected in the egg and the central cell of mature ovule (Fig. 1E). In the male gametophyte, both subunits localized in sperm cells and vegetative cell nuclei of pollen and pollen tubes, however, PRP8B was only reliably detected in hydrated pollen grains and we frequently observed vegetative cytosolic localization of PRP8B (Fig. 1E). Post-fertilization, PRP8A localized in nuclei of developing embryo and suspensor cells and in heterogeneous nuclear speckles in volcano cells of mature seeds, whereas PRP8B was not reliably detectable.

#### **Double mutant** *prp8a prp8b* **is female and male sterile**

To asses PRP8A and PRP8B function, we characterised three T-DNA insertion alleles that disrupt the U6-snRNP interacting domain of PRP8A and PRP8B, designated as *prp8a*-12, *prp8a*-13 and *prp8b*-1 (Fig. 2A). Two other *PRP8B* alleles, *prp8b*-2 and *prp8b*-3, were also screened (Fig. 2A). *prp8a*-12 is embryo lethal as previously reported from other *prp8a* alleles (Schwartz et al., 1994; Tzafrir et al., 2004; Meinke et al., 2008; Marquardt et al., 2014; Deng et al., 2016), and therefore, heterozygous *PRP8Aa*-12 and homozygous *prp8bb*-1 plants were selected for further characterization, and henceforth *prp8a*-12 and *prp8b*-1 mutant alleles will be referred as *prp8a* and *prp8b* respectively. RT-qPCR revealed that both *prp8a* and *prp8b* are knockdown alleles (Fig. 2B). Screen of heterozygous *PRP8Aa* and homozygous *prp8b* mutant plants showed no developmental or gametophyte abnormalities, defects in pollen tube growth, gamete cell fate specification or any reduction in transmission of the mutant alleles ( 2 test = 1.53, 0.53; *p* value > 0.22) (Fig. S1B, C). Segregation of self-pollinated *PRP8Aa* plants confirmed recessive homozygous embryo lethality of *prp8a* allele (Fig. S1D). Our results implied a functional redundancy between PRP8A and PRP8B in both gametophytes. We therefore generated double mutant *prp8a prp8b* and investigated developmental phenotypic defects. No sporophytic defects could be observed throughout plant development (Fig. 2C). Instead, analysis of *PRP8Aa;Bb* and *PRP8Aa;bb* plants revealed that segregation and transmission of *prp8a prp8b* double mutant alleles was severely reduced through both the female and the male gametophytes, TE<sup>f</sup> = 2.02%, TE<sup>m</sup> = 1.04% (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2D, E). Rarely, in <4% of the cases in self-fertilized plants, *prp8a prp8b* alleles could be transmitted by either male or the female gametophyte from the *PRP8Aa;bb* genotype plants (Fig. 2D). These results imply that the *prp8a prp8b* genotype is lethal in the gametophyte. In support, dissection of *PRP8Aa;bb* siliques revealed approximately 46% of unfertilized ovules (n = 422/502) (Fig. 2F). To confirm the origin of this phenotype, we complemented *PRP8Aa;bb* plants with *PRP8A*pro-*PRP8A*-3UTR and *PRP8B*pro-*PRP8B*::*GFP* constructs. Both constructs fully complemented the unfertilized ovule phenotype with the pro*PRP8A*-*PRP8A*-3UTR construct showed better complementation efficiency (Fig. 2F, G). Collectively, our results imply that PRP8A and PRP8B function redundantly in the gametophyte, and that PRP8A/PRP8B dual activities are essential for successful fertilization.

#### **PRP8A and PRP8B Act Redundantly to Control Pollen Tube Attraction**

The full penetrance of the *prp8a prp8b* aborted ovule phenotype hinted on a possibility of defect in pollen tube attraction, reception or fertilization. To investigate, we analysed pollen tube attractiveness, receptivity and fertilization events in *prp8a prp8b* ovules *in vivo* by live cell imaging by pollinating *PRP8Aa;bb* pistils with pollen grains expressing double markers for vegetative and sperm cells (LAT52pro::GFP;HTR10pro-HTR10::RFP) respectively. Approximately 8 hours after pollination (8 HAP), we observed accurate pollen tube attraction to the micropyle, pollen tube discharge and sperm cells release in approximately 81 % (n = 228/282) of ovules when wild type pistisl of *ms1* plants were pollinated with the double marker expressing pollen grains (Fig. 3A). When *PRP8Aa;bb* pistils were pollinated with the same double marker pollen grains, only 49 % (n = 194/400) of the ovules were successfully targeted referring to the *PRP8A;b* heterozygous ovule genotype (Fig 3A). In contrast, 44% (n = 175/400) of the adjacent *prp8a prp8b* double homozygous mutant ovules were defective in pollen tube attraction or no pollen tubes approached the ovules proximity (Fig. 3A). Rarely, we noticed single or multiple pollen tubes (polytubey) attraction by the *prp8a prp8b* ovules at the micropyle, however, without detectable GFP discharge, suggesting a failure in correct pollen tube attraction or reception (Fig. 3A). Polytubey attraction into a single ovule results from pollen tube reception failure (Beale et. al., 2012), thus, supporting our observation on the lack of *prp8a prp8b* successful pollen tube reception. This was independently verified by the lack of female or male transmission of *prp8a prp8b* genotype as well as in segregation distortion in the F2 siblings from *PRP8Aa;bb* selfing plants (Fig. 2E).

To independently test the pollen tube attraction defect *of prp8a prp8b* ovules, we stained callose of *in vivo* pollen tubes with aniline blue in self-pollinated *PRP8Aa;bb* plants or in an outcross of *PRP8Aa;bb* female pollinated with wild type Col-0 pollen grains 18HAP to further emphasise the ovule phenotype of *prp8a prp8b* double mutant (Fig. 3B). Almost 50% (n = 276/541) of the ovules in *PRP8Aa;bb* pistil either failed in pollen tube attraction to the micropyle entry or no pollen tubes appeared near the mutant ovules micropyle entry in self-pollinated or in an outcross experiment (Fig. 3B). We classified the ovule phenotypes into four sub-types; Type I, wild type-like ovule (*PRP8A;b*) with normal PT reception, Type II, double mutant *prp8a;b* ovule with normal PT reception, Type III, ovule with no PT nearby and Type IV, ovules with PT nearby but failed to attract PT into the micropyle (= attraction defect) (Fig. 3B). In the progeny of *PRP8Aa;bb* self-pollinated plants, 39% were identified as type I, 8% - as type II, 27% were type III ovules and 26% were type IV (Fig. 3B, Movie 1 and 2). In the progeny cross of *PRP8Aa;bb* x WT, 45% were type I, 6% were type II, 31% were type III ovules and 18% were type IV (Fig. 3B, Movie 1 and Movie 2).

The *prp8a*-12 allele is a blue-SAIL T-DNA insertion containing LAT52pro-GUS transgene that co-segregate with the T-DNA insertion of the mutant allele. We therefore took advantage and performed blue dot histochemical assay (Palanivelu et al., 2006), by pollinating wild type *ms*1 pistils and *PRP8Aa;bb* pistils with wild type pollen grains or with pollen grains expressing LAT52pro-GUS or with (Fig. S2). Since *Lat52* promoter activity should be restricted to pollen only (Twell et a., 1990; Twell et al., 1991), we observed ectopic GUS staining in *prp8a prp8b* embryo sac prefertilization that we did not observed in control plants (Fig. S2). However, this ectopic staining was distinguishable from GUS staining of ovules targeted with LAT52pro-GUS expressing pollen tubes (Fig. S2). Therefore we proceeded with quantification by distinguishing the two populations in *PRP8Aa;bb* pistils. Quantification of targeted ovules supported our previous results that *prp8a prp8b* ovules fail in pollen tube attraction (Fig. S2). Collectively, our results suggest that simultaneous loss of *PRP8A*  and *PRP8B* severely impairs pollen tube attraction, long-distance pollen tube guidance and fertilization in Arabidopsis.

# **PRP8A/PRP8B Function Is Essential for Pollen Tube Response to Female Attraction Signals**

The near-complete block of *prp8a prp8b* transmission through the male also hinted a role of PRP8A/PRP8B in pollen tubes. Viability test and nuclear staining revealed that *prp8a prp8b* pollen grains are viable and morphologically normal. We then exploited the LAT52pro-GUS tagged T-DNA insertion of the *prp8a-*12 SAIL allele (Fig. 2A) to specifically follow *prp8a prp8b* mutant pollen tubes growth *in vitro* as well as within pistil by GUS staining. GUS staining revealed that *in vitro prp8a prp8b* pollen tube growth is indistinguishable from wild type pollen tubes (Fig. 4A). *In vivo*, double mutant *prp8a prp8b* pollen tube growth also appeared normal in the transmitting tract and the double mutant pollen tubes were able to reach the very bottom of the pistil, suggesting no growth or navigation defects in the transmitting tract (Fig. 4B). However, when compared to control LAT52pro-GUS pollen tubes, the *prp8a prp8b* pollen tubes failed to turn towards the funiculus or target any ovules in wild type pistils (0% targeted, n = 0/391 ovules) (Fig. 4B). These results are in agreement with the blocked transmission of *prp8a prp8b* alleles through the male gametophyte. Together, our data suggest that PRP8A/PRP8B co-ordinately regulate pollen tube competence to respond to funiculus/micropylar attraction signals secreted by the ovules for pollen tube guidance.

## *prp8a prp8b* **Double Mutant Ovules Might Exhibit abnormal Embryo Sac**

To better understand the defects of *prp8a prp8b* ovules, we emasculated flowers from *PRP8Aa;bb* plants at stage 13-14 with fully developed embryo sac (Christensten et al., 2002), and left them to mature for 48 hours. DIC imaging revealed that, mature *prp8a prp8b* double mutant ovules showed a likely malformed embryo sac morphology in ~35% of the ovules (Fig. 5A-F). Morphologically, the characteristic conical projection associated with wild type embryo sac micropyle pole was not observed in *prp8a prp8b*  ovules (Fig. 5B). Instead, *prp8a prp8b* embryo sac appeared protruding to the very close proximity of the micropyle entry site or pressed against the inner integuments of the ovule maternal tissues (Fig. 5C-D). In other ovules, *prp8a prp8b* embryo sac appeared either enlarged or collapsed (Fig. 5E, F). When compared to wild type dissected ovules at same stage, only 3% of the ovules showed unusual embryo sac morphology (Fig. 5G). Despite the abnormal embryo sac morphology of *prp8a prp8b*  ovules, nuclear staining revealed no obvious defects in nuclear proliferation, nuclear fusion or cellularisation (Fig. 5H, I).

To further assess the impact of *prp8a prp8b* on the female gametophyte, we monitored the expression of FGR7.0, an embryo sac triple cell marker for synergid cell specific (LURE1.2pro-NLS:x3 GFP, AT5G43510), egg cell (EC1pro-NLS"x3 mCherry) and the central cell specific expression (CRP defensin-like LCR80 DD22pro-NLS:YFP, AT5G38330) (Völz et al., 2013). Live cell imaging in wild type embryo sac revealed 88.5% ±9 (n = 357/403) of the ovules showed correct expression of the FGR7.0 in all three cell types with strong expression in the synergid and the egg cell and a weak expression in the central cell (Fig. 5J, K). Contrarily, only 50.4% ±3 (n = 646/1288) of the ovules from *PRP8Aa;bb* dissected pistils correctly expressed the FGR7.0 marker (Fig. 5J, K). Instead, ∽44.8% ±4 (n = 578/1288) of the embryo sacs from *PRP8Aa;bb*

pistils showed no or reduced expression in synergid and the egg cell (Fig. 5J, K). Remarkably, of the *prp8a prp8b* ovules with reduced FGR7.0 expression, ∽4.9% ±5 (n = 64/1288) showed higher FGR7.0 expression in the central cell than in synergid cells, and the egg cell appeared to adopt the expression either of the synergid or the central cell marker and lost the EC1-mCherry expression (Fig. 5K). These results imply that PRP8A and PRP8B could redundantly function in regulating gene expression and defining correct cell fate specification during ovule maturation.

# **RNA-seq Revealed Splicing Defects and Deregulated Gene Expression In** *prp8a prp8b* **Ovules**

The fact that *prp8a prp8b* double mutant did not show pleiotropic effect suggests that PRP8A and PRP8B are not strictly constitutive regulators of the splicing machinery. To unveil the extent of affected genes and define the molecular phenotype following *prp8a prp8b* knockdown, we isolated RNA from dissected ovules of *PRP8Aa;bb* pistils and conducted 150bp paired-end RNA-seq transcripts profiling. We analysed differential gene expression (DGE), intron retention (IR) and differential exon-usage (DEU) relative to WT ovules. Pearson pairwise correlation clustering confirmed reproducibility of the replicates for individual RNA-seq libraries (Fig. 6A). Using DESeq2 R studio with a false-discovery rate (FDR) of <1%, RNA-seq data revealed that 764 genes (2.9%) were classified as down-regulated and 779 genes (3%) were up-regulated in *prp8a prp8b* ovules (Fig. 6B). At >2 Log2 fold-change and *p* < 0.05, 321 genes were considered down-regulated and 163 genes were classified as upregulated in *prp8a prp8b* ovules (Fig. 6B, Supplemental Dataset 1A, 1B).

Analysis of the RNA-seq data with IRFinder platform identified 629 genes (2.9%) that showed >2 fold significant intron retention (IR) in *prp8a prp8b* ovules compared to wild type ovules (Fig. 6C). Of these, 55% showed reduced IR, whereas 45% showed increased IR events in *prp8a prp8b* ovules (Fig. 6B, Supplemental Dataset 2A, 2B). Analysis for DEU using DEXSeq R script (Anders et al., 2012) revealed 220 genes that showed significant DEU in *prp8a prp8b* ovules, indicating pre-mRNA mis-splicing in *prp8a prp8b* ovules (Fig. 6D, 6E, Supplemental Dataset 3A, 3B, 3C). Collectively, the RNA-seq data analyses suggest that PRP8A/PRP8B participates in the pre-mRNA splicing of only subset of genes that are expressed in ovules.

# **PRP8A and PRP8B Co-Regulate Expression of MYB98 and Cysteine-Rich Polypeptide (CRP) Genes**

To investigate at the molecular level the likely cause of *prp8a prp8b* defect in pollen tube attraction, we analysed the RNA-seq data for the expression of genes encoding Cysteine-rich proteins (CRPs), CRP subfamily Defensin-like peptides (DEFLs), Rapid alkalinization factors (RALFs), MLO proteins, and MYB98 transcription factor, family of genes that have been implicated to mediate pollen tube attraction and reception (Dresselhaus et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2017; Higashiyama and Yang., 2017; Leydon et al., 2017). In *prp8a prp8b* ovules, 55 genes belonging to the CRP family were misexpressed representing 11% of the total mis-expressed genes in *prp8a prp8b* ovules (Fig. 7A). 34 of these genes encode proteins belonging to the low-molecular weight CRPs subfamily and 21 encode DEFL protein subfamily (Fig. 7A). Of the 34 misexpressed low-molecular weight CRPs, 8 genes (24%) were up-regulated by up to 5 fold and the remaining 26 genes (76%) were down-regulated by up to 7-fold (Fig. 7A). Of the 21 *DEFL* subfamily genes, all were down-regulated by up to 6-fold in *prp8a prp8b* ovules (p < 0.05, Fig. 7A). Of notice, all of the *LURE* genes, *AtLURE1.1-1.5*, were down-regulated in *prp8a prp8b* ovules (Fig. 7A). Similar analysis using edgeR (Robinson et al., 2010) consistently revealed down-regulation of *LURE1.1* by 1.3 Log2 fold and *LURE1.2*-to-1.5 by > 2 Log2 fold (p < 0.05) in *prp8a prp8b* ovules.

*LURE*s gene expression is controlled by synergid cell-specific MYB98 transcription factor, which itself is under the regulation by central cell-specific *CENTRAL CELL GUIDANCE* (CCG; Chen et al., 2007) and CCG BINDING PROTEIN1 (CBP1; Li et al., 2015). Analysis of our RNA-seq data reliably detected down-regulation of MYB98 and up-regulation of CBP1 in *PRP8Aa;bb* derived RNA-seq expression in all three replicates (Fig. 7C). To support this observation, we monitored the expression of MYB98pro-NLS:GFP and MYB98pro-PM:GFP decorating synergid cell nuclear and the plasma membrane respectively in *prp8a prp8b* ovules. Compared to 90.2% ±5 (n = 121/134) of positively expressing ovules in wild type control, live cell imaging revealed complete down-regulation of MYB98pro-NLS:;GFP expression in the synergid cell nuclei of ∽46.9% ±4 (n = 444/946) ovules from *PRP8Aa;bb* dissected pistils, representing the *prp8a prp8b* double mutant population (Fig. 7D). We obtained similar results using proMYB98pro-PM:;GFP. Mutants *myb98*, *ccg1* and *cbp1*, all result in down-regulation of CRPs including LURE gene expression (Okuda et al., 2009; Li et al., 2015). Furthermore, all members of the Egg Cell 1 family were also down-regulated in *prp8a prp8b* ovules (Fig. 7C). These results suggest that the downregulation of MYB98 is likely the cause of LURE and other CRPs downregulation impacting pollen tube attraction further circumvent the *prp8a prp8b* ovules from any successful gamete fusion and fertilization.

RALFs family of genes were recently shown to regulate pollen tube guidance and homeostasis (Mecchia et al., 2017; Ge et al., 2017). In *prp8a prp8b*, *RALF20* was more than 5-fold up-regulated, whereas *RALF21*, *RALF28* and *RALF29* were up to 5 fold down-regulated (Fig. 7A). Two *MLO* genes were also identified, *MLO6* was approximately 4-fold up-regulated and *MLO12* was 2-fold down-regulated (Fig. 7A). However, the promoter activities of *MLO6* and *MLO12* were shown not to be active in ovules and were only detected in stigma and style respectively (Davis et al., 2017).

We then tested whether *AtLURE1.1-1.6*, *FERONIA* and *LORELEI* genes could be misspliced in *prp8a prp8b* ovules. Intron profiling by semiRT-PCR and a test for the frequency of Exon-usage (DEU) and IR events revealed no significant alterations in pre-mRNA processing of *LURE*s, *FER* or *LRE* in *prp8a prp8b* ovules (Fig. 7B, Fig. S3).

It is also evident that protein secretion and the endomembrane system plays a pivotal role in pollen tube guidance (von Besser et al., 2006, Li et al., 2011, Hafidh et al., 2016b). We therefore extended our RNA-seq analysis to include genes of the secretory pathways. Analysis of IR frequency revealed a plethora of secretory pathway genes to be aberrantly spliced in *prp8a prp8b* mutant ovules. Among them include genes of ER-golgi transport (*GRIP*), Golgi-vacuole transport for sorting (*DRP2A* and *VSR-1*) and Golgi-plasma membrane as well as exocytosis (*SFH3/SEC14* and *EXO70*) (Fig. S4). Additional secretory genes were identified to show statistically significant DEU compared to wild type controls. They include *SFH3*, *Got1/SFT2,* DUF ER vesicle transporter (AT1G36050) and *AtRABC2b* (Fig. S4). In summary, our RNAseq data revealed down-regulation of upstream regulators of *LURE* genes expression and a plethora of other CRPs as well as aberrant splicing of key secretory pathway genes in *prp8a prp8b* ovules which might impair secretion of CRPs and therefore exacerbate pollen tube attraction defects of *prp8a prp8b* ovules.

#### **PRP8A/PRP8B Splicing Activity Targets More Central Cell Expressed Genes**

To investigate if there is a cell-biased effect within the embryo sac following *prp8a prp8b* loss-of-function, we mapped all of the affected genes from IR, DGE and DEU to the female gametophyte (FG) RNA-seq transcriptome from Wuest et al., 2010. Of the 629 genes showing IR in *prp8a prp8b* ovules, 567 (90%) could be mapped to the FG transcriptome, of which 214 IR genes (34%) were classified as reliably expressed in all three replicates in at least one FG cell types, synergid cells (SY), central cell (CC) or in egg cell (EC) (Fig. 8A, Supplemental Dataset 2B). We observed that 79 genes (36.9%) with significant IR were CC-specific, 38 genes (17.8%) were EC-specific and no genes with significant IR were identified to be SY-specific (Fig. 8A, Supplemental Dataset 2B). This distribution implies that *prp8a prp8b* splicing defects impact central cell expressed genes twice as much compared to egg cell encoded genes. In comparison, of the 484 genes that showed significant DGE, 280 genes (58%) could be mapped to the FG transcriptome (Fig. 8A). Of these, 43 genes (8.9%) were reliably expressed in all three replicates in at least one FG cell type (Fig. 8A). Unlike IR events, DGE showed less biased on cell type specific deregulated expression (Fig. 8A). Similar results were also obtained with genes showing DEU, in which of the 220 genes that exhibited significant DEU, 53 genes (24.1%) were reliably expressed in the female gametophyte but showed no significant biased on cell type specific effect (Fig. 8A). Keyword enrichment analysis (*p* <0.01) of central cell genes with significant IR highlighted genes involved in alternative splicing, transit peptide and chloroplast/plastid differentiation as among the predominantly affected genes (Fig. 8B). Of the genes showing DEU, the gene encoding VHS/GAT-domain protein was identified as central cell-specific, and genes encoding U-box domain protein kinase and 3-keto-acyl-coA thiolase were identified as egg cell-specific affected genes (Fig. 8B). These results imply that *prp8a prp8b* loss-of-function affects predominantly central cell expressed genes through IR as the most common molecular phenotype within the embryo sac.

# **Mutant** *prp8a prp8b* **Ovules Display Differential Exon Usage (DEU) of Female Gametophyte Expressed Genes and Embryo Sac Developmental Genes**

To assess the possible molecular phenotype resulting in the *prp8a prp8b* embryo sac morphological defects, we searched the RNA-seq data with emphasis on mRNAs known to regulate embryo sac development (Pagnussat et al., 2005). Using DEXSeq analysis, genes of diverse subcellular roles were identified to show differential exon usage compared to their expression in wild type ovules. They include genes encoding plasma membrane signalling proteins (GPI- anchored protein), transcription factors and chromatin remodelling factors (DEIH-box helicase and COL13), metabolism (ATPHS2), splicing factors (PWI-domain splicing factor) and signalling kinases (CPK29) (Fig. S5A and Fig. S6). More specific, we identified 12 genes with a direct role in female gametophyte and embryo sac development to be differentially spliced showing DEU, IR or changes in gene expression (Fig. S6). DEU informed RNA-seq highlighted *EDA9*, *EDA30* and *MAP3K*1 to show statistically significant differential exon-usage in *prp8a prp8b* ovules (Fig. S5). EMBRYO SAC ARREST 9 (*EDA9*, AT4G34200) is a 3-phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase that is essential for embryo and pollen development and *eda9* mutant ovules are arrested at two nuclear stage of the embryo sac development (Pagnussat et al., 2005). EMBRYO SAC ARREST 30 (*EDA30*, AT3G03810) is a O-fucosyltrensferase family protein and is essential for polar nuclei fusion (Pagnussat et al., 2005). Whereas, MAP3K EPSILON PROTEIN KINASE 1 (*MAP31*) is expressed in developing ovules and a double mutant *map3k1 map3k2* displays plasma membrane irregularities in pollen grains which result in pollen lethality (Chaiwongser et al., 2012; Mithoe et al., 2016). Post-fertilization, MAP3K1 is a downstream regulator of YODA MAP kinase signaling pathway and is essential for the specification of suspensor cell fate at 1-2 cells stage of zygote development (Bayer et al., 2009). IR and DGE analyses identified additional embryo sac developmental genes that showed either significant intron retention (+/-IR ≥2fold, *p* < 0.05) or down-regulation of their transcription in *prp8a prp8b* mutant ovules (≥ 2 fold change, p < 0.05) (Fig. S6).

# **PRP8A and PRP8B Exhibit Autonomous Regulation of Their pre-mRNA Splicing and Splicing of other Spliceosome Factors**

Since PRP8A and PRP8B are subunits of the spliceosome machinery, we analysed the RNA-seq data to investigate whether the spliceosome complex is autonomously regulated by comparing the splicing patterns of *PRP8A* and *PRP8B* in *prp8a prp8b* ovules. DEU analysis revealed that exons 1-3 and exon 18-20 of *AtPRP8A* were differentially expressed in *prp8a prp8b* mutant ovules, suggesting defective splicing of PRP8A pre-mRNA (Fig. S5B), whereas *AtPRP8B* showed significant differential expression of exon 19 (Fig. S5B). DEXSeq also revealed differential expression of exon 10 of a PIWI domain containing splicing factor (AT2G29210) (Fig. S5A). IRFinder further revealed mis-splicing of two genes encoding SER/ARG-rich protein splicing factor 34 (SRP34) and SRP34A in *prp8a prp8b* ovules among others spliceosome subunits encoding genes (Fig. S5C). SRP34 regulates alternative splicing through binding in exonic enhancer and suppressor elements. Our results identified an autoregulation of core subunits of the spliceosome machinery that likely culminate the molecular phenotype of *prp8a prp8b* knockdown.

## **PRP8A Coimmunoprecipitate With U2-associated SF3A1 splicing enhancer**

In human and yeast, the N-terminus of PRP8 interacts with the core of the NineTeen Complex (Prp19/NTC) for the activation of the spliceosome machinery, whereas the two domains at the C-terminal tail, the JAB1 (MPN) domain and the U6-RNaseH domain, interact with BRR2 U5-snRNP, U2-snRNPs, such as human homolog of SF3A1 (Tresini et al., 2015, Zhang et al., 2017), and human SLU7 step II factor for the selection of 3'-splice site junction (Zang et al., 2017). Since the T-DNA insertions in both *prp8a*-12 and *prp8b*-1 interrupt the U6-RNaseH domain resulting in female and male sterility, we tested if the relationship of PRP8 with U2-snRNPs identified in human is also conserved in plants to get a hint on PRP8A/PRP8B mechanisms of action. We co-expressed pro*PRP8A-PRP8A*::GFP with *AtSF3A*1-mCherry (AT1G14650) in Arabidopsis roots. PRP8A-GFP colocalized with AtSF3A1-mCherry in a gradient dependent manner within the nuclei of all cells at the root tip (Fig. 9A). A close observation of nuclear speckles confirmed that PRP8A and AtSF3A1 colocalized in some subdomains of the nuclei but they also formed distinct subdomains within the nucleoplasm (Fig. 9A). Using these stable lines, we then performed *in vivo*

co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiment using nuclear enriched proteome subfraction and a total cytosolic proteome from 7 days old seedlings expressing proPRP8A-PRP8A::GFP as a bait. Western dot blot analysis of PRP8A immunoprecipitated proteins revealed that PRP8A-GFP complex strongly coimmunoprecipitated with AtSF3A1 in the nuclear enriched and the total fractions, suggesting that PRP8A associates with SF3A1 in Arabidopsis (Fig. 9B). We further observed similar affinity Co-IP of PRP8A with histone H3 variant (Fig. 9B). In human, histone modifications, in particular H3K36me3 and H3K4me3, are strongly linked with alternative splicing through interaction of Histone adaptor (MRG15) and PTBdependent exon inclusion (Luco et al., 2010; Luco et al 2011), and recent reports demonstrated association of several histone variants with splicing factors (Xu et al., 2018). A nuclear localized Translationally Controlled Tumor protein 1 (TCTP1), a nonspliceosome subunit from Arabidopsis, was used as a negative control to check Co-IP specificity (Fig. 9B). Our results revealed that PRP8A associates with U2-snRNP SF3A1 and histone H3 in Arabidopsis. We next investigated if PRP8A associates with SF3A1 via direct interaction. We performed ratiometric Biomolecular Fluorescence Complementation (rBiFC) (Grefen and Blatt., 2012) with PRP8A-nYFP and SF3A1 cYFP in *N. benthamiana* leaves. As a positive control, we included TCTP1-nYFP and CPN60-cYFP interaction. We observed no reconstitution of YFP fluorophore when PRP8A-nYFP and SF3A1-cYFP were co-expressed (Fig. 9C). The lack of interaction indicates that in Arabidopsis PRP8A associates with SF3A1 likely indirectly.

#### **DISCUSSION**

The spliceosome complex required for pre-mRNA splicing functions uniquely in a tissue and developmental specific manner and some of its subunits have been genetically characterised to be essential regulators of gamete development (Gross-Hardt et al., 2007; Moll et al., 2008; Staiger D and Brown J., 2013). In mice, alternative splicing of acrosomal matrix protein, preacrosin-binding protein (ACRBP), plays essential role in the formation of secretory functional acrosome and competent sperm cells capable of fertilization (Kanemori et al., 2016). In Drosophila, a mutation in the NTC/Prp19 pre-spliceosome complex specifically impairs pre-mRNA splicing of early zygotic but not maternally encoded transcripts, suggesting a spatio-temporal regulation of the spliceosome subcomplexes (Guilger et al., 2014). In Arabidopsis, *lachesis* mutants (*lis*) with depleted U4/U6 splicing factor PRP4 are defective in lateral inhibition of accessory cell development resulting in accessory cells with gametic cell fate identity (Gross-Hardt et al., 2007), whereas CLOTHO/GAMETOPHYTIC FACTOR 1 (CLO/GFA1), a U5 subunit of the spliceosome machinery and interactor of PRP8A (Liu et al., 2009), is specifically required for egg and central cell fate specification and tissue-specific expression of LIS (Moll et al., 2008). Similarly, *ATROPOS* (*ATO*, *SF3A60*) encodes a human homolog of SF3a60, a subunit of the SF3a-U2 complex and a member of which SF3a1 coimmunoprecipitates with PRP8A (Fig. 9). *ato* (*sf3a60*) mutant is female gametophytic lethal and also shows severe reduction in male transmission with a maximum of 17.44% transmission efficiency (Moll et al., 2008). At ovule maturity, 20% of *ato* (*sf3a60)* embryo sac contained less than seven nuclei and the two female gametic-cell fate markers were deregulated (Moll et al., 2008). In this study, *prp8a prp8b* double mutant embryo sac might have lost synergid and egg cell fates by not expressing MYB98, LURE1.2, and EC1 markers (Fig. 5 and Fig. 7). Unlike, *lis/gfa1* or *ato*, *prp8a prp8b* double mutant does not show early gametophytic developmental defects. Even though GFA1 interacts with PRP8A (Liu et al., 2008) and both are components of the U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP (Bartels et al., 2002;Steiger and Brown., 2013), the lack of direct phenocopy between *prp8a prp8b*  double mutant and the *gfa1*, *lis* or *ato* (*sf3a60*) mutants, exemplify the complex regulation of the spliceosome individual subunits and a non-universal role of the spliceosome machinery. The closest resemblance of the *prp8a prp8b* double mutant phenotype is with that of *ato* (*sf3a60*) mutant which might explain the close association of PRP8A with SF3a1 (Fig. 9).

Moreover, PRP8A and PRP8B are fully redundant in their function in the gametophyte as shown by the normal transmission of *prp8a* and *prp8b* single mutations and that proPRP8A-PRP8A::3UTR and proPRP8B-PRP8B::GFP could fully complement *prp8a prp8b* fertilization defects (Fig. 2). However, this redundancy is restricted to the gametophyte as *prp8a* alone is embryo lethal (Shwartz et al., 1998; Meinke et al., 2006). Our results hint that PRP8A/PRP8B spliceosome subunits are selectively involved in splicing of subsets of pre-mRNAs with cell and developmental specific roles, and unlike other U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP splicing subunits, the PRP8A/PRP8B function might not be essential for early gametophyte development.

#### **Intron Mis-splicing Predominate Central Cell-expressed Genes in** *prp8a prp8b*

The post-transcriptional effect in *prp8a prp8b* ovules revealed a combined effect on intron splicing and differential exon-usage of approximately 4.18% (849 genes) compared to 1.8% (484 genes) on differential gene expression at ≥2 fold (Fig. 8). This observation suggest that PRP8A/PRP8B directly participate in intron splicing of ovule expressed genes and likely *prp8a prp8b* knockdown has indirect effect on altered gene expression. In support, we have shown that PRP8A co-immunoprecipitates with a human homolog of SF3A1 as well as with histone H3 variant (Fig. 9). How histones influence splicing was not known until recently. In humans, histone modifications such as H3K4me3, H3K9me3, H3K36me3, and H3acetyl all positively influence recruitment of histone adaptor proteins, MRG15, GCN5, CHD1 and HP1 that facilitate recruitment of splicing factors Polyuridine (polypyrimidine tract binding protein) binding proteins (PTB), U2 snRNP and hnRNPs to influence exon inclusion and alternative splicing (Luco et al., 2011). This was exemplified through splicing of two PTBdependent mutually exclusive exons of the human fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2) where two histone modifications, H3K36me3 and H3K4me1 act antagonistically to influence exon inclusion or exclusion respectively. Moreover, crosslinking and immunoprecipitation experiments demonstrated direct interaction of histone variants H1, H2A, H2B, H3 and H4 with splicing factors Rbfox2, Rbfox3 and proline and glutamine rich protein (SFPQ) (Kim et al., 2018). In plants, the direct link between alternative splicing and either chromatin structure or RNA Pol II elongation rates has yet to be demonstrated, however recent studies in Arabidopsis have shown a positive correlation (Cui et al., 2017).

The observed biased effect on intron splicing over gene expression in *prp8a prp8b*  embryo sac extended to a cell-type specific origin in the female gametophyte. Assessment of the IR events of only genes expressed in all three replicates of the female gametophyte (214 genes of the 397 IR genes, Fig. 8), revealed that genes confined in the central cell are twice as much affected on their intron splicing (79/214 genes, 36.9%) compared to those encoded by the egg cell (17.8%) (Fig. 8, Dataset 2B). No affected genes were specific to the synergid cell, however, several genes expressed in the synergid cells were also mis-spliced (Fig. 8, Dataset 2B). Central cell has a direct role in pollen tube attraction. Mutations in central cell specific genes, *ccg* (Chen et al., 2007, *cbp1* (Li et al., 2015)*, maa3* (Shimizu et al., 2008) or *gex3* (Alandete-Saez et al, 2008), pollen tube attraction is completely abolished. CENTRAL CELL GUIDANCE (CCG; Chen et al., 2007) and CCG BINDING PROTEIN1 (CBP1; Li et al., 2015) are thought to mediate pollen tube attraction by regulating transcription of synergid cell-specific MYB98 transcription factor, an upstream regulator of *LURE1* transcription (Okuda et al., 2009; Li et al., 2015). We propose that aberrant splicing of central cell encoded genes together with those affected in the synergid cells might kick-start the molecular cascade leading to loss of *prp8a prp8b* ovule competence in pollen tube attraction. Although mis-splicing is the major event observed in *prp8a prp8b* particularly in central cell, it is difficult to draw conclusion whether this is the major factor (over changes in gene expression) leading to pollen tube attraction defects and lack in fertilization of *prp8a prp8b* ovules.

# **Downregulation of MYB98 and CRPs Could be the Hallmark Cause of Pollen Tube Attraction Defect in** *prp8a prp8b*

Although there was a prevalent effect on pre-mRNA splicing in *prp8a prp8b* double mutant ovules, transcripts encoding CRPs stood out the most representing 20% (55/284 genes) of the total downregulated genes by ≥2 fold (Fig. 7). Upstream of CRPs, our RNA-seq data supported by the MYB98pro-NLS:GFP reporter constructs, revealed downregulation of MYB98 and upregulation of CBP1, both of which are essential transcriptional regulators of LURE CRPs and pollen tube attraction (Fig. 7) (Kasahara et al., 2005; Okuda et al., 2009; Li et al., 2015). CRPs are the major family of proteins that have been implicated to function directly in pollen tube attraction (Higashiyama and Yang, 2017). Among the downregulated CRPs, they included all six members of the LURE subfamily expressed in the synergid cell (Fig. 7). Also, of significant note, three out of four of the central cell-specific CRPs that are also downregulated in *ccg* and *cbp1* pollen tube attraction mutants, DOWN-REGULATED IN *dif*22 (*DD22, LCR80*, At5g38330), *DD36* (At3g24510) and AT3G04540, were also downregulated by >2.5 fold in *prp8a prp8b* ovules (Fig. 7). In line with the misregulation of CRPs in *pr8a prp8b* ovules, we have detected ectopic promoter activities of a pollen-specific CRP Ole e 1 subfamily gene LAT52, LAT52pro-GUS, initially in *prp8a prp8b* ovules and later confirmed in *prp8a* single mutant ovules (Fig. S2). LAT52 is secreted by pollen tubes and function as a ligand perceived by pollen specific receptor-like kinases (RLK's), PRK2 and PRK4, to regulate pollen tube growth by endocrine signalling (Tang et al., 2002; Muscheitti et al.,1994, Hafidh et al., 2016). The -572 bp LAT52 promoter fragment confers transcriptional regulatory elements that provide pollen (vegetative cell) specificity and enhanced transcriptional activity specifically in pollen (Twell et al., 1990; Twell et al., 1991). The ectopic activation of LAT52 expression in ovules culminate on the effect of *prp8a prp8b* molecular phenotype on CRPs miss-regulation. It will be informative to further investigate which other male specific expressed genes are ectopically activated in *prp8a prp8b* ovules. This will be key in defining the sexual identity and cell-fate specification of *prp8a prp8b* ovules. We propose that the downregulation of MYB98 and many CRPs is most likely the hallmark cause of pollen tube attraction defect observed in *prp8a prp8b* ovules.

#### **CONCLUSION**

This work has uncovered a molecular signature through which PRP8A/PRP8B subunits act redundantly to define male-female signalling competence for successful pollen tube attraction in Arabidopsis. PRP8A/PRP8B facilitate pollen tube attraction by controlling transcription of LURE pollen tube attractants and several other CRP genes via MYB98 transcriptional regulation as well as through splicing predominantly of central cell expressed genes. These defects in *prp8a prp8b* double mutants might be coupled with loss in female gametic cell fate specification at ovule maturity, and thus could contribute to overall ovule incompetence for pollen tube attraction. It will be important to uncouple these two events and also establish how PRP8A/PRP8B control pollen tube pathfinding competence to achieve successful fertilization. Collectively, PRP8A/PRP8B module hint on a spliceosome autoregulation mechanisms and provide a potential pathway to isolate upstream regulators for pollen tube attraction.

#### **MATERIALS AND METHODS**

**Plant material and growth conditions.** Arabidopsis thaliana plants were grown at 22°C and 60% humidity in Conviron PGC Flex growth chambers under 16h light/8h dark conditions. Seeds of Col-0 wild type (WT), PRP8A (At1g80070) and PRP8B (At4g38780) plants were obtained from The European Arabidopsis Stock Centre. T-DNA insertion lines SAIL\_274\_D02 T-DNA (*prp8a*-12), SALK\_085295C and SALK\_085295 (*prp8b*-3), SALK\_044221C and SALK\_044221 (*prp8b*-1) and SAIL\_100\_G10 (*prp8b*-2) were used in this study. T-DNA insertion lines were genotyped using SALK LBb1.3 and SAIL LB2 oligonucleotides in combination with gene specific oligonucleotides (Supplemental Table S1). Exact position of the T-DNA insertions were established by sequencing and are marked in Fig. 2A.

**RNA-seq library construction and sequencing.** Ovules were isolated by dissecting flowers from stage 13-14 (FG7) containing fully developed embryo sac (Christensten et al., 2002) from *PRP8Aa;bb* double mutant plants and wild type control plants. RNA extracted from three biological replicates of each was used for Next generation (NGS) RNA Sequencing. Total RNA was isolated from dissected ovules using Qiagen Plant RNA extraction kit (Qiagen). Total RNA was DNase treated (Ambion) and RNA integrity (RIN) was analysed with Bioanalyser. 800ng of RNA with RIN >7 was processed using TrueSeq stranded total RNA library with RiboZero plant prep kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) as per the manufacturer protocol. Samples were run on HiSeq4000 2x100bp paired-end run Illumina sequencing machine (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) by Macrogen (Geumcheon-gu, Seoul, S.Korea).

**mRNA-Seq Data Analysis.** The raw sequenced data were subjected to quality control and trimming using FastQC and Timmomatic (Bolger et al, 2014). Resulting reads were mapped to the TAIR 10 genome with STAR aligner, version 2.6 (Dobin et al, 2013) using default parameters. Feature Count program from the subread package, version 1.6.2 (Liao et al., 2014) was used to generate the count matrix by following steps described in the documentation.

R programs DESeq2, version 1.22.1 (Love et al, 2014) was utilized to determine differential gene expression (DGE) while DEXSeq, version 1.28.0 (Anders et al, 2012) to determine differential exon usage (DEU) between wild type and *prp8a prp8b*  mutant. The count matrix was used as inputs in DESeq2. False discovery rates (FDR) were calculated for the gene-specific p-values < 0.05 using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). Gene expression levels were then quantified using rlog normalized counts.

Counts, as input for DEXSeq, were prepared with included Python scripts. Generalized linear model (GLM) was used to detect DEU with FDR adjusted to the overall gene expression level with p-values < 0.1. IRFinder (Middleton et al, 2017) with default parameters was applied to determine differentially retained introns between the wild-type and *prp8a prp8b* double mutant. Introns were considered differentially retained if the Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value was < 0.05 while IR ratio was ≥ 2. All biostatistics analyses were implemented and performed using in-house scripts written in Unix shell and R. For graphical display, all genes identified to show statistically significant exon usage in *prp8a prp8b* ovules are presented in HTML interactive format (Supplementary Dataset 3A). The data discussed in this article are in a process to be submitted to NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus and are accessible through GEO #

**In vivo Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) with nuclei fraction.** The full length coding sequences (CDSs) of PRP8A together with its native promoter (997 bp) were amplified and cloned into pB7FWG,0 to create proPRP8A-PRP8A::eGFP constructs. A 35S-AtSF3A1::mCherry construct was a donation from Andreas Nebenfuhr lab. For detail cloning information, see below section. Stable Arabidopsis transformants were generated expressing both constructs. Total proteins from approximately 2g of 4 days old seedlings expressing proPRP8A-PRP8A::eGFP and 35S-AtSF3A1::mCherry constructs were isolated according to (Deng et al., 2016) using Honda lysis buffer (25 mM Tris·HCl, pH 7.4, 2.5% Ficoll 400, 5% dextran T40, 0.4 M sucrose, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM DTT, and 1× complete protease inhibitor cocktail). The homogenate was filtered through 50 μm nylon mesh to clear larger fragments and Triton X-100 was added to a final concentration of 0.5%. For nuclei enrichment, the flow through was centrifuged at 1,500 xg for 5 min at 4 <sup>0</sup>C. . The pellet was gently resuspended twice with nuclei resuspension buffer (10 mM Tris·HCl, pH 8.0, 0.25 M sucrose, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1 mM PMSF, protease inhibitors) and centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 5 min 4 <sup>0</sup>C. Nuclei were then resuspended in ice-cold nuclei lysis buffer [200 mM (NH4)2SO4, 20% glycogen, 25 mM Hepes, pH 7.9, 5 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, protease inhibitors] and gently mixed for 2 h at 4 °C to release the nuclear proteins. The nuclear fraction was centrifuged at 17,950 × g for 10 min to remove remaining debris. Total and nuclear enriched proteomes were used immediately for Co-IP. GFP-Trap-A agarose (Chromotek) were used in the subsequent Co- IP assays. The immunoprecipitates were detected by anti-PRP8A (Abcam 79237, 1:1000), anti-SF3A1 (Abcam 128898, 1:1000), anti-TCTP (Abcam 37506, 1:500), anti-H3 (Abcam 1791, 1:1000) and anti-UGPase (Agrisera AS05086, 1:1500) antibodies, respectively. To monitor primary antibodies specificity, strips with omitted primary antibodies were used as a negative control. Appropriate Alkaline phosphatase conjugated anti-Rabbit and anti-mouse secondary antibodies were used for detection using the nitro-blue tetrazolium chloride (NBT) and 5-bromo-4-chloro-3' indolyphosphate p-toluidine salt (BCIP) chemiluminescent detection.

**Bimolecular fluorescence complementation analysis.** To create ratiometric PRP8Agenomic-nYFP and AtSF3A1-cYFP constructs, genomic fragment of PRP8A coding sequence and CDSs of SF3A1 from cDNA of rosette leaves were cloned into the 35S:pBiFCt-2in1-CC vector to generate PRP8Agenomic-nYFP SF3A1cds-cYFP co-expression construct (Grefen et al., 2012). Similarly, CDSs of PRP8A was amplified from leaves cDNA and together with SF3A1 were cloned into 35S:pBiFCt-2in1-CC vector to generate PRP8Acds-nYFP SF3A1cds-cYFP co-expression construct. All cloning were performed using multisite gateway technology (Invitrogen). The specific oligonucleotides used are described in Supplemental Table S1. These constructs were subsequently introduced into *Agrobacterium* strain GV3101 and used for infilitration into leaves of *N. benthamiana*. After transformation, plants were grown in light condition for 48 h before observation. The YFP fluorescence was observed using a Zeiss LSM880 microscope.

**Constructs and plant transformation.** All PCRs to amplify cloned fragments were performed using high-fidelity Phusion polymerase (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Oligonucleotides sequences are listed in Table S2. For the full-length PRP8A-GFP under native promoter, a fragment of 14,523 bp including 997bp of promoter and 5'UTR was amplified with/without stop codon and cloned into the pENTR/D-TOPO vector and recombined into the destination vector pK7FWG,0 (Karimi et al. 2002) bearing the eGFP marker to generate proPRP8A-PRP8A::eGFP construct. To generate proPRP8A-PRP8A-3'UTR, an extended 3'-end oligonucleotides were used to incorporate the 498 bp 3'UTR and the entry clone was used in LR reaction with pH7FWG,0 backbone to generate proPRP8A-PRP8A-3'UTR expression construct. Full length proPRP8B-PRP8B::GFP was amplified and cloned by a similar approach. All constructs were transformed into Arabidopsis via floral dipping (Clough and Bent, 1998).

**Transmission analysis.** *PRP8Aa* heterozygous plants were back-crossed into Col-0 wild type background for determining female transmission and crossed into male sterile *ms1*-/- (Yang et al. 2007) to study male transmission. To generate the *PRP8Aa ;bb* double mutant plants, flowers of *PRP8Aa* were emasculated and pollinated with homozygous *prp8bb* pollen grains and inversely *prp8bb* flowers were emasculated and pollinated with *PRP8Aa* pollen grains. For transmission analysis of the double mutant, *PRP8Aa;bb* flowers were emasculated and pollinated with Col-0 wild type pollen grains and used to calculate female transmission efficiency or pollen grains from the *PRP8Aa;bb* double mutant plants were used to pollinate male sterile *ms1*-/ pistils and resulting progeny were used to calculate male transmission efficiency. Progeny of these crosses were genotyped by PCR for the presence of T-DNA insertion and transmission efficiency was calculated.

**Imaging of embryo sacs.** Stage 12a (FG1) and stage 13–14 (FG7) flowers (Christensten et al., 2002) were dissected on double- sided tape. The placentas bearing the developing ovule primordia were excised and placed immediately in chloral hydrate clearing solution. After two days the fixative was removed and the placentas were mounted onto microscope slides in a 30% glycerol solution. During mounting the two placentas strands were separated and excess tissue was removed. Ovule primordia were observed under Zeiss AxioImager Apotome 2. Images were collected with a 40x/1.2 W C-Apochromat objective.

**Live cell imaging of** *prp8ab* **ovules.** Pistils from *PRP8Aa;bb* were emasculated and left to mature for two days. Emasculated pistils as well as control *ms1* pistils were pollinated with pollen expressing the LAT52:GFP;HTR10:RFP marker line. After 24h, pollinated pistils were placed on a double sided tape and the placentas bearing the developing ovule primordia were excised with a 25G needle and placed immediately on microscope slide on a drop of 30% glycerol. Fertilization events were captured using Zeiss LSM 880 confocal laser scanning microscope with C-Apochromat 40x/1.2 WI, excitation 514 nm, emission recorded 566-719 nm for the red channel and excitation 490 nm and emission recorded 509-520 nm for the green channel. Representative single slice confocal sections were used for presentation. Image processing were done using Fiji application.

**Analysis of ovule attractivity by aniline blue staining.** Pistils were harvested 18HAP and subjected to aniline blue staining as described below. Samples were analyzed by Zeiss Axioimager Apotome2 and images captured with ZEN software.

**Confocal and DIC Microscopy.** For mutant embryo phenotypic screening, seeds were cleared in a solution of chloral hydrate. Overnight clearing was sufficient for all developmental stages. Samples were observed under the DIC optics on a Nikon TE-2000 microscope. Images were processed in the NIS Elements software (LIM). Confocal images were taken on a Nikon Eclipse Ti confocal microscope equipped with CSU-X1 spinning disk module and Andor iXon3 EMCCD camera, as well as with Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope and captured with ZEN 2.3v software. Images were analyzed and assembled with ImageJ/Fiji (http://imagej.net/http://fiji.sc/Fiji), Adobe

Photoshop CS6 [\(www.adobe.com\)](http://www.adobe.com/), Ink-scape (www.inkscape.org) and NIS Elements (LIM) software.

**Pollen viability stain and Aniline blue staining.** For aniline blue staining, pistils of self-pollinated WT and *PRP8Aa;bb* plants or cross-pollinated *ms1* plants with WT or *PRP8Aa;bb* pollen were stained for callose according to the protocol by Mori et al., 2006.

**Blue dot assay.** Young buds of WT and *PRP8Aa;bb* plants before anthers dehiscence were emasculated and let to mature for 48h until the stigma papillae fully developed. Pistils were then pollinated with pollen expressing LAT52pro-GUS construct, pollen grains derived from *PRP8Aa;bb* plants or control wild type pollen grains, and pistils were collected 18-20 HAP. Fertilized pistils were dissected under the binocular dissection microscope (Leica) and strings of ovules attached to septum were transferred to GUS staining solution (0.2 M Na2HPO4, 0.2 M NaH2PO4, 10 mM EDTA, 0,1% Triton X-100, 1mM K3Fe(CN)6, 2 mM X-Gluc). Samples were stained between 2-48 hrs. Stained tissues were cleared from chlorophyll by bleaching in ethanol series of 90%, 70%, 50% and 0% (v/v) prior to imaging. Stained pistils were observed under the Nikon TE-2000 microscope equipped with DIC optics for the presence of a blue dot or with Zeiss binocular containing CCD camera for overall pollen tube growth through the pistil.

**Pollen tube cultivation.** *In vitro* pollen tube growth was performed according to the protocol by Boavida and McCormick (2007). Pollen tube length was measured after 8 hrs of *in vitro* growth using the NIS Elements software. For semi-*in vivo* pollen tube growth assay, sterile *ms1* pistils were pollinated with WT or *PRP8Aa;bb* pollen and pistils were collected 1-2 hrs HAP. Pistils were excised at the stigma shoulder with a needle and transferred to the growth medium (Palanivelu et al., 2006) on a small Petri dish. Pistils were tilted to enable the pollen tubes to emerge onto the solid medium surface and cultivated in a humidified growth chamber at 22°C for 24 hrs. Pollen tube length was measured using the NIS Elements software from the point where they emerged from the cut pistil.

**Statistics.** All statistical analysis was performed using Welch's-analysis of unequal variances, Chi square test and student t-test where appropriate.

**RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis.** Tissue samples were collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen. RNA was extracted using RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) and treated with RQ1 RNAse-free DNase I (Promega). First strand cDNA synthesis was conducted using recombinant M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega). qRT-PCR measurements were obtained using LightCycler 480 Instrument (Roche). Relative expression quantification was obtained using the ∆∆CT method following manufacture instructions. Oligonucleotides were designed to distinguish between Arabidopsis *PRP8A* and *PRP8B* genes. Specificity of the oligonucleotides was verified by sequencing of purified PCR products. All measurements were conducted in three biological and two technical replicates.

**Sequences, alignment and phylogenetic analysis.** Protein sequences of AtPRP8A and its homologs were downloaded from Phytozome v12.1 [\(www.phytozome.jgi.doe.gov\)](http://www.phytozome.jgi.doe.gov/) and NCBI [\(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) databases. Multiple sequence alignment was done using T-Coffee algorithm (Notredame et al., 2000) or Clustal Omega and processed in Jalview alignment editor (Waterhouse et al., 2009). The phylogenetic tree was constructed using PhyML software (Guindon et al., 2010). Numbers at nodes of the phylogenetic tree correspond to approximate likelihood ratio test with SH-like (Shimodaira-Hagesawa-like) support from maximum likelihood method. Scale bar indicating the rates of substitutions/site is shown in corresponding tree. Phylogenetic tree was visualized and processed in Mega 6 software (Tamura et al., 2013). If multiple copies of PRP8 were present in one organism, they were labeled as "a" or "b" variants. Variants "a" were sequentially more similar to AtPRP8A. This labeling does not reflect phylogenetic relationship between homologs from different organisms.
