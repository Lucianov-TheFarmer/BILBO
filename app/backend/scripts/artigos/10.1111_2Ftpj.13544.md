Running title: N<sub>2</sub>O biosynthesis by C. reinhardtii

**Key words:** Climate change; Greenhouse gases; Microalgae; Nitrous oxide; Nitric Oxide; Nitrite response

#### **SUMMARY**

Over the last decades, several studies have reported emissions of nitrous oxide (N<sub>2</sub>O) from microalgae cultures and aquatic ecosystems characterized by a high level of algal activity (e.g. eutrophic lakes). As N<sub>2</sub>O is a potent greenhouse gas (GHG) and an ozone depleting pollutant, these findings suggest that large scale microalgae cultivation (and possibly, natural eutrophic ecosystems) could have significant environmental impacts. Using the model unicellular microalga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, this study was conducted to investigate the molecular basis of microalgal N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis. We thus report that C. reinhardtii supplied with nitrite (NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup>) under aerobic conditions can reduce NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> into nitric oxide (NO) using either a mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase (COX) or a dual enzymatic system of nitrate reductase (NR) and amidoxime-reducing component, and that NO is subsequently reduced into N<sub>2</sub>O by the enzyme NO reductase (NOR). Based on experimental evidence and published literature, we hypothesize that when nitrate  $(NO_3)$  is the main N source and the intracellular concentration of NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> is low (i.e. under physiological conditions), microalgal N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis involves NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction into NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> by NR followed by NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction into NO by the dual system involving NR. This microalgal N<sub>2</sub>O pathway has broad implications in environmental science and algal biology because the pathway of NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> assimilation is conserved among microalgae, and because its regulation may involve NO.

# INTRODUCTION

Nitrous oxide (N<sub>2</sub>O) is a potent greenhouse gas and a major ozone depleting pollutant (Ravishankara et al., 2009) that can be emitted from algal cultures (Weathers, 1984; Weathers and Niedzielski, 1986), including verified axenic cultures (Guieysse et al., 2013), and eutrophic ecosystems characterized by a high level of primary activity (Mengis et al., 1997; Wang et al., 2006). Although little is known about N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis in microalgae, nitrite (NO<sub>2</sub>) has long been suspected as substrate (Weathers, 1984; Weathers and Niedzielski, 1986). Guieysse et al. (2013) later proposed that Chlorella vulgaris synthesizes N<sub>2</sub>O via the reduction of NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> into nitric oxide (NO) or nitroxyl (HNO) by nitrate reductase (NR), followed by the reduction of NO into N<sub>2</sub>O by a NO reductase (NOR) or the spontaneous dimerization of HNO into N2O. However, if NO can indeed be reduced into N2O under oxia, several alternative NO-generation scenarios could also lead to N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis. Firstly, a dual system of NR and the amidoxime-reducing component NOFNiR (NO-forming nitrite reductase) was recently shown to mediate NO production (both in vitro and in vivo) in Chlamydomonas sp. when the intracellular concentration of NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> increases in the presence of NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> (Chamizo-Ampudia et al., 2016). Secondly, NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction into NO could be catalyzed in vitro and under anoxia by molybdoenzymes similar to the xanthine oxidase/dehydrogenase found in animals (Maia and Moura, 2014) and vascular plants (Gupta et al., 2011). Thirdly, NO2<sup>-</sup> can be reduced into NO by mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase (COX) and alternative oxidase (AOX), as previously proposed in plants (Planchet et al., 2005; Gupta et al., 2016) and for the microalga Chlorella sorokiniana (Tischner et al., 2004). Fourthly, L-arginine can be oxidized to NO by a nitric oxide synthase (NOS) activity described in plants, although the protein involved has not yet been identified (an animal-type NOS exist in several algae; Gupta *et al.*, 2011; Jeandroz *et al.*, 2016). Finally,  $NO_2^-$  can be reduced to NO by hemoglobin under hypoxic/anoxic conditions in vascular plants and many cyanobacteria (Sturms *et al.*, 2011; Tiso *et al.*, 2012; Ciaccio *et al.*, 2015).

This study was conducted to determine the pathway of N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis in microalgae with the view that this knowledge is critical to the understanding of the nitrogen cycle in aquatic ecosystems (Weathers, 1984; Hayatsu et al., 2010; Kamp et al., 2013). This information will also help us better assess the environmental impact of algal biotechnologies and anthropogenic eutrophication (Guieysse et al., 2013). The model unicellular green microalga *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* is especially suitable for investigating N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis because i) nitrogen assimilation and regulation have been extensively studied in this species (Navarro et al., 2000; Fernández and Galván, 2008; Schmollinger et al., 2014; Park et al., 2015; Sanz-Luque et al., 2015a); ii) the cellular reduction of NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> to NO has already been shown (Sakihama et al., 2002; Wei et al., 2014; Chamizo-Ampudia et al., 2016); and iii) biological and genomic resources are available for this organism (Navarro et al., 2000; Harris, 2001; Gonzalez-Ballester et al., 2005; Pröschold et al., 2005; Merchant et al., 2007; Chlamydomonas centre: http://www.chlamycollection.org/). As this alga was not known to synthesize N<sub>2</sub>O prior to the current study, this ability was first demonstrated in axenic cultures. The pathway(s) involved in N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis was then determined using mutants and/or specific inhibitors and these findings were elaborated using RNA sequencing analysis.

## RESULTS

## Nitrous oxide emissions from axenic C. reinhardtii cultures

While significant N<sub>2</sub>O emissions (average  $1770 \pm 500 \text{ nmole} \cdot \text{g-DW}^{-1}$  after 24 h, n = 41) were recorded from *C. reinhardtii* 6145c cultures supplied NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> in the dark (Figure 1), N<sub>2</sub>O production was negligible in autoclaved cultures or sterile medium (Table S1). The N<sub>2</sub>O production recorded from *C. reinhardtii* cultures was unlikely to be a result from bacterial or

archaeal contamination because sensitive PCR based detection assays provided no evidence for the presence of these microorganisms (Appendix S1). These results conclusively demonstrate that *C. reinhardtii* can synthesize N<sub>2</sub>O under oxia, which was so far unknown. The production of N<sub>2</sub>O was confirmed for all *C. reinhardtii* strains tested, although the rates of emissions were strain-dependent (Table S2). N<sub>2</sub>O production was linearly correlated with microalgae biomass concentration (Figure S1), providing further evidence that biological processes in the algae were the source of N<sub>2</sub>O. In darkness, the kinetics of N<sub>2</sub>O biosynthesis was characterized by an immediate and short period of N<sub>2</sub>O production lasting 1 - 4 h, followed by phase of slow production lasting until approximately 20 h of incubation, and a final phase of vigorous production over the remaining duration of the experiment (Figure S2a). The initial short phase of N<sub>2</sub>O production was not observed in culture incubated under illumination, where N<sub>2</sub>O biosynthesis started approximately 20 h after NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> addition (Figure S2b). The implications of the kinetics, and the influence of light on N<sub>2</sub>O production, are discussed below in the context of the potential pathways involved.

#### NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> acts as substrate during microalgal N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis

Supplying  $NO_3^-$  or  $NH_4^+$  to the axenic *C. reinhardtii* 6145c cultures did not trigger significant  $N_2^-$ O synthesis (Table S1), suggesting that this process was depending on the availability of  $NO_2^-$  as a substrate. Supporting this idea,  $N_2O$  production in darkness was found to be linearly correlated to extracellular  $NO_2^-$  concentration (Figure 2). This strong association between  $NO_2^-$  reduction and  $N_2O$  synthesis is in agreement with previous studies (Weathers, 1984; Weathers and Niedzielski, 1986; Guieysse *et al.*, 2013; Kamp *et al.*, 2013; Alcántara *et al.*, 2015).  $NO_2^-$ -independent  $N_2O$  synthesis via L-arg conversion by NOS was unlikely because  $N_2O$  emissions were not observed in  $NO_2^-$  free cultures supplied with L-arg and because  $N_2O$  production in  $NO_2^-$ -laden cultures increased slightly in the presence of the NOS

inhibitor L-NNA (Table S1). These results are consistent with previous findings achieved over shorter durations (4 h) using *C. reinhardtii* (Sakihama *et al.*, 2002) and *C. vulgaris* (Guieysse *et al.*, 2013), and the fact that NOS appears to be absent in *C. reinhardtii* (Jeandroz *et al.*, 2016).

#### NO is a key intermediate during N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis

Guieysse et al. (2013) suggested NO was an intermediate during N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis in C. vulgaris, and this compound is indeed known to be synthesized by C. reinhardtii and other microalgae (Mallick et al., 1999; Mallick et al., 2000; Sakihama et al., 2002; Tischner et al., 2004; Estevez and Puntarulo, 2005; Kim et al., 2008; Chang et al., 2013; Wei et al., 2014; Sanz-Luque et al., 2015b; Chamizo-Ampudia et al., 2016). In C. reinhardtii, NO synthesis has been associated with nitrogen assimilation (Sanz-Luque et al., 2013, 2015a) and both the hypoxic and nitrogen stress response (Zhang and Mehta, 2008; Hemschemeier et al., 2013; Wei et al., 2014). In our study, the generation of green fluorescence in C. reinhardtii 6145c cells pre-incubated with DAF-FM diacetate (inside cells, this permeable and non-fluorescent chemical is converted into the non-permeable DAF-FM and the latter reacts with NO to form a fluorescent derivative, see Appendix S2 for details) and subsequently exposed to  $NO_2^{-1}$ suggests NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction caused NO synthesis, in agreement with the results of Chamizo-Ampudia et al, (2016). This fluorescence was not observed when the NO scavenger bovine hemoglobin was added to the microalgal suspension before incubation with DAF-FM diacetate and  $NO_2^-$  supply (see the full set of results from positive and negative controls in Appendix S2). Taken together these results demonstrate that the conditions triggering  $N_2O$ synthesis (Table 1) also trigger NO production in C. reinhardtii.

As HNO has also been proposed as possible intermediate in algal N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis via NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction (Guieysse *et al.*, 2013), an additional control was performed where *C. reinhardtii* 6145c cells pre-incubated with DAF-FM diacetate were supplied with Angeli's salt as HNO donor (Appendix S2). The fluorophore DAF-FM is known to react with the NO oxidation products N<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> and NO<sup>+</sup>, but the aqueous degradation of Angeli's salt releases NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> and HNO under physiological condition, (Dutton *et al.*, 2004; Miranda *et al.*, 2005), but can also generate NO at low pH (Miranda *et al.*, 2005). The weak fluorescence reported in these experiments (Appendix S2) may have therefore been caused by 'indirect' NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> supply causing NO synthesis, or direct NO synthesis in low-pH cell compartments (Kurkdjian and Guern, 1989). While our results strongly suggest that NO was generated during N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis, further work is needed to fully verify this hypothesis given that the knowledge on the biochemistry of HNO production is still very limited (Fukuto *et al.*, 2005).

## Immediate N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis involves NR but late synthesis involves other enzymes.

The catalysis of  $NO_2^-$  reduction to NO by NR has long been suspected in plants and *C. reinhardtii* (Yamasaki, 2000; Rockel *et al.*, 2002; Sakihama *et al.*, 2002; Meyer *et al.*, 2005). More recently, this enzyme was shown to provide electrons to a NO forming nitrite reductase, thereby enabling  $NO_2^-$  reduction into NO by a dual NR-NOFNiR system of two molybdoenzymes (Chamizo-Ampudia *et al.*, 2016). In *C. vulgaris*, the involvement of NR during N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis was evidenced by the repression of N<sub>2</sub>O production in cells precultivated with tungstate, an inhibitor of molybdoenzymes such as NR (Deng *et al.*, 1989). The hypothesis of NR-mediated N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis was therefore tested in *C. reinhardtii*.

Tungstate is known to partially inhibit NR activity in *C. renhardtii* (Llamas *et al.*, 2000) and pre-cultivation of 6145c strain with this compound indeed caused partial repression of the immediate  $N_2O$  production (Table S3). Interestingly, tungstate inhibits the terminal activity

of NR without affecting the diaphorase-associated activity of this enzyme (Barea *et al.*, 1976). Tungstate therefore likely inhibited immediate N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis by inhibiting the NOFNiR activity of the dual molybdoenzymes system catalyzing NO synthesis (Chamizo-Ampudia *et al.*, 2011; Chamizo-Ampudia *et al.*, 2016). To confirm this, N<sub>2</sub>O emissions were recorded during the cultivation of NR-deficient *C. reinhardtii* 2929 (Table 1). Immediate N<sub>2</sub>O production was not recorded in NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup>-replete cultures of the NR-deficient strain, although similar 0-24 h N<sub>2</sub>O emissions were recorded in the 2929 mutant (2100  $\pm$  700 nmole·g-DW<sup>-1</sup>) and the 6145c control strain (1770  $\pm$  500 nmole·g-DW<sup>-1</sup>) after 24 h of incubation. Furthermore, fluorescence was not observed in NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup>-replete NR mutant 2929 cultures pre-incubated with DAF-FM diacetate (this protocol could only be used following short-term exposure to NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> due to the reactivity of DAF-FM, Appendix S2). These results indicate that N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis involved NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction into NO by the dual system NR-NOFNiR immediately following NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> addition and is consistent with a different enzyme system being involved in the late N<sub>2</sub>O response.

## NR and NiR activities impacts $N_2O$ synthesis under $NO_3^-$ supply.

 $NO_3^-$  is the main nitrogen source available to microalgae in many 'natural' environments (Raven and Giordano, 2013) and it is commonly used during commercial microalgae cultivation (Borowitzka, 2005). NR and NiR sequentially catalyze the assimilatory reduction of  $NO_3^-$  into  $NO_2^-$  and  $NH_4^+$ , thereby contributing to regulate the intracellular concentration of  $NO_2^-$ . As  $NO_2^-$  'fuels'  $N_2O$  synthesis via NO, the activities of NR and NiR likely impacts  $N_2O$  emissions under physiological conditions (when  $NO_3^-$  is the main N source and the intracellular concentration of  $NO_2^-$  is relatively low): this was demonstrated when the NiR-deficient mutants supplied with  $NO_3^-$  produced large amounts of  $N_2O$  under illumination (Table 1). These mutants could carry out the stoichiometric reduction of  $NO_3^-$  into  $NO_2^-$ ,

because they possess an NR activated by light and NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> (Navarro *et al.*, 2000; Kaiser and Huber, 2001), but could not reduce NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> into NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>. Nitrite therefore likely over-accumulated in illuminated NiR-deficient cells (Navarro *et al.*, 2000), which resulted in increased NO turnover boosting N<sub>2</sub>O emissions. Unlike the case of *C. reinhardtii* 6145c NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup>-replete cultures, where N<sub>2</sub>O production was repressed under darkness (Table S1) as cells could not sustain NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction into NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup>, NiR-deficient mutants supplied with NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> could release N<sub>2</sub>O in light and dark conditions (Table 1), further highlighting the role of NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> as substrate for N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis. In agreement with past studies (Weathers, 1984; Weathers and Niedzielski, 1986; Guieysse *et al.*, 2013; Kamp *et al.*, 2013), illumination repressed immediate N<sub>2</sub>O production by *C. reinhardtii* supplied with NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> (Table 1) probably due to NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction by NiR during photosynthesis (Guieysse *et al.*, 2013).

#### Late N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis involves NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction to NO by mitochondrial COX

Nitrite reduction via the mitochondrial electron transport chain was first demonstrated in the green microalgae *Chlorella sorokiniana* (Tischner *et al.*, 2004) and mitochondria are a known source of NO in higher plants (Planchet *et al.*, 2005; Igamberdiev *et al.*, 2014; Gupta *et al.*, 2016). Mitochondrial COX or AOX can reduce  $NO_2^-$  into NO under hypoxic and anoxic conditions (Gupta and Igamberdiev, 2011; Tischner *et al.*, 2004; Planchet *et al.*, 2005) and, at a low rate, under normoxia (Tischner *et al.*, 2004; Planchet *et al.*, 2005). As experimental evidence linking these mitochondrial enzymes to N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis was previously lacking, the wildtype *C. reinhardtii* 6145c and the NR-deficient mutant *C. reinhardtii* 2929 strains were supplied with  $NO_2^-$  in the presence of CN<sup>-</sup>, an inhibitor of heme-containing enzymes (Sakihama *et al.*, 2002; Tischner *et al.*, 2004) known to affect mitochondrial COX (Way, 1984) at concentration lower than 5 mM (Gans and Wollman, 1995). This treatment with 2 mM CN<sup>-</sup> resulted in immediate inhibition of both N<sub>2</sub>O (Table 1) and NO (Appendix S2),

which could be explained by the fact that  $CN^{-}$  can also inhibit NR (Tischner *et al.*, 2004; Planchet *et al.*, 2005). However, and in contrast to NR chemical inhibition or repression, N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis was inhibited by 90% and 96% in the wildtype and NR mutant cultures, respectively, after 24 h of exposure to  $CN^{-}$  and  $NO_{2}^{-}$  (Table 1). Together, these results suggest that mitochondrial COX catalyzes  $NO_{2}^{-}$  reduction into NO during the late period of N<sub>2</sub>O production.

Given that N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis by *C. reinhardtii* 6145c was not completely eliminated following CN<sup>-</sup> addition, AOX may also be partially responsible for NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction into NO. Tischner *et al.* (2004) indeed demonstrated that *C. sorokiniana* NiR mutant continuously generated high levels of NO under anoxia and normoxia, and that this production could be catalyzed by both COX and AOX. AOX-mediated NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction to NO has also been demonstrated in the model plant *Arabidopsis thaliana* (Gupta *et al.*, 2016). The potential involvement of AOX could not be experimentally dismissed because we found that the chemical reaction of the known AOX inhibitor salicylhydroxamic acid (SHAM) generated NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> (44 ± 8 nmole N<sub>2</sub>O from abiotic tests incubated for 3 h). It should also be noted that NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> is a known antidote to cyanide, and that NO may also impact the inhibitory effect of CN<sup>-</sup> (Leavesley *et al.*, 2008).

## N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis by C. reinhardtii involves NO reduction into N<sub>2</sub>O by NOR

Although concurrent NO and N<sub>2</sub>O syntheses in response to NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> supply was repeatedly observed in *C. reinhardtii* cultures, NO is typically oxidized into nitrogen dioxide (NO<sub>2</sub>) and other products (e.g. ONOO<sup>-</sup>) under oxia (Murphy *et al.*, 1998; Lamattina *et al.*, 2003). *C. reinhardtii* however harbors the gene CYP55, which is similar to a fungal gene encoding a NOR (Chang *et al.*, 2011; Guieysse *et al.*, 2013; and further evidence below) capable of

reducing NO into N<sub>2</sub>O under hypoxia (Morozkina and Kurakov, 2007; Shoun *et al.*, 2012). When supplied with NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup>, a *C. reinhardtii* NOR-knock down mutant (amiCYP55 strain with gene CYP55 silenced using artificial micro iRNA, Appendix S3) mutant synthesized 70-90% less N<sub>2</sub>O than its respective parent (Table 1), suggesting wildtype *C. reinhardtii* synthesizes N<sub>2</sub>O via NOR-mediated NO reduction under oxia. Interestingly, N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis increased when nitrite-laden *C. reinhardtii* 6145c was incubated under anoxic conditions (Table S1). These results may be explained by the absence of competition between NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> and O<sub>2</sub> for the COX reaction sites (Gupta *et al.*, 2005), as well as reduced rates of NO oxidation (Gupta *et al.*, 2016) favoring NO reduction into N<sub>2</sub>O by NOR. N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis was not fully inhibited likely due to the fact NOR expression was not fully repressed in the amiCYP55 strain (e.g. 54% of the wildtype strain 704).

To study the changes in expression of key enzymes potentially involved in N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis in *C. reinhardtii* (e.g. NR, NOR, COXs), RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on cultures exposed to NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup>- in darkness (treatment) and compared to NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup>-free cultures grown under identical conditions (control). RNA-seq was carried out on triplicate samples collected at 0.25, 3, and 24 hours after NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> addition, based on the three phases of N<sub>2</sub>O kinetic observed in darkness (Figure S2a). As shown in Table 2, genes encoding NR, NiR, and NOR were not significantly differentially expressed (adjusted *P* value threshold of 0.05) between the treatments and the controls at any of the time points tested. Overall, the transcriptomics data was consistent with the biochemical evidence that NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction into NO leading to 'late' N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis is not NR-mediated. NR would only be needed immediately following NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> supply. Nitrite response via transcriptional regulation of NOR may not have been

triggered/required under the experimental conditions tested, but the data clearly provides evidence for the presence of abundant NOR transcripts (the average normalized read counts across all samples for NOR was 3156, n=18).

In contrast to the genes discussed above, THB1 and THB2 genes were significantly differentially expressed after 24 h of NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> exposure (Table 2). Plant hemoglobins are known to reduce NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> to NO (Hoy and Hargrove, 2008; Gupta et al., 2011) and a microalgal hemoglobin could catalyze this reaction under hypoxic/anoxic conditions (Sturms et al., 2011), a mechanism thought to be related to either NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> detoxification (Sturms et al., 2011) or survival under anoxia (Hemschemeier et al., 2013). C. reinhardtii possesses 12 truncated hemoglobins (THB 1-12) and some of these enzymes can indeed reduce NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> (Ciaccio et al., 2015; Huwald et al., 2015). C. reinhardtii THB1, which was significantly differentially expressed in the treatment cells after 3 and 24 h of NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> exposure (log2FC of 0.7 and 0.5), is also involved in NO signaling, NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> assimilation, NR activity regulation and, of particular relevance, NO scavenging under normoxia via its dioxygenase activity (Sanz-Luque et al., 2015b). Consequently, during  $NO_3^-$  assimilation, THB1 may help to regulate NO intracellular accumulation by promoting the transformation of NO into NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> in parallel to NO reduction to N<sub>2</sub>O. The change in transcript abundances for THB1 (positive log2FC) and THB2 (negative log2FC) agrees with the transcriptional NO response described by Sanz-Luque et al. (2015b), and therefore provide additional evidence of both the involvement of NO as N2O intermediate, and the involvement of THBs in the response to NO synthesis.

Transcriptomic data also showed that while numerous transcripts for several COX gene could be identified, only one COX gene was significantly differentially expressed (log2FC of -0.5) after 24 h of  $NO_2^-$  exposure (Table 2). been required under the experimental conditions tested. In contrast, AOX1 and AOX2 were significantly differentially expressed (log2FC of 0.5 and 1.4 respectively) after 24 h of NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> exposure. AOXs are regulatory enzymes balancing electrons transport in mitochondria (Vanlerberghe and McIntosh, 1997). Considering that NO has been shown to induce AOX in Arabidospis (Huang et al., 2002), AOX synthesis could have been activated in response to NO generation in C. reinhardtii. Further analysis is still needed to evaluate the full metabolic effect of high NO2<sup>-</sup> in C. reinhardtii as 180, 740 and 3914 genes were significantly differentially expressed between control and treatment groups after 0.25, 3, and 24 h of nitrite exposure, respectively. The transcriptomic data also showed significantly lower transcript abundances of several eukaryotic initiation factors in NO<sub>2</sub>-replete cultures (Table S4), indicative of cells that have activated stress response pathways (Langland et al., 1996; Hinnebusch, 1997; Roy et al., 2010; Pakos-zebrucka et al., 2016). Activation of these pathways possibly explains the large amount of differential expression we observed in our transcriptomic comparisons and this complicates the identification of other genes potentially involved in this process. A detailed analysis of the RNA-seq study is currently being carried out to fully characterize the molecular responses to NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> loading.

#### **Biological implications**

This study showed that axenic C. reinhardtii supplied NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> can synthesize N<sub>2</sub>O under oxia via NO2<sup>-</sup> reduction into NO followed by NO reduction into N2O. NO2<sup>-</sup> reduction into NO was initially catalyzed by the dual NR:NOFNiR enzyme system, but this activity rapidly ceased and was later substituted by NO2<sup>-</sup> reduction into NO by COX. Under physiological conditions,  $NO_3^-$  is the main N source and the intracellular concentration of  $NO_2^-$  is low. The reduction of NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> into NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> by NR therefore provides both the nitrogen source for growth and, under specific circumstances, the substrate for NO biosynthesis by the dual NR:NOFNiR

system (Chamizo-Ampudia et al., 2016). Moderate NO production would be expected to take place at moderate cytosolic NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> concentrations and, under such condition, the dioxygenase activity of THB1 could efficiently modulate the intracellular concentrations of NO, NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> and NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> (Sanz-Luque *et al.*, 2015b). In contrast, significant exposure to intracellular high NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> concentrations (as done in this work) appears to cause COX to reduce significant amounts of NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> into NO as indicated in the model proposed in Figure 3. Both NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> and NO are known to generate harmful products (Faure et al., 1991; Beckman and Koppenol, 1996) and cells have developed mechanisms to prevent the intracellular accumulation of these molecules, such as NO oxidation into NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> by THBs (Sanz-Luque et al., 2015b) and NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> excretion (Faure et al., 1991; Navarro et al., 2000). NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> reduction into N<sub>2</sub>O via NO could therefore provide algae cells periodically experiencing exposure to anoxia or hypoxia (e.g. wastewater stabilization ponds) with a mean to detoxify NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> and NO. This activity maybe the fortuitous consequence of COX activity under oxia, or it may provide additional detoxification capacity in microalgae (i.e. NOR would be acting as a security valve eliminating NO). Interestingly, the yield of algal N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis was lower under oxia (0.001 - 0.03 % g-N-N<sub>2</sub>O synthesized g N-input<sup>-1</sup> after 24 h of incubation in darkness for strain 6145c) than under anoxia (0.8 % g- $N-N_2O$  synthesized - g N-input<sup>-1</sup> after 24 h of incubation in darkness for strain 6145c), suggesting the existence of a competitive NO metabolism under oxia (e.g. NO<sub>x</sub> generation, Lamattina et al., 2003). The low yield of N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis under oxia also likely explains why NO reduction to N<sub>2</sub>O has seldom been reported in past studies focusing on N metabolism in algae. Clearly, our understanding of the function and regulation of the enzymes and substrates involved in N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis in microalgae remains limited. Nevertheless, the evidence herein presented (such as the ability of C. reinhardtii to reduce NO into N<sub>2</sub>O under oxia) provides new advances in algal biology in light of the importance of nitrate assimilation and NO metabolism in these organisms.

#### **Environmental implications**

As discussed above, N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis under physiological conditions is linked to  $NO_3^{-1}$ assimilation in C. reinhardtii in a mechanism similar to the fungal N<sub>2</sub>O denitrification pathway (Shoun et al., 2012), with the significant difference that the microalgae carry out  $N_2O$  synthesis under oxia. This finding has broad implications because the pathway of  $NO_3^{-1}$ assimilation is conserved among microalgae (Sanz-Luque et al., 2015a) and its regulation involves NO (de Montaigu et al., 2010; Sanz-Luque et al., 2013; Sanz-Luque et al., 2015a). Moreover, several microalgae species have the ability to synthesize NO (Mallick *et al.*, 1999; Tischner et al., 2004; Kim et al., 2008; Kumar et al., 2015) and/or N<sub>2</sub>O (Weathers, 1984; Weathers and Niedzielski, 1986; Guieysse et al., 2013; Kamp et al., 2013; Alcántara et al., 2015), and Chlorella variabilis harbors a close homolog of the Chlamydomonas NOR gene (Guieysse et al., 2013). These observations suggest numerous microalgae species could be capable of N<sub>2</sub>O synthesis, which in turn could explain why correlations between primary productivity and N<sub>2</sub>O emission rate have been reported for decades (Pierotti and Rasmussen, 1980; Outdot et al., 1990; Mengis et al., 1997; Wang et al., 2006), even under very low exogenous  $NO_2^-$  concentration (probably due to intracellular  $NO_2^-$  generation during  $NO_3^$ assimilation; Pierotti and Rasmussen, 1980). Because microalgae are ubiquitous in the environment and often associated with anthropogenic pollution (e.g. algal blooms), the potential global significance of microalgal N<sub>2</sub>O biosynthesis needs to be assessed. Fortunately, the evidence that mandates a broader recognition of the potential issue also provides guidance for mitigation strategies: Distinct differences in the abilities (Weathers, 1984; Weathers and Niedzielski, 1986; Guieysse et al., 2013; Kamp et al., 2013) and kinetics of microalgal N<sub>2</sub>O emissions have been reported as, for example, nitrite-laden C. vulgaris was previously shown to constantly produce N<sub>2</sub>O over 48 h of incubation in darkness

(Guieysse *et al.*, 2013). These divergences indicate differences in pathways and regulatory mechanisms that, in turn, could be exploited to reduce  $N_2O$  emissions during algal cultivation.

#### **EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES**

#### Strains selection and maintenance

The wildtype and mutant *C. reinhardtii* strains 6145c, 704, 409, 217, 112, 530, 124- and 125+ (Table S5) were used in this research. The amiCYP55 strain was created from strain 704 via transformation with the vector pChlamiRNA3-CYP55 by following the protocol described by Molnar et al. (2009). Specific primers for artificial miRNA were amiForCYP55 and amiRevCYP55 (Appendix S3).

All strains were maintained on sterilized solid medium containing 15 g·L<sup>-1</sup> of agar agar in buffered TAP medium (Harris, 2009), i.e. using NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> as N source (0.75 M), and cultivated in TAP medium. The NR- and NiR-deficient mutants were cultivated in similar media where NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup> was substituted to NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> as N source because these strains were unable to grow on NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> and NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup>. Each week, 250 mL Erlenmeyer-flasks (E-flasks) were filled with 125 mL of autoclaved medium and inoculated with a single colony of *C. reinhardtii*. The microalgae were cultivated in a Minitron incubator (Infors HT, Switzerland) at 25 ± 1°C under continuous agitation (180 rpm) and illumination (21 W of PAR m<sup>-2</sup> at the culture surface, using five 15 W Sylvania GRO-LUX coolwhite tubes; Australia), and under an atmosphere of 2% (vol.) CO<sub>2</sub> in air to prevent CO<sub>2</sub> limitation. The E-flasks were closed with cotton tops and autoclaved with the medium prior inoculation.

#### Nitrous Oxide synthesis bioassays

N<sub>2</sub>O production was quantified using gas-chromatography as described in Guieysse *et al.* (2013) and the results herein presented show the total amount of N<sub>2</sub>O produced in the flasks assuming equilibrium between the aqueous and gaseous N<sub>2</sub>O concentration (considering N<sub>2</sub>O losses and pressure changes, Appendix S4). Biomass concentration was quantified as cell dry weight (DW) using the protocol described by Béchet *et al.* (2015) while aqueous NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> and NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> concentrations were quantified using ionic chromatography as described by *Alcántara et al.* (2015). Culture purity was assessed by extracting and amplifying DNA using the primers and methods described by Guieysse *et al.* (2013). To provide algae cultures free of extracellular N, 25-50 mL of 5.5-10 days old axenic *C. reinhardtii* cultures (described above) were centrifuged at 2900 g for 3.5 min, and the cells pellet were re-suspended in N-free and acetate-free TAP medium to reach a concentration of 0.25 g-algae·L<sup>-1</sup> to serve as inocula in subsequent assays. Glass flasks were filled with N-free algal suspension, sealed with rubber septa, and incubated for 24-48 h at  $25\pm1^{\circ}$ C under continuous agitation (180 rpm) and either under continuous illumination (21 W PAR·m<sup>-2</sup>) or in darkness. The following experiments were thus conducted (see Table S1 and S2 and corresponding results for further details):

- 1. *C. reinhardtii* 6145c cultures were supplied different N sources (10 mM  $NO_2^-$ ,  $NO_3^-$ , or  $NH_4^+$ ) or  $NO_2^-$  at various concentrations (2-24 mM).
- 2. Cultures of various C. reinhardtii strains were supplied 10 mM NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> in darkness.
- 3. Cultures of *C. reinhardtii* 6145c were pre-cultivated in modified TAP where molybdate  $(MoO_2^{4-})$  was replaced by orthotungstate  $(WO_2^{4-})$  to inhibit NR.
- 4. C. reinhardtii 6145c cultures were supplied 10 mM of the NOS substrate L-arginine (L-arg ≥ 98%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and/or the NOS-inhibitor Nω-nitro-L-arginine (L-NNA ≥ 98%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA).

- 5. Cultures of *C. reinhardtii* 6145c and *C. reinhardtii* NR mutant 2929 were supplied cyanide (KCN  $\geq$  97%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 2 mM as COX inhibitor.
- 6. Cultures of *C. reinhardtii* NiR mutant M3 and M4 were supplied 10 mM NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> or NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> in darkness or under illumination.
- Cultures of *C. reinhardtii* 704 and the NOR knock down mutant amiCYP55 were supplied 10 mM NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> in darkness.

Over the duration of the study, a positive control (cultures of wildtype 6145c supplied 10 mM nitrite in darkness) was repeated 40 times (Figure 1). The amount of 'background' N<sub>2</sub>O level initially found in the flask was experimentally estimated to  $1.45 \pm 0.18$  nmole (average  $\pm$  SE; n = 56). Based on this value and the standard error of 57% recorded at t = 0.25 h (Figure 1), the limit of quantification was estimated to 2.9 nmole in the flasks (i.e. 200% of the background value). When N<sub>2</sub>O production was higher than this threshold, specific production values (nmole·g·DW<sup>-1</sup>) were calculated as the amount of N<sub>2</sub>O produced a time t (nmole) minus the background level (1.45 nmole), divided by the initial cell concentration (g-DW·L<sup>-1</sup>).

#### **RNA-sequencing assays**

Aliquots of *C. reinhardtii* 6145c microalgal suspensions were withdrawn and poured in serum flasks incubated in darkness (centrifugation was avoided to prevent mechanical stress). Six flasks were thus incubated at constant temperature  $(25 \pm 1^{\circ}C)$  and agitation (180 rpm) for 1 h before NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> (10 mM) was injected into three of the flasks, henceforth referred to as the 'treatments', and the remaining flasks were used as controls. Based on the N<sub>2</sub>O kinetics recorded in darkness (see Results), gas and liquid samples were withdrawn from each flask after 1.25 (t1, 15 min after NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> supply), 4 (t2, 3 h after NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> supply) and 25 hours (t3, 24 h

after NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> supply) of incubation to quantify N<sub>2</sub>O and extract RNA (Appendix S5). This protocol was repeated three times to generate biological replicates for the RNA-seq experiment. The amount of N<sub>2</sub>O recorded for each time point between triplicates over the three repeated experiments never exceeded 14% variability (for each time point n = 9, P = 0.05).

RNA was extracted using a plant NucleoSpin RNA kit (Macherey-Nagel, Germany). The presence of high quality RNA was confirmed in each sample using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer System (Agilent Technologies, Germany). Illumina TruSeq stranded mRNA libraries were synthesized from poly-A purified RNA and the resulting libraries were sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq2500 machine (Otago Genome and Bioinformatics Facility, New Zealand). Adaptor sequences were removed from the 125 bp paired end of resulting reads using the software *fastqMCF* (Aronesty, 2013) before quality trimming (with P = 0.01) using SolexaQA++ v.3.3 (Cox *et al.*, 2010). The reads were aligned to the *C. reinhardtii* v.5.5 genome model (Merchant *et al.*, 2007) using TopHat v2.1.0 (Kim *et al.*, 2013). Read counts were generated from the alignments using htseq-count (Anders *et al.*, 2015) based on the *C. reinhardtii* v.5.5 genome model. Finally, differential expression analysis was performed on the dataset using the R-package DESeq2 (Love *et al.*, 2014) as described in the package vignette. The RNA-seq data has been deposited in the SRA archive under the number GSE90609.

#### Nitric Oxide detection

The fluorescent dye sensitive to NO oxidation DAF-FM diacetate (10  $\mu$ M) was added to microalgae suspensions that were then incubated for 0.5-1 h in serum flasks (25°C, constant agitation at 180 rpm). Aliquots (5 mL) of these suspensions were withdrawn, centrifuged, resuspended in the same volume of N-free medium and supplied with 10 mM NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> in darkness.

The cell suspensions (200 µL) were then observed using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51 microscope, Japan) equipped with a U-MWIBA2 filter block (excitation 460 - 490, emission 510 - 550 nm) (Chang *et al.*, 2013). The specificity of DAF-FM for NO or HNO was tested using Diethylamine NONOate (DEA NONOate  $\geq$ 98%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and Angeli's salt (HNO donor  $\geq$ 99%; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), respectively.
