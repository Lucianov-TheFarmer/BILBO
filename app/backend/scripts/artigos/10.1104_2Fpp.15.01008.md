## 31 **ABSTRACT**

32 The flavonoids are phenylpropanoid-derived metabolites that are ubiquitous in plants, playing 33 many roles in growth and development. Recently, we observed that fruit rinds of yellow casaba 34 melons (*Cucumis melo* Inodorous Group) accumulate naringenin chalcone, a yellow flavonoid 35 pigment. RNA-Seq analysis of bulksbulked segregants, representing the tails of a population 36 segregating for naringenin chalcone accumulation, followed by fine mapping and genetic 37 transformation, we identified a Kelch domain-containing F-box protein coding gene (*CmKFB*) 38 that, when expressed, negatively regulates naringenin chalcone accumulation. Further 39 metabolite analysis indicated that downstream flavonoids are accumulated together with 40 naringenin chalcone, while CmKFB expression diverts the biochemical flux towards coumarins 41 and general phenylpropanoids. These results demonstrate that *CmKFB* functions as a

42 post-transcriptional regulator which diverts flavonoid metabolic flux.

#### 43 **INTRODUCTION**

44 Melons, *Cucumis melo* L*.* (Cucurbitaceae), exhibit extreme diversity in fruit traits, varying in size, 45 shape, external color, aroma, sugar content, acidity, pigmentation and climacteric or 46 non-climacteric fruit ripening (Burger et al., 2010; Cohen et al., 2014). Various classifications of 47 melons have been proposed over the years. The most comprehensive yet easily understood 48 classification distinguishes two subspecies, *agrestis* and *melo,* with 5 and 11 groups respectively 49 (Pitrat et al., 2000).

50 The phenylpropanoid pathway gives rise to a large range of phenolic compounds, 51 including flavonoids, lignins, lignans, coumarins, stilbenes and sinapate esters (Veitch and 52 Grayer, 2008; Petersen et al., 2010), which are involved in many aspects of plant physiology. 53 Phenylpropanoids have roles related to photoprotection (Kim et al., 2008), crosstalk with 54 hormones (Besseau et al., 2007; Yuan et al., 2013), *Rhizobium* symbiosis (Wasson et al., 2006), 55 pollination (Mo et al., 1992), structural components (Pizzi and Cameron, 1986), and 56 plant-animal interactions (Haribal and Renwick, 1996). Recently flavonoids have gained 57 increasing interest as health benefit agents in the human diet (Butelli et al., 2008; Romano et 58 al., 2013).

59 Due to the wide interest in flavonoids, considerable efforts have focused on 60 understanding the regulatory mechanisms of the different branches of their synthesis pathway. 61 Our general understanding is that flavonoid accumulation is largely controlled at the 62 transcriptional level, by transcription factors, regulating the expression of the pathway 63 structural genes (Hartmann et al., 2005; Koes et al., 2005). However, pathway regulation 64 downstream to the transcriptional level is less understood (Tanaka and Uritani, 1977; Pairoba 65 and Walbot, 2003; Deguchi et al., 2013). Lately, Kelch domain containing F-box proteins (KFB) 66 were shown to be involved in regulation of the phenylpropanoid pathway (Shao et al., 2012; 67 Zhang et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2015). F-box proteins serve as substrate recruitment domains 68 in the SCF (Skp1, Cullin, F-box) E3 ubiquitin ligase complexes, targeting protein substrates to 69 26S proteasome dependent degradation, in which the F-box serves as the target recognition 70 factor, mediating the specificity of the complex (Jonkers and Rep, 2009). In plants, the F-box

71 superfamily seems to have gone through vast duplication and specialization. Although the 72 human and *Drosophila* genomes contain only 68 and 33 *F-box* genes, respectively (Ou et al., 73 2003; Jin et al., 2004), the *Arabidopsis* genome contains about 700 members of this superfamily 74 (Gagne et al., 2002). One subgroup of this family, the KFB, found as a single ortholog in the 75 human genome, is highly represented in the *Arabidopsis* genome, comprising at least 103 KFB 76 sequences, most of them still uncharacterized (Schumann et al., 2011). A rice KFB homolog was 77 found to negatively regulate flavonoid accumulation (Shao et al., 2012). Four members of the 78 KFB subfamily in *Arabidopsis* were shown to physically interact with different phenylalanine 79 ammomia-lyases (PAL) isozymes, mediating their degradation, and subsequently decreasing 80 accumulation of an array of various phenylpropanoids, implicating these KFBs as general 81 repressors of the phenylpropanoid pathway (Zhang et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2015).

82 Little is known about the regulation of the flavonoid pathway in the Cucurbitaceae. 83 Flavone derivatives are accumulated in leaves of *Cucumis sativus* (cucumber) and *C. melo* 84 (Krauze-Baranowska and Cisowski, 2001). Kaempferol and quercetin glycosides (flavonol 85 derivatives) accumulate in the reproductive organs of some cucurbits (Imperato, 1980). 86 Recently, we showed that naringenin chalcone (NarCh) is accumulated as the main flavonoid in 87 the fruit rind of some melon varieties, including the yellow casaba melon, 'Noy Amid' (NA; 88 Tadmor et al., 2010).

89 In this study, we used a combination of RNA-Seq of pools of F3 segregants together with 90 fine mapping, to identify *CmKFB*, a protein coding gene which negatively regulates flavonoid 91 accumulation in melon. The repression of the flavonoid pathway results in biochemical flux 92 alternation towards the accumulation of coumarin and other general phenylpropanoids 93 derivatives. Functional verification of *CmKFB* was performed by its transient expression in 94 melon leaves and stable transformation of tomato resulting in altered phenylpropanoid 95 patterns. Our results provide experimental evidence of regulation that alters the flow between 96 the phenylpropanoid pathway branches.

#### 97 **RESULTS**

#### 98 **Characterization of biochemical differences between two melon genotypes differentiating in**

#### 99 **NarCh accumulation**

100 To obtain a general view of the morphological and biochemical differences between the NarCh 101 accumulating (NA) and non-accumulating (TVT) melons, microscopic examinations were 102 performed. Slices of melon rind were stained with DPBA (diphenyl boric acid aminoethyl ester), 103 which fluorescently dyes flavonoid glycosides (Stracke et al., 2007). Under visible light the 104 upper cuticle layer of NA was yellow, probably due to the accumulation of NarCh, while in TVT 105 this layer seems transparent. After DPBA staining and under UV illumination, NA samples 106 exhibited a greenish fluorescence in the tissue that is located at approximately 2 mm under the 107 upper cuticle layer, while the cuticle of TVT exhibited blue fluorescence and lacked the greenish 108 fluorescence under the cuticle (Fig. 1, A-H). This differential fluorescence display suggests that 109 both NA and TVT accumulate different flavonoid compounds in addition to NarCh. The cuticle 110 of NA exhibited no fluorescence after DPBA staining, suggesting the NarCh is accumulated in 111 the cuticle as aglycone and not a glycoside that would be fluorescent under UV light. To further 112 investigate whether the additional fluorescent compounds accumulated in different tissues in 113 NA and TVT are associated with NarCh accumulation, methanol extracts of mature fruit rinds of 114 19 F3 families were fractionated by thin layer chromatography (TLC), stained with DPBA and 115 visualized under UV light. The 19 families were generated from a cross between NA and TVT (12 116 'yellow' and 7 'white' F3 families in "Materials and Methods"; Supplemental Fig. S1). Under 117 these conditions, the fluorescence pattern of the 'yellow' F3 families (NarCh accumulating) was 118 identical with that of the NA parent, while the 'white' F3 families exhibited the same 119 fluorescence pattern as TVT, the non-NarCh accumulating parent, showing association of these 120 fluorescent compounds with NarCh accumulation after two generations (Fig. 1I).

121 DPBA mediated fluorescence of additional tissues of the two melon types indicated that 122 leaves and flowers, regardless the presence/absence of NarCh in their fruits, exhibited similar 123 fluorescence patterns (Supplemental Fig. S2 A and B). The differences in fruit rind fluorescence 124 between the two melon types were evident from 10 days after anthesis (DAA). During fruit 125 development, the fluorescence intensity of yellow/green compounds decreased in 'white' and 126 increased in 'yellow' F3s while blue fluorescent compounds gradually increased during 'white' 127 fruit development and were absent in 'yellow' fruit (Supplemental Fig. S2 C, D and E).

128 Similar staining experiments performed in *Arabidopsis* identified yellow and green DPBA 129 mediated fluorescent compounds as quercetin and kaempferol (flavonols), and blue 130 fluorescence emitting compounds as sinapate glycoside derivatives (Stracke et al., 2007; 131 Stracke et al., 2009). Application of additional staining including Shinoda's test and Zn/HCl 132 (Pew's test) on 'yellow' and 'white' F3 methanol extracts (Supplemental Fig. S3) showed color 133 accumulation in 'yellow' was correlated with the accumulation of flavonols and 134 dihydroflavonols (Pew, 1948), respectively, in contrast to 'white' which was negative to these 135 stainings, suggesting that additional flavonoids were accumulated in addition to NarCh.

136 To further identify these fluorescent compounds, methanolic extracts of both parents (NA 137 and TVT) and bulks of F3 families ('white' and 'yellow') were analyzed with LC-MS 138 (Supplemental Fig. S4, A-D). A similar pattern to the previously presented fluorescence on TLC 139 was observed: the NarCh accumulating parent and the 'yellow' F3 bulk possessed similar 140 characteristics, which are distinctive from the non-NarCh accumulating TVT and the 'white' F3 141 group. An untargeted metabolic analysis was conducted to identify the main compounds that 142 were differently accumulated in these two populations using Agilent's MPP metabolic tools. The 143 means of the total ion counts of the different compounds found in 'yellow' and 'white' groups 144 (each group is comprised of a parental line and a bulk of F3 families) were compared by a 145 volcano plot using a threshold of at least 10 fold difference between the means and a P value of 146 0.05. A total of 99 masses that significantly differentiated between these two groups were 147 identified (Supplemental Fig. S4E). The software could predict molecular formula for 64 148 compounds of these 99 masses, many of them matching different flavonoid glycosides 149 (Supplemental Table S2). To further identify the aglycone backbones which underlined these 150 differences, enzymatic hydrolysis was performed on the samples, after which aglycones were 151 isolated and analyzed with LC-MS. A corresponding similar pattern of statistically significant 152 differential accumulation of compounds between the two groups was observed (Fig. 2; 153 Supplemental Table S3). Identification of the aglycones was performed *via* comparison with 154 authentic standards. Eight compounds were identified, among which caffeic acid, ferulic acid, 155 and aesculetin showed significantly higher accumulation in the non-NarCh accumulating fruit 156 rinds (TVT and 'white' F3 families), while, naringenin, luteolin, kaempferol, quercetin, and traces

157 of dihydrokaempferol were found to specifically accumulate in the NarCh accumulating tissues 158 (NA and 'yellow' F3 families; Supplemental Table S4; Supplemental Fig. S5). Thus, a clear change 159 of the pathway flux could be noted, as evidenced by the metabolic patterns observed between 160 the two groups (Fig. 3).

161 NarCh-accumulating lines accumulate additional downstream flavonoids including: 162 luteolin (a flavone), dihydrokaempferol (a dihydroflavonol), quercetin and kaempferol 163 (flavonols). The non-NarCh accumulating lines over-accumulate mainly caffeic and ferulic acids, 164 in addition to aesculetin (a coumarin), which are branched prior to the flavonoid biosynthetic 165 pathway. Although clear differences in accumulation of caffeic and ferulic acids was observed 166 between the NarCh accumulating types, smaller levels of caffeic and ferulic acids were 167 detectable in the NarCh accumulating lines (retention times 3.7, 6.3 minutes in Fig. 2 ,A and B; 168 Supplemental table S4), whereas flavonoids were not detected in the non-NarCh accumulating 169 types.

#### 170 **Genetic mapping of the locus governing flavonoid accumulation**

171 Previous studies on fruit rind NarCh accumulation in an F2 population derived from the cross NA 172 × TVT, suggested that this trait is governed by a single gene (Tadmor et al., 2010). To better 173 identify the genetic factor regulating flavonoid accumulation in melon fruit rind, we 174 comparatively analyzed the transcriptome of the 'yellow' and 'white' bulks of F3 families, a 175 methodology that is based on the classical 'bulk segregant analysis' approach (Michelmore et 176 al., 1991; Supplemental Fig. S1; "Materials and Methods"). RNA extracted from developing 177 fruits of 'yellow' and 'white' F3 families was bulked and sequenced (Supplemental Table S5). As 178 at the time we carried out this portion of the research the melon genome sequence was not yet 179 available, a *de-novo* assembled transcriptome was developed that included 87,336 unigenes. All 180 RNA-Seq reads of the two bulks at three developmental stages were aligned to the unigenes for 181 the identification of single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) that existed between the 'yellow' 182 and 'white' bulks. Upon the availability of the melon genome (Garcia-Mas et al., 2012) SNPs 183 were mapped to the melon genome. One hundred and seventy nine SNPs were found to 184 differentiate between the two bulks (Supplemental Table S6), 164 of them are located on

185 scaffold 16 (Linkage group 10) of the melon genome, and the majority of them are localized in a 186 region of about 0.5Mb (Fig. 4A), suggesting that the gene controlling NarCh accumulation in 187 melon fruit rind is located on scaffold 16 of chromosome 10. The remaining 15 SNPs were 188 scattered randomly throughout the genome suggesting that they are not specifically related to 189 flavonoid accumulation.

190 For a more detailed characterization of the genetic locus that governs NarCh 191 accumulation, analysis was performed on additional 784 plants, derived from 14 F3 families 192 segregating for fruit rind NarCh accumulation. Mature fruit of this additional plant material 193 exhibited three phenotypes in the field: yellow rind, pale yellow rind and white rind (Fig. 4B). 194 Phenotypic distribution of these F3 individuals supported a 1:2:1 ratio (yellow : pale yellow : 195 white), expected for a single co-dominant gene (chi-square = 2.593, *P* = 0.27; Fig. 4C). Yellow 196 fruit were assumed to be homozygous for the dominant allele of the gene governing NarCh 197 accumulation in melon fruit rind, white fruit were assumed to be homozygous recessive and 198 pale yellow, heterozygotes. DNA extracted from these F3 individuals was used to map the locus 199 governing NarCh accumulation by the analysis of recombinants.

200 At first, three SNPs, UN22458, 06224 and 10338, were selected for genotyping of the 201 segregating population (SNP locations are marked with arrows in Fig. 4A; Supplemental Tables 202 S6 and S8) to screen the segregating population. UN10338 showed the highest genotype to 203 phenotype association (Fig. 4E). Fine mapping using recombinants, which were identified by 204 these three markers, was carried out using nine selected SNPs, dispersed in the genomic region 205 restricted by UN10338 and UN06224 (Supplemental Fig. S6; Supplemental Table S8). A number 206 of informative recombinants were identified, including two plants with white fruit (no. 1030 207 and 826), three pale yellow fruit plants (no. 539, 1041 and 1281), and two plants with yellow 208 fruits (no. 1442 and 720; Fig. 4G). Combined phenotypic and genotypic data of the plants 209 indicated that the genetic factor regulating flavonoid accumulation in the melon segregating 210 population is located between high resolution melt (HRM) markers no. 2 and 4 (Fig. 4G). All the 211 recombinant F3 plants that were genotyped showed a complete co-segregation between their 212 genotype at this chromosomal region and their fruit color phenotype. Seventeen protein coding 213 genes, from *MELO3C01178* to *MELO3C01194* (Supplemental Table S7)*,* are located within this

214 approximately 100 Kb chromosomal region, from position 2,156,094 to position 2,255,982 on 215 scaffold 16 of chromosome 10.

#### 216 **Characterization of a candidate gene regulating flavonoid accumulation**

MELO3C011980, which is annotated as being similar to F-box/kelch-repeat protein At1g23390 (*Arabidopsis thaliana*) is one of the 17 genes that are located in the region restricted by HRM markers no. 2 and no. 4. *At1g23390* KFB protein coding gene hasn't been functionally characterized (Supplemental Fig. S7). MELO3C011980 was the most significantly differentially expressed gene (DEG) between the two bulks; while thousands of raw reads were generated in 'white' bulks during all three analyzed developmental stages, only a few reads were detected in 'yellow' bulks (Supplemental Fig. S8A). We named MELO3C011980 *CmKFB.* The RNA-Seq data could not detect any sequence polymorphism between *CmKFB* 'yellow' and 'white' alleles due to the extremely low number of reads derived from the 'yellow' allele. To identify DNA sequence polymorphism that might affect *CmKFB* differential expression, sequencing of the upstream portion of the gene was performed. A deletion of 12-bp in the 5' UTR of *CmKFB* was found in 'TVT' parental lines (Supplemental Fig. S8B). In order to indicate function of this deletion we screened a range of C. melo accessions differing in peel color. This deletion was present in eight analyzed melon accessions that do not accumulate NarCh and is absent in all analyzed melon accessions that accumulate NarCh (Supplemental Fig. S8C, Supplemental Table 232 S1).

233 As indicated earlier, DPBA staining of leaves exhibited similar yellow UV fluorescence 234 patterns in both 'white' and 'yellow' F3 plants, suggesting both types accumulate flavonoids in 235 their leaves (Supplemental Fig. S2A). To further associate the role of *CmFBK* with flavonoid 236 accumulation, we conducted qRT-PCR analysis of *CmKFB* in leaves. As expected, a very low 237 expression of *CmKFB* was found in leaves irrelevant to fruit rind NarCh accumulation 238 (Supplemental Fig. S8D).

239 Further analysis of the effect of *CmKFB* expression on the flavonoid pathway was 240 performed on individual F3 plants derived from the self-pollination of *CmKFB* heterozygous F2 241 plants. The fruit rinds of five F3 individuals from each of the visual phenotypic groups, white, 242 pale yellow and yellow, were analyzed for *CmKFB* expression by qRT-PCR. All white fruits

243 showed strong expression of *CmKFB* and all yellow fruits showed very weak expression, similar 244 to its expression in the F3 bulks derived from the RNA-Seq analysis (Supplemental Fig. S8A). The 245 pale yellow fruit showed intermediate expression of *CmKFB* gene (Fig. 5B), which is correlated 246 with the intermediate color phenotype and intermediate fluorescence pattern of DPBA stained 247 fruit rind methanol extracts fractionated on TLC and visualized under UV light (Fig. 5C).

248 PCR analysis of the *CmKFB* 5' UTR deletion area was performed on genomic DNA of the 249 analyzed F3 plants, verified that 'white' are homozygotes to the TVT allele, 'yellow' are 250 homozygotes to the NA allele, while the pale yellow are heterozygotes (Fig. 5D). Interestingly, 251 similar PCR analysis with cDNA suggests heterozygous to accumulate only the TVT 252 non-flavonoid accumulating parent allele (Fig. 5E).

253

254 We used the RNA-seq data to analyze the expression level (RPKM) of all gene family 255 members of the genes coding for major enzymes of the flavonoid pathway, including *PAL*, 256 cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (*C4H*), and chalcone synthase (*CHS*). Only one major ortholog in each 257 family was found to be significantly up regulated during development (*MELO3C025786*, 258 *MELO3C019585*, and *MELO3C014767* respectively; Supplemental Fig. S9), in correlation with 259 NarCh accumulation pattern in NA (Supplemental Fig. S1), and phenylpropanoid accumulation 260 in TVT, as observed by the increased blue fluorescence (Supplemental Fig. S2), suggesting only a 261 single ortholog of these gene families participated in the fruit rind pathway in both melon 262 types. On the other hand, 4-Coumarate:CoA ligase (*4CL*) gene family members exhibited various 263 expression patterns during fruit development, one of which matched the expression pattern of 264 the previous three genes (*MELO3C024724,* Supplemental Fig. S9). No significant differences in 265 transcript abundance were found between 'white' and 'yellow' that could explain the 266 biochemical differences between these two bulks.

267 The AtKFBs homologs (KFB01, KFB20, KFB39, and KFB50) which were reported to regulate 268 the phenylpropanoid pathway, have been shown to be involved in PAL degradation resulting in 269 down regulation of the phenylpropanoid pathway (Zheng et al 2013; Zheng et al 2015). The 270 biochemical evidence presented in this study suggests CmKFB to regulate a downstream point

271 to these Arabidopsis KFBs. In order to access this point melon KFB homologs (Supplemental 272 Table S9) and the Arabidopsis KFBs (Zhang et al 2015) were phylogenetically analyzed 273 (Supplemental Figure S11). This analysis shows the four AtKFBs to cluster with the melon 274 ME3C014678 and MELO3C009596 homologs, while CmKFB clusters with the *Arabidopsis* 275 At1g23390 in a subtree which is not tightly linked to the previous one.

#### 277 **Functional analysis of** *CmKFB*

278 To functionally validate the ability of CmKFB to divert the flavonoid pathway biochemical flux, 279 two different transformation systems were applied: A virus induced gene expression (VIGE) of 280 *CmKFB* in melon leaves and a stable transformation of tomato MP1 plants with *CmKFB* under 281 the constitutive 35S promoter.

282 VIGE is an established experimental procedure to analyze the effect of gene 283 over-expression in cucurbits (Harpaz-Saad et al., 2007). Our chemical analysis of melon leaves 284 suggested that flavonoid accumulation in leaves is irrelevant to flavonoid accumulation in 285 melon fruit rind (Supplemental Fig. S2A), which is associated with very low expression of *CmKFB* 286 (Supplemental Fig. S8D), thus overexpression of *CmKFB* in leaves appeared like a suitable 287 experimental system to validate *CmKFB* function. Fourteen days after VIGE infection, *CmKFB* 288 expression was significantly increased as indicated by qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 6C). Leaf methanol 289 extracts of VIGE-*CmKFB* and the two controls (virus only and VIGE-GFP) were fractionated with 290 TLC, stained with DPBA and observed under UV light. *CmKFB* overexpression exhibited 291 enhanced blue fluorescence and reduction in yellow-green fluorescence accumulation in 292 comparison to the controls (Fig. 6E). Further LC-MS analysis of leaf methanol extracts showed 293 that VIGE*-CmKFB* accumulated higher amounts of compounds (retention time 2.2-4.5 minutes) 294 while lower amounts of other compounds eluting from the column during the time range of 5.5 295 to 9.5 minutes when compared to the two controls (Fig. 6, F-H). To identify the main aglycones 296 differentiating between the controls and VIGE-*CmKFB*, enzymatic hydrolysis was performed and 297 the hydrolyzed samples were analyzed by LC-MS and compared to authentic standards. Three 298 aglycones were identified to be up-regulated by VIGE-*CmKFB*: caffeic acid, salycilic acid, and 299 aesculetin and three down-regulated: luteolin, naringenin, and quercetin (Fig. 6, I-J). These 300 differences are less significant than those observed in fruits most probably due smaller 301 difference in *CmKFB* expression, and incomplete infection of the virus, as observed in the 302 AG-GFP control (Fig. 6A). VIGE-*CmKFB* down-regulated compounds were flavonoids while the 303 upregulated compounds belong to side chains of the phenylpropanoid and coumarin pathways, 304 upstream of the flavonoid pathway, indicating *CmKFB* expression in melon leaves mediated a 305 metabolic flux alternation as was observed in melon fruits.

306 To functionally analyze *CmKFB* in a heterologous system we used a stable transformation 307 of tomato MP-1 cultivars. Tomato plants accumulate anthocyanins in vegetative tissues in 308 response to stress and accumulate NarCh, kaempferol and quercetin in their fruit peels 309 (Mintz-Oron et al., 2008). A binary vector harboring *CmKFB* and a *GFP* reporter gene was used 310 for stable transformation. Two transgenic plants were isolated. These plants did not accumulate 311 visible amounts of anthocyanins in any observed tissues: leaves, stems, flowers (Supplemental 312 Fig. S10). At the ripe fruit stage, fruit peels of both transformed T0 plants accumulated 313 significantly reduced NarCh in the cuticle, compared to the MP-1 control (Fig. 7 A and B). In 314 addition, fruit sections under UV light after DPBA staining exhibited a shift from yellow to blue 315 fluorescence in the *CmKFB* transgenic fruit (Fig. 7C), similar to VIGE-*CmKFB* leaves (Fig. 6E), and 316 to melon fruit rinds (Fig. 1I). T1 seeds from both transgenic plants were germinated and grown 317 under cold stress conditions. After 15 days, a clear difference could be observed between 318 anthocyanin accumulating seedlings and seedlings which did not accumulate anthocyanins. The 319 T1 plantlets with anthocyanins did not exhibit GFP fluorescence while those that did not 320 accumulate anthocyanins exhibited GFP fluorescence (Fig. 7D) , indicating overexpression of 321 *CmKFB* in tomato to repress flavonoid accumulation in a similar manner as observed in melon.

## 323 **DISCUSSION**

## 324 **Bulk segregant analysis of RNA-Seq and genetic mapping**

325 Bulk segregant analysis (BSA) is a method developed to identify genetic markers linked to a 326 specific trait (Michelmore et al., 1991). BSA has been widely used ever since, uncovering

327 genetic markers and genes in a wide variety of plants. The rapid development of new 328 sequencing technologies and computation systems allows a very efficient use of BSA while 329 using next generation sequencing technologies to discover polymorphism that discriminate 330 between the bulks (Liu et al., 2012; Trick et al., 2012). Combining BSA with RNA-Seq and 331 detailed metabolite analyses enabled a relatively rapid and accurate isolation and 332 characterization of *CmKFB*, the gene of interest in this work.

333 A high level of recombination occurred in the 2 Mbp region in the F3 segregating 334 population (Fig. 4, D-F), Such a recombination rate in this genomic region was a significant 335 factor enabling high resolution mapping, which resulted in less than 200 SNP that differentiate 336 between the bulks, most of them localized in an 0.5 Mbp genomic region in scaffold 16 of 337 chromosome 10 (Fig. 4A), while using a relatively small number of participants in each bulk (12 338 F3 families in the 'yellow' bulk and only 7 in the 'white). Recombinant analysis of the 339 segregating population enabled to further fine map the genetic loci associated with NarCh 340 accumulation to a genomic region of 100 Kb, which consisted of 17 protein coding genes. 341 *CmKFB* was one of these 17 coding genes, and showed the most significant difference in 342 expression between the 'yellow' and the 'white' F3 bulks, suggesting *CmKFB* as a primary 343 candidate gene.

344 RNA-Seq analysis of 'yellow' (accumulating NarCh) and 'white' (do not accumulate NarCh) 345 developing fruit of F3 segregant pools indicated that *CmKFB* is expressed at a very low level in 346 fruits that accumulate NarCh while it is highly expressed in NarCh non-accumulating fruits. Due 347 to the low abundance of *CmKFB* RNA-Seq reads obtained in the 'yellow' bulks we could not 348 define sequence variation between the 'yellow' and 'white' alleles of this gene. DNA sequence 349 analysis identified a 12-bp deletion in the 5' UTR of the 'white' bulk and TVT. The 5' UTR 12-bp 350 deletion was consistently associated with flavonoid accumulation in our segregating population 351 (Fig. 5D) and in different melon accessions (Supplemental Fig. S8C). Interestingly, monitoring 352 allelic expression on cDNA (Fig. 5E), showed heterozygotes F3 to accumulate only the TVT 353 non-flavonoid accumulating parent allele, suggesting *CmKBF* allelic expression to be affected by 354 allelic variation. Motif analysis using JASPAR (Mathelier et al., 2014) suggested this deletion to 355 disrupted a putative binding site of HMG-1 (High Mobility Group box), which is a chromatin

356 remodeling factor (Grosschedl et al., 1994; Webster et al., 1997; Ikeda et al., 2011), suggesting 357 differential epigenetic allelic states to result in differential allelic *CmKFB* expression. 358 Nevertheless, the association of the 12-bp deletion with *CmKFB* expression, the tissue 359 specificity of this expression and the co-dominant nature of *CmKFB* expression need further 360 studies.

361 **Metabolites analysis and** *CmKFB* **function.** 

362 There is growing interest in manipulating the metabolic flux through different phenylpropanoid 363 pathway branches for a number of reasons, for example, their association with plant tolerance 364 to different biotic and abiotic stresses, commercial and consumer health impact of increased 365 bioactive compounds in agricultural products, and alteration of the lignin biosynthesis pathway 366 (Liu et al., 2014). An example of such metabolic flux alternation is the silencing of *Arabidopsis* 367 HCT (hydroxycinnamoyl CoA: shikimate hydroxycinnamoyl transferase) that led to repression of 368 lignin biosynthesis and redirection of the metabolic flux toward the flavonoid pathway (Besseau 369 et al., 2007).

370

371 Various transcription factors were shown to divert the phenylpropanoid metabolic flux. 372 *ZmMYB3*, a direct repressor of *COMT* and *AtF5H* genes, over-expression in *Arabidopsis* results 373 in reduction in sinaopylmalate and lignin accumulation while enhancing accumulation of ferulic 374 acid, coumaric acid and anthocyanins (Fornalé et al., 2010). In addition, the colorless fruit peel *y* 375 mutant in tomato was found to be a mutation causing down regulation of *SlMYB12*, a TF that 376 acts as a positive regulator of an array of phenylpropanoid structural genes. Interestingly, this 377 mutation exhibits elevated accumulation of caffeic and ferullic compounds, accompanied by 378 reduction in flavonoid content (Adato et al., 2009), similarly to the flux alteration observed in 379 this work.

380

381 Biochemical analysis of melon fruit rinds showed that NarCh accumulation is 382 accompanied by the accumulation of downstream flavonoids, as observed by DPBA staining

383 (Fig. 1), Pew's and Shinoda's tests (Supplemental Fig. S3), and LC-MS analysis (Fig. 2). This 384 accumulation is associated with significant decline in *CmKFB* transcription in a dosage 385 dependent manner (Fig. 5), and reduction in caffeic acid, ferulic acid and aesculetin 386 accumulation, which are branched from the phenylpropanoid to flavonoid pathway (Fig. 3). No 387 significant differences in the expression pattern of structural genes of the flavonoid pathway 388 were found based on our RNA-Seq data analysis (Supplemental Fig. S9), which could explain the 389 biochemical differences between 'yellow' and 'white'. The flux alternation demonstrated here 390 can be explained by a negative post translational regulation of CmKFB at the entry point of the 391 flavonoid pathway. This biochemical flux alternation was observed both when CmKFB was 392 transiently expressed in melon leaves, resulting in elevation of salycilic acid, caffeic acid and 393 aesculetin accumulation, accompanied by reduction in naringenin, luteolin and quercetin 394 accumulation (Fig. 6), and by stable expression in tomato, resulting in elevated blue 395 fluorescence accompanied by reduction in NarCh accumulation in fruits, and anthocyanins in 396 leaves and flowers (Fig. 7; Supplemental Fig. S10). The functionality of CmKFB in the tomato 397 system indicates the possible general role of this gene in plants. CmKFB is most probably 398 involved in proteasome dependent protein degradation mechanism mediated by the SCF E3 399 ubiquitin ligase complex, as has been previously reported in *Arabidopsis* (Zhang et al., 2013; 400 Zhang et al., 2015). In these studies, four closely related KFB orthologs (AtKFB01, AtKFB20, 401 AtKFB39 and AtKFB50) were found to target different PAL isozymes, which serve as the entry 402 point to the phenylpropanoid pathway, to proteasome dependent degradation, resulting in 403 reduced accumulation of a wide array of compounds: flavonols, anthocyanins, condensed 404 tannis, sinapic acid esters and lignin. In this study, PAL doesn't appear to be a suitable 405 candidate as a target of CmKFB. The expression pattern of the single PAL ortholog 406 (*MELO3C025786*, Figure S9), which matches both the flavonoid and phenylpropanoid 407 accumulation patterns during development (Supplemental Figs. S1 and S2) suggests that this 408 gene is functionally active in both melon types. Phylogenetic analysis of melon and *Arabidopsis*  409 KFB proteins shows the four characterized AtKFBs and CmKFB to cluster in two distinct 410 phylogenetic clades (Supplemental Figure S11), suggesting the presence of two distinctive 411 functional groups, which are conserved between the *Brassicales* and the *Cucurbitales* orders,

412 further suggesting different substrate specificity. Observing the biochemical branch point 413 between the two melon types, a natural candidate as a target of CmKFB is 4CL. This enzyme is 414 encoded in all plants by multiple genes, which differ in substrate specificity of the paralogs, and 415 is considered one of the main regulatory points in directing the phenylpropanoid metabolic 416 pathway (Ehlting et al., 1999; Hamberger and Hahlbrock, 2004). This option is further supported 417 by significant expression of multiple paralogs of 4CL in melon fruit (Supplemental Fig. S9). On 418 the other hand, the degradation of MELO3C014767, which is the only significant ortholog of 419 *CHS* expressed in melon fruit rind might also result in the biochemical differences which were 420 observed. This work suggests that CmKFB regulates either 4CL or CHS enzymatic function via a 421 protein degradation mechanism, either directly by targeting one of these enzymes to 422 proteolytic degradation, or by modulating an additional factor, which regulates one of these 423 enzymes function.

424

425 In summary, we have identified a Kelch repeat F-Box coding gene (*CmKFB*) which 426 negatively regulates flavonoid accumulation in melon fruit by shifting the metabolic flux 427 towards general phenylpropanoid products, including caffeic and ferulic acids and coumarin 428 (aesculetin). Characterization of this natural variation in melon might prove useful for plant 429 breeders in tailoring new cultivars.

## 431 **MATERIALS AND METHODS**

## 432 **Plant material**

433 A set of 18 melon cultivars were selected to represent rind color variation in *Cucumis melo*. 434 They included the parental lines of the analyzed segregating population and representatives of 435 five major taxonomic groups of melon, which accumulate or lack NarCh, with both climacteric 436 and non-climacteric modes of fruit ripening (Supplemental Table S1). Plants were grown under 437 standard conditions in the field and greenhouse at the Newe Ya'ar Research Center.

## 438 **F3 population establishment**

439 A population segregating for fruit NarCh accumulation was established by crossing two casaba 440 melons, 'Noy Amid' (NA) – a NarCh accumulating melon inbred line with a canary yellow fruit 441 rind and 'Tendral Verde Tardio' (TVT) – a NarCh lacking inbred line with a green fruit rind. Two 442 hundred and thirteen F2 plants derived from this cross visually segregated to four fruit rind 443 color phenotypes: green-yellow, green, yellow and white fitting the expected 9:3:3:1 Mendelian 444 ratio for two independent genes, green and yellow. All F2 fruit rinds were analyzed by HPLC for 445 NarCh content (Tadmor et al., 2010). To define F3 families that are homozygous to either NarCh 446 fruit accumulation or non-accumulation, 50 F3 families were selected, 30 with a yellow fruit rind 447 F2 progenitors and 20 with a white fruit rind F2 progenitors. Thirty plants from each family were 448 grown in the winter of 2010 at Ein Tamar in the Northern Arava Valley, Israel. Of these 50 449 families, 12 accumulating NarCh were selected phenotypically, the fruits of all plants in each 450 family having yellow rinds. In addition, seven F3 families, non-accumulating NarCh were 451 selected phenotypically, for the fruits of all plants in each family having white rinds. Thirty 452 plants of each of these 19 families were grown in open field of Newe Ya'ar in the spring of 453 2010. Female flowers were tagged on the day of anthesis and three biological replicates of fruit 454 rind from each F3 family were sampled at three fruit developmental stages: 10 days after 455 anthesis (DAA), 20DAA, and ripe.

#### 456 **RNA-Seq, data processing and de novo assembly**

457 Fruit rind RNA extraction for RNA-Seq analysis was performed according to Portnoy et al. 458 (2011). Twelve samples (two replicates of three developmental stages: 10 DAA, 20 DAA and 459 mature fruit rind of 'yellow' and 'white' F3 families) were used to construct RNA-Seq libraries 460 following the standard Illumina protocol. The libraries were sequenced on the Illumina 461 HiSeq2000 system with the paired-end mode. The sequencing was performed at the 462 Biotechnology Center at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. The raw Illumina 463 RNA-Seq reads were first processed to remove low quality regions and adaptor sequences using 464 an in-house perl script. The RNA-Seq reads were further aligned to the ribosomal RNA database 465 (Quast et al., 2013) using Bowtie (Langmead et al., 2009) allowing two mismatches and the

466 mappable reads were discarded. The resulting high-quality cleaned reads were assembled *de*  467 *novo* into contigs using Trinity with "min\_kmer\_cov" set to 2 (Grabherr et al., 2011). To remove 468 the redundancy of Trinity-generated contigs, they were further assembled *de novo* using 469 iAssembler with minimum percent identify (-p) set to 99 (Zheng et al., 2011).

#### 470 **Gene expression quantification and differential expression analysis**

471 The high-quality cleaned RNA-Seq reads were aligned to the assembled melon transcripts with 472 the Bowtie program (Langmead et al., 2009) allowing one mismatch. Following alignments, raw 473 counts for each melon transcript and each sample were derived and normalized to reads per 474 kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (RPKM). Differentially expressed genes 475 between white and yellow fruits at the same stages were identified with the DESeq package 476 (Anders and Huber, 2010). Raw p-values of multiple tests were corrected using FDR (Benjamini 477 and Hochberg, 1995).

#### 478 **SNP and small indel identification from the RNA-Seq data**

479 To identify SNPs and small indels between yellow and white genotypes, identical RNA-Seq reads 480 from each library were first collapsed into a single sequence. The resulting unique reads were 481 then aligned to the assembled melon transcripts using BWA (Li and Durbin, 2009) allowing one 482 mismatch, one gap opening and one gap extension. Following mapping, SNPs and small indels 483 were identified based on the mpileup files generated by SAMtools (Li et al., 2009). The 484 identified SNPs and small indels were supported by at least five independent reads and had an 485 allele frequency of at least 0.8.

## 486 **Phylogenetic analysis**

Melon KFB protein sequences were retrieved through BLAST searches in the melon genome website (https://melonomics.net/) and conserved domains were verified against the IterPro protein database. Arabidopsis KBF homologs were adopted from Zhang et al. (2015). Protein sequences were aligned by the ClustalW program. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the MEGA package version 6 (Tamura et al., 2013) using the neighbor-joining method, with the pairwise deletion option.

#### 493 **Fluorescent probes**

494 UN22458 SNP (SNP no. 86, Supplemental Table S6) genotyping was performed in ABI PRISM 495 7000 sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems). Each reaction contained 0.25 µL Assay 496 mix (SNP genotyping mix, Biosearch Technologies), 5 µL reaction mix (Genotyping ToughMix, 497 ROX, Quanta Biosysyems), 1 µL DNA, and 3.75 µL water. UN06224 and UN10338 SNP (SNPs no. 498 39 and 54, Supplemental Table S6) genotyping was performed in StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR 499 system (Applied Biosystems), and each reaction contained 4 µL Mix (TaqMan genotyping 500 master mix, Applied Biosystems), 0.4 µL Assay mix (TaqMan SNP genotyping Assay, Applied 501 Biosystems), 0.8 µL DNA, and 2.8 µL water. Primers are listed in Supplemental Table S8.

#### 502 **High Resolution Melt (HRM) analysis**

503 DNA samples were diluted into equal concentrations of 100 ng/µL using NanoDrop. Samples 504 were amplified on an Eco Real-Time PCR system (Illumina). Each sample contained 1 µL DNA, 505 0.2 µL of 10 mM of each primer, 5 µL reaction mix (FastSYBR green master mix, Applied 506 Biosystems), and 3.6 µL ddH2O, programmed as specified by the enzyme manufacturer, and 507 analyzed with the Eco version 4 software. Primers for each HRM SNP marker are specified in 508 Supplemental Table S8.

#### 509 **Virus-induced gene expression**

510 A ZYMV-AGII (AGII) potyvirus-based vector system (Arazi et al., 2001) was used. To overexpress 511 *CmKFB,* a GFP AGII vector was digested with *Pst*I and *Sal*I enzymes to remove the GFP fragment. 512 *CmKFB* coding sequence (CDS) was amplified by PCR from TVT melon cDNA with primers 513 containing linkers to the corresponding *Pst*I and *Sal*I restriction sites (Supplemental Table S8), 514 and ligated to the digested vector (T4 DNA ligase, Thermo Scientific). Particle bombardment 515 inoculation of AG-GFP and AG-CmKFB cDNA was performed using a handgun device (Gal-On et 516 al., 1997) on cotyledons of 14 day old NA melon seedlings. After inoculation, seedlings were placed in a growth chamber, with a 16h photoperiod and at 25<sup>o</sup> 517 C for 14 days.

#### 518 **Tomato stable transformation**

519 *CmKFB* was amplified from TVT melon cDNA in two PCR steps, isolating the CDS with additional

520 39-bp linkers on each side of the gene, generating truncated AttL sites for direct recombination 521 to pK7WG2D Gateway binary destination vector (Invitrogen) by LR clonase (Fu et al., 2008; 522 Ischebeck et al., 2011; Supplemental Table S8). Cotyledons of the 'MP1' tomato were 523 transformed with *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* GV3101 that harbored the vector. After 48 h of 524 co-cultivation in the dark (3% glucose, 0.1 mg/L IAA, 1 mg/L Zeatin, 100 µM AS), explants were 525 transferred to a shoot-induction medium (3% glucose, 0.1 mg/L IAA, 1 mg/L Zeatin, 100 mg/L 526 kanamycin, 500 mg/L claforan), under light. Regenerated shoots were moved to a 527 shoot-elongation medium (3% glucose, 0.1 mg/L Zeatin, 100 mg/L kanamycin, 500 mg/L 528 claforan). Elongated shoots were transferred to a rooting medium (1/2 MS, 2% sucrose, 2 mg/L 529 IBA).

#### 530 **RT-PCR**

531 Reactions were performed on an Eco RT-PCR system (Illumina). Each sample contained 1µL 532 cDNA, 0.2 µL of each primer (10 mM), 5 µL reaction mix (FastSYBR green master mix, Applied 533 Biosystems), and 3.6 µL ddH2O, programmed as specified by manufacturer, and analyzed with 534 Eco version 4 software.

#### 535 **Flavonoid extraction and staining methods**

536 Fresh fruit rind or leaf samples were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80o 537 C. Before extraction, samples were ground to a fine powder with a mortar and 538 pestle in the presence of liquid nitrogen. Two grams of ground tissue were extracted in 5 mL methanol (MeOH), vortexed for 10 seconds, incubated for 10 minutes in 60 539 <sup>o</sup>C and vortexed 540 again. Samples were then centrifuged at 10,000 *g* for 5 minutes and the upper methanol phase 541 was collected. For DPBA (diphenyl boric acid aminoethyl ester) staining, 1 µL of the methanolic 542 extract was applied on an HPTLC (silica gel 60, Merck) and developed with ethyl acetate:acetic 543 acid:H2O in ratio 67:17.1:17.1 (v/v). After separation, the plate was dried with a hair dryer, 544 sprayed with DPBA (Sigma) dissolved in MeOH (1% w/v), dried again, and visualized under a UV 545 light (320 nm).

546 Additional flavonoid staining protocols performed on the methanol extracts included the 547 Zinc/HCl test (Pew's Test), for which Zinc powder and a drop of 5N HCl were added to the plant

548 flavonoid extract, and the Magnesium/HCl test (Shinoda's Test) which was applied in the same 549 way as the Zinc/HCl test, except that magnesium wire was used instead of Zinc powder.

#### 550 **LC-MS**

551 The method used was modified from De Vos et al. (2007). Methanol extraction was carried out 552 as described above. Enzymatic hydrolysis was conducted using 1 mL of the evaporated 553 methanol extracts. Samples were speed-vac evaporated and supplemented with an enzymatic 554 solution containing 1 mg/mL of each: cellulase, pectinase, and β-glucosidase (Sigma), dissolved in 0.1M NaAc pH 5.3 solution, and incubated at 37<sup>o</sup> 555 C overnight. Aglycones were extracted with 556 500 mL ethyl acetate. The upper ethyl acetate phase was removed, evaporated, and pellet 557 re-dissolved in 300 µL MeOH. Both samples, before and after hydrolysis, were supplemented 558 with ddH₂O and formic acid (final concentrations of 25% and 0.1% respectively) and filtered 559 through a PFE 0.2 µm filter (Pall Life Sciences).

560 Mass spectral analysis was conducted using an LC-MS-TOF (LC: 1290 infinity system 561 Agilent Technologies, MS-TOF: 6224-TOF-LC-MS Agilent Technologies), in ESI negative mode. 562 Separation of compounds was performed using Zorbax Extended C18 RRHT 2.1\*50 mm, 1.8 µm 563 column. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (Phase 'A'), and 0.1% formic 564 acid in acetonitrile (phase 'B'). A gradient program was used as follows: 95% to 65% 'A' over 15 565 minutes, 65% to 25% 'A' over 40 seconds, 25% 'A' held over 2 minutes, 25% to 95% 'A' over 1.5 566 minutes, 95% held over 2 minutes at constant flow rate of 0.19 ml/min. Analysis of the masses 567 was performed with the MassHunter qualitative analysis software (version B.05.00, Agilent 568 Technologies). Determination of differential mass expression between samples was performed 569 using the Mass Profiler Professional (MPP) software (Agilent Technologies).

#### 570 **Microscopy**

571 DPBA-stained tissue of handmade slices were observed with an Olympus BX61 microscope 572 coupled with a U-HGLGPS illumination system, Olympus 20x/0.50 objective, using a U-MNBV2 573 filter (excitation 420-440nm, emission >475nm). Images were collected with a digital camera 574 (DP73, Olympus), and processed with cellSens Dimension software (Olympus).

575 Tomato seedling fluorescence was observed with a Leica M205FA stereomicroscope, 576 Leica 10472649 Planapo 0.63x objective, under a GFP3 filter. Images were collected with a 577 digital camera (DFC495, Leica) and processed with the Leica Application Suit V3.8 software.
